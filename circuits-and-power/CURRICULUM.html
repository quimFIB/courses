Circuits and Power

Seventeen units on how electrical energy gets from where it is made to where it is used — starting from the fact that it does not travel inside the wire. Circuit theory is derived from Maxwell's equations as an approximation with stated hypotheses, network theory is done as linear algebra on a graph, and every part ends at a real system: a transformer on a pole, a 400 kV line, a DC cable under the Pyrenees, a phone on a charging pad. Assumes Mechanics to Relativity through unit 24.

The premise

Four commitments. The first says what circuit theory is; the second answers the question the course exists for; the third is how it is taught; the fourth is what keeps it honest.

Circuit theory is an approximation to Maxwell's equations, and the course says which one. Most courses start from Ohm's and Kirchhoff's laws as axioms and never return to them. Here they are theorems with hypotheses. Unit 02 derives the current law from charge conservation and the voltage law from Faraday's law, under three stated conditions — the circuit is small against a wavelength, charge piles up only inside capacitors, changing flux stays inside inductors — and then shows the experiment in which the voltage law fails: two voltmeters on the same two points, reading 0.1 V and 0.9 V of opposite sign, both correct. From then on the course knows exactly when it may forget the fields, and each time it must remember them — transmission lines, the skin effect, antennas, wireless charging — it says which hypothesis has been dropped.

Energy flows through the fields, and the course follows it. The answer to how is power transferred? is Poynting's theorem, which Mechanics to Relativity unit 24 already proved: energy reaches a resistor through its sides, from the space around the wire, and the wire's job is to guide the field. Unit 01 computes this for a cable and then proves the general statement — the Poynting flux into any quasi-static component equals v·i — so that p = vi becomes a theorem, with the conditions under which it holds. Unit 08 finds the energy riding in a cable's insulation, unit 15 watches it cross an air gap, and branch 16 follows it into the far field.

Mathematics carries the definitions, and the engineering is why they matter. Kirchhoff's laws say that branch currents lie in the kernel of a graph's incidence matrix and branch voltages in the image of its transpose; those are orthogonal complements, and that orthogonality is Tellegen's theorem — conservation of power for any network whatever its components, linear or not. A passive impedance is a positive-real function, the Smith chart is a Möbius transformation, three-phase symmetrical components are the discrete Fourier transform on ℤ/3, and the stability of a synchronous grid is Mechanics to Relativity's pendulum. Every engineering rule of thumb that survives is derived; the ones that do not survive are named.

Every part ends at a real system, with numbers. Why power lines run at 400 kV: a line that loses 0.6% of 100 MW at 400 kV would dissipate more than the whole 100 MW at 20 kV. Why the grid never operates at maximum power transfer although radio receivers and antennas do. Why the France–Spain interconnector under the Pyrenees is DC. Why a phone charges over millimetres and not metres. Numbers are checked against published figures where they exist, and anything not derived here is labelled cited.

What a unit is

The same contract as Mechanics to Relativity and Quantum Computing, so the tooling and the habits carry over. A unit is done when the checkpoint is true, not when the deck has been read.

Theory

A deck of 25–45 slides, speaker notes on every content slide. Every claim is proved or labelled cited. Drawn whenever it can be drawn — field lines around a wire, the Poynting vector entering a resistor, the phasor diagram, the bounce diagram, the Smith chart, the pendulum's phase portrait — and a slide is split rather than a figure dropped.

Worked examples

One running example per unit, introduced on the overview slide, returned to in every section and closed at the end, plus three or four fully worked examples. Every result is checked twice on the slide: its dimensions, and its energy balance — power in equals power dissipated plus the rate at which stored energy grows.

Problems and solutions

Ten to thirteen problems, each with a hint ladder and a full worked solution folded under it. At least one per unit puts a number on a real device, from a stated data sheet or a cited published figure.

Checkpoint

One falsifiable claim about what you can now do, usually ending in a number. It is the deck's last slide and heads the problem sheet.

No referee

Paper only, like Mechanics to Relativity. Where a problem invites a numerical check — Newton–Raphson on three buses, the bounce diagram run for many reflections — it says so, in any tool you like, and the solution never depends on it.

Part A — from fields to circuits

Three units that derive circuit theory from Maxwell's equations, and find the energy outside the wire

What a current is, where the energy it delivers actually travels, and the three hypotheses under which a field problem collapses to a circuit — with the experiment that breaks one of them. Three units, 17.5 h.

00

Conduction: Ohm's law from a model, and what the model gets wrong

spine

needs nothing here MR 23MR 242 + 3.5 h

Theory
The running example is a kettle drawing 10 A through 2.5 mm² copper. The electrons in that cable drift at about 0.3 mm/s, yet the kettle starts the moment the switch closes — so whatever carries the energy is not the electrons' motion. The Drude model: free charges, collisions at mean time τ, a steady drift, and J = σE with σ = ne²τ/m — Ohm's law as linear response. Joule heating J·E is the work the field does on the charges, per unit volume. In a steady state the interior of a conductor is neutral and the field that drives the current is held in place by surface charge on the wire, which is what steers it round a bend. Drude is honest about σ ∝ τ and wrong about why τ has the value it does: the electrons move at the Fermi speed, a quantum fact (cited from Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1–2).
Tools
The continuity equation in a conductor and the decay of interior charge as e−σt/ε₀ — and why the naive time this gives copper, 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁹ s, is meaningless, being shorter than the collision time 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ s it was derived from. Resistance R = ρL/A as the solution of a boundary-value problem from MR 23, and what replaces it when the cross-section varies.
Real system
The kettle's cable: 6.7 mΩ and 0.67 W per metre at 10 A, and why wiring tables rate a cable by the temperature it reaches rather than by any voltage.
Problems
Drift velocity and resistance from data; the resistance of a cone and of a washer, as Laplace problems; the Hall effect as a measurement of carrier density and sign; Drude's Wiedemann–Franz ratio, and why its near-success was an accident of two compensating errors (cited).
Checkpoint. You derive J = σE from the Drude model, compute the kettle cable's drift velocity and per-metre dissipation — 0.29 mm/s and 0.67 W/m — and say why neither the drift speed nor the Fermi speed is the speed at which the kettle learns that the switch has closed.
01

Where the energy flows: the Poynting vector around a circuit, and p = vi as a theorem

spine

needs 00 MR 242 + 3.5 h

Theory
The running example is a battery driving a resistor through a long coaxial cable. In the insulation between the conductors, E is radial (from the surface charge on the inner conductor) and B is azimuthal (from the current), so S = E × B/μ₀ points along the cable, from battery to resistor — in the insulation, not in the copper. Integrated over the annulus it is exactly VI, whatever the radii. At the resistor S turns inward and enters through the side; at the battery it comes out. Inside the copper S points slightly inward: the copper's own small loss, also arriving from outside. Then the general theorem: if the fields outside a two-terminal component are quasi-static, the Poynting flux through any closed surface around it equals v·i. That is what makes p = vi true, and says when it stops being true. The bridge from the everyday picture: the electrons' own kinetic energy carries about 10⁻²¹ of the power; what they do instead is make B by moving, make E as surface charge, and turn the field's energy into heat where they collide. The conveyor-belt picture of charges carrying energy is exact bookkeeping for totals — by the theorem — and wrong about where the energy is and when it arrives, which units 06, 08 and 15 each show.
Tools
Poynting's theorem from MR 24, now applied in the quasi-static regime; the fields of a long cylindrical conductor carrying both current and surface charge; the proof that ∮ S·dA = vi, from E = −∇φ outside the component and ∇·J = 0.
Real system
The two-wire cable to the kettle of unit 00, drawn: field lines, surface charge, and the 2.3 kW flowing in the space between and around the conductors.
Problems
The Poynting flux through a resistor's side equals I²R; a qualitative Poynting map for a battery, two parallel wires and a bulb; the energy stored per metre of cable against the energy delivered per second; MR 24's charging capacitor, which takes its energy in through its rim, revisited as a case of this unit's theorem.
Checkpoint. You compute the Poynting flux in the insulation of a coaxial cable carrying 12 V and 2 A and get 24 W without using the radii, show that the flux into the inner conductor per metre is its own I²R′, and prove that the flux into any quasi-static two-terminal component is vi — and show for the kettle cable that the electrons' own motion carries about 10⁻²¹ of the power.
02

The lumped approximation: three hypotheses under which Kirchhoff's laws are true

spine

needs 01 MR 23MR 242.5 + 4 h

Theory
Circuit theory replaces the fields by a finite list of numbers, one voltage across and one current through each component. Three hypotheses make that legitimate: the circuit is small against a wavelength, so retardation (MR 24) can be ignored; charge accumulates only inside capacitors; changing magnetic flux stays inside inductors. Under them, the current law follows from ∇·J + ∂ρ/∂t = 0 and the voltage law from Faraday's law. The running example breaks the one about flux: Lewin's circuit, a 100 Ω and a 900 Ω resistor in a ring around a solenoid whose changing flux induces 1 V. Two voltmeters on the same two points read 0.1 V and 0.9 V, with opposite signs, and both are right, because “the voltage between two points” depends on the path once ∇ × E ≠ 0. Circuit symbols and the passive sign convention arrive here.
Tools
Capacitance and inductance defined from stored energy, ½CV² and ½LI²; mutual inductance by Neumann's formula, symmetric in the two circuits; the inductance matrix is positive semi-definite because magnetic energy is non-negative, so M² ≤ L₁L₂. The element laws v = Ri, i = C dv/dt, v = L di/dt as consequences of the three hypotheses, not definitions. A linear dielectric multiplies a capacitance by εr — one slide, cited, since Mechanics to Relativity left dielectrics out — and an ideal voltage or current source as unit 01's EMF made ideal.
Real system
Why a 50 Hz installation 100 m across is lumped (the wavelength is 6000 km) and a 2 GHz phone board is not (15 cm in vacuum, and shorter in the board's insulation).
Problems
Inductance and capacitance per metre of a coaxial cable from MR 23's fields, and the identity L′C′ = με, which unit 08 cashes in; Lewin's circuit with the voltmeter leads routed both ways; the 8 V spike from a 1 cm hairpin of leads (about 8 nH) when 10 A switches off in 10 ns.
Checkpoint. You derive both of Kirchhoff's laws from Maxwell's equations under three stated hypotheses, and in Lewin's circuit predict the two voltmeter readings — 0.1 V and 0.9 V, of opposite sign — and name the hypothesis that failed.

Part B — networks, as linear algebra on a graph

Two units in which Kirchhoff's laws are a pair of orthogonal subspaces, and conservation of power is a fact about graphs

Once the fields are gone, what is left is a graph with a number on each edge. Its topology alone forces power conservation and reciprocity; Ohm's law adds a Laplacian, a variational principle and Thévenin's theorem — and the maximum-power theorem with its 50% price. Two units, 13.5 h.

03

Kirchhoff's laws as topology: cycle space, cut space, and Tellegen's theorem

spine

needs 022.5 + 4 h

Theory
A circuit's wiring is a directed graph with n nodes and m edges, and its incidence matrix A. The current law says Ai = 0; the voltage law says v = ATφ for some node potentials φ. So currents live in ker A, the cycle space, and voltages in im AT, the cut space — orthogonal complements in ℝm. Tellegen's theorem is that orthogonality: vTi = 0 for any voltages obeying the voltage law and any currents obeying the current law, even from different circuits on the same graph or at different times. Conservation of power in a network therefore needs no assumption about its components — linear, nonlinear or time-varying. The running example is the Wheatstone bridge: four nodes, six edges, cycle and cut spaces of dimension three each.
Tools
The rank of an incidence matrix, n − c for c components; spanning trees, with fundamental cycles and cuts as bases; mesh and nodal analysis as the two natural choices of basis; planar graphs, meshes and Euler's formula.
Real system
The strain-gauge bridge in a kitchen scale: why a bridge measures a resistance change of a part in ten thousand when a single resistor would not show it.
Problems
Dimension counts on several graphs; Tellegen applied to two different networks on one graph to prove reciprocity; a circuit with a diode, where Tellegen still holds and superposition does not; the bridge's sensitivity as a function of its ratio arms.
Checkpoint. You prove Tellegen's theorem from the orthogonality of ker A and im AT, and use it to prove reciprocity — in any network of resistors, the voltage at port 2 per ampere into port 1 equals the reverse — checked with numbers on the bridge.
04

Resistive networks: the graph Laplacian, Thomson's principle, Thévenin, and maximum power

spine

needs 032.5 + 4.5 h

Theory
Ohm's law on every edge turns the current law into Lφ = s, with L = AGAT the weighted graph Laplacian: symmetric, positive semi-definite, kernel the constants on each component. Node potentials exist and are unique up to a constant — a discrete Dirichlet problem, the circuit version of MR 23's uniqueness theorem. Thomson's principle: the actual currents minimise dissipated power among all flows with the same sources; Rayleigh monotonicity follows. Thévenin's theorem is a statement about affine maps: any linear resistive one-port is an EMF in series with a resistance. Maximum power transfer: a load receives the most power when it equals the source resistance, and the efficiency at that point is exactly 50%. The running example is a car battery cranking a starter motor — and why your headlights dim.
Tools
Kron reduction: eliminating interior nodes is a Schur complement, and the result is again a Laplacian — Thévenin at many ports at once, used again in unit 13. Effective resistance as a metric. Foster's theorem, Σe Reff(e)/Re = n − 1, proved as the trace of an orthogonal projection onto the cut space.
Real system
Why a power station refuses the matched point that a radio receiver accepts from its antenna: at 50% efficiency it would burn twice the fuel for the same delivered energy, whereas the antenna's energy is free and only the power received matters. (An audio amplifier is not the example: it is built with an output resistance far below its loudspeaker's, not matched to it.)
Problems
The cube of twelve resistors; the infinite square grid, adjacent nodes, R/2, by superposition — with the limit argument the infinite network actually needs; the star–delta transformation as a Kron reduction; Thomson's principle by Lagrange multipliers; bounding a resistance you cannot compute, by Rayleigh.
Checkpoint. You prove the maximum-power theorem and its 50% efficiency, prove Foster's theorem, and compute the cube's corner-to-opposite-corner resistance, 5R/6, two ways: by symmetry, and by Kron reduction.

05

Circuits that remember: transients, phasors, and impedance as a positive-real function

spine

needs 0402 MR 06MR 202.5 + 4.5 h

Theory
With capacitors and inductors the network is a linear ODE system whose state is the capacitor voltages and inductor currents; its order is the number of independent energy stores. The running example is a car's ignition coil: switched on, current rises with time constant L/R; switched off, L di/dt demands a few hundred volts across the opening contact, which unit 07's turns ratio turns into a spark. A series RLC circuit is MR 06's oscillator with R playing γ. Then the sinusoidal steady state: eiωt is an eigenfunction of d/dt, so a linear circuit acts by multiplication — phasors, and the impedance Z(iω). Network functions are rational, with poles in the left half-plane exactly when the circuit is stable — MR 20's causality, again. The passivity theorem: the impedance of any network of positive R, L, C is positive-real, Re Z(s) ≥ 0 for Re s > 0 — proved from Tellegen's theorem with complex phasors.
Tools
The one-sided Laplace transform, built from MR 20's Fourier transform with the properties used here proved; RMS phasors; impedance and admittance; Foster's reactance theorem for lossless networks — reactance rises with frequency, poles and zeros alternate — proved.
Real system
The ignition coil, and the snubber that protects whatever switches it.
Problems
Time constants; charging a capacitor from a fixed voltage through any resistance wastes exactly half the energy drawn — independent of R; Q and bandwidth of a tuned circuit, against MR 06; deciding whether a given rational function is positive-real; the Foster form of an LC impedance.
Checkpoint. You prove that charging a capacitor through a resistor wastes exactly half the energy drawn whatever the resistance, and that the impedance of every RLC network is positive-real.
06

AC power: real, reactive, complex — and conjugate matching

spine

needs 052 + 4 h

Theory
At 50 Hz the instantaneous power v(t)i(t) oscillates at 100 Hz about a mean P = VI cos φ. Its other part, the reactive power Q = VI sin φ, is energy moving back and forth between the source and the load's fields twice a cycle: it does no net work, but the current that carries it heats every wire on the way. Complex power S = VI* = P + iQ is conserved across any network — Tellegen again, since the conjugate of a current obeying the current law still obeys it. Power factor, and correcting it with a capacitor. With a complex source impedance the maximum-power theorem becomes conjugate matching, ZL = Zs*, still at 50% efficiency. The running example is a workshop drawing 20 kW at power factor 0.7 from 230 V.
Tools
RMS values; complex power and the power triangle; the conservation theorem; the conjugate-matching theorem, proved.
Real system
Why industrial tariffs charge for reactive energy, and the capacitor banks in factory switch rooms.
Problems
Power factor of motor loads; sizing a correction capacitor; complex power balance on a three-load network; conjugate matching of an inductive source; why over-correcting is also a loss.
Checkpoint. For the workshop, you compute the capacitor that brings the power factor from 0.7 to 1 — about 1.2 mF at 230 V, 50 Hz — and the factor by which it cuts the supply cable's losses: 0.49.
07

Transformers, and why power lines run at 400 kV

spine

needs 06022 + 3.5 h

Theory
Two coupled inductors, and the coupling coefficient k = M/√(L₁L₂) ≤ 1 from unit 02's energy argument. The ideal transformer is a limit — k → 1 and L → ∞ with L₁/L₂ fixed — in which voltages scale by n, currents by 1/n, power passes unchanged, and impedances seen through it scale by n². The real one has magnetising current, leakage inductance, and core losses (hysteresis, and eddy currents that laminations defeat). Then the reason for high voltage: at fixed power P, a line of resistance R loses P²R/V². What stops voltage rising further — insulation, corona, clearance — with figures cited. The running example is the distribution transformer at the end of a street: 20 kV to 400 V, 400 kVA.
Tools
The coupled-inductor equations; the transformer as a two-port; reflected impedance; the T equivalent circuit and how its parameters are measured by open- and short-circuit tests.
Real system
The street transformer's nameplate, and the chain 400 kV → 132 kV → 20 kV → 400 V.
Problems
Eddy-current loss in a lamination scales as its thickness squared; matching an 8 Ω speaker to a valve amplifier with a turns ratio; the ignition coil of unit 05, finished — the secondary's voltage; open- and short-circuit test data turned into an equivalent circuit.
Checkpoint. You derive the ideal transformer as a limit of coupled inductors, prove that a transformer's efficiency peaks at the load where copper loss equals core loss, and show that sending 100 MW down a 10 Ω line loses 0.6% at 400 kV and more than the whole 100 MW at 20 kV.

08

Transmission lines: telegrapher's equations, characteristic impedance, and reflection

spine

needs 0501 MR 182.5 + 4.5 h

Theory
For a line whose fields are transverse — a coaxial cable, a pair of wires — Maxwell's equations reduce exactly to the telegrapher's equations ∂v/∂z = −L′ ∂i/∂t, ∂i/∂z = −C′ ∂v/∂t: MR 18's wave equation, at speed 1/√(L′C′) = c/√εr, with characteristic impedance Z₀ = √(L′/C′), the ratio of forward voltage to forward current. A load reflects with Γ = (ZL − Z₀)/(ZL + Z₀); MR 18's fixed and free ends are Γ = −1 and +1. A forward wave carries v₊²/Z₀, and that is the Poynting flux in the insulation from unit 01. Loss terms R′, G′ bring attenuation and dispersion; Heaviside's distortionless condition R′/L′ = G′/C′ removes the dispersion, and is why long telephone lines were later fitted with loading coils (Heaviside's analysis and its history cited from Nahin's biography). The running example is the puzzle of a battery, a switch and a bulb 1 m apart joined by two wires that run 300 000 km out and back: the bulb lights after about (1 m)/c, dimly, at a current set by the lines' characteristic impedance rather than by the bulb — computed in a transmission-line model whose limits are stated.
Tools
The telegrapher's equations from Maxwell's for transverse fields; d'Alembert's solution; reflection and transmission coefficients; the bounce diagram; the propagation constant √((R′ + iωL′)(G′ + iωC′)).
Real system
50 Ω coaxial cable — polyethylene, radius ratio about 3.5, signals at 2 × 10⁸ m/s — and a 400 km power line, which at 50 Hz is a fifteenth of a wavelength: nearly lumped, but not quite. And a lightning stroke, as the thread's sharpest case of energy that is not carried by the charges: before the stroke the energy sits in the electric field between cloud and ground; the leader makes a conducting channel, and the return stroke is a current wave running up it at a fraction of c — a wave on a lossy line — while the field's energy pours into the channel through its surface as a Poynting flux, as into unit 01's resistor, and leaves as heat, light, sound and a radio pulse. Its figures (peak currents, return-stroke speed, charge lowered) are cited from Rakov & Uman and checked before the unit is built; the energy of a flash, whose published estimates disagree widely, is computed from a stated model and not quoted.
Problems
Z₀ of a coaxial line; bounce diagrams for mismatched loads and sources; locating a fault in a buried cable from its echo (time-domain reflectometry); Heaviside's condition, proved; the long power line's correction to its lumped model; the lightning channel as a charged line discharging into ground — where its energy was, and where it goes.
Checkpoint. You derive the telegrapher's equations from Maxwell's for a coaxial line, compute Z₀ = 50 Ω for polyethylene with b/a = 3.49, and draw the bounce diagram for a 12 V source with 10 Ω internal resistance driving a 150 Ω load through 10 m of that cable — reflection after reflection, converging on 11.25 V.
09

Matching on a line: standing waves, the quarter-wave transformer, and the Smith chart as a Möbius map

branch

needs 08062 + 3.5 h

Theory
Sinusoidal steady state on a line: the input impedance of a terminated length, standing waves and the standing-wave ratio, and delivered power as forward minus reflected. A quarter-wave section inverts impedance about Z₀²: Zin = Z₀²/ZL. The Smith chart is the Möbius map Γ = (z − 1)/(z + 1), which sends the right half-plane — where unit 05 proved every passive impedance lives — onto the unit disc; moving along the line is a rotation; the chart's grid lines are circles because Möbius maps send generalised circles to generalised circles. The running example matches a 12.5 Ω load to a 50 Ω line.
Tools
Möbius transformations and their circle-preserving property, proved; the input-impedance formula; single-stub matching on the chart.
Real system
The antenna matching network at the end of a radio's feed line, and why a mismatched transmitter heats up.
Checkpoint. You prove that the Smith chart's grid is circles, compute the standing-wave ratio of the unmatched 12.5 Ω load — 4 — and match it two ways: a 25 Ω quarter-wave section, and a single shunt stub located on the chart.
10

The skin effect: why alternating current avoids the middle of the wire

branch

needs 050007 MR 09MR 18MR 192 + 3.5 h

Theory
Inside a conductor the displacement current is negligible, and Maxwell's equations with Ohm's law give a diffusion equation, ∂B/∂t = (μσ)⁻¹∇²B — MR 18's parabolic type. Into a half-space, field and current decay as e−z/δ while their phase turns, with skin depth δ = √(2/ωμσ): 9.2 mm in copper at 50 Hz, 0.21 mm at 100 kHz. In a round wire the solution is a Bessel function of complex argument (MR 09; MR 19 for separation in polar coordinates), with its asymptotics cited. AC resistance therefore rises with frequency; the proximity effect of neighbouring conductors is cited.
Real system
Overhead conductors of aluminium strands around a steel core; tubular busbars in substations; the Litz wire in unit 15's charging coils.
Problems
Skin depths across materials and frequencies; the high-frequency AC/DC resistance ratio of a thick round wire; why a tube conducts 50 Hz almost as well as a solid bar of the same outside diameter; eddy currents in unit 07's laminations, revisited.
Checkpoint. You derive the skin depth from Maxwell's equations and Ohm's law, get 9.2 mm for copper at 50 Hz and 0.21 mm at 100 kHz, and explain from those two numbers why a substation busbar can be a tube and a wireless charger's coil is wound from many insulated strands.

11

Three-phase power: constant power, fewer conductors, and a field that rotates

spine

needs 06072 + 3.5 h

Theory
Three sinusoids 120° apart. In a balanced three-phase system the total instantaneous power is constant — no 100 Hz pulsation, so a motor sees no pulsating torque — because Σ cos²(ωt − 2πk/3) = 3/2. For equal power, equal loss and equal voltage between conductors, three wires use 75% of the conductor that two would. Star and delta, line and phase quantities, and the √3. Three coils 120° apart, fed 120° apart, make a field of constant magnitude rotating at ω — one line with complex numbers. Unbalance: Fortescue's symmetrical components are the discrete Fourier transform on ℤ/3, so any three phasors split uniquely into positive, negative and zero sequence, and only the zero sequence returns through the neutral. The running example is the 400/230 V supply from unit 07's transformer to a workshop with one heavy single-phase load.
Tools
Balanced polyphase sets; star–delta conversion; the order-3 DFT as a change of basis on ℂ³; sequence networks.
Real system
The four wires of a European low-voltage feeder, and why a broken neutral is dangerous.
Problems
Constant power; the 75% figure; star–delta; neutral current for unbalanced loads; an argument (partly cited) for why grids settled on three phases and not two or six.
Checkpoint. You prove that balanced three-phase power is constant in time, derive the 75% conductor figure, and compute the workshop's neutral current two ways — by symmetrical components and directly — and get the same number.

Machines: torque from co-energy, and the generator that sets the grid's clock

branch

needs 11 MR 122.5 + 4 h

Theory
Electromechanical conversion from energy alone: for a lossless coupling with flux linkage λ(i, θ), the torque is ∂W′/∂θ at constant current, where W′ is the co-energy — proved by an energy balance, and recovered as the Lagrangian of a system with electrical and mechanical coordinates (MR 12). Reluctance torque; the synchronous machine, whose rotor locks to unit 11's rotating field and delivers P = EV sin δ/Xs; the induction motor, whose rotor slips behind the field so that currents are induced in it, with its torque–slip curve from an equivalent circuit.
Real system
A two-pole steam turbo-generator at 3000 rpm beside a many-pole hydro generator turning slowly; the induction motor in every pump.
Checkpoint. You derive a reluctance motor's torque from co-energy, find the speed of a 40-pole synchronous generator on a 50 Hz grid — 150 rpm — and locate an induction motor's maximum torque on its torque–slip curve.

Power flow and stability: the grid as a pendulum

spine

needs 1107 MR 02 MR 16 MR 232.5 + 4.5 h

Theory
Two AC buses joined by a reactance X exchange P = V₁V₂ sin δ/X, while reactive power depends mostly on the voltage magnitudes: real power flows down phase differences and reactive power down voltage differences. The transfer has a maximum, V₁V₂/X at δ = 90°, the static stability limit. Many buses give the nonlinear power-flow equations, solved by Newton–Raphson. Dynamics: each synchronous machine obeys the swing equation Mδ″ = Pm − Pmax sin δ, a pendulum with a constant torque — MR 02's phase portrait, tilted. Whether a machine survives a fault is decided by the equal-area criterion, which is energy conservation on that portrait. Frequency is the grid's shared state: an imbalance anywhere changes every machine's speed, inertia sets how fast, and reserves restore it. The running example is a two-area model of the Iberian peninsula joined to the rest of Europe by a few interconnectors of limited capacity — a textbook two-machine system, not a reconstruction of any particular event.
Tools
Bus admittance matrices (unit 04's Laplacian, with complex weights); Newton–Raphson, with its convergence theorem stated; the swing equation's first integral.
Real system
Continental Europe as one synchronous machine at 50 Hz, and the design rule it is operated to: the grid must ride through the sudden loss of a reference incident of 3000 MW of generation (cited from the ENTSO-E operating rules), which unit 13's inertia and reserves turn into a worst-case frequency dip.
Problems
Transfer limits; a three-bus Newton–Raphson by hand; the frequency drop after losing 1 GW in a system of given inertia; why reactive power has to be produced near where it is used.
Checkpoint. You derive the two-bus transfer formula and its maximum, and use the equal-area criterion to find the critical clearing angle for a machine running at δ₀ = 30° when a fault cuts the transfer to zero — about 79.6°.

Power electronics and HVDC: switching, and when the grid goes DC

spine

needs 130508 MR 192.5 + 4 h

Theory
An ideal switch dissipates nothing — at every instant either its voltage or its current is zero — so efficient conversion is done by switching and then filtering. The buck converter: averaging its two switched states gives Vout = DVin, and unit 05's LC filter sets the ripple. Inverters make AC from DC by pulse-width modulation, whose spectrum (MR 19's Fourier series) says what the filter must remove. HVDC: an AC cable's charging current, ωCV per kilometre, uses up its rating beyond a critical length; DC has no charging current, no reactive power and no skin effect, and pays instead for converter stations — the break-even distance, overhead and under sea, as a cost model with its assumptions stated. Finally, generation through inverters (solar, wind, HVDC) has no rotating inertia: grid-following against grid-forming control, and what each does to unit 13's swing equation. The running example is INELFE, the France–Spain link between Baixas and Santa Llogaia: two 1000 MW links at 320 kV DC, 64.5 km underground, crossing the Pyrenees in an 8.5 km tunnel (Red Eléctrica's project brochure, checked; the ± and the bipolar arrangement are confirmed against the converter and cable makers' descriptions before they are stated). As an AC cable the same route would spend about two thirds of a cable's rating on charging current: long, though not past the critical length, which is why the deck gives the operator's own reason beside the computation.
Real system
INELFE; and, at the other end of the scale, a phone charger.
Problems
Duty cycle and ripple of a buck converter; the harmonics of a PWM waveform; the critical length of an AC cable from its capacitance per kilometre; DC against AC losses on a given route.
Checkpoint. You prove the buck converter's averaged output DVin from its switched equations, and compute the length at which a given AC cable's charging current reaches its thermal rating (99 km for the deck's 400 kV cable), and the share of that rating INELFE's 64.5 km would spend on it as AC: 65%.

Wireless power: two coupled resonators, and the one number that decides the efficiency

spine

needs 0706 MR 072 + 4 h

Theory
A charging pad and a phone are two coils, loosely coupled: unit 07's transformer with k well below 1 (0.5 or below for Qi, per the Wireless Power Consortium, against about 0.9999 for unit 07's street transformer). Untuned, the secondary barely loads the primary and most of the drive circulates as reactive power. Tune both coils to resonance with capacitors and solve the coupled pair — MR 07's two coupled oscillators — and the efficiency turns out to depend only on the figure of merit U = k√(Q₁Q₂), with maximum η = U²/(1 + √(1 + U²))², reached at a load that is not the conjugate match: maximum efficiency and maximum power transfer are different design points, again. For coils far apart, k falls as the cube of the distance (unit 02's Neumann formula), which is why going from millimetres to metres needs very high Q. The energy crosses the gap as a Poynting flux, as in unit 01.
Real system
Qi phone chargers (typically 87–205 kHz, millimetres; the Wireless Power Consortium's Qi Specification: Introduction, v1.3, 2021); electric-car charging pads (a nominal 85 kHz for SAE J2954, per SAE International's 2013 announcement; power classes to 11 kW; a gap set by the car's ground clearance); and the 2007 MIT experiment that lit a 60 W bulb from 2 m away at roughly 40% efficiency (Kurs et al., Science 2007; the bulb from MIT News, 2007).
Problems
Derive η(U) and the optimal load; k for two coaxial loops; why the coils are wound from Litz wire (branch 10, or stated); what a stray coin or scrap of foil on the pad does, and why chargers detect foreign objects.
Checkpoint. You derive ηmax = U²/(1 + √(1 + U²))² for two coupled resonators and evaluate it: 17% at U = 1, 82% at U = 10.

Antennas: radiation resistance, reciprocity, and why beaming power is hard

branch

needs 1508 MR 242 + 3.5 h

Theory
From MR 24's Larmor formula to an oscillating current element: it radiates as if it were a resistor, the radiation resistance Rrad = (2π/3)η₀(l/λ)² ≈ 789(l/λ)² Ω for a uniform current (the familiar 80π² takes η₀ ≈ 120π), whose “heat” leaves as an electromagnetic wave. A short antenna is mostly reactive, and once its reactance is tuned out with a real coil, mostly lossy as well; the half-wave dipole's 73 Ω is cited. Near field against far field: unit 15's coupling falls as 1/r³, radiated intensity as 1/r². Reciprocity (proved in a special case, the general Lorentz theorem cited); effective aperture Ae = Gλ²/4π (derived from reciprocity and the short dipole, with the thermodynamic argument sketched); the Friis equation Pr/Pt = GtGr(λ/4πd)².
Real system
A phone's antenna and a base station; rectennas and proposals for beaming power from orbit (cited), and what Friis says about them.
Checkpoint. You derive the short dipole's radiation resistance from Larmor — 790(l/λ)² Ω — and show by Friis that 1 W at 2.4 GHz between isotropic antennas 1 km apart delivers about 0.1 nW.
