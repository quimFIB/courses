# Every unit, in order: what it teaches. Nothing may be used before the unit that introduces it.

Assumed throughout: everything in Mechanics to Relativity units 00–24 (cited as MR nn; the decks are at ../mechanics-to-relativity/units/), plus the mathematical background AUTHORING.md lists. Generated from CURRICULUM.html's cards by build/mkindex.py; the page wins if they differ.

- **00 Conduction: Ohm's law from a model, and what the model gets wrong** (needs —; MR 23, MR 24; spine) — The running example is a kettle drawing 10 A through 2.5 mm² copper. The electrons in that cable drift at about 0.3 mm/s, yet the kettle starts the moment the switch closes — so whatever carries the energy is not the electrons' motion. The Drude model: free charges, collisions at mean time τ, a steady drift, and J = σE with σ = ne²τ/m — Ohm's law as linear response. Joule heating J·E is the work the field does on the charges, per unit volume. In a steady state the interior of a conductor is neutral and the field that drives the current is held in place by surface charge on the wire, which is what steers it round a bend. Drude is honest about σ ∝ τ and wrong about why τ has the value it does: the electrons move at the Fermi speed, a quantum fact (cited from Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1–2). Tools: The continuity equation in a conductor and the decay of interior charge as e−σt/ε₀ — and why the naive time this gives copper, 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁹ s, is meaningless, being shorter than the collision time 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ s it was derived from. Resistance R = ρL/A as the solution of a boundary-value problem from MR 23, and what replaces it when the cross-section varies.
- **01 Where the energy flows: the Poynting vector around a circuit, and p = vi as a theorem** (needs 00; MR 24; spine) — The running example is a battery driving a resistor through a long coaxial cable. In the insulation between the conductors, E is radial (from the surface charge on the inner conductor) and B is azimuthal (from the current), so S = E × B/μ₀ points along the cable, from battery to resistor — in the insulation, not in the copper. Integrated over the annulus it is exactly VI, whatever the radii. At the resistor S turns inward and enters through the side; at the battery it comes out. Inside the copper S points slightly inward: the copper's own small loss, also arriving from outside. Then the general theorem: if the fields outside a two-terminal component are quasi-static, the Poynting flux through any closed surface around it equals v·i. That is what makes p = vi true, and says when it stops being true. The bridge from the everyday picture: the electrons' own kinetic energy carries about 10⁻²¹ of the power; what they do instead is make B by moving, make E as surface charge, and turn the field's energy into heat where they collide. The conveyor-belt picture of charges carrying energy is exact bookkeeping for totals — by the theorem — and wrong about where the energy is and when it arrives, which units 06, 08 and 15 each show. Tools: Poynting's theorem from MR 24, now applied in the quasi-static regime; the fields of a long cylindrical conductor carrying both current and surface charge; the proof that ∮ S·dA = vi, from E = −∇φ outside the component and ∇·J = 0.
- **02 The lumped approximation: three hypotheses under which Kirchhoff's laws are true** (needs 01; MR 23, MR 24; spine) — Circuit theory replaces the fields by a finite list of numbers, one voltage across and one current through each component. Three hypotheses make that legitimate: the circuit is small against a wavelength, so retardation (MR 24) can be ignored; charge accumulates only inside capacitors; changing magnetic flux stays inside inductors. Under them, the current law follows from ∇·J + ∂ρ/∂t = 0 and the voltage law from Faraday's law. The running example breaks the one about flux: Lewin's circuit, a 100 Ω and a 900 Ω resistor in a ring around a solenoid whose changing flux induces 1 V. Two voltmeters on the same two points read 0.1 V and 0.9 V, with opposite signs, and both are right, because “the voltage between two points” depends on the path once ∇ × E ≠ 0. Circuit symbols and the passive sign convention arrive here. Tools: Capacitance and inductance defined from stored energy, ½CV² and ½LI²; mutual inductance by Neumann's formula, symmetric in the two circuits; the inductance matrix is positive semi-definite because magnetic energy is non-negative, so M² ≤ L₁L₂. The element laws v = Ri, i = C dv/dt, v = L di/dt as consequences of the three hypotheses, not definitions. A linear dielectric multiplies a capacitance by εr — one slide, cited, since Mechanics to Relativity left dielectrics out — and an ideal voltage or current source as unit 01's EMF made ideal.
- **03 Kirchhoff's laws as topology: cycle space, cut space, and Tellegen's theorem** (needs 02; spine) — A circuit's wiring is a directed graph with n nodes and m edges, and its incidence matrix A. The current law says Ai = 0; the voltage law says v = ATφ for some node potentials φ. So currents live in ker A, the cycle space, and voltages in im AT, the cut space — orthogonal complements in ℝm. Tellegen's theorem is that orthogonality: vTi = 0 for any voltages obeying the voltage law and any currents obeying the current law, even from different circuits on the same graph or at different times. Conservation of power in a network therefore needs no assumption about its components — linear, nonlinear or time-varying. The running example is the Wheatstone bridge: four nodes, six edges, cycle and cut spaces of dimension three each. Tools: The rank of an incidence matrix, n − c for c components; spanning trees, with fundamental cycles and cuts as bases; mesh and nodal analysis as the two natural choices of basis; planar graphs, meshes and Euler's formula.
- **04 Resistive networks: the graph Laplacian, Thomson's principle, Thévenin, and maximum power** (needs 03; spine) — Ohm's law on every edge turns the current law into Lφ = s, with L = AGAT the weighted graph Laplacian: symmetric, positive semi-definite, kernel the constants on each component. Node potentials exist and are unique up to a constant — a discrete Dirichlet problem, the circuit version of MR 23's uniqueness theorem. Thomson's principle: the actual currents minimise dissipated power among all flows with the same sources; Rayleigh monotonicity follows. Thévenin's theorem is a statement about affine maps: any linear resistive one-port is an EMF in series with a resistance. Maximum power transfer: a load receives the most power when it equals the source resistance, and the efficiency at that point is exactly 50%. The running example is a car battery cranking a starter motor — and why your headlights dim. Tools: Kron reduction: eliminating interior nodes is a Schur complement, and the result is again a Laplacian — Thévenin at many ports at once, used again in unit 13. Effective resistance as a metric. Foster's theorem, Σe Reff(e)/Re = n − 1, proved as the trace of an orthogonal projection onto the cut space.
- **05 Circuits that remember: transients, phasors, and impedance as a positive-real function** (needs 04, 02; MR 06, MR 20; spine) — With capacitors and inductors the network is a linear ODE system whose state is the capacitor voltages and inductor currents; its order is the number of independent energy stores. The running example is a car's ignition coil: switched on, current rises with time constant L/R; switched off, L di/dt demands a few hundred volts across the opening contact, which unit 07's turns ratio turns into a spark. A series RLC circuit is MR 06's oscillator with R playing γ. Then the sinusoidal steady state: eiωt is an eigenfunction of d/dt, so a linear circuit acts by multiplication — phasors, and the impedance Z(iω). Network functions are rational, with poles in the left half-plane exactly when the circuit is stable — MR 20's causality, again. The passivity theorem: the impedance of any network of positive R, L, C is positive-real, Re Z(s) ≥ 0 for Re s > 0 — proved from Tellegen's theorem with complex phasors. Tools: The one-sided Laplace transform, built from MR 20's Fourier transform with the properties used here proved; RMS phasors; impedance and admittance; Foster's reactance theorem for lossless networks — reactance rises with frequency, poles and zeros alternate — proved.
- **06 AC power: real, reactive, complex — and conjugate matching** (needs 05; spine) — At 50 Hz the instantaneous power v(t)i(t) oscillates at 100 Hz about a mean P = VI cos φ. Its other part, the reactive power Q = VI sin φ, is energy moving back and forth between the source and the load's fields twice a cycle: it does no net work, but the current that carries it heats every wire on the way. Complex power S = VI* = P + iQ is conserved across any network — Tellegen again, since the conjugate of a current obeying the current law still obeys it. Power factor, and correcting it with a capacitor. With a complex source impedance the maximum-power theorem becomes conjugate matching, ZL = Zs*, still at 50% efficiency. The running example is a workshop drawing 20 kW at power factor 0.7 from 230 V. Tools: RMS values; complex power and the power triangle; the conservation theorem; the conjugate-matching theorem, proved.
- **07 Transformers, and why power lines run at 400 kV** (needs 06, 02; spine) — Two coupled inductors, and the coupling coefficient k = M/√(L₁L₂) ≤ 1 from unit 02's energy argument. The ideal transformer is a limit — k → 1 and L → ∞ with L₁/L₂ fixed — in which voltages scale by n, currents by 1/n, power passes unchanged, and impedances seen through it scale by n². The real one has magnetising current, leakage inductance, and core losses (hysteresis, and eddy currents that laminations defeat). Then the reason for high voltage: at fixed power P, a line of resistance R loses P²R/V². What stops voltage rising further — insulation, corona, clearance — with figures cited. The running example is the distribution transformer at the end of a street: 20 kV to 400 V, 400 kVA. Tools: The coupled-inductor equations; the transformer as a two-port; reflected impedance; the T equivalent circuit and how its parameters are measured by open- and short-circuit tests.
- **08 Transmission lines: telegrapher's equations, characteristic impedance, and reflection** (needs 05, 01; MR 18; spine) — For a line whose fields are transverse — a coaxial cable, a pair of wires — Maxwell's equations reduce exactly to the telegrapher's equations ∂v/∂z = −L′ ∂i/∂t, ∂i/∂z = −C′ ∂v/∂t: MR 18's wave equation, at speed 1/√(L′C′) = c/√εr, with characteristic impedance Z₀ = √(L′/C′), the ratio of forward voltage to forward current. A load reflects with Γ = (ZL − Z₀)/(ZL + Z₀); MR 18's fixed and free ends are Γ = −1 and +1. A forward wave carries v₊²/Z₀, and that is the Poynting flux in the insulation from unit 01. Loss terms R′, G′ bring attenuation and dispersion; Heaviside's distortionless condition R′/L′ = G′/C′ removes the dispersion, and is why long telephone lines were later fitted with loading coils (Heaviside's analysis and its history cited from Nahin's biography). The running example is the puzzle of a battery, a switch and a bulb 1 m apart joined by two wires that run 300 000 km out and back: the bulb lights after about (1 m)/c, dimly, at a current set by the lines' characteristic impedance rather than by the bulb — computed in a transmission-line model whose limits are stated. Tools: The telegrapher's equations from Maxwell's for transverse fields; d'Alembert's solution; reflection and transmission coefficients; the bounce diagram; the propagation constant √((R′ + iωL′)(G′ + iωC′)).
- **09 Matching on a line: standing waves, the quarter-wave transformer, and the Smith chart as a Möbius map** (needs 08, 06; branch) — Sinusoidal steady state on a line: the input impedance of a terminated length, standing waves and the standing-wave ratio, and delivered power as forward minus reflected. A quarter-wave section inverts impedance about Z₀²: Zin = Z₀²/ZL. The Smith chart is the Möbius map Γ = (z − 1)/(z + 1), which sends the right half-plane — where unit 05 proved every passive impedance lives — onto the unit disc; moving along the line is a rotation; the chart's grid lines are circles because Möbius maps send generalised circles to generalised circles. The running example matches a 12.5 Ω load to a 50 Ω line. Tools: Möbius transformations and their circle-preserving property, proved; the input-impedance formula; single-stub matching on the chart.
- **10 The skin effect: why alternating current avoids the middle of the wire** (needs 05, 00, 07; MR 09, MR 18, MR 19; branch) — Inside a conductor the displacement current is negligible, and Maxwell's equations with Ohm's law give a diffusion equation, ∂B/∂t = (μσ)⁻¹∇²B — MR 18's parabolic type. Into a half-space, field and current decay as e−z/δ while their phase turns, with skin depth δ = √(2/ωμσ): 9.2 mm in copper at 50 Hz, 0.21 mm at 100 kHz. In a round wire the solution is a Bessel function of complex argument (MR 09; MR 19 for separation in polar coordinates), with its asymptotics cited. AC resistance therefore rises with frequency; the proximity effect of neighbouring conductors is cited.
- **11 Three-phase power: constant power, fewer conductors, and a field that rotates** (needs 06, 07; spine) — Three sinusoids 120° apart. In a balanced three-phase system the total instantaneous power is constant — no 100 Hz pulsation, so a motor sees no pulsating torque — because Σ cos²(ωt − 2πk/3) = 3/2. For equal power, equal loss and equal voltage between conductors, three wires use 75% of the conductor that two would. Star and delta, line and phase quantities, and the √3. Three coils 120° apart, fed 120° apart, make a field of constant magnitude rotating at ω — one line with complex numbers. Unbalance: Fortescue's symmetrical components are the discrete Fourier transform on ℤ/3, so any three phasors split uniquely into positive, negative and zero sequence, and only the zero sequence returns through the neutral. The running example is the 400/230 V supply from unit 07's transformer to a workshop with one heavy single-phase load. Tools: Balanced polyphase sets; star–delta conversion; the order-3 DFT as a change of basis on ℂ³; sequence networks.
- **12 Machines: torque from co-energy, and the generator that sets the grid's clock** (needs 11; MR 12; branch) — Electromechanical conversion from energy alone: for a lossless coupling with flux linkage λ(i, θ), the torque is ∂W′/∂θ at constant current, where W′ is the co-energy — proved by an energy balance, and recovered as the Lagrangian of a system with electrical and mechanical coordinates (MR 12). Reluctance torque; the synchronous machine, whose rotor locks to unit 11's rotating field and delivers P = EV sin δ/Xs; the induction motor, whose rotor slips behind the field so that currents are induced in it, with its torque–slip curve from an equivalent circuit.
- **13 Power flow and stability: the grid as a pendulum** (needs 11, 07; MR 02, MR 16, MR 23; spine) — Two AC buses joined by a reactance X exchange P = V₁V₂ sin δ/X, while reactive power depends mostly on the voltage magnitudes: real power flows down phase differences and reactive power down voltage differences. The transfer has a maximum, V₁V₂/X at δ = 90°, the static stability limit. Many buses give the nonlinear power-flow equations, solved by Newton–Raphson. Dynamics: each synchronous machine obeys the swing equation Mδ″ = Pm − Pmax sin δ, a pendulum with a constant torque — MR 02's phase portrait, tilted. Whether a machine survives a fault is decided by the equal-area criterion, which is energy conservation on that portrait. Frequency is the grid's shared state: an imbalance anywhere changes every machine's speed, inertia sets how fast, and reserves restore it. The running example is a two-area model of the Iberian peninsula joined to the rest of Europe by a few interconnectors of limited capacity — a textbook two-machine system, not a reconstruction of any particular event. Tools: Bus admittance matrices (unit 04's Laplacian, with complex weights); Newton–Raphson, with its convergence theorem stated; the swing equation's first integral.
- **14 Power electronics and HVDC: switching, and when the grid goes DC** (needs 13, 05, 08; MR 19; spine) — An ideal switch dissipates nothing — at every instant either its voltage or its current is zero — so efficient conversion is done by switching and then filtering. The buck converter: averaging its two switched states gives Vout = DVin, and unit 05's LC filter sets the ripple. Inverters make AC from DC by pulse-width modulation, whose spectrum (MR 19's Fourier series) says what the filter must remove. HVDC: an AC cable's charging current, ωCV per kilometre, uses up its rating beyond a critical length; DC has no charging current, no reactive power and no skin effect, and pays instead for converter stations — the break-even distance, overhead and under sea, as a cost model with its assumptions stated. Finally, generation through inverters (solar, wind, HVDC) has no rotating inertia: grid-following against grid-forming control, and what each does to unit 13's swing equation. The running example is INELFE, the France–Spain link between Baixas and Santa Llogaia: two 1000 MW links at 320 kV DC, 64.5 km underground, crossing the Pyrenees in an 8.5 km tunnel (Red Eléctrica's project brochure, checked; the ± and the bipolar arrangement are confirmed against the converter and cable makers' descriptions before they are stated). As an AC cable the same route would spend about two thirds of a cable's rating on charging current: long, though not past the critical length, which is why the deck gives the operator's own reason beside the computation.
- **15 Wireless power: two coupled resonators, and the one number that decides the efficiency** (needs 07, 06; MR 07; spine) — A charging pad and a phone are two coils, loosely coupled: unit 07's transformer with k well below 1 (0.5 or below for Qi, per the Wireless Power Consortium, against about 0.9999 for unit 07's street transformer). Untuned, the secondary barely loads the primary and most of the drive circulates as reactive power. Tune both coils to resonance with capacitors and solve the coupled pair — MR 07's two coupled oscillators — and the efficiency turns out to depend only on the figure of merit U = k√(Q₁Q₂), with maximum η = U²/(1 + √(1 + U²))², reached at a load that is not the conjugate match: maximum efficiency and maximum power transfer are different design points, again. For coils far apart, k falls as the cube of the distance (unit 02's Neumann formula), which is why going from millimetres to metres needs very high Q. The energy crosses the gap as a Poynting flux, as in unit 01.
- **16 Antennas: radiation resistance, reciprocity, and why beaming power is hard** (needs 15, 08; MR 24; branch) — From MR 24's Larmor formula to an oscillating current element: it radiates as if it were a resistor, the radiation resistance Rrad = (2π/3)η₀(l/λ)² ≈ 789(l/λ)² Ω for a uniform current (the familiar 80π² takes η₀ ≈ 120π), whose “heat” leaves as an electromagnetic wave. A short antenna is mostly reactive, and once its reactance is tuned out with a real coil, mostly lossy as well; the half-wave dipole's 73 Ω is cited. Near field against far field: unit 15's coupling falls as 1/r³, radiated intensity as 1/r². Reciprocity (proved in a special case, the general Lorentz theorem cited); effective aperture Ae = Gλ²/4π (derived from reciprocity and the short dipole, with the thermodynamic argument sketched); the Friis equation Pr/Pt = GtGr(λ/4πd)².
