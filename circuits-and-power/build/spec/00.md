## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
The learner has Maxwell's equations and electrostatics of conductors from Mechanics to Relativity, where a conductor in equilibrium has **E** = 0 inside. A wire carrying current is not in equilibrium, and they have never been told what happens then. This unit answers: what moves, how fast, why the current is proportional to the field, where the dissipated energy goes, what holds the field inside a bent wire, and — honestly — which parts of the classical answer are wrong and need quantum mechanics.

It must not become: a Poynting-vector unit (that is unit 01 — this unit may say "where the energy travels *to* the wire is unit 01's question" and stop); a circuits unit (no circuit diagrams, no Kirchhoff, no sign conventions — unit 02); a solid-state physics course (Fermi speed, Sommerfeld, band theory are cited, never derived); an AC unit beyond the single slide on σ(ω) that shows Ohm's law holds far beyond any frequency this course uses (skin effect is branch 10). Say on the overview slide what is left to units 01 and 02.

## Physics, in order

**What moves.** Copper has one conduction electron per atom. From handbook density and molar mass, n = 8.96 × 10³ / 0.06355 × 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 8.49 × 10²⁸ m⁻³; Ashcroft & Mermin's table gives 8.47 × 10²⁸ — use 8.47 (AUTHORING.md) and show the estimate agrees. Current density **J** = −ne**v**_d for electrons; the sign (electrons drift against **J**) gets a `box warn`.

**The Drude model (1900).** Electrons as classical particles; between collisions they accelerate freely; a collision happens with probability dt/τ in each interval dt and leaves the electron with a random velocity of mean zero. Averaging gives the relaxation-time equation for the mean momentum, d**p**/dt = −**p**/τ − e**E** (derive it, as Ashcroft & Mermin §1 do, in the notes under **Full proof**). Steady state: **v**_d = −eτ**E**/m, so **J** = σ**E** with σ = ne²τ/m. Ohm's law is a *linear response*: valid while the drift is a small perturbation of the random motion (show the ratio v_d / v_F ≈ 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ for the kettle cable). τ is not predicted by the model: read it off the measured resistivity, τ = m/(ne²ρ) ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ s for copper at 20 °C.

**Switching on.** With **E** switched on at t = 0, the mean momentum solves a first-order linear ODE (MR 00/01) and reaches 99 % of its steady value after τ ln 100 ≈ 1.1 × 10⁻¹³ s. Then **AC response**: with the course convention e^{+iωt}, σ(ω) = σ₀/(1 + iωτ); |σ| falls to σ₀/√2 at ω = 1/τ, i.e. f = 1/(2πτ) ≈ 6.4 THz. So Ohm's law with the DC σ holds at every frequency this course uses: |σ(ω)|/σ₀ differs from 1 by about 7 × 10⁻⁸ at 2.4 GHz, and by far less at 50 Hz (the difference is below double-precision resolution there — say "utterly negligible", not a number). One slide, with a figure of |σ(ω)|/σ₀ on log axes.

**Joule heating.** The field does work on the drifting charges at rate **J**·**E** per unit volume; collisions hand it to the lattice as heat; in a uniform wire this integrates to I²R. In steady state the electrons' mean kinetic energy is constant, so all of the work is dissipated — prove it from the equation of motion (power balance: rate of change of drift kinetic energy = work by field − loss to collisions).

**Resistance as a boundary-value problem.** Steady current with uniform σ: ∇·**J** = 0, **J** = σ**E**, **E** = −∇φ, so ∇²φ = 0 in the conductor, with φ given on the electrodes (Dirichlet) and **J**·**n** = 0 on insulated surfaces (Neumann). Uniqueness for these mixed conditions, by the energy method of MR 23 (Green's first identity: ∫σ|∇ψ|² = 0 for the difference ψ of two solutions). Hence R = V/I is well defined and depends only on geometry and σ. The uniform wire: R = ρL/A. For non-uniform σ the equation is ∇·(σ∇φ) = 0; same uniqueness proof.

**Why the field follows the wire: surface charge.** In a homogeneous conductor in steady state, ∇·**E** = ∇·**J**/σ = 0, so the interior carries no charge. Yet **E** inside must point along the wire, including round a bend, and the field of the battery alone does not do that. The field is produced by *surface charge* on the wire, graded along its length, and at any interface between two conductors (Jackson 1996, whose abstract names three roles in this order: maintaining the potential around the circuit, providing the field outside the conductors — unit 01's subject — and assuring the confined flow of current along the wire). At a junction between conductors of resistivity ρ₁ and ρ₂ carrying normal current density J, continuity of J_n and the jump in E_n give a surface charge ε₀J(ρ₂ − ρ₁) — derive it. A figure: a bent wire with surface-charge signs graded along it and the interior field following the bend (qualitative, after Jackson's figures and Chabay & Sherwood; say it is a sketch).

**How fast does interior charge disappear?** Naively, combining ∇·**J** + ∂ρ/∂t = 0, **J** = σ**E** and Gauss's law gives ∂ρ/∂t = −(σ/ε₀)ρ, decay time ε₀/σ ≈ 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁹ s for copper — shorter than τ itself, so the DC σ cannot be used on that timescale and the answer is meaningless. Done properly with the Drude equation of motion instead of **J** = σ**E**: ρ̈ + ρ̇/τ + ω_p²ρ = 0 with ω_p² = ne²/(ε₀m) — **MR 06's damped oscillator**. For copper ω_p ≈ 1.64 × 10¹⁶ rad/s and ω_pτ ≈ 410, so the charge rings at the plasma frequency and dies with amplitude e^{−t/2τ}, on a timescale of about 2τ ≈ 5 × 10⁻¹⁴ s. A pleasing payoff: the learner's own oscillator, in a lump of copper. (One sentence, cited Ashcroft & Mermin ch. 1: ω_p is also why metals reflect visible light.)

**What Drude gets wrong, and the honest bill.** Classical thermal speed √(3k_BT/m) ≈ 1.15 × 10⁵ m/s at 293 K would make the mean free path ≈ 3 nm; the electrons actually move at the Fermi speed v_F = 1.57 × 10⁶ m/s (Ashcroft & Mermin table 2.1, a quantum result — Sommerfeld, 1928, cited), so ℓ = v_Fτ ≈ 39 nm, many lattice spacings. The model's σ = ne²τ/m survives because the formula does not depend on the random speed — only τ's interpretation changes. Two further failures, stated and cited only: the Hall coefficient R_H = −1/(ne) has the wrong sign for some metals (Ashcroft & Mermin ch. 1; explained by band theory, not here — state this qualitatively, do not quote measured values); the Wiedemann–Franz ratio came out near the experimental value only through compensating errors (Ashcroft & Mermin ch. 1–2). A `box good` summary: what to trust (J = σE, Joule heating, σ(ω) at all frequencies used here), what not to (the microscopic picture of τ).

## The running example
**The kettle cable.** A 2.3 kW kettle on 230 V draws 10 A through 2.5 mm² copper conductors. Introduced on the overview slide with the puzzle: the electrons in it drift at about 0.3 mm/s — an hour to cross a metre — yet the kettle starts the moment the switch closes. Threaded:
- section "what moves": n, then J = 4.0 × 10⁶ A/m², v_d ≈ 0.29 mm/s, time to drift 1 m ≈ 57 min;
- section "Drude": τ from ρ; the field in the cable, E = ρJ ≈ 0.067 V/m; v_d / v_F ≈ 2 × 10⁻¹⁰;
- section "Joule heating": 6.72 mΩ and 0.672 W per metre of conductor; for a 10 m run (two conductors, 20 m of copper) 13.4 W lost and 1.34 V dropped, about 0.6 % of the power; why IEC 60364-5-52 rates cables by conductor temperature (PVC 70 °C);
- section "resistance as a boundary-value problem": R per metre as the uniform-wire special case, and what changes at a bend or a crimped joint;
- section "surface charge": the cable's bend, and the copper-to-element junction inside the kettle;
- closed on the last content slide: three speeds — drift 0.29 mm/s, Fermi 1.57 × 10⁶ m/s, and the speed at which the *field* re-arranges along the cable, a large fraction of c (unit 08 computes it; say only "a large fraction of c, set by the insulation" here) — and the answer to the puzzle: the kettle learns at the third, and what carries the energy to it is unit 01's question.

## Worked examples (three to five, in `box ex`)
1. The kettle cable: n from density, J, v_d, the hour per metre — every number on the slide, with units checked.
2. τ from ρ for copper, and v_d / v_F.
3. The resistance between two concentric spherical caps cut from a cone of solid angle Ω (radii r₁ < r₂): R = ρ(1/r₁ − 1/r₂)/Ω, by solving Laplace's equation (radial symmetry); its small-angle limit ρL/(πab) for a truncated cone with end radii a, b, checked against ρL/A when a = b.
4. Charge relaxation done properly: the oscillator equation for ρ, ω_p for copper, ω_pτ ≈ 410, and the decay — compared with the naive 1.5 × 10⁻¹⁹ s.
5. (optional) The surface charge at a copper–resistance-wire junction for the kettle current (numbers as in the problem below).

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and several that end in numbers. Every number computed with python3 before it goes on the page.
- **Kettle cable** (routine): J, E, v_d, R per metre, loss and voltage drop over a 10 m run.
- **Counting electrons** (routine): n for copper from density and molar mass, one electron per atom, against Ashcroft & Mermin's 8.47 × 10²⁸ m⁻³; the same for aluminium with three electrons per atom (density 2.70 g/cm³, molar mass 26.98 g/mol) — n ≈ 1.81 × 10²⁹ m⁻³.
- **τ and the mean free path** (medium): τ from ρ; ℓ with the classical thermal speed and with v_F; which is many lattice spacings (copper's nearest-neighbour distance ≈ 0.256 nm, handbook value).
- **Switching on** (routine): solve the momentum ODE after a step in E; time to 99 %.
- **AC conductivity** (medium): derive σ(ω) = σ₀/(1 + iωτ) with e^{+iωt}; evaluate |σ(ω)|/σ₀ − 1 at 50 Hz and at 2.4 GHz; the frequency where |σ| = σ₀/√2.
- **Power balance in the Drude model** (medium, proof): from the equation of motion, the field's work per unit volume J·E equals the rate of energy loss to collisions in steady state.
- **Uniqueness with mixed boundary conditions** (medium, proof): steady current in a conductor with electrodes at fixed potential and insulated elsewhere; the energy method; why R is well defined.
- **Spherical caps and the cone** (medium): R = ρ(1/r₁ − 1/r₂)/Ω and its small-angle limit.
- **Radial resistance of an annulus** (routine): a washer of thickness t, inner and outer radii a, b, current flowing radially: R = ρ ln(b/a)/(2πt); the same formula gives a cable insulation's leakage resistance per metre (a remark; no insulation data needed).
- **Junction charge** (medium, number): copper (ρ₁ = 1.68 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m) joined to a resistance wire with ρ₂ = 1.10 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m (a representative nichrome value, *labelled as assumed for the problem*), carrying J = 4.0 × 10⁶ A/m²: surface charge ε₀J(ρ₂ − ρ₁) ≈ 3.8 × 10⁻¹¹ C/m², about 240 electron charges per mm², and its sign.
- **Charge relaxation as an oscillator** (hard): derive ρ̈ + ρ̇/τ + ω_p²ρ = 0 from continuity, Gauss and the Drude equation; ω_p and ω_pτ for copper; why ε₀/σ is meaningless here.
- **The Hall effect in Drude** (medium): derive R_H = −1/(ne) in steady state; the Hall voltage across a copper strip 0.1 mm thick carrying 10 A in 1 T, ≈ 7.4 μV; why measuring R_H measures n and the carriers' sign — with the caveat (cited, qualitative) that some metals give the wrong sign.
- **Wiedemann–Franz** (medium, optional): Drude's κ/(σT) = (3/2)(k_B/e)² ≈ 1.11 × 10⁻⁸ W·Ω/K²; Sommerfeld's (π²/3)(k_B/e)² ≈ 2.44 × 10⁻⁸ (cited); the compensating errors (Ashcroft & Mermin ch. 1–2).
