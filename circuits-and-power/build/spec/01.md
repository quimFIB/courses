## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 00 ended on a question: the electrons drift at 0.29 mm/s, so what carries 2.3 kW from the fuse box to the kettle? This unit answers it with Poynting's theorem, which the learner already has from MR 24 in exactly the form needed (u = ε₀E²/2 + B²/2μ₀, **S** = **E** × **B**/μ₀, ∂u/∂t + ∇·**S** = −**J**·**E**). The energy travels through the space around and between the conductors; it enters a resistor through its sides; the wire's job is to guide the field. Then the unit proves the general statement that makes circuit theory's p = vi true, and says exactly when it is.

It must not become: a circuits unit (no diagrams, no Kirchhoff, no sign convention — unit 02; "terminal voltage" here is the potential difference between two leads, defined from φ as in unit 00); a transmission-line unit (no waves, no characteristic impedance, no switch-on transient — unit 08, though one remark may say the 83 Ω below will reappear there); an AC-power unit (one closing remark that with a reactive load **S** reverses for part of each cycle, pointing to unit 06, and no more); a dielectrics unit — **treat all insulation as vacuum** (ε₀, μ₀), state this once in a `box warn`, and note that the results used here (E fixed by the potential difference, B fixed by the current, the flux VI) do not change with a non-magnetic insulator, while the stored energy does; linear dielectrics arrive in unit 02. Notation: MR 24 writes **S** = **E** × **B**/μ₀ with no **H**; keep that. Unit 00 wrote ϱ and Σ for volume and surface charge density (σ, ρ being conductivity and resistivity); keep that.

## Physics, in order

**The puzzle and the tool.** Restate unit 00's closing question with the kettle cable. Recall Poynting's theorem from MR 24 on one slide (not re-derived; MR 24 proves it), with its integral form: field energy in a region falls by what flows out through the boundary plus the work done on charges inside. **J**·**E** > 0 in a resistor is unit 00's Joule heat; **J**·**E** < 0 marks a source.

**Sources: the battery as an EMF.** Unit 00 left the source unmodelled. Define it as in Griffiths §7.1: inside a source a non-electrostatic force per unit charge **f**_s (chemical in a battery) drives the charges, so **J** = σ(**E** + **f**_s), and the *electromotive force* is ε = ∮**f**·d**l** = ∫ **f**_s·d**l** through the source. In an ideal source (σ → ∞ inside) **E** = −**f**_s there, so **J**·**E** < 0: the field gains energy in the battery, at the rate εI for a source with no internal resistance (show it). One figure: battery, resistor, two leads, with **E** inside the battery pointing against **J**.

**The running example: a battery, a coaxial cable, a resistor.** A 12 V battery drives a 6 Ω resistor at the far end of a long coaxial cable (inner radius a = 0.5 mm, outer radius b = 2.0 mm, vacuum between — dimensions chosen for the example, say so). I = 2 A, 24 W. In the gap: **E** is radial, E = V/(r ln(b/a)) from Laplace's equation in the annulus (MR 23; a dielectric would not change this, because V fixes it); **B** is azimuthal, B = μ₀I/(2πr) from Ampère's law (MR 23). So **S** points along the cable, from battery to resistor, with S = VI/(2πr² ln(b/a)). Integrated over the annulus: exactly VI = 24 W, whatever the radii. Field magnitudes for the example: E from 17.3 kV/m at r = a to 4.33 kV/m at r = b; B = 0.80 mT at r = a. Figure: the cross-section with **E** radial, **B** circles, and **S** out of the page, shaded by magnitude (∝ 1/r²).

**Into the copper, and into the resistor.** The inner conductor has resistance R′ = ρ/(πa²) = 21.4 mΩ/m for copper (unit 00's ρ), so inside it there is an axial field E_z = IR′ = 42.8 mV/m; with the azimuthal B inside (B = μ₀Ir/(2πa²)), **S** points radially *inward*, and its flux through the conductor's surface per metre is exactly I²R′ = 85.6 mW/m — the copper's own Joule heat arriving from outside. By continuity of tangential **E**, the same axial component exists just outside the wire, so **S** in the gap tilts slightly inward near the inner conductor: most of the flow runs along the cable, a little peels off into the copper. At the resistor (a short cylinder of resistive material bridging inner to outer conductor at the end — or a cylindrical resistor in series with the inner conductor; pick one geometry and draw it), **S** enters through its surface and its flux equals I²R. Figure: a longitudinal section showing streamlines of **S** from battery to resistor, bending into the copper slightly and into the resistor entirely (a sketch, labelled as such).

**The general theorem: p = vi.** Let a closed surface Σ enclose one two-terminal component, crossed only by its two leads. Hypotheses on Σ (quasi-static): ∂**B**/∂t = 0 there, so **E** = −∇φ on Σ; the displacement current ε₀∂**E**/∂t through Σ is negligible; each lead crosses Σ at an equipotential cross-section. Then the power flowing in, −∮_Σ **S**·d**A**, equals (φ_A − φ_B) i where current i enters at lead A. Proof (on the slide, details in the notes): ∇φ × **B** = ∇ × (φ**B**) − φ ∇ × **B**; the curl term integrates to zero over a closed surface (Stokes / divergence of a curl — MR 04–05); with ∇ × **B** = μ₀**J** on Σ, −∮**S**·d**A** = −∮ φ **J**·d**A** = φ_A i − φ_B i. Then the honest list: which hypothesis each step used, and that unit 02 turns exactly these hypotheses into the lumped approximation. Check on the example: a surface round the resistor gives 24 W; round a 1 m length of inner conductor gives 85.6 mW.

**The same theorem for a whole cable.** For any two-conductor cable whose fields are static, the axial Poynting flux through a cross-section equals VI (problem; same identity in two dimensions). The kettle's two-wire cable: field lines of two parallel wires carrying ±λ and ±I in the thin-wire picture; **S** is along the cable everywhere in the cross-section, concentrated between and around the conductors, and falls off as 1/r⁴ far away (E and B both fall as 1/r² at large distance — a two-dimensional dipole field). Figure computed (thin-wire approximation, stated): E lines, B lines, and |S| shading. 2.3 kW flowing through that cross-section, in the air and the insulation, not in the copper.

**Two pictures, one number: what the electrons actually do.** This section is the bridge the learner asked for (2026-10-27): from the everyday simplification "the electrons flow through the copper and carry the energy" to what does carry it. Give it three or four slides, and make it the deck's centre of gravity, not an aside.
- *The simplification, stated fairly.* The circuit picture says each coulomb passing through the kettle gives up 230 J, and 10 C pass per second, so 2.3 kW — a conveyor belt of charge, each charge carrying "potential energy qV" from source to load. As bookkeeping this is exact in steady state: it is ∫**J**·**E** dV = VI from unit 00.
- *Why it cannot be the mechanism.* The electrons' own kinetic energy per metre of the kettle cable is n A ½ m v_d² ≈ 8.4 × 10⁻¹⁵ J/m, and it moves at v_d, a flow of ≈ 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁸ W — about 10⁻²¹ of the 2.3 kW. With 50 Hz mains no electron travels from the fuse box to the kettle at all: each oscillates about 1.3 μm either way (unit 00), and a steady current would take over 9 hours to drift one along a 10 m run. And the "potential energy qV" an electron supposedly carries is not *in* the electron: it is energy of the field, a property of the arrangement of all the charges.
- *What the electrons do instead — three jobs, none of them carrying.* (1) Moving, they are the current that makes **B** (Ampère). (2) A tiny fraction of them, displaced to the surfaces, are the surface charge that makes **E** inside and outside the wire (unit 00; Jackson's first and second roles). (3) Colliding, they hand the field's energy to the lattice as heat — the only place they touch the energy, and they touch it where it is *dissipated*, not where it travels. The wire is a guide that shapes the field; the field carries the energy; the electrons in the load are where it is turned into heat.
- *Why both pictures give the same number.* The general theorem above: the Poynting flux through any closed surface round a component equals vi. So for *totals* — what circuit theory needs — the conveyor-belt picture is never wrong; that is exactly why the simplification survives. The two pictures disagree only about *where* the energy is and *when* it arrives, and the course shows each disagreement observably, later: when a switch closes, the energy reaches the load at the field's speed, set by the insulation, long before any electron could (unit 08, the light-second puzzle); with alternating current, energy flows steadily one way while the electrons go nowhere (unit 06); and across an air gap, energy crosses with no electrons at all (unit 15, the charging pad) — the decisive case, since there the conveyor belt has no belt. Name these three forward links on the slide.
- A figure pair: left, the conveyor-belt cartoon (charges carrying parcels of energy along the wire), labelled "bookkeeping"; right, the same circuit with **S** streamlines in the space round the wires and entering the load, labelled "location" — both captioned with the same 24 W (running example) so the learner sees the agreement and the disagreement at once.

**How fast can energy move? |S| ≤ cu.** Pointwise in vacuum: |**E** × **B**|/μ₀ ≤ |E||B|/μ₀ ≤ c(ε₀E²/2 + B²/2μ₀) by the AM–GM inequality, using c² = 1/(μ₀ε₀) — so energy never flows faster than light (proof on the slide). For the coaxial example: field energy per metre W′ = πε₀V²/ln(b/a) + μ₀I² ln(b/a)/(4π) = 2.89 nJ/m (electric) + 0.555 μJ/m (magnetic), and VI/W′ ≈ 4.3 × 10⁷ m/s, well below c. Equality VI/W′ = c holds exactly when V/I = (1/2π)√(μ₀/ε₀) ln(b/a) ≈ 83 Ω for this cable — the electric and magnetic energies are then equal. Say only that this resistance will reappear in unit 08 with a name; do not name it here.

**Honesty about S.** MR 24 noted that **S** is fixed only up to a divergence-free addition; Feynman (vol. II ch. 27) shows a static charge beside a magnet with a circulating **S** and no change anywhere. So: flux through closed surfaces (the theorem above) is physics; the streamlines on the figures are the standard, conventional choice, and their consistency with every closed-surface balance is what justifies drawing them. One slide, `box warn`.

**Closing.** Back to the kettle: the 2.3 kW travels through the space round the two conductors at 230 V and 10 A, enters the copper only to the extent of its 0.672 W/m (unit 00), and enters the element through its surface. One remark: with mains AC and a resistive load, v and i change sign together, so **S** points toward the kettle at every instant; with a load that stores energy it reverses for part of each cycle — unit 06's reactive power. Then: the theorem's hypotheses are unit 02's lumped approximation.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and several numbers, all computed with python3.
- **The coax by numbers** (routine): E, B, S at given radii; the flux; field magnitudes for another voltage and current.
- **Into a resistor through its side** (routine): a uniform cylindrical resistor of length L and radius r carrying I: E and B at its surface, S, and the flux 2πrL·S = I²R.
- **Into the copper** (routine): the inner conductor's inward flux per metre equals I²R′, for the example and in general.
- **p = vi, proved** (medium, proof): the theorem with every hypothesis named; then which hypothesis fails for a surface that cuts through a capacitor's gap.
- **Any two-conductor cable** (medium, proof): for static fields in a cable of arbitrary cross-section, the axial Poynting flux through a cross-section is VI (two-dimensional version of the same identity).
- **Two parallel wires** (medium): in the thin-wire picture, S on the plane of symmetry between the wires as a function of position; its far-field 1/r⁴ decay.
- **The battery** (medium): with internal resistance r_i, the battery emits εI − I²r_i through its surface; the maximum over load resistance is not asked here (unit 04) — ask only for the energy balance.
- **The conveyor belt, audited** (medium, number): the electrons' kinetic energy per metre and its flow for the kettle cable and for unit 00's thinner 1.5 mm² variant; the ratio to the power delivered; then the "potential energy" version — show that the energy qV attributed to a charge changes when other charges move, so it is a property of the configuration (the field), not of the charge.
- **Energy can't outrun light** (medium, proof): |S| ≤ cu pointwise in vacuum; when is it an equality (E ⊥ B, |E| = c|B|).
- **Stored versus delivered** (medium): W′ for the coax; the ratio VI/W′ as a function of the load resistance; its maximum c at ≈ 83 Ω (for b/a = 4).
- **Charging capacitor, revisited** (medium): MR 24's capacitor, with **S** entering through the rim, written as an energy balance; then a closed surface round the whole capacitor and its leads, and what the theorem needs there.
- **The ambiguity** (medium): show that adding ∇ × **F** to **S** changes no closed-surface flux; Feynman's charge-beside-a-magnet example, qualitatively (cited).
- **AC, one remark** (routine): with v = V̂ cos ωt across a resistor, show the flux into it is never negative; with an ideal capacitor across the same source, the flux into it averages zero.
