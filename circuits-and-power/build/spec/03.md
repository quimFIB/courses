## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 02 derived Kirchhoff's two laws from Maxwell's equations and ended by promising that they are "a pair of orthogonal subspaces of ℝᵐ". This unit keeps the promise. It turns a circuit diagram into a directed graph and its incidence matrix, shows that the current law and the voltage law say exactly that currents lie in ker A and voltages in im Aᵀ, and proves Tellegen's theorem as the orthogonality of those two subspaces. Two consequences carry the rest of the course: power is conserved in *any* lumped network, whatever its components (unit 02 only checked this for one loop, and says so twice — slide *Worked: the kettle, one loop* and problem P2 — make both forward references land); and a network of resistors is reciprocal (unit 02's notes promise reciprocity "again as a theorem about whole networks" — land that too).

The learner knows the linear algebra cold and has never seen a graph used this way. Graph vocabulary (node, edge, orientation, path, cycle, connected component, tree, spanning tree, cut) is defined here from nothing, briefly, as the unit needs it.

It must not become: the graph Laplacian and its properties (unit 04 — nodal analysis is set up and a 3 × 3 system solved for the running example, but symmetry, positive semi-definiteness, existence and uniqueness of node potentials, and L = AGAᵀ as an object are unit 04's; say so where the system appears); Thévenin, maximum power, effective resistance (unit 04); dynamics, capacitors and inductors in a network (unit 05 — the element laws here are resistors, ideal sources and one ideal diode; Tellegen's statement for arbitrary elements is made, and unit 05's use of it with complex phasors is named, not done); algorithms for large networks (sparse solvers, the matrix-tree theorem — out).

## Physics and mathematics, in order

**From a diagram to a graph.** Each element is an *edge* with an orientation — the direction of its current arrow, which under the passive sign convention (unit 02) also fixes its voltage's polarity, + at the tail. Each node is a *node*. n nodes, m edges, c connected components. The incidence matrix A (n × m) with A_ke = +1 if edge e leaves node k, −1 if it enters (AUTHORING.md's convention). Draw the running example's diagram and its graph side by side, and write its A. Note what the graph forgets (which element is which) and what it keeps (everything the two laws care about). Ideal wires are not edges: they are what a node *is* (unit 02's equipotential node).

**The two laws as subspaces.**
- KCL: (Ai)_k is the net current leaving node k through its edges, so the current law (unit 02) is Ai = 0 for an isolated network; with currents injected from outside at the nodes, Ai = s (AUTHORING.md). Currents obeying it form ker A — the *cycle space*, because (proof) it is spanned by the signed indicator vectors of cycles.
- KVL: v = Aᵀφ for node potentials φ, since v_e = φ_tail − φ_head. Unit 02 proved that the voltage law holds exactly when such potentials exist (its telescoping argument); here the equivalence is restated: v sums to zero round every cycle ⟺ v ∈ im Aᵀ. Voltages obeying it form im Aᵀ — the *cut space*, spanned by the signed indicators of cuts (the edges crossing between a set of nodes S and its complement: Aᵀ applied to the indicator of S).
- **Rank**: ker Aᵀ is the potentials equal across every edge, i.e. constant on each component, so dim ker Aᵀ = c and rank A = n − c (proof). Hence dim(cut space) = n − c and dim(cycle space) = m − n + c, by rank–nullity. Name m − n + c the *cyclomatic number*.
- **Orthogonality**: ker A = (im Aᵀ)^⊥ in ℝᵐ (the assumed fundamental theorem of linear algebra). The two laws are orthogonal complements, and together fix m numbers: m − n + c from the currents' freedom, n − c from the voltages'. Count: m unknown currents + m unknown voltages, m element laws, (n − c) independent KCL equations and (m − n + c) independent KVL equations — exactly 2m equations. This is why a circuit problem is well posed in count (not yet in solvability — that is unit 04's, for resistors).

**Bases from a spanning tree.** A spanning tree T (n − 1 edges for a connected graph, proof that it exists and has n − 1 edges, or cite Bollobás ch. I) gives both bases at once: each *chord* (edge not in T) closes exactly one cycle with T — the *fundamental cycles*, m − n + 1 of them, independent because each contains its own chord and no other; each *tree edge* determines exactly one cut, by deleting it from T — the *fundamental cuts*, n − 1 of them. Figures: the bridge with one spanning tree drawn bold, its three fundamental cycles and three fundamental cuts. The bridge's graph is K₄, which has 16 spanning trees (count them on a figure in the problems, no theorem). History sentence in the notes: Kirchhoff's 1847 paper and its fundamental cycles (Sources).

**Mesh and nodal analysis as choices of basis.**
- *Nodal*: parametrise the cut space by node potentials with one node per component grounded (φ = 0): n − c unknowns, and KCL at the other nodes gives the equations. For the bridge: 3 unknowns, 3 equations; with the source an ideal voltage source, one potential is fixed and 2 unknowns remain (the running example does this).
- *Mesh / loop*: parametrise the cycle space by loop currents round a basis of cycles (fundamental cycles, or for a plane graph the bounded faces — *meshes*): m − n + c unknowns, KVL round each basis cycle gives the equations.
- Which is smaller depends on the graph: a ladder of many rungs has few nodes per loop, a dense mesh the reverse. For K₄ both are 3. Say outright that the system nodal analysis produces has a matrix with special properties that unit 04 studies.
- **Planar graphs and Euler's formula**: for a connected graph drawn in the plane without crossings, n − m + f = 2, where f counts faces including the outer one, so the bounded faces number m − n + 1 = the cycle space's dimension, and the meshes are a basis (proof: each mesh contains an edge on the outer boundary or an edge shared with a mesh already used — or by induction on the chords of a spanning tree, with the Jordan curve theorem cited). A non-planar graph (K₅ or K₃,₃, figure) has no meshes, and loop analysis with fundamental cycles still works.

**Tellegen's theorem.** For any v ∈ im Aᵀ and any i ∈ ker A on the same oriented graph, vᵀi = Σ_e v_e i_e = 0. Proof on the slide: vᵀi = (Aᵀφ)ᵀi = φᵀ(Ai) = 0. Then what it does *not* need, and why that matters:
- no element law at all is used — linear, nonlinear, time-varying, active or passive;
- v and i may come from *different* networks on the same graph, or from the same network at *different* times: v(t₁)ᵀi(t₂) = 0;
- with v and i from the same network at the same instant, Σ_e v_e i_e = 0 is conservation of power: under the passive sign convention, the power absorbed by all elements sums to zero, sources' negative terms included. This is the theorem unit 02 only checked for one loop.
- with injected currents (Ai = s): vᵀi = φᵀs — the power delivered at the ports. Used for reciprocity below and in unit 04.
- one honest slide (`box warn`): Tellegen is a statement about the lumped model; it is true because the model is consistent with unit 01's theorem (the flux of **S** through a surface round each element is v_e i_e), not a new physical law. Unit 01 + unit 02's hypotheses ⇒ Tellegen; Tellegen does not say where the energy is.

**Reciprocity.** A network of linear resistors with two pairs of terminals marked as ports. Run it twice: experiment ˆ drives port 1, experiment ˜ drives port 2. Tellegen across the two experiments (v̂ᵀĩ = 0 and ṽᵀî = 0), with v_e = R_e i_e on every internal edge, makes the internal sums Σ R_e î_e ĩ_e identical in both, so the port terms balance: v̂₁ĩ₁ + v̂₂ĩ₂ = ṽ₁î₁ + ṽ₂î₂ (signs fixed by the port convention — state it with a figure). Drive port 1 with current I and leave port 2 open, then the reverse: the open-circuit voltage at port 2 per ampere into port 1 equals the open-circuit voltage at port 1 per ampere into port 2. The transfer resistances are equal: z₂₁ = z₁₂. Where it fails: a network containing a dependent source or anything whose element law is not symmetric — name that the transistor amplifier is the standard non-reciprocal case, and that reciprocity in fields (the Lorentz theorem) is branch 16's; unit 02's M₁₂ = M₂₁ was a special case. A diode network is not even linear, so the statement does not apply.

**Superposition, and where it stops.** For a network of linear resistors and ideal sources, the response is linear in the sources (the solution map is linear — the equations are linear and, for the bridge, uniquely solvable; the general uniqueness is unit 04's, say so). With one ideal diode in the network, superposition fails while Tellegen still holds: the problem sheet's diode circuit shows both. One slide.

## The running example: the Wheatstone bridge
Four nodes A, B, C, D. Arms R₁ = 100 Ω (A→B), R₂ = 200 Ω (B→C), R₃ = 300 Ω (A→D), R₄ = 400 Ω (D→C); a detector of resistance R_g = 50 Ω across the diagonal B→D; an ideal 10 V source across the other diagonal, oriented C→A under the passive convention (so its v = φ_C − φ_A = −10 V). The values are chosen for the example (say so). Six edges, four nodes, one component: K₄, the complete graph on four nodes. Both subspaces have dimension 3. All numbers below are exact (computed with python3 fractions); the writer recomputes them:
- φ_A = 10 V, φ_C = 0, φ_B = 780/121 V ≈ 6.446 V, φ_D = 760/121 V ≈ 6.281 V;
- detector current i_g = (φ_B − φ_D)/R_g = 2/605 A ≈ 3.306 mA, from B to D;
- arm currents ≈ 35.54 mA (R₁), 32.23 mA (R₂), 12.40 mA (R₃), 15.70 mA (R₄);
- source current 29/605 A ≈ 47.93 mA, source delivers 58/121 W ≈ 479.3 mW; the five resistors absorb 126.3 + 207.8 + 46.1 + 98.6 + 0.55 mW — Σ v_e i_e = 0 (Tellegen, checked).
- **Balance**: i_g = 0 exactly when R₁R₄ = R₂R₃, whatever R_g and the source (proof, from the two dividers). Here R₁R₄ = 40 000 Ω² and R₂R₃ = 60 000 Ω², so it is unbalanced. The classical use: with R₄ unknown and R₃ adjustable, balance gives R₄ = R₂R₃/R₁ without trusting the source or the detector's calibration.
- **Tellegen across two networks**: keep the graph and orientation, change every resistor and the source (for instance arms 150, 150, 600, 250 Ω, R_g = 20 Ω, source 3 V): v(first)ᵀi(second) = 0 and the other way round, though no physical quantity pairs them.
- **Reciprocity on the bridge**: ports AC and BD, with the five resistors (arms and R_g) as the network. 1 A into A out of C gives φ_B − φ_D = 100/29 V ≈ 3.448 V; 1 A into B out of D gives φ_A − φ_C = 100/29 V too. Without R_g (detector open) both are (R₂R₃ − R₁R₄)/(R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + R₄) = 20 Ω, which is visibly symmetric under swapping the ports; with R_g it is not visibly symmetric, and is still equal — that is the theorem earning its keep.
Threaded: overview (the bridge, and the puzzle "why does the detector's resistance not matter at balance?"); graph section (its A, its dimensions, its 16 spanning trees); bases section (its three fundamental cycles and cuts, its nodal and mesh equations — 2 unknown potentials once the source fixes φ_A; 3 mesh currents for the three faces of its plane drawing); Tellegen section (the power check above, and the cross-network check); reciprocity section (100/29 Ω both ways); closing (the kitchen scale).

## The real system, at the close: the strain-gauge bridge in a kitchen scale
A load cell carries strain gauges, resistors whose resistance changes in proportion to their strain: ΔR/R = GF·ε with gauge factor GF ≈ 2 (Sources: Vishay TN-505, or labelled representative). A part-in-ten-thousand change, ΔR/R = 10⁻⁴ (ε = 50 × 10⁻⁶ at GF = 2), on a 350 Ω gauge is 35 mΩ.
- *One resistor, measured directly*: in a divider fed with 5 V, its voltage moves from 2.5 V by 125 μV — a change of 5 × 10⁻⁵ of a reading that itself drifts with the supply: a supply drift of 10⁻⁴ moves the reading by 250 μV, twice the signal.
- *The bridge* (quarter bridge: one active gauge, three fixed 350 Ω arms, 5 V excitation, detector of high resistance): output V·x/(2(2 + x)) with x = ΔR/R, ≈ V·x/4 = 125 μV (exactly 124.99 μV), measured against *zero*, and proportional to V, so the same 10⁻⁴ supply drift moves it by 10⁻⁴ of itself — 12.5 nV.
- *Temperature*: if two adjacent arms change by the same fraction (two identical gauges, one strained, one not, at the same temperature), their ratio and hence the output are unchanged — derived from the balance condition, one line.
- *Choice of ratio arms*: the output's sensitivity at balance is V·a/(1 + a)² for ratio a = R₁/R₂, largest (V/4) at a = 1. In the problems.
Say once that a commercial scale's electronics (amplifier, converter) are beyond the course, and claim nothing about any particular product.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least two proofs and several numbers, all computed with python3.
- **Dimension counts** (routine): n, m, c, the cyclomatic number and the dimensions of both subspaces for several graphs drawn on the page — a ladder of k rungs, a cube's edge graph (8, 12, 5 cycles), two disjoint triangles (c = 2), K₅.
- **Rank of the incidence matrix** (medium, proof): rank A = n − c, via ker Aᵀ.
- **Cycles span ker A** (medium, proof): every vector in ker A is a combination of cycle indicators — peel a cycle off the support of a nonzero circulation.
- **Spanning trees of K₄** (routine): draw all 16 (or argue the count: 4^{4−2} by Cayley's formula, *cited*, checked by listing); choose one and write the fundamental cycle and cut matrices.
- **Euler's formula** (medium, proof): by induction on chords of a spanning tree, with the Jordan curve theorem cited; consequence: meshes are a basis of the cycle space for a plane graph.
- **Tellegen at two times** (routine): a network whose source changes between t₁ and t₂; v(t₁)ᵀi(t₂) = 0 with numbers.
- **Reciprocity from Tellegen** (medium, proof — the checkpoint's): for any network of linear resistors, z₂₁ = z₁₂; checked on the bridge with R_g included (100/29 Ω).
- **A diode, two sources** (medium): a small network with two ideal sources and one ideal diode in series with a resistor (values on the page — the bridge with a second source in an arm will do); solve with each source alone and with both; the diode's state changes, superposition fails, Tellegen holds in all three.
- **Non-reciprocal on purpose** (medium): a two-port with a dependent source (a current-controlled voltage source, defined on the page as an element relation) where z₂₁ ≠ z₁₂; find where the reciprocity proof breaks.
- **Bridge sensitivity** (medium, number): output V·a/(1 + a)² · x at balance to first order in x = ΔR/R; maximum at a = 1; at a = 10 the sensitivity falls to 8.26% of V instead of 25%.
- **The kitchen scale's bridge** (routine, number): 350 Ω arms, 5 V, x = 10⁻⁴: 124.99 μV exact against 125 μV linearised; the nonlinearity error, x/(2 + x) against x/2, relative size about x/2 = 5 × 10⁻⁵.
- **Temperature compensation** (routine): two gauges in adjacent arms, both heated by the same fraction, one strained; the output responds to strain only.
