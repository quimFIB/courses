## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 05 built phasors and proved Re Z ≥ 0 for passive networks. This unit asks what the power is. The answer has two parts, and the second is the new idea: the instantaneous power splits into a part with a non-zero mean that does work (P) and a part that averages to nothing but costs current (Q) — energy sloshing between the source and the load's fields twice a cycle. Complex power S = VI* packages both, and it is conserved across any network by unit 03's Tellegen theorem (unit 03's notes promise exactly this). Then two engineering consequences: power-factor correction, and conjugate matching — unit 04's maximum-power theorem with complex impedances (unit 04 promises this too).

This unit is also the second station of the **energy-flow thread** from unit 01 (unit 01's closing promised it): in unit 01's two pictures, the conveyor belt and the Poynting flux, an AC load shows the belt picture at its weakest — the electrons go nowhere, yet energy is delivered — and the reactive load shows the field picture at its most vivid — the flux runs backwards for part of every cycle. One section, with figures, calling back to unit 01's pictures by name.

It must not become: three-phase (unit 11 — the workshop is treated as single-phase here, said so, and unit 11 gives it three phases); transformers (unit 07); harmonics and non-sinusoidal power (unit 14 — RMS is defined generally in unit 05, but distortion power, and the non-sinusoidal generalisations of Q, are out; one sentence that Q as defined here belongs to sinusoids); tariff economics beyond the one sourced fact below.

## Physics and mathematics, in order

**Instantaneous power at 50 Hz.** With v(t) = √2 V cos ωt and i(t) = √2 I cos(ωt − φ) (RMS magnitudes V, I; φ = arg V − arg I as in AUTHORING.md):
p(t) = VI cos φ (1 + cos 2ωt) + VI sin φ sin 2ωt.
The first term never changes sign and averages to P = VI cos φ; the second averages to zero and has amplitude Q = VI sin φ. So p oscillates at 100 Hz between P − S and P + S, S = VI. A figure: v, i and p against t for a resistor, an inductor, and the workshop. MR 06's power slide gave the mean power for peak amplitudes as ½F₀²ω|χ| sin φ, with its φ the displacement's lag behind the force — the same formula once current is read as velocity (so its angle is 90° from this one) and the ½ is absorbed into RMS (land unit 05's "unit 06 develops AC power").

**Real, reactive, apparent, complex.** Definitions with units: P in W, Q in var, S = |S| in VA — and why the units are given different names when they are dimensionally the same (to say which product is meant). **Complex power** S = V I* = P + iQ (AUTHORING.md: Q > 0 for an inductive load, current lagging). For an impedance, S = Z|I|² = |V|²/Z*, so P = R|I|² and Q = X|I|²: the resistance absorbs real power and the reactance reactive power. The power triangle, drawn. Power factor cos φ = P/|S|, lagging or leading.
- **What Q is, physically.** For a network of R, L, C: Q = 2ω(⟨W_m⟩ − ⟨W_e⟩), twice ω times the excess of mean magnetic over mean electric stored energy. Prove it from unit 05's Tellegen-with-conjugates sum (the same sum that proved Re Z ≥ 0, now with s = iω and its imaginary part). For an inductor alone it is ω L|I|² = 2ω⟨W_m⟩; for a capacitor −2ω⟨W_e⟩. So Q is the rate of the energy's back-and-forth, in units of power, and a load with equal mean magnetic and electric energy (a tuned circuit at resonance) draws none.

**Conservation of complex power.** Tellegen (unit 03) with v the branch phasors (obey KVL) and the *conjugates* of the branch currents (obey KCL, because A is real): Σ_e V_e I_e* = 0. Hence Σ S_e = 0 over every element of any network, and P and Q are conserved *separately*: the reactive power the source supplies equals the sum of the loads' Q, element by element. Land unit 03's "unit 06 uses the same pairing to prove that complex power is conserved". Note what is *not* conserved: |S| (apparent powers do not add) — a three-load example with numbers.

**The energy-flow thread: unit 01's two pictures, on AC.** (Unit 01's closing named this unit for it.)
- *The belt goes nowhere.* In the workshop's supply cable (35 mm² copper, 124.2 A RMS — below), the conduction electrons drift with amplitude √2 I/(neA) = 0.370 mm/s and so move back and forth through 1.18 μm (amplitude; unit 00's n), 50 times a second. No electron makes the trip from the fuse box to the workshop, or ever will; yet 20 kW arrive. Unit 01's resistive-load figure (1.3 μm for the kettle) gave the same verdict; here it is for a real working load.
- *The flux runs backwards.* E in the cable's insulation follows v(t) and B follows i(t), so unit 01's Poynting flux through a cross-section is p(t) at every instant (unit 01's theorem, quasi-static). With the workshop's power factor 0.7, p(t) swings between −8.57 kW and +48.6 kW: the flux points back towards the supply for 25.3% of every cycle — the fraction is exactly φ/π (a problem). A figure: the cable's cross-section with **S** arrows at four instants, two of them reversed; and a plot of p(t) with the negative stretches shaded. Q is the amplitude of the part that reverses; P is the net forward flow.
- *The bill.* The current that carries Q flows in the copper and heats it (unit 01's inward flux into the conductor, I²R′ per metre), though Q delivers nothing. That is the whole case for correcting the power factor.

**Power-factor correction.** A capacitor across the load supplies the load's Q locally: Q_C = −ωCV². Sized for unity: C = Q/(ωV²). After correction the supply current falls from |S|/V to P/V, and the cable's loss by the factor (cos φ)² — exactly, for a cable that is purely resistive (say the cable's own reactance is neglected, and what it would change). Partial correction to a target cos φ: Q_C = P(tan φ₁ − tan φ₂). Over-correction: with twice the capacitor, the load becomes leading at 0.7 and the current returns to its uncorrected value — the loss returns too.

**Conjugate matching.** A source with Thévenin phasor V_th and impedance Z_s = R_s + iX_s (unit 05's Thévenin-for-phasors) feeding Z_L = R_L + iX_L: P_L = |V_th|² R_L/((R_s + R_L)² + (X_s + X_L)²). Maximised over X_L by X_L = −X_s (cancel the reactance), then over R_L by unit 04's theorem: Z_L = Z_s*. P_max = |V_th|²/(4R_s), and the efficiency is again exactly 50%. If only R_L can vary, the best is R_L = |Z_s| (prove), and it delivers less. Land unit 04's "the maximum-power theorem becomes conjugate matching (unit 06)". Unit 15 will meet a problem in which the maximum-efficiency load is not the conjugate match; name it.

## The running example: a workshop drawing 20 kW at power factor 0.7
Single-phase 230 V, 50 Hz (an assumption, and unit 11 gives it three phases); 20 kW at cos φ = 0.7 lagging. Supply cable 50 m of two-core 35 mm² copper: R = ρ·100 m/35 mm² = 48.0 mΩ (cable reactance neglected; said so). Computed (python3; the writer recomputes):
- |S| = 28.57 kVA, Q = 20.40 kvar, I = 124.2 A; φ = 45.57°.
- p(t) between −8.57 kW and +48.57 kW, negative 25.3% of each cycle; ⟨W_m⟩ − ⟨W_e⟩ = Q/2ω = 32.5 J.
- Cable: loss 741 W, resistive drop 5.96 V.
- Corrected to unity: C = 1.228 mF (Q_C = 20.40 kvar at 230 V, 50 Hz); current 86.96 A; cable loss 363 W — the factor 0.490 = 0.7²; drop 4.17 V.
- Corrected to 0.95: C = 0.832 mF, current 91.5 A.
- Over-corrected with 2.456 mF: leading 0.7, 124.2 A, 741 W again.
- Electrons in the cable: drift amplitude 0.370 mm/s, displacement amplitude 1.18 μm.
