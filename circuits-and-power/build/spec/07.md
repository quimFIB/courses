## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 02 built the coupled pair and bounded M² ≤ L₁L₂, and promised: "unit 07 names M/√(L₁L₂)", "unit 07 takes the limit as the ideal transformer", "Unit 07 builds the transformer from this". Unit 04 promised "the reason lines run at high voltage: the loss in a line of fixed resistance at fixed delivered power falls as 1/V²". Unit 05 left the ignition coil's secondary open for this unit. This unit keeps all three promises: the ideal transformer as a limit of coupled inductors (a theorem, not a definition), the real one as the ideal one plus three named imperfections each with a physical cause, and then the reason for the whole high-voltage grid — and what stops voltages rising further.

The learner has never met a magnetic material. Iron is needed (it is why L can be large and why the coupling is tight), so it gets the treatment unit 02 gave dielectrics: one slide, cited, as far as the unit needs — a linear relative permeability μ_r multiplying the inductance of a closed core, saturation as the limit of that linearity, and hysteresis as the loss per cycle — and no more.

It must not become: three-phase (unit 11 — the street transformer is modelled here as one single-phase transformer of the same rating and ratio, and the deck says so on the overview; unit 11 gives it three phases and star–delta); magnetic circuits and reluctance as a subject (one slide at most, the toroid); machines (unit 12); transmission-line effects (unit 08 — a 400 km line is still lumped here, and the overview says unit 08 revisits it); the weakly coupled limit (unit 15 — k ≪ 1 is named as unit 15's subject in one sentence).

## Physics and mathematics, in order

**Coupled inductors, and the coupling coefficient.** Unit 02's pair: v₁ = L₁ di₁/dt + M di₂/dt, v₂ = M di₁/dt + L₂ di₂/dt (passive convention at both ports, dot marking explained once with a figure — the dot says which terminal of each winding the shared flux makes positive together). Name k = M/√(L₁L₂); unit 02's energy argument gives |k| ≤ 1 (cite it, one line). In phasors: V₁ = iωL₁I₁ + iωMI₂, V₂ = iωMI₁ + iωL₂I₂ — the transformer as a two-port with a symmetric impedance matrix (unit 03's reciprocity, again; land that). Note c = 2 in unit 03's dimension count for two magnetically coupled circuits (unit 03 named this transformer for it).

**Iron, in one slide (cited).** A closed core of a linear material with relative permeability μ_r multiplies the flux per ampere-turn by μ_r (Griffiths ch. 6; Fitzgerald ch. 1): for a toroid of mean length l and cross-section A, L = μ_rμ₀N²A/l — derive the μ_r = 1 case from Ampère's law (MR 23) and cite the factor. Two consequences used later: L can be made very large (μ_r in the thousands for transformer steel — cite a range from Fitzgerald, or say "thousands" only if confirmed), and nearly all the flux stays in the core, so k is close to 1. The linearity fails at saturation (B̂ of order 1.5–2 T for silicon steel — cite or omit the figure).

**The ideal transformer as a limit (the checkpoint's first part).** With windings N₁, N₂ on one core, L₁ ∝ N₁², L₂ ∝ N₂², M ∝ N₁N₂ (same core, same flux per ampere-turn), so √(L₁/L₂) = N₁/N₂ = n. Theorem: as k → 1 and L₁ → ∞ with L₁/L₂ = n² fixed, the two-port tends to V₁ = nV₂ and I₁ = −I₂/n (passive convention), for any load. Proof: at k = 1, V₁/V₂ = √(L₁/L₂) exactly for any currents (subtract the equations); solve the primary equation for I₁ with a load Z_L on the secondary and let L₁ → ∞ — the *magnetising current* V₁/(iωL₁) vanishes. Consequences: power passes unchanged (V₁I₁* = −V₂I₂*: check with unit 06's S); an impedance Z_L on the secondary is seen at the primary as n²Z_L (*reflected impedance*). Units: n is dimensionless; check the energy: the ideal transformer stores nothing.

**The real transformer: the T equivalent circuit.** For k < 1 and finite L, the exact equivalent: a series *leakage* inductance L₁ − nM on the primary, n²(L₂ − M/n) on the secondary side referred, and a shunt *magnetising* inductance nM, followed by an ideal transformer of ratio n (derive by matching the two-port equations; the choice of n is free and N₁/N₂ is the natural one). Then the losses: winding resistances in series (*copper loss*, ∝ I²), and a shunt resistance R_c for the *core loss* (∝ V², nearly independent of load). Each element's physical cause:
- *Leakage*: flux of one winding that does not link the other (k < 1).
- *Magnetising current*: finite L₁ — the current needed to make the flux at all.
- *Hysteresis*: the energy per cycle per unit volume is the area ∮H dB of the B–H loop (derive from the work done by the source on the winding, with H as cited), so the power is that area times f times the volume.
- *Eddy currents*: Faraday's law in the core's own conducting steel drives currents round the flux; for a lamination of thickness t (thin against the skin depth — branch 10's, named), the classical loss per volume is σω²B̂²t²/24 = π²f²B̂²t²σ/6 (derive from Faraday and Ohm in the sheet, with E linear across the thickness). It scales as t²: laminating defeats it. Numbers: ρ_steel = 0.5 μΩ·m (assumed for silicon steel, unless Fitzgerald gives one to cite), B̂ = 1.5 T, 50 Hz, t = 0.3 mm: 1.67 kW/m³, i.e. 0.218 W/kg at 7650 kg/m³ (assumed density); at t = 0.5 mm, 0.605 W/kg. Say these are the classical estimate, which real steel's measured losses exceed.

**Measuring the circuit: the open- and short-circuit tests.** Open-circuit test, at rated voltage on one side with the other open: the current is the magnetising current, the power is the core loss → R_c and the magnetising reactance. Short-circuit test, at rated current with the other side shorted, at reduced voltage: the power is the copper loss, the voltage the leakage drop → the series R and X. Why each test isolates its branch (orders of magnitude from the running example). The street transformer in numbers (below).

**Efficiency, and where it peaks (the checkpoint's second part).** At load fraction x of rated S and power factor cos φ: η(x) = xS cos φ/(xS cos φ + P₀ + x²P_k). Theorem: η is greatest where x²P_k = P₀ — copper loss equals core loss — at x = √(P₀/P_k), for any cos φ. Proof by differentiating, or by AM–GM on P₀/x + xP_k. Why a distribution transformer is designed with P₀ ≪ P_k: it is energised all year and fully loaded rarely.

**Why power lines run at high voltage.** Delivering P at voltage V through a line of total resistance R: current P/V, loss P²R/V² (unit 04's I²R; at unity power factor, and unit 06 adds that reactive current costs the same). The checkpoint's numbers: 100 MW through 10 Ω at 400 kV: 250 A, 0.625 MW, 0.6%; at 132 kV: 758 A, 5.74 MW, 5.7%; at 20 kV: 5000 A and a "loss" of 250 MW — more than the whole 100 MW, meaning the line cannot deliver it at all (the voltage at the far end would collapse; unit 13 makes this precise). Stated as a single-phase or DC-equivalent model with the 10 Ω as the total loop resistance (say so). The transformer is what makes the choice of V free at each stage: the chain 400 kV → 132 kV → 20 kV → 400 V (IEC 60038 values, confirmed), each step a transformer.

**What stops the voltage rising.** Three limits, named with figures only where cited:
- *Corona*: the field at a conductor's surface ionises the air when it exceeds air's breakdown strength (≈3 MV/m, cited). Estimate from MR 23's two-wire field (thin-wire, single-phase model, said so): surface field ≈ V̂/(2r ln(D/r)) for peak voltage V̂ between conductors of radius r at spacing D. At 400 kV RMS (565.7 kV peak), D = 10 m: r = 0.5 cm gives 7.4 MV/m, r = 1.5 cm gives 2.9 MV/m — at the threshold. A bundle of four 1.5 cm subconductors on a 45 cm square acts for the far field like one conductor of radius 0.21 m (geometric-mean radius, derive), and brings each subconductor's surface field to about 1.2 MV/m. That is why 400 kV lines carry bundles; at 20 kV, a 5 mm conductor 1 m from its partner sees 0.53 MV/m and needs none. Figures computed; the bundle's equivalent radius derived; Peek's corrections cited if used.
- *Insulation*: insulators and the air gaps to towers and ground must grow with V (qualitative unless a standard is confirmed).
- *Cost and reactive power*: higher voltage lines need bigger towers and draw more charging current — named as unit 08's and unit 14's subject (HVDC), one sentence.
