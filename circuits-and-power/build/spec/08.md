## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 02's third hypothesis — the circuit is small against a wavelength — fails for a long line, and unit 02 promised "unit 08 replaces the lumped model there". This unit does it exactly for lines whose fields are transverse to their length (TEM): Maxwell's equations reduce to two first-order PDEs in z and t for v and i, which are MR 18's wave equation. Everything follows from that and from MR 18: speed, characteristic impedance, reflection at a load, the bounce diagram, and the steady state as the sum of infinitely many reflections. It also keeps a promise made three times in unit 01 ("Unit 08 — when"): energy is seen travelling, as a wave front of E and B between the conductors, at the speed the insulation sets, carrying v₊²/Z₀ — unit 01's Poynting flux with a time stamp. And, at the learner's request, a lightning stroke: the sharpest case in the course of energy not carried by the charges.

The learner knows MR 18's wave equation well and has unit 02's coaxial L′ and C′. They have never met a transmission line, a reflection coefficient or a bounce diagram.

It must not become: sinusoidal steady state on lines in depth (branch 09 — input impedance of a terminated line, standing-wave ratio, stubs and the Smith chart are 09's; this unit uses the propagation constant only for loss and for the long power line, and stops); the skin effect (branch 10 — R′ is taken as given, and its frequency dependence named as 10's); waveguides and non-TEM modes (one sentence: they exist when the cross-section is comparable to a wavelength); antennas and radiation (branch 16 — the lightning channel's radio pulse is named, not computed); three-phase lines (unit 11, unit 13).

## Physics and mathematics, in order

**From Maxwell to the telegrapher's equations.** A two-conductor line along z, uniform cross-section, perfect conductors and a uniform lossless insulator (ε, μ₀), fields transverse (E_z = B_z = 0). Then in each cross-section E and B are static 2D fields: E = −∇_⊥φ with ∇²_⊥φ = 0 (MR 23), so a voltage v(z, t) between the conductors is well defined in each cross-section — this is exactly where unit 02's (H1) holds *in the plane* while failing *along the line* (say so). The z-components of Faraday's and Ampère–Maxwell's laws, integrated over the right path and surface in the cross-section, give
∂v/∂z = −L′ ∂i/∂t, ∂i/∂z = −C′ ∂v/∂t,
with L′ and C′ the per-metre inductance and capacitance of unit 02 — the checkpoint's derivation, for the coax in full (a figure: the path for Faraday between the conductors over dz; the surface for charge conservation round the inner conductor). That L′C′ = με for every TEM line (unit 02 noted it for the coax) is proved here in general (the cross-sectional field problem for E and for B is the same Laplace problem). Lossy line: add R′ (series, conductors) and G′ (shunt, insulation) — as a model, stated; R′ at high frequency is branch 10's.

**Waves, speed, and Z₀.** Eliminating i: ∂²v/∂z² = L′C′ ∂²v/∂t², MR 18's wave equation, speed u = 1/√(L′C′) = c/√ε_r — the speed at which signals and energy move along a cable (land unit 00's "that rearrangement travels at a large fraction of c, set by the insulation — unit 08 computes it", and unit 02's "unit 08 turns this into a wave speed"). d'Alembert (MR 18, cited): v = v₊(t − z/u) + v₋(t + z/u), and the second telegrapher's equation gives i = (v₊ − v₋)/Z₀ with **Z₀ = √(L′/C′)**, the characteristic impedance: the ratio of forward voltage to forward current, a real resistance for a lossless line, though nothing dissipates. Units: √(H/F) = Ω. For the coax: Z₀ = (η₀/2π√ε_r) ln(b/a), η₀ = √(μ₀/ε₀) = 376.7 Ω. **Polyethylene, b/a = 3.49: Z₀ = 49.96 Ω ≈ 50 Ω** (exactly 50 Ω at b/a = 3.493), L′ = 250 nH/m, C′ = 100.1 pF/m, u = 1.999 × 10⁸ m/s — the checkpoint's number. Unit 01's coax (vacuum, b/a = 4) has Z₀ = 83.1 Ω. Two parallel wires of radius r at spacing D in air: Z₀ = (η₀/π) arccosh(D/2r) (derive from MR 23's two-line-charge potential, or cite Pozar).
- *Energy*: a forward wave carries power v₊i₊ = v₊²/Z₀; check that this equals the Poynting flux through the insulation (unit 01's integral VI, now for a travelling wave), and that the wave's energy per metre ½C′v² + ½L′i² is split equally and moves at u. The front of a wave switched on at t = 0 is where the field ends: ahead of it there is no E, no B, no energy — and the charges on the conductors behind it are what terminates the field (unit 00's surface charge, now moving). Land unit 01's "Unit 08 shows that a wave travelling along this cable carries exactly this ratio of voltage to current" (unit 01's actual words, about R_* = Z₀; the spec first misquoted them as "exactly VI").

**Reflection.** A line of Z₀ ending in a resistance R_L: at the end v = R_L i forces a reflected wave v₋ = Γ_L v₊ with Γ_L = (R_L − Z₀)/(R_L + Z₀). Open end Γ = +1, short Γ = −1, matched Γ = 0: MR 18's free and fixed ends of a string (cite; say which is which and why the voltage behaves like the string's displacement at one end and like its slope at the other). Transmission coefficient 1 + Γ at a junction of two lines. Energy check: reflected power Γ²v₊²/Z₀, delivered (1 − Γ²)v₊²/Z₀. At a source of EMF ℰ and resistance R_s: the launched wave is ℰZ₀/(R_s + Z₀), and the source reflects with Γ_s = (R_s − Z₀)/(R_s + Z₀). Reactive terminations (a capacitor, an inductor) produce reflections that change shape — one slide, with the RC time constant Z₀C (unit 05) as the example; no general theory.

**The bounce diagram (the checkpoint's third part).** z against t, each wave a diagonal line, each reflection a corner, with the voltage at any point the sum of the waves that have passed it. **Checkpoint example**: 12 V source, R_s = 10 Ω, through 10 m of the 50 Ω polyethylene cable (one-way delay T = 50.03 ns), to a 150 Ω load. Launched 10 V; Γ_L = +0.5, Γ_s = −2/3. Load voltage after t = T, 3T, 5T, 7T, 9T, …: 15, 10, 11.667, 11.111, 11.296, 11.235, 11.255, … V, converging geometrically (ratio Γ_LΓ_s = −1/3) on 11.25 V = 12 × 150/160 — the lumped answer, recovered as the sum of a geometric series (prove the limit in general: v(∞) = ℰR_L/(R_s + R_L)). Draw it; and a plot of the load voltage against t. The source-end voltage: 10 V until 2T, then 11.667, 11.111, …
- **Unit 01, revisited**: unit 01's 12 V battery, 6 Ω load and vacuum coax (Z₀ = 83.1 Ω): Γ_L = −0.865; with an ideal battery (Γ_s = −1) the load voltage converges on 12 V with ratio 0.865 per round trip — unit 01's static fields are the end state of a bounce process, and its "energy flows from battery to resistor in the insulation" is the limit of travelling waves. One slide.

**The running example: the battery, the bulb and the 300 000 km wires.** A 12 V battery with a switch, and a bulb (12 V, 6 W, so 24 Ω — a fixed resistance, assumed; a cold filament is not ohmic: say so) a metre apart. From each, wires run 300 000 km out and back, so the circuit is two long rectangles; near the battery–bulb gap, the battery's wire and the bulb's wire run parallel, 1 m apart, forming two parallel-wire lines (one above the gap, one below), each shorted at its far end where the rectangle closes. Wire radius 1 mm (assumed): Z₀ = (η₀/π) arccosh(500) = 828.4 Ω per line.
- *The first nanoseconds*: within about (1 m)/c = 3.3 ns of the switch closing, the near-end loop — battery, upper line's port, bulb, lower line's port — carries current I₀ = ℰ/(2Z₀ + R_bulb) = 7.14 mA, and the bulb takes 1.22 mW (2 × 10⁻⁴ of its 6 W): it is not visibly lit, and the slide says so plainly — the point is that current flows and energy arrives in nanoseconds, not that it glows. The energy crosses the 1 m gap as a Poynting flux from the battery's wire to the bulb's: the thread's station "energy at the field's speed".
- *The model's limits* (`box warn`): a TEM line needs its conductor spacing small against the distances over which the fields change; for the first few nanoseconds the 1 m gap is not small against c × (rise time), and radiation, the lumped "near-end loop" and the switching edge all matter. The model gives the right order of magnitude and the right picture, not a precise figure, and that is all that is claimed.
- *After 2 s*: the waves return from the shorted far ends (round trip 600 000 km, 2.00 s) inverted, and the current steps up at each return: 21.2 mA after one round trip, 131 mA after 10, 473 mA after 100, approaching ℰ/R = 0.5 A over a few hundred seconds (the near-end reflection coefficient is −0.971, so each round trip closes only 2.9% of the gap). Cross-check with a lumped L/R: the loop's inductance (2 × 300 000 km × L′ — two rectangles, each a 300 000 km two-wire line — corrected after the writer checked) is about 1.66 kH, L/R ≈ 69 s — the same slow approach, as it must be once many round trips have averaged out.

**The long power line (the real system, part 1).** At 50 Hz a wavelength is c/f ≈ 6000 km, and 400 km is 1/15 of it: βl = 0.419 rad = 24.0°. Sinusoidal steady state on a lossless line in one formula (V(z) as forward plus backward phasors; the convention e^{+iωt} with forward waves e^{i(ωt − βz)}, and a slide translating MR 24's e^{i(kx − ωt)}: they differ by complex conjugation, and the course's choice is MR 06's). One consequence, computed: an unloaded (open) 400 km lossless line's far-end voltage exceeds its sending-end voltage by the factor 1/cos βl = 1.095 — a 9.5% rise caused by the line's own capacitance drawing charging current through its inductance. (The name *Ferranti effect* only if a standard power-systems text confirms it — Bergen & Vittal, or Kundur.) Unit 07 asked "whether a line is a lumped resistance at all"; the answer is "nearly, but not quite", and unit 13 uses the line's series reactance; unit 14 its charging current.

**Loss, dispersion and Heaviside's condition.** With R′ and G′: in phasors, the propagation constant γ = √((R′ + iωL′)(G′ + iωC′)) = α + iβ and Z₀ = √((R′ + iωL′)/(G′ + iωC′)), both functions of ω: a pulse attenuates (α) and spreads (β not proportional to ω — dispersion, in MR 20's sense of phase and group velocity; cite). **Theorem (Heaviside's distortionless condition)**: if R′/L′ = G′/C′ then α is independent of ω and β = ω√(L′C′) exactly, so every frequency travels at the same speed and is attenuated equally: a pulse keeps its shape, shrinking. Proof by factoring. Real telephone pairs have R′/L′ ≫ G′/C′; raising L′ (loading coils, cited history) moves them toward the condition. A problem with representative values (assumed) shows the distortion with and without loading.

**Lightning (the real system, part 2) — how a lightning stroke carries its energy.** At the learner's request, and as the energy-flow thread's sharpest case:
- *Before the stroke*: a stepped leader has lowered negative charge from the cloud along a channel kilometres long (Rakov & Uman, confirmed). The energy is in the electric field around that charged channel and between cloud and ground — in the air, as in unit 01's insulation.
- *The model (stated, not sourced)*: treat the leader channel as a vertical line of length H carrying a uniform charge Q over a "charge sheath" of radius r, above a conducting ground (image charge, MR 23). Its potential is about λ/(2πε₀) ln(2H/r) with λ = Q/H, and its electrostatic energy about ½QV. For Q = 5 C (order of magnitude to be confirmed against Rakov & Uman's tabulated charge per stroke; otherwise labelled assumed), H = 5 km, r = 1–10 m: V ≈ 124–166 MV and W ≈ 310–414 MJ — a few hundred megajoules, insensitive to the uncertain sheath radius because it enters through a logarithm. Say clearly that this is an order-of-magnitude model of one stroke, not a measured value, and that published estimates of the energy per flash disagree widely.
- *The return stroke*: when the leader touches ground (or meets an upward connecting leader), the channel's bottom is suddenly at ground potential and a discharge wave runs up the channel at a fraction of c (confirmed figure) — a wave on a lossy line, with the charged channel discharging as unit 08's charged line does (a problem: a line charged to V₀, shorted at one end, discharges as a wave of current V₀/Z₀ travelling away from the short — compute from the telegrapher's equations; the current in the real channel is not V₀/Z₀ of any simple line, and the slide does not claim it is).
- *Where the energy goes*: the field's energy flows into the channel through its surface as a Poynting flux (unit 01's resistor, but the resistor is a column of ionised air) and leaves as heat, light, the shock wave heard as thunder, and an electromagnetic pulse (branch 16's radiation, named). No percentages are quoted unless a confirmed source gives them.
- *What carries it*: not the electrons. The charge lowered moves a few kilometres at most, and the drift of the channel's electrons carries a negligible share of the energy, exactly as in unit 01; what carries it is the field, and the stroke is the moment the field's energy finds a path to be dissipated.
Figures: the channel and its field before the stroke (E lines from the channel to the ground plane, with the image); the return-stroke wave front moving up with the current behind it; S arrows entering the channel behind the front.
