## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 00 found that a conductor responds to 50 Hz exactly as to DC — *locally*, through σ. This branch shows the other half: globally, a changing current induces fields in the conductor itself that push the current outward, so at high enough frequency or large enough size the middle of a conductor carries almost nothing. Units 01 and 02 named it twice ("Unit 10 meets a different way a lead's cross-section stops being uniform"; "at high frequency the current crowds into a layer near the conductor's surface"), and unit 07 stated the skin depth without proof as its laminations' validity condition. This unit derives it — a diffusion equation from Maxwell plus Ohm — solves it for a half-space and for a round wire, and turns the two numbers 9.2 mm and 0.21 mm into the shape of real hardware.

It must not become: a course on eddy-current machines or induction heating (one sentence each at most); the proximity effect beyond its statement (cited); transmission-line attenuation (unit 08 takes R′ as given; one sentence that R′ rises as √f once δ ≪ a, and that this is the R′ unit 08 used); magnetic materials beyond unit 07's μ_r (steel's δ is computed with unit 07's assumed μ_r = 1000 and the warning that μ_r of steel is not a constant).

## Physics and mathematics, in order

**The diffusion equation.** Inside a good conductor, Ohm's law J = σE and Maxwell. The displacement current is negligible: |ε₀∂E/∂t|/|J| = ωε₀/σ = 4.7 × 10⁻¹⁷ for copper at 50 Hz (and still 2 × 10⁻⁹ at 2.4 GHz — compute) — the same comparison unit 00 made for charge relaxation. Then ∇ × B = μ₀σE, ∇ × E = −∂B/∂t, ∇·B = 0 give ∂B/∂t = (μσ)⁻¹∇²B (and the same for J and E): MR 18's parabolic type with diffusivity 1/μσ — magnetic field soaks into a conductor the way heat does (cite MR 18's heat-equation slide, and its infinite-propagation-speed remark as the sign that the approximation has dropped retardation, as unit 02's (H3) did). Units: (H/m · S/m)⁻¹ = m²/s. For copper, 1/μ₀σ = 1.34 × 10⁻² m²/s.

**Into a half-space.** A field B₀e^{iωt} parallel to the flat surface of a conductor filling z > 0 (course convention e^{+iωt}): B(z) = B₀ e^{−(1+i)z/δ}, δ = √(2/ωμσ) — the field and the induced current decay as e^{−z/δ} while their phase lags by z/δ radians: a heavily damped wave going in. The checkpoint's numbers: **copper, δ = 9.23 mm at 50 Hz and 0.206 mm at 100 kHz** (2.06 mm at 1 kHz, 65 μm at 1 MHz, 1.3 μm at 2.4 GHz); aluminium 11.6 mm at 50 Hz; steel (unit 07's assumed μ_r = 1000, ρ = 0.5 μΩ·m) 1.59 mm at 50 Hz. Figures: |J| and its phase against depth; the current as a rotating helix of phasors.
- **The surface layer theorem**: the total current per unit width and the power dissipated per unit area are exactly those of a uniform current in a layer of thickness δ: surface resistance R_s = 1/(σδ) per square (prove by integrating |J|² and J). This is the "skin". Energy check: the dissipated power equals the time-averaged Poynting flux into the surface — unit 01's picture again, now with the field penetrating only δ.

**A round wire.** A long wire of radius a carrying total current I e^{iωt}: J_z(r) satisfies Bessel's equation of order 0 with k² = −iωμσ, so J(r) = J(a) J₀(kr)/J₀(ka), k = (1 − i)/δ — a Bessel function of complex argument (cite MR for J₀ and its series; the complex argument changes nothing in the series). The internal impedance per metre Z_i = (kρ/2πa) J₀(ka)/J₁(ka) (derive from E_z at the surface and Ampère's law for H at the surface). Limits:
- *Low frequency* (a ≪ δ): Z_i → ρ/πa² + iωμ₀/8π — the DC resistance and unit 02's internal inductance μ₀/8π per metre (land unit 02's "at high frequency the current is not uniform (unit 10) and this part shrinks").
- *High frequency* (a ≫ δ): with the large-argument asymptotics (cited), R_ac/R_dc → a/2δ + 1/4: the wire behaves as a tube of thickness δ.
- Numbers (computed with the exact Bessel formula, the solution's working not depending on the tool): copper at 50 Hz, R_ac/R_dc = 1.0000 for a 1 mm radius, 1.0018 for 5 mm, 1.028 for 10 mm, 1.615 for 25 mm, 2.977 for 50 mm (the asymptotic formula gives 1.605 and 2.960 for the last two). A 1 mm-radius copper wire at 100 kHz: 2.69; at 1 MHz: 7.92 (asymptotic 2.67 and 7.92).
- Figure: |J(r)|/|J(a)| across a 50 mm-radius copper bar at 50 Hz, and across a 1 mm wire at 1 MHz.

**The two numbers, read as hardware (the checkpoint's third part).**
- *Why a substation busbar can be a tube.* At 50 Hz in copper δ = 9.2 mm, so a bar 100 mm across carries little current in its middle: R_ac = 6.37 μΩ/m against R_dc = 2.14 μΩ/m. A tube of the same outside diameter with a 10 mm wall has R_ac = 6.56 μΩ/m (3% more) with 36% of the copper; with a 14.5 mm wall it reaches its minimum, 5.91 μΩ/m — *less* than the solid bar, with half the copper (computed with Bessel functions J and Y for the annulus — derive the formula). The optimum wall, 1.57δ, is πδ/2: prove the plane-slab version (a slab fed from one side has least resistance at thickness πδ/2) as a problem. And a tube is stiffer for its weight and cooled from inside and out (qualitative, no figures unless cited).
- *Why a wireless charger's coil is wound from many insulated strands.* At unit 15's frequencies (150 kHz assumed here) δ = 0.168 mm in copper; a solid 2 mm conductor would carry its current in a thin skin; strands 0.1 mm across each have R_ac/R_dc = 1.0002 — the current fills them. Twisting the strands so each visits every position (transposition) defeats the proximity effect, the crowding caused by the neighbours' fields (cited, Ramo et al.). This is *Litz wire* (name cited); unit 15 uses it.
- *Overhead conductors* (qualitative unless EN 50182 is confirmed): aluminium strands around a steel core; at 50 Hz aluminium's δ is 11.6 mm, comparable to such a conductor's radius, so the outer aluminium carries the current and the core is chosen for strength — the skin effect does not forbid putting steel in the middle, and a little helps.

**Unit 07's laminations, revisited.** Unit 07's classical eddy loss σω²B̂²d²/24 assumed the lamination thin against δ. The exact slab solution (field diffusing in from both faces) multiplies it by F(x) = (3/x)(sinh x − sin x)/(cosh x − cos x), x = d/δ (derive). With unit 07's steel (δ = 1.59 mm): F = 1.0000 for d = 0.3 mm, 0.9998 for 1 mm, 0.981 for 3 mm, and 0.479 for a 10 mm slab — where the classical formula would overstate the loss twofold. Unit 07's numbers stand; the thin-lamination condition is now proved, not stated.

## Worked examples (three to five, in `box ex`)
1. The diffusion equation from Maxwell and Ohm, with the displacement-current estimate (4.7 × 10⁻¹⁷).
2. The half-space solution and δ: 9.23 mm and 0.206 mm for copper; the surface-layer theorem.
3. The round wire's low-frequency limit: ρ/πa² and μ₀/8π, matching units 00 and 02.
4. Bar against tube at 50 Hz: 6.37 against 6.56 and 5.91 μΩ/m, and the copper saved.
