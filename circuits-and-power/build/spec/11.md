## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 06 found that a single-phase load's instantaneous power pulses at 100 Hz, swinging between −8.57 kW and +48.57 kW for the workshop, and promised "Unit 11 gives the workshop three phases, and shows that a balanced three-phase load's total p(t) does not pulse at all". Unit 07 modelled the street transformer as one single-phase transformer and promised three things: unit 11 gives it its three phases (line currents 1/√3 of the single-phase model's); its turns ratio differs from its voltage ratio by √3 under star and delta; and when the workshop loads one phase winding only, its copper loss is larger. Unit 04 promised "Unit 11 uses the [star–delta] transformation with complex weights for star- and delta-connected three-phase loads". This unit keeps all of these promises. It does so with two pieces of mathematics that make three-phase more than bookkeeping. First, an identity: Σ cos²(ωt − 2πk/3) = 3/2. Second, a change of basis: Fortescue's symmetrical components are the discrete Fourier transform on ℤ/3, and that transform diagonalises every rotationally symmetric impedance matrix.

It must not become any of the following:
- **Machines.** The rotating field is derived as a field, and turning it into torque is branch 12's job, named in one sentence.
- **Power flow.** Unit 13 does that. Here the network is one source, one cable and one customer.
- **Fault analysis with sequence networks** beyond the single idea that only zero sequence returns through the neutral. Sequence networks get one slide, as the reason the DFT pays off. Fault calculations are named as a use, without computing a fault.
- **Harmonics.** Triplen harmonics in the neutral get one sentence at most, and are unit 14's territory.
- **The per-unit system.** Unit 13 introduces it where it earns its keep.

## Physics and mathematics, in order

**Balanced three-phase sets.** Three sources of equal RMS magnitude, 120° apart, in sequence a, b, c with b lagging a: V_a = V, V_b = α²V, V_c = αV, where α = e^{i2π/3}. That is the course convention (AUTHORING.md), and α avoids clashing with phase a. Facts proved on the slide: 1 + α + α² = 0, and α³ = 1. Star (Y) connection gives line-to-neutral (phase) voltages. The line-to-line voltages are V_ab = V_a − V_b = √3 V_a e^{iπ/6}, which gives the √3 and the 30° (derive from the phasor triangle). In numbers, 230 V phase and 400 V line; IEC 60038's pair is 230/400, and √3 × 230 = 398.4, so say that the nominal values are rounded. Delta (Δ) connection: phase currents and line currents differ by √3 and 30° in the same way.

**Constant power (the checkpoint's first part).** For a balanced load with phase voltage amplitude √2V, phase current √2I and angle φ, the total instantaneous power is p(t) = Σ_k 2VI cos(ωt − 2πk/3) cos(ωt − 2πk/3 − φ) = 3VI cos φ, constant. Proof: the product-to-sum formula leaves 3VI cos φ plus Σ_k VI cos(2ωt − 4πk/3 − φ), and that sum is Re of e^{i(2ωt−φ)}(1 + α⁻² + α⁻⁴) = 0. The identity Σ cos²(ωt − 2πk/3) = 3/2 is the special case φ = 0. Consequence: a balanced three-phase motor sees no 100 Hz pulsating torque, and a generator sees none. Energy check: the stored energy in the load's reactances still sloshes, so Q = 3VI sin φ is still defined and still costs current. It sloshes *between the phases*, not back to the source in total. Land unit 06 here: the workshop's −8.57 to +48.57 kW swing becomes a flat 20 kW. Say plainly that Q survives as a per-phase quantity although no net power returns.

**Fewer conductors: the 75% figure (the checkpoint's second part).** Deliver power P, with equal total I²R loss, over the same distance, with the same voltage between conductors.
- *Single-phase, two wires at voltage V*: I = P/V, loss 2I²ρl/A₁.
- *Three-phase, three wires with line voltage V*: I = P/(√3V), loss 3I²ρl/A₃ = (P/V)²ρl/A₃.
- Equal loss gives A₃ = A₁/2, so the conductor volume is 3A₁/2 against 2A₁, which is 75%.
- The neutral of a four-wire system carries nothing when balanced and is usually smaller. Do not claim a size ratio unless it is sourced; say only that it carries no current when the load is balanced.

**Why three, and not two or six (a problem, partly cited).** For n phases in star with phase voltage V_p, at fixed P and fixed total loss, the conductor volume is proportional to 1/V_p² (derive: each of the n wires carries P/(nV_p)). Hold fixed instead the *largest voltage between any two conductors*, V_max = 2V_p sin(π⌊n/2⌋/n), which is what the insulation must withstand. Then the copper is proportional to 4 sin²(π⌊n/2⌋/n):
- equal to 4 for every even n (single-phase, two-phase as four wires, six-phase), giving 100% of single-phase;
- 3 for n = 3 (75%), 3.618 for n = 5 (90.5%), 3.80 for n = 7 (95.05%).

So three phases minimise the copper among n-phase star systems under this constraint (prove: for odd n the factor is 4cos²(π/2n), increasing in n). Two-phase in quadrature also gives constant power and a rotating field, as a problem. The parts that are not copper (more phases mean more windings, switches and transformers) are stated qualitatively, or cited to Bergen & Vittal if it says so (confirm). No historical claim about who chose three, or why, without a source.

**The rotating field.** Three identical coils with axes at 0°, 120° and 240° in a plane, each making a field along its axis proportional to its current (MR 23: a coil's field on its axis is linear in its current). Fed with currents √2I cos(ωt − 2πk/3), the total field, as a complex number in the plane, is B(t) = Σ_k B̂ cos(ωt − 2πk/3) e^{i2πk/3} = (3/2) B̂ e^{iωt}. Proof in one line: expand the cosine into exponentials, and the e^{−iωt} terms sum to zero. A field of constant magnitude rotates at ω. Swapping two phases reverses it, which is why swapping two wires reverses a motor (derived). A figure: the three pulsating vectors and their rotating sum at four instants. Name branch 12 in one sentence, as the unit that turns this into torque.

**Star–delta with complex weights (land unit 04).** Unit 04's star–delta transformation holds verbatim with impedances. For a balanced load, Z_Δ = 3Z_Y (derive from it, and check by power: same S at the same line voltage). The workshop's balanced motor load as a star is Z_Y = 3.888 + 3.967i Ω per phase, or 11.66 + 11.90i Ω per phase as a delta. The per-phase equivalent: a balanced network is solved as one phase, from line to neutral, and multiplied by 3. This is the method unit 13 uses throughout.

**The street transformer, three-phase (land unit 07).** 400 kVA, 20 kV/400 V, delta on the high-voltage side and star with earthed neutral on the low-voltage side (an assumption about this example; see Sources).
- Line currents: 11.55 A at 20 kV and 577.4 A at 400 V. These are unit 07's 20 A and 1000 A divided by √3. Its losses and percentages are unchanged.
- The delta winding carries 11.55/√3 = 6.67 A at 20 kV; the star winding carries 577.4 A at 230.9 V.
- The turns ratio per limb is 20 000/230.9 = 86.6 = 50√3, not the voltage ratio of 50 (land unit 07's √3).
- The line-to-line voltages on the two sides differ in phase by 30° (derive from the connection).
- Why delta on the high-voltage side helps: zero-sequence currents (below) circulate inside the delta and do not appear in the 20 kV lines. Prove this from the definition of zero sequence (equal currents in all three windings sum to zero line current at each delta corner).

**Unbalance: symmetrical components as the DFT on ℤ/3.** Three phasors (I_a, I_b, I_c) are a vector in ℂ³.
- The DFT on ℤ/3 with α as the root of unity gives:
  - I₀ = (I_a + I_b + I_c)/3 (zero sequence);
  - I₁ = (I_a + αI_b + α²I_c)/3 (positive sequence);
  - I₂ = (I_a + α²I_b + αI_c)/3 (negative sequence).
- The inverse: I_a = I₀ + I₁ + I₂, I_b = I₀ + α²I₁ + αI₂, I_c = I₀ + αI₁ + α²I₂. The decomposition is unique: the transform matrix is invertible, with F⁻¹ = F*/3 up to the normalisation chosen (prove from 1 + α + α² = 0).
- Positive sequence is a balanced a-b-c set, negative sequence a balanced a-c-b set, zero sequence three equal phasors. Derive these from the inverse.
- **Theorem: only zero sequence returns through the neutral.** I_n = I_a + I_b + I_c = 3I₀. Corollary: a load connected only between lines draws no neutral current, whatever its imbalance.
- **Theorem: a rotationally symmetric impedance matrix is diagonalised by the DFT.** Take a circulant Z, such as three identical lines with equal mutual impedances, or a balanced load. Its eigenvectors are the columns of F, and its eigenvalues are the sequence impedances Z₀, Z₁ and Z₂. Proof: a circulant matrix is a polynomial in the cyclic shift, and the shift's eigenvectors are the Fourier vectors. Consequence: an unbalanced *source or load* on a symmetric network splits into three *uncoupled* single-phase problems, the sequence networks. One slide, with the cable as the example: Z₁ = Z₂ = Z_s − Z_m and Z₀ = Z_s + 2Z_m for self impedance Z_s and mutual impedance Z_m (derive). Name fault analysis as the main use, in one sentence, without doing it.
- Negative sequence makes a field rotating backwards (from the rotating-field formula with α and α² swapped), which is why unbalanced supply heats motors. State this qualitatively, and say branch 12 has the machine.

**The running example: the workshop, rebuilt on three phases.** The workshop of unit 06 now has a three-phase supply from the street transformer: 230/400 V, four wires, through the same 35 mm² copper cable of 50 m, now with four cores. Its loads (all assumed, said on the overview):
- *Motors*: unit 06's 20 kW at power factor 0.7 lagging, now as balanced three-phase motors. Line current 41.41 A, against 124.2 A single-phase. Constant p(t) = 20 kW. Cable loss 3 × 41.41² × 0.024 = 123.5 W, against 740.7 W single-phase: a sixth.
- *A kiln* (the "heavy single-phase load"): 9.2 kW resistive, between phase a and neutral, 40.0 A.
- *Lighting and sockets*: 2.3 kW resistive, between phase b and neutral, 10.0 A.

Computed line currents: I_a = 75.06 A ∠−23.20°, I_b = 48.93 A ∠−157.18°, I_c = 41.41 A ∠74.43°. The neutral current is 36.06 A ∠−13.90° both ways:
- *directly*, as the sum of the line currents, which is also |40 + 10α²| = √(40² + 10² − 40·10) = √1300 = 36.06 A;
- *by symmetrical components*: I₀ = 12.02 A ∠−13.90°, so 3I₀ = 36.06 A; I₁ = 54.39 A ∠−32.93°; I₂ = 12.02 A ∠+13.90°.

The motors contribute only positive sequence (prove), which is why the neutral sees only the single-phase loads. The neutral's own loss in its 50 m of 35 mm² is 31.2 W.

The street transformer's copper loss (land unit 07's P10):
- Unit 07's single-phase workshop, 28.57 kVA, in the single-phase model: (28.57/400)² × 3250 = 16.6 W.
- The same 28.57 kVA as one single-phase load on the real three-phase transformer loads one phase winding of rating 133.3 kVA. That winding loses (28.57/133.3)² × 3250/3 = 49.7 W, three times as much.
- Balanced over three phases, the loss is back to 16.6 W.
- The running example's loads give 31.4 W.

**The broken neutral (the real system's danger, derived).** If the neutral conductor breaks between the transformer and a group of single-phase loads, the loads' star point is no longer held at earth. It floats to V_N = Σ V_k Y_k / Σ Y_k (Millman; derive from KCL at the star point, which is unit 03's current law). Example (assumed loads on three phases of a feeder): 460 W, 2300 W and 1150 W resistive at 230 V, on a, b and c. Intact, the neutral carries 7.0 A. Broken: V_N = 94.7 V ∠−141.8°, and the loads see 310.0 V, 146.3 V and 260.9 V. The lightly loaded phase gets 35% over-voltage and 1.82 times its rated power (835.6 W in a 460 W load), while the heavily loaded one browns out. That is the danger: appliances on the lightly loaded phase are over-stressed. Say no more than that, and say nothing about fire statistics.

**What else to say about the real system.** The four wires of a European low-voltage feeder: three lines and a neutral. Single-phase customers are spread across the phases so that the feeder is nearly balanced, which is why the neutral is mostly idle and why losing it matters only when the loads are unequal. Keep this qualitative, with no claims about distribution practice beyond that unless sourced.

**Energy flow (unit 01's thread, one slide, optional).** In a balanced three-phase cable the total power is constant, so the Poynting flux integrated over the cable's cross-section is steady. Energy flows into the load without the 100 Hz backward flux that unit 06 found for a single-phase reactive load. One sentence and a figure at most; the thread's next station is unit 15.
