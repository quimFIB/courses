## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 07 showed that 100 MW cannot be sent through a 10 Ω line at 20 kV, and promised: "Unit 13 makes this precise for AC, where the line's reactance sets the limit", and "Unit 13 derives it, P_max = V₁V₂/X, and its stability". Unit 04 promised that its Laplacian is "unit 13's bus admittance matrix with complex weights, and Kron reduction its network equivalent". Unit 06 promised that "unit 13 shows that on a network reactive power and voltage magnitude go together", and that "Unit 13's power-flow equations are the conservation of complex power at every bus of a grid". Unit 07 promised that unit 13 uses transformer reactance in fault and stability calculations. This unit keeps all of these, in two halves:
- **statics**: two buses and a reactance, then many buses and Newton–Raphson;
- **dynamics**: the swing equation as MR 02's pendulum with a constant torque, the equal-area criterion as energy conservation on its phase portrait, and frequency as the grid's shared state.

It must not become any of the following:
- **Protection engineering.** Clearing times are numbers in problems; relays are named only.
- **Voltage stability** beyond one slide. The nose curve of the two-bus system is the one figure, and its theory is named.
- **Small-signal modal analysis** beyond the linearised swing equation's frequency.
- **Economic dispatch or markets.**
- **A reconstruction of any real event.** The running example is a textbook two-area system, said so on the overview and in the closing. The Iberian blackout of 28 April 2025 is not mentioned unless the writer can confirm and cite an official *final* report (AUTHORING.md's rule). Even then it gets at most one sentence pointing to that report, not modelled.

## Physics and mathematics, in order

**Per unit, in one slide.** Choose a base power (100 MVA) and base voltages per voltage level. Impedances become dimensionless, and a transformer's ratio disappears from the per-unit diagram. Prove the last point for unit 07's ideal transformer, where the base impedances on its two sides differ by n². The street transformer's 4% *is* a per-unit impedance (0.04 pu on its own base): land unit 07's "uk". Fitzgerald's per-unit section (unit 07 deferred it to unit 11; unit 11 deferred it here) is the reading.

**Two buses and a reactance.** Two buses with phasor voltages V₁∠δ₁ and V₂∠δ₂, joined by a lossless series reactance X, per phase. The power sent from bus 1, from S = VI*, is:
- P = V₁V₂ sin δ/X with δ = δ₁ − δ₂;
- Q₁ = (V₁² − V₁V₂ cos δ)/X;
- and at the receiving end Q₂ = (V₁V₂ cos δ − V₂²)/X.

Derive both from unit 06's S, per phase, and multiply by 3 with phase voltages, or equivalently use line-to-line voltages directly (prove the equivalence with unit 11's √3). Units check: V²/Ω = W. Energy check: P is the same at both ends, since a reactance absorbs no real power, while Q₁ − Q₂ = I²X is the reactance's own Q (prove).
- *Real power flows down phase differences*: for small δ, P ≈ V₁V₂δ/X.
- *Reactive power flows down voltage differences*: at δ ≈ 0, Q₁ ≈ V₁(V₁ − V₂)/X.
- Both follow by linearising, and that is the decoupling every power-flow method exploits. Land unit 06's promise that Q and voltage magnitude go together.

**The static limit, and unit 07's 20 kV line.** P is greatest at δ = 90°, where P_max = V₁V₂/X. Close unit 07's example by treating its 10 Ω as a reactance (assumed; say that unit 07's line was resistive):
- at 20 kV line-to-line, P_max = (20 kV)²/10 Ω = 40 MW, so 100 MW is impossible;
- at 400 kV it is 16 GW.

Place it beside unit 04's resistive limit V²/4R = 10 MW at 20 kV through 10 Ω. A resistive line limits the transfer by heating and voltage drop, a reactive one by phase. Both say "not at 20 kV", now for a reason that does not depend on the line's resistance at all.

**Lines as reactances, three-phase (derived, not imported from unit 08).** Use unit 07's geometry: a 400 kV line with bundles of equivalent radius 0.2097 m and 10 m spacing. With balanced currents, the per-phase series inductance is (μ₀/2π) ln(D/r_eq) = 0.773 mH/km. Derive it from MR 23's field of a long wire, as the flux linking one conductor per unit length when the three currents sum to zero, in the equilateral case. It is half the single-phase loop's. Over 400 km at 50 Hz, X = 97.1 Ω, so P_max = 400²/97.1 = 1647 MW. A remark for learners who took unit 08: its loop L′ = 1.55 mH/km is twice this, and its "Unit 13 keeps its series reactance" is this slide. The remark is not used.

**The nose curve (one slide).** A lossless line feeding a load at power factor 1 from a stiff source. The receiving voltage against P has two branches that meet at a maximum power (derive for this case). The upper branch is the operating one. Voltage collapse is the nose. Name voltage stability as its subject, cite Kundur's chapter on voltage stability (confirm), and stop.

**Many buses: the admittance matrix is unit 04's Laplacian.** Unit 04's AGAᵀ with complex weights: Y_bus = A diag(y_e) Aᵀ, with y_e = 1/(iX_e) for lossless lines.
- It is symmetric. For lossless lines Y_bus = −iB with B a real weighted Laplacian, which has the zero row sums of a Laplacian once shunt elements are absent (prove).
- Kron reduction (unit 04) eliminates buses that have no injection, to give an equivalent between the generator buses. Land unit 04's "Unit 13 reduces a power grid's buses this way".
- **The power-flow equations**: S_k = V_k Σ_j Y_kj* V_j* at every bus. This is conservation of complex power at each node (unit 06's Tellegen, bus by bus); land unit 06's promise.
- Bus types: the slack bus fixes V and δ; PV buses (generators) fix P and |V|; PQ buses (loads) fix P and Q. Count the equations against the unknowns and show they match.
- **The DC power flow**: with flat voltages, small angles and lossless lines, P = Bθ. This is *exactly* unit 04's Kirchhoff problem, with angles as potentials and susceptances as conductances. It is linear, and its solution is unique up to the slack (unit 04's theorem on the Laplacian's kernel). One slide, and the first Newton step below reproduces it.

**Newton–Raphson.** Linearise the mismatch F(x) = 0 and iterate x ← x − J⁻¹F. The convergence theorem is stated and cited: local and quadratic. Why the flat start (all |V| = 1, all δ = 0) is a good initial guess: the grid is operated near it.

**The three-bus example** (per unit on 100 MVA; assumed):
- *Lines*: X₁₂ = 0.1, X₁₃ = 0.2, X₂₃ = 0.25.
- *Buses*: bus 1 is the slack, 1.0∠0. Bus 2 is a PV bus with P = 1.0 and |V| = 1.0. Bus 3 is a PQ bus carrying a load of 1.5 + 0.5i.
- *Y_bus* is diagonal −15i, −14i, −9i, with off-diagonal entries 10i, 5i and 4i.
- *Flat-start mismatch*: (−1.0, 1.5, 0.5).
- *Iterates* (δ₂, δ₃ in degrees, then |V₃|):
  - after one step: 1.5626°, −8.8548°, 0.9444;
  - after two: 1.5891°, −9.6677°, 0.9239;
  - after three: 1.5894°, −9.6941°, 0.92331.
- *Maximum mismatch*: 1.5 → 0.146 → 4.2 × 10⁻³ → 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ → 4 × 10⁻¹²; quadratic, as the theorem says.
- *Solution*: slack S₁ = 0.500 + 0.453i, and generator 2's Q = 0.382.
- The first iterate's angles equal the DC power flow's (1.5626°, −8.8548°). Prove that this holds at a flat start for lossless lines: the P–θ block of the Jacobian is B.
- The problem sheet asks for the first iteration by hand and the second as an optional numerical check. The worked solution does not depend on a tool: give the Jacobian entries.

**Dynamics: the swing equation.** A synchronous machine's rotor (or a whole area's rotating mass) obeys Newton for rotation, Jω̇_m = T_m − T_e. Multiply by ω_m to get power. Write it with the inertia constant H, the stored kinetic energy at synchronous speed divided by the rating, in seconds:

(2H/ω_s) δ̈ = P_m − P_e  (per unit),

where δ is the rotor angle in electrical radians relative to a synchronously rotating reference (derive it, with ω_s = 2π·50). With P_e = P_max sin δ this is **MR 02's pendulum with a constant torque**: "The pendulum with a constant torque" and "The torque, drawn: where sin θ = T" are MR 02's slides; cite them.
- Equilibria at sin δ = P_m/P_max: a centre at δ₀ and a saddle at π − δ₀ (MR 02's classification).
- The phase portrait is the tilted-washboard pendulum.
- The first integral is (H/ω_s)δ̇² − P_mδ − P_max cos δ = const, energy conservation (prove by multiplying by δ̇).

**The equal-area criterion (the checkpoint).** A fault drops P_e to zero, the rotor accelerates, and the fault is cleared at δ_c, restoring P_max sin δ. The machine survives if the decelerating area above P_m, up to the saddle δ_max = π − δ₀, is at least the accelerating area. Prove that this *is* the first integral: the separatrix through the saddle.
- With fault P_e = 0: cos δ_c = (P_m/P_max)(π − 2δ₀) + cos(π − δ₀).
- **For δ₀ = 30° (P_m/P_max = 1/2): δ_c = 79.56°.** Check by the areas.
- The critical *time* follows from δ̈ = ω_s P_m/(2H) during the fault: t_c = √(4H(δ_c − δ₀)/(ω_s P_m)). That is 0.332 s for a single machine with H = 5 s at P_m = 0.5 pu (assumed).

**The two-area running example: the Iberian peninsula and the rest of Europe.** Model Continental Europe as an infinite bus. Model Iberia as one machine of H = 4 s on 50 GW (assumed), exporting 2000 MW over an equivalent tie of 40 Ω per phase at 400 kV (assumed), so P_max = 4000 MW and δ₀ = 30°. Each end supplies 535.9 Mvar to the tie at this operating point (compute and explain: the tie's reactive loss I²X is shared between the ends because V₁ = V₂).
- *A fault on the tie* that cuts the transfer to zero: δ̈ = ω_s × 2000/(2 × 4 × 50 000) = 1.571 rad/s². δ_c = 79.56° is reached after **t_c = 1.05 s**. Explain why a large area tolerates a long fault: its inertia is large against the power it was exporting.
- *Small oscillations*: linearised about δ₀, the inter-area mode has frequency √(ω_s P_max cos δ₀/(2HS)) = 0.263 Hz, a period of 3.8 s. Compute it, and say only that oscillations at frequencies like this between areas are observed in large grids, cited to Kundur or Machowski if a statement there is confirmed. No claim about measured Iberian oscillations.
- *Why the interconnection's capacity matters*: the 2800 MW exchange capacity (REE) against a European system of hundreds of gigawatts. Say only what the model shows: the weaker the tie (larger X), the smaller P_max, and the closer a given export sits to the stability limit.

**Frequency as the grid's shared state (the real system).** With every machine synchronised, the whole of Continental Europe behaves, for slow events, like one machine. Its aggregate swing equation is (2HS/f₀) dΔf/dt = −ΔP + (reserves) − (load damping)·Δf (derive from the swing equation summed over machines).
- **The reference incident.** Losing 3000 MW (the regulation's reference incident for CE) with S = 300 GW and H = 5 s (assumed; stored energy 1500 GJ) gives an initial rate of change of f₀ΔP/(2HS) = 0.050 Hz/s.
- **The ramp model, closed form (not a bound: the verifier found that Art. 154(7) only requires at least 50% of the reserve by 15 s and 100% by 30 s, rising at least linearly from 15 to 30 s, so the regulation's minimum delivery gives 0.94 Hz in this model; the deck says so).** Suppose the frequency containment reserve ramps linearly to its full 3000 MW over the regulation's 30 s and load damping is neglected. Then the frequency falls to its lowest point at t = 30 s, with Δf_min = f₀ΔP T/(4HS) = **0.75 Hz**. Derive this by integrating. Set it beside the regulation's 800 mHz maximum instantaneous deviation.
- **The steady state.** The reserve is fully active at 200 mHz deviation, which is also the regulation's maximum steady-state deviation. A droop sized so that 3000 MW is fully deployed at 200 mHz leaves the frequency settled at 0.2 Hz below nominal until frequency *restoration* reserves (15 minutes) bring it back (derive the droop's steady state).
- Say that these are a simple model's numbers, that the model is conservative, and that the regulation's figures are what the system is operated to; do not claim the rule was derived this way.
- With load damping of 1% of load per hertz (assumed), the computed minimum is 0.39 Hz, at 18 s. That is a problem's numerical extension and is not needed for the worked solution.
- **Losing 1 GW**: an initial rate of 0.0167 Hz/s and a ramp-model dip of 0.25 Hz.

**Why reactive power has to be made near where it is used.** From Q₁ ≈ V₁(V₁ − V₂)/X: moving Q across a reactance needs a voltage *difference*. Since the voltage must stay within narrow limits everywhere, Q cannot travel far, and real power can (it needs only a phase difference). That is why capacitor banks (unit 06) and generators' field control (branch 12, named) supply Q locally. Derive it with numbers: on the 400 km line (X = 97.1 Ω), sending 200 Mvar requires a voltage difference of about 48.6 kV (compute: Q ≈ V(V₁ − V₂)/X at 400 kV, so ΔV ≈ QX/V).

**Transformer reactance (land unit 07).** The street transformer's 4% is 0.04 pu on 400 kVA. In a power-flow model it is a series reactance like a line's, and in a fault it limits the current to 1/0.04 = 25 times rated. Name fault calculation as the main use, without computing beyond this.
