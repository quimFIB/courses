## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 05 promised "unit 14's buck converter filters with an LC pair whose ripple is a section 2 computation" (its section 2 is the series RLC). Unit 06 promised "Unit 14 meets currents that are not sinusoids, where this unit's Q stops being the whole story". Unit 07 said a longer, higher-voltage line draws charging current, "the reason unit 14 turns to direct current for long cables". Unit 10 said "Unit 14's HVDC has 'no skin effect' at DC". This unit keeps all of them. Three ideas carry it:
- an ideal switch dissipates nothing, so efficient conversion is switching and then filtering;
- a cable's capacitance spends its current rating on charging itself beyond a critical length, which DC does not do;
- generators that are inverters have no rotating inertia, which changes unit 13's swing equation.

- **Control theory** beyond the averaged model and the swing equation's modification.
- **Modular multilevel converter internals.** INELFE's converters are named as voltage-source converters with "more than 5,400 power modules" per station (REE), with no circuit detail.

## Physics and mathematics, in order

**The ideal switch.** At every instant either v = 0 (on) or i = 0 (off), so p = vi = 0: it converts without loss.
- A resistor divider can make 5 V from 12 V only by wasting 7/12 of the power. Compute: at 2 A, 14 W wasted for 10 W delivered.
- A switch and a filter can do it with none wasted.
- *Switching loss*: during a transition both v and i are non-zero. For an inductive load clamped by a diode, the energy per transition is ½VIt_c for a crossover time t_c (derive with a linear ramp of the current at full voltage, then of the voltage at zero current, or whichever model is stated). So the loss is ∝ f.
- At 12 V, 2 A, 20 ns per transition and 500 kHz, two transitions per cycle cost 0.24 W (assumed values).
- That is why switching frequency is bounded, and why it is pushed up anyway: filters and transformers shrink as 1/f.

**The buck converter (the checkpoint's first part).** A switch connects the input V_in to an LC filter for a fraction D of each period T = 1/f. A diode carries the inductor current in the rest of the period. The two switched states:
- on: L di/dt = V_in − v_o;
- off: L di/dt = −v_o.

**Theorem (averaged output)**: in periodic steady state, V_o = DV_in. Proof: the inductor current returns to its value after one period, so ∫v_L dt = 0 over a period (volt-second balance). That gives D(V_in − V_o) − (1 − D)V_o = 0. The small-ripple approximation (v_o ≈ V_o within a period) is stated and justified by the filter (below).
- Energy check: input current averages DI_o, so V_in DI_o = V_o I_o. The conversion is lossless, like an ideal DC transformer of ratio D (prove).

**Ripple, from unit 05's LC pair.**
- Inductor ripple: ΔI = (V_in − V_o)DT/L, peak to peak.
- Output voltage ripple ΔV = ΔI/(8fC). Derive this from the capacitor's charge in a half-period of the triangular ripple current.

Running numbers (assumed): V_in = 12 V, V_o = 5 V, I_o = 2 A, L = 10 μH, C = 22 μF, f = 500 kHz.
- D = 0.4167.
- ΔI = 0.583 A peak to peak.
- ΔV = 6.6 mV peak to peak.
- The filter's resonance 1/(2π√LC) = 10.7 kHz is far below the switching frequency. Show that this is why the LC pair passes the average and rejects the switching (unit 05's second-order low-pass, at 500 kHz/10.7 kHz ≈ 47 times its resonance).
- Continuous conduction holds down to I_o = ΔI/2 = 0.29 A.

**PWM and inverters: making AC from DC.**
- **The half-bridge.** Its output switches between ±V_dc/2. Varying the duty cycle slowly, sinusoidally, makes the *average* a sinusoid; this is pulse-width modulation.
- **The single-pulse waveform (MR 19).** A quasi-square wave of amplitude V with notches of half-width α at the zero crossings has b_n = (4V/nπ) cos nα for odd n.
  - At α = 0 (a square wave): b₁ = 1.273V, b₃ = 0.424V, b₅ = 0.255V.
  - α = 30° removes the third harmonic: b₁ = 1.103V, b₃ = 0, b₅ = −0.221V.
  - α = 18° removes the fifth.
  - Derive the coefficients; they are computed in the deck.
- **Sine–triangle PWM.** With modulation index m and carrier ratio 21, the computed spectrum has a fundamental of exactly m (0.800 at m = 0.8), and the largest harmonics sit at the carrier (21st, 0.818) and its sidebands (19th and 23rd, 0.22) and near twice the carrier (41st and 43rd, 0.31). It is a computed figure. The lesson is what the filter must remove: harmonics far above the fundamental, removed cheaply.
- **Three-phase inverter.** Three half-bridges driven 120° apart give unit 11's balanced set. Triplen harmonics cancel in the line-to-line voltages (prove: the 3rd harmonics of the three legs are in phase).

**Non-sinusoidal currents (land unit 06).** A sinusoidal voltage V and a distorted periodic current with RMS I, fundamental RMS I₁ and displacement φ₁.
- By Parseval (MR 19), only the fundamental carries mean power: P = VI₁ cos φ₁.
- So the power factor is P/(VI) = (I₁/I) cos φ₁, a *distortion factor* times a *displacement factor*.
- A square-wave current in phase with the voltage has I₁/I = 2√2/π = 0.900, and a power factor of 0.900 with no phase shift at all.
- The "rest" of VI is not one reactive power. Name the competing definitions, cite IEEE 1459 if confirmed, and say this is unit 06's warning made concrete. A mains adapter's capacitor-input rectifier draws current in peaks near the voltage maxima; that is qualitative, from the circuit's behaviour (the capacitor recharges only when the line voltage exceeds it), derived in words.

**Why the grid goes DC: the cable's charging current (the checkpoint's second part).**
- A cable is a long capacitor (unit 08's coax). Per phase it draws a charging current ωC′V_ph per kilometre, which in the lumped estimate is in quadrature with the voltage.
- For the assumed 400 kV XLPE cable (a = 30 mm, b = 57 mm, ε_r = 2.4): C′ = 2πε₀ε_r/ln(b/a) = 0.208 μF/km. The charging current is 15.09 A/km at 230.9 kV to earth.
- **Critical length**: fed from one end, the charging current alone reaches the 1500 A thermal rating at l_c = 1500/15.09 = **99.4 km**. Beyond that, the cable can carry no useful power at all. Derive this; say it is the lumped estimate, which unit 08 showed is accurate when βl is small. Check that here: the cable's own wave speed is about c/√2.4, so βl is small at 100 km and 50 Hz.
- Compensating with shunt reactors at both ends doubles the length to about 199 km (derive: the charging is split). Name it; do not develop it.
- **INELFE's 64.5 km as an AC cable** would carry 973 A of charging current at one end: **65% of the rating**. The remaining active current is √(1500² − 973²) = 1141 A. The cable would also make 674 Mvar that the grid at the ends must absorb.
- So the honest statement is narrower than the card's. 64.5 km is not beyond this cable's critical length. At that length an AC cable would spend most of its rating charging itself, and would need compensation. DC has none of this. The operator's own stated reason (REE) is that DC "allows underground connections that run over long distances". Say both, and do not claim the critical length alone decided the project.
- **Overhead lines, for comparison**: with a per-phase C′ ≈ 14.4 nF/km for unit 07's geometry (twice unit 08's single-phase loop C′ = 7.20 nF/km, since each conductor's capacitance to the neutral point is twice the line-to-line one; prove the factor), the charging current is 1.04 A/km at 400 kV. The critical length would be about 1400 km, where unit 08's lumped picture has long failed (a quarter wave is 1499 km). Cables are the case where charging current decides; overhead lines are limited first by the reactance limit of unit 13.
- **What DC removes** (each derived in one line):
  - **No charging current** in steady state, since C dV/dt = 0.
  - **No reactive power**, since there is no phase.
  - **No skin effect**, since current fills the conductor at DC. This is branch 10's low-frequency limit; if the learner skipped branch 10, say only that at 50 Hz the current crowds toward the conductor's surface, so the AC resistance exceeds the DC resistance, and name branch 10.
  - **Two conductors instead of three.**
  - **A power flow set by the converters**, not by a phase difference (unit 13): the DC link's P is a control setting.
- **What it pays**: converter stations at both ends, with their losses and cost.

**The DC link in numbers.** INELFE, under the ±320 kV bipolar assumption (see Sources):

- Converter losses: give no figure unless a named source is confirmed.

**The break-even** (model, or cited; see Sources). Total cost C_AC(l) = a_AC·l and C_DC(l) = F + a_DC·l with a_DC < a_AC, so l* = F/(a_AC − a_DC). For cables, a_AC includes compensation and the critical length caps l outright, so l* is short; for overhead lines it is long. One assumed set of numbers, said so, or the cited figures.

**Generation through inverters, and unit 13's swing equation.** A synchronous machine's rotor stores kinetic energy, and its δ̈ term (2H/ω_s)δ̈ resists any change in frequency for free. An inverter has no rotor.
- **Grid-following**: it measures the grid's phase with a phase-locked loop and injects a set current. It contributes no H unless its controller is told to inject P = −K df/dt, "synthetic inertia". To mimic H on rating S, K = 2HS/f₀, which is 200 MW per Hz/s for H = 5 s and S = 1 GW (compute).
- **Grid-forming**: it behaves as a voltage source behind a reactance, with its own internal angle, and its controller can be written to obey the swing equation with a chosen H and damping (virtual synchronous machine). One slide with the equations, showing which terms each type supplies.
- Consequence with unit 13's numbers: if a fraction x of S's 300 GW is grid-following without synthetic inertia, H falls to 5(1 − x) s, and the 3000 MW rate of change and worst-case dip rise by 1/(1 − x). At x = 0.5 they become 0.1 Hz/s and 1.5 Hz. That is above the 800 mHz instantaneous limit in this crude model, unless reserves act faster or the inverters supply inertia. Say this is the model's conclusion, not a forecast or a claim about any grid.
