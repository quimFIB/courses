## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become

Seven built units point here, and this unit must answer each of them (list under "Forward references"). The core debts are four:
- unit 02 estimated "789(ℓ/λ)² Ω" in its notes and said "Unit 16 derives this as the radiation resistance";
- unit 03 named "the Lorentz reciprocity theorem, which branch 16 cites";
- unit 04 said a receiving antenna "acts as a source with an internal resistance (branch 16 shows why, and what the resistance is)", and asked what the "lost" half in R_a is physically;
- unit 06 said "branch 16 shows that a short antenna is mostly reactive".

Three ideas carry the unit.
- A current that changes radiates, by MR 24's Larmor formula summed *coherently* over the moving charges. Averaged over a cycle that is ½R_radÎ²: to the circuit the antenna is a resistor whose heat leaves as a wave.
- An antenna is a linear two-port with the space between the antennas inside it, and that two-port is reciprocal. Reciprocity alone makes a receiving antenna a Thévenin source whose internal impedance is the antenna's own input impedance. It also fixes the ratio of collecting area to gain, A_e/G = λ²/4π, for every antenna.
- The Friis equation then says what fraction of the power arrives: (λ/4πd)² times the gains. This is why the phone that unit 15 charged across 5 mm cannot be charged from a base station 1 km away, and why power beamed from orbit needs apertures of order √(λd), kilometres across.

It must not become any of the following.
- **An antenna-design course.** No arrays beyond one sentence, no Yagis, patches, horns or reflectors beyond naming one as "an aperture". No numerical method-of-moments. The half-wave dipole's 73 Ω is cited, and its numerical check is optional.
- **A propagation course.** Free space only. Ground reflection, buildings, fading and the atmosphere are named once, as reasons real links receive less than Friis, with no figures.
- **A wireless-communications course.** No modulation, no noise budgets beyond the thermodynamic sketch, and no claims about any real network's frequencies, powers or antenna gains. Every number about the phone and the base station is an assumption, said so.
- **A space-solar-power advocacy piece or critique.** Glaser's proposal and Brown's history are cited for what they are. No proposal's sizes, costs or efficiencies appear unless a named source is confirmed. The λd condition is derived, and the two assumed apertures are labelled.
- **Health or exposure claims.** None. If the base station's power density at the phone is computed, it is only a number, with no comparison to any limit.

## Physics and mathematics, in order

**Convention, once, in a `box warn`.**
- Time dependence is e^{+iωt} (AUTHORING.md), so an outgoing spherical wave is e^{i(ωt − kr)}, k = ω/c = 2π/λ. MR 24 and Jackson write e^{−iωt}; replace i by −i to translate (unit 08 already said so for lines).
- Radiation resistance is defined with the **peak** current. P̄ = ½R_radÎ², with Î the amplitude at the feed (the current maximum, for every antenna here). With unit 05's RMS phasors the same R gives P̄ = R_rad|I|². The slide says both, so the course's RMS convention is not broken.

**1. Radiation from a current: the radiation resistance (the checkpoint's first part).**
- *The current element.* A straight segment of length l, short against λ, carrying a uniform current i(t). Continuity (unit 00) then demands charge ±q(t) at its ends, with dq/dt = i. A uniform current is the model; in practice it needs somewhere for the charge to go, say end plates. Say this as a model, and give no device claims.
- *Its dipole moment.* By MR 24's identity ∫J d³y = ṗ, the element has ṗ = l i(t), so p̈ = l di/dt.
- **Sum coherently, not by powers** (`box warn`).
  - MR 24's radiation field is linear in p̈, and p̈ = Σ q_j a_j over all the moving charges. So the radiated power is μ₀|Σ q_j a_j|²/(6πc), not Σ q_j²a_j²/(6πε₀c³).
  - For N electrons moving together the two differ by a factor N: N² against N.
  - Larmor's single-charge form applies to the *total* dipole moment. The lattice ions do not move and contribute nothing. Unit 00's drifting electrons have tiny accelerations, and their number makes up for it.
- **Time-domain result**: P(t) = μ₀l²(di/dt)²/(6πc). A current radiates in proportion to the square of its rate of change. This is what unit 08's nanosecond switch and its lightning stroke referred to. Give no number for either: unit 08's near end is about 1 m across, while c × 1 ns = 0.3 m, so MR 24's condition d ≪ cT fails there and the formula gives at best an order of magnitude. Say exactly that.
- **Sinusoidal steady state.** With i = Î cos ωt, the mean power is P̄ = μ₀l²ω²Î²/(12πc). Defining P̄ = ½R_radÎ² gives

  **R_rad = μ₀ω²l²/(6πc) = (2π/3)η₀(l/λ)² = 789.0(l/λ)² Ω.**

  This is unit 02's formula, now derived. Land its sentence.
- *Units check*: μ₀ω²l²/c has units H m⁻¹ · s⁻² · m² · s m⁻¹ = H/s = Ω ✓.
- *Energy check*: P̄ equals MR 24's ∮S·dA over a large sphere, with p₀ = lÎ/ω. That is MR 24's μ₀p₀²ω⁴/(12πc) ✓.
- **789 against 80π².** (2π/3)η₀ = 789.03 Ω with η₀ = 376.73 Ω, while 80π² = 789.57 Ω comes from rounding η₀ to 120π = 376.99 Ω. The two differ by 0.07%. Both round to the card's 790, and the slide states both.
- **Triangular current** (a thin centre-fed wire with nothing at its ends). The current must vanish at the tips, and for a short wire it falls linearly. Then ṗ = Î l/2 (the average current times l), and R_rad referred to the feed current is a quarter: (π/6)η₀(l/λ)² = 197(l/λ)² Ω, the 20π²(l/λ)² of textbooks. Jackson §9.2 treats this antenna; *confirm before citing its form*. This is a problem, and it shows that R_rad depends on the current's shape through ṗ alone.

**2. The fields of a current element: near against far.**
- **The vector potential, exactly.** For a point current element J = Î l ẑ δ³(x)e^{iωt}, MR 24's retarded potential gives A = (μ₀Îl/4π)(e^{−ikr}/r)ẑ (times e^{iωt}), with no approximation. The source is a point, so the retardation error MR 24 dropped is zero.
- **B from A**, one line: ∇ × (f(r)ẑ) = f′(r) r̂ × ẑ, so

  B_φ = (μ₀Îl sin θ/4π)(ik/r + 1/r²)e^{−ikr}.

  The 1/r² term is MR 23's Biot–Savart field of a current element, and the ik/r term is MR 24's radiation field.
- **E from Ampère–Maxwell** in the source-free region: E = (c²/iω)∇ × B. Derive E_θ:

  E_θ = (iη₀kÎl sin θ/4πr)[1 + 1/(ikr) − 1/(kr)²]e^{−ikr}.

  E_r ∝ cos θ is also present (state it; Jackson §9.2 gives both, cited). Check: the 1/r³ term of E is the static dipole field of MR 23 with p = Îl/(iω), i.e. q = Î/(iω), continuity ✓.
- **Where near becomes far.** The 1/r² and 1/r terms of B are equal in magnitude at kr = 1, that is at r = λ/2π. This is MR 24's "d ≪ r ≪ cT" with the boundary made exact for this source.

  | f | λ/2π |
  |---|---|
  | 140 kHz (unit 15's pad) | 341 m |
  | 9.9 MHz (Kurs et al.) | 4.82 m |
  | 100 MHz | 0.477 m |
  | 2.4 GHz | 1.99 cm |

  So the pad and its phone sit entirely in each other's near field. The base station 1 km away is at kr = 5 × 10⁴ from the phone, deep in the far field.
- **Complex power through a sphere** (derived; sympy-checked by the spec writer). Integrating ½E_θH_φ* over a sphere of radius r gives

  P_rad[1 − i/(kr)³], with P_rad = η₀k²l²Î²/(12π) = ½R_radÎ².

  - The real part does not depend on r: energy conservation in a lossless region ✓.
  - The imaginary part is the reactive power (unit 06), negative, that is capacitive. It grows as 1/(kr)³ as the sphere shrinks towards the antenna.
  - **This is "mostly reactive" derived**: the reactive power stored outside a sphere of radius r exceeds the radiated power by 1/(kr)³. For the 10 cm dipole at 100 MHz with r = l/2 = 5 cm, kr = 0.105 and 1/(kr)³ = 869.
  - Name Chu (1948) as the general bound only if confirmed (see Sources).
- **Near field against far field, the part's thesis.**
  - The quasi-static fields that unit 15's coils couple through fall as 1/r³. So M, and with it k and U, fall as d⁻³, and at U ≪ 1 the efficiency η_max ≈ U²/4 falls as d⁻⁶. Show this with unit 15's numbers: U = 0.55 at 80 mm gives η = 0.066 (U²/4 = 0.076); U = 0.08 at 160 mm gives η = 0.0016 (U²/4 = 0.0016).
  - The radiated intensity falls as 1/r², so received power falls as d⁻².
  - Doubling the distance costs a factor 64 near and a factor 4 far.
  - A log–log figure of the two slopes (computed, lean), with no claim about where they cross for any real device: they describe different machines.
- **Does unit 15's pad radiate?** A problem part, with the small-loop formula *cited* (Balanis ch. 5; or Jackson §9.3 by duality, confirm): R_rad = (η₀π/6)N²(ka)⁴ (≈ 20π²N²(C/λ)⁴ with η₀ ≈ 120π, C = 2πa). For N = 10, a = 20 mm and 140 kHz this gives 2.34 × 10⁻¹³ Ω, against the coil's 0.0833 Ω: a fraction 2.8 × 10⁻¹². The charger is not an antenna.
- **Directivity and gain** (definitions, as in Balanis ch. 2):
  - directivity D(θ, φ) = 4πU(θ, φ)/P_rad, with U the power per unit solid angle; an isotropic radiator has D = 1;
  - gain G = e_rad D, with radiation efficiency e_rad = R_rad/(R_rad + R_loss);
  - dB, dBi and dBm defined once.

**3. A short antenna: mostly reactive, and when it is mostly lossy (corrects the card; see the report).**
- *The circuit model.* At its feed, a transmitting antenna is a one-port Z_a = R_rad + R_loss + iX_a (unit 05's positive-real impedance; Re Z_a ≥ 0 ✓).
- *Loss.* At these frequencies the current flows in a skin of depth δ = √(2ρ/ωμ₀), stated from branch 10. So a wire of radius a ≫ δ has R_loss = ρl/(2πaδ) for a uniform current, and a third of that for a triangular one (a problem).
- *Reactance, order of magnitude* (derived, labelled as an estimate).
  - Each arm of length h = l/2 is a thin conductor. A uniform line charge on a segment has potential ≈ (λ_q/2πε₀)ln(h/a) at its middle (MR 23), so the arm's capacitance is C_arm ≈ 2πε₀h/ln(h/a).
  - The two arms in series give C ≈ πε₀l/[2 ln(l/2a)] and X_a ≈ −1/(ωC).
  - This is an estimate with factors of order one dropped. Balanis's formula (ch. 4, *if confirmed*) gives the comparison values in the table below.
- Numbers (wire radius a = 1 mm, copper; uniform current):

  | | 10 cm at 100 MHz | 1 m at 1 MHz |
  |---|---|---|
  | R_rad | 0.878 Ω | 8.78 mΩ |
  | δ | 6.52 μm | 65.2 μm |
  | R_loss | 0.0410 Ω | 0.0410 Ω |
  | e_rad (wire alone) | **95.5%** | **17.6%** |
  | e_rad, triangular current | 94.1% | 13.8% |
  | X_a, capacitance estimate | −4.48 kΩ (C = 0.356 pF) | −71.1 kΩ (C = 2.24 pF) |
  | X_a, Balanis's formula, if confirmed | −3.32 kΩ | −59.7 kΩ |
  | Q_a = \|X_a\|/R_rad | 3785 (formula) or 5099 (estimate) | 6.8 × 10⁶ or 8.1 × 10⁶ |

  The two cases have the same R_loss only because the second is ten times longer and its δ is ten times larger (f is 100 times lower), so l/δ is the same. State that arithmetic, not a general law.
- **What is true** (the card says "mostly reactive and mostly lossy").
  - *Mostly reactive: always.* |X_a| ≫ R_rad for any short antenna. The complex-power result above gives the reason, and the table gives the size, Q_a in the thousands to millions.
  - *Mostly lossy: it depends on what is counted.* The wire alone is 95.5% efficient at 10 cm and 100 MHz, and lossy, 17.6%, only at 1 m and 1 MHz.
  - But to deliver power (unit 06's conjugate match), the reactance must be cancelled, for instance by a series coil with X_L = +|X_a|. A real coil of quality factor Q_L adds a loss resistance |X_a|/Q_L (unit 05's definition of Q), so

    e ≈ R_rad/(R_rad + R_loss + |X_a|/Q_L) → Q_L/Q_a when Q_a ≫ Q_L (derive).

**4. Reciprocity (proved in a special case; the Lorentz theorem cited).**
- **The two-port.** Two antennas, each with a port. Space and both conductors form one linear system (Maxwell's equations are linear), so V₁ = z₁₁I₁ + z₁₂I₂ and V₂ = z₂₁I₁ + z₂₂I₂. This is unit 03's two-port, with the field between the antennas inside the box. Unit 03 proved z₁₂ = z₂₁ for networks of resistors and symmetric couplings. Here the network is the electromagnetic field.
- **Theorem (special case, proved on the slide): for two current elements, z₁₂ = z₂₁.**
  - Model each antenna as a current element whose current shape is fixed by its own source (thin, short, one mode). The external field E does work on the element's current at the rate ∫J·E dV = I l l̂·E. Under the passive convention that is power the port delivers, so the port voltage it produces is V = −l l̂·E.
  - So z₂₁ = −l₂ l̂₂·E₁(r₂)/I₁ and z₁₂ = −l₁ l̂₁·E₂(r₁)/I₂.
  - By section 2, the field of element 1 has the form E₁ = [a(r)l̂₁ + b(r)(l̂₁·r̂)r̂]I₁l₁, since ∇ × ∇ × (f(r)ẑ) has only those two pieces. Hence z₂₁ = −l₁l₂[a l̂₁·l̂₂ + b(l̂₁·r̂)(l̂₂·r̂)].
  - This is symmetric under exchanging 1 and 2, and r̂ → −r̂ leaves it unchanged. So z₁₂ = z₂₁. ∎
  - *Units*: Ω ✓. *The one step that used the physics*: the field kernel is a symmetric matrix, the analogue of unit 03's symmetric R_e î_e ĩ_e.
- **The general theorem, cited** (Balanis ch. 3, *confirm*; Pozar as an alternative).
  - In linear media with symmetric ε and μ, ∫E_a·J_b dV = ∫E_b·J_a dV for any two source distributions a and b at one frequency (the Lorentz reciprocity theorem). Land unit 03's sentence.
  - Notes give the derivation sketch: ∇·(E_a × H_b − E_b × H_a) = E_b·J_a − E_a·J_b from Maxwell with symmetric ε, μ (two lines). The surface term at infinity vanishes because the far fields are transverse with H = r̂ × E/η₀ (MR 24's E = cB × r̂). The cross terms then cancel identically (show the vector identity).
  - Say where it fails: media whose ε or μ is not a symmetric matrix. Name magnetised plasmas or ferrites as examples only if a source is checked; otherwise state the mathematical condition alone.
- **What it buys.** An antenna's pattern is the same transmitting and receiving. The path between two antennas loses the same fraction in both directions at one frequency. That is the running example's uplink and downlink at the same assumed frequency, and *no claim that real networks use one frequency both ways*.

**5. The receiving antenna: a Thévenin source, its effective aperture, and the lost half.**
- **Thévenin, from the two-port** (answers unit 04's "why, and what the resistance is"). For antenna 2 receiving, V₂ = z₂₁I₁ + z₂₂I₂. This is exactly a source V_oc = z₂₁I₁ in series with z₂₂.
  - z₂₂ is antenna 2's own input impedance, R_rad + R_loss + iX, the same impedance it shows when transmitting.
  - So unit 04's R_a is the radiation resistance plus the loss resistance. Unit 06's reactive source impedance is X_a, and section 3 showed it is large for a short antenna.
  - This is unit 04's Thévenin theorem applied to a linear system with one port left open: derived, not assumed.
- **The open-circuit voltage of a short element.** From z₂₁ above, a field E arriving from distant sources gives V_oc = −E·l̂ l (uniform current), and −E·l̂ l/2 for a triangular current: the "effective length".
- **Effective aperture of a short dipole, derived.** A plane wave of peak amplitude E₀ polarised along the element carries S = E₀²/(2η₀).
  - Conjugate matched and lossless, the element delivers V̂_oc²/(8R_rad), with peak amplitudes.
  - So A_e = P/S = η₀l²/(4R_rad) = **3λ²/8π**, whatever l. With a triangular current, V_oc halves and R_rad quarters, so A_e is unchanged (a problem).
  - At 2.4 GHz that is 18.6 cm²: *a lossless 1 cm dipole collects as much as a 6 cm one*. Shrinking an antenna costs efficiency and bandwidth (section 3), not collecting area.
  - Check: Gλ²/4π with G = 1.5 is 3λ²/8π ✓.
- **A_e = Gλ²/4π for every antenna.**
  - *Step 1, reciprocity.* Antennas a and b, d apart, both matched and in each other's far field. Transmitting from a, P_b/P_a = G_aA_b/(4πd²), by the definitions. Transmitting from b, G_bA_a/(4πd²). Section 4's z_ab = z_ba makes the two ratios equal: each is |z_ab|²/(4R_aR_b) in the weak-coupling limit (prove). So G_aA_b = G_bA_a, and A_e/G is the same constant for all antennas (in each direction, for matched polarisation).
  - *Step 2.* The short dipole fixes the constant as λ²/4π. Derived in full.
- **The thermodynamic argument, sketched and cited.** An independent route that needs no particular antenna.
  - Put a lossless matched antenna in a black enclosure at temperature T. Join it by a matched lossless line (unit 08) to a resistor at T outside.
  - The resistor sends kT dν down the line (Nyquist 1928, quoted above), and the antenna radiates it into the enclosure.
  - The enclosure's radiation arrives with brightness 2kT/λ² per unit bandwidth, area and solid angle (Rayleigh–Jeans). The antenna takes one polarisation, kT/λ². Sketch the mode count: MR 24's "every mode is an oscillator", 8πν²/c³ modes per unit volume and bandwidth, two polarisations, kT each by equipartition as Nyquist uses it, and brightness = energy density × c/4π.
  - The received power is kT dν × (1/λ²)∫A_e dΩ. By the second law the two flows balance in every band (Nyquist's own filter argument), so ∫A_e dΩ = λ².
  - With A_e ∝ G and ∫G dΩ = 4π (lossless), A_e = Gλ²/4π.
  - Validity: hν/kT = 3.8 × 10⁻⁴ at 2.4 GHz and 300 K, so the classical equipartition applies.
  - Label it a sketch. Rayleigh–Jeans and equipartition are cited, as Nyquist uses them, not proved here. *Attribute the antenna version of the argument to no one unless a source is confirmed.*
- **What the "lost" half in R_a is physically** (unit 04's question). This is derived in the special case and cited as limited in general.
  - The receiving element with load Z_L carries I = V_oc/(Z_a + Z_L). That current is a current element, so it radiates, by section 1, P_re = ½R_rad|Î|². This is the re-radiated (scattered) power.
  - At the conjugate match, lossless: Î = V̂_oc/(2R_rad), P_L = V̂_oc²/(8R_rad), and **P_re = V̂_oc²/(8R_rad) = P_L**. The power "dissipated" in the Thévenin R_rad is exactly the power the antenna sends back out as a wave. With R_loss > 0, part of the internal power is genuine heat.
  - The wave loses P_L + P_re = 2P_L in all, by energy conservation (Poynting's theorem around the antenna). The incident and scattered fields interfere to remove it from the forward direction; state that qualitatively.
  - **Limits, in a `box warn`.** This used the one-mode model, where the port current is the only current and no part of the structure scatters independently of the load. For antennas in general, equating the Thévenin internal dissipation with the scattered power is not correct (Collin 2003, cited for this warning). Kahn & Kurss (1965) name the class for which it holds, only if confirmed.
  - Two other loads, as a problem: open-circuited, I = 0 and nothing is re-radiated in this model. A tuned element shorted (Z_L = −iX_a) has Î = V̂_oc/R_rad and re-radiates 4P_L, a scattering cross-section 4A_e = 3λ²/2π = 0.477λ². So a resonant short wire scatters strongly.

**6. Friis: how much arrives (the checkpoint's second part).**
- **Derivation.** A transmitter of power P_t and gain G_t produces S = P_tG_t/(4πd²) at distance d. A receiver of area A_r = G_rλ²/4π collects SA_r, so

  **P_r/P_t = G_tG_r(λ/4πd)²**, or equivalently A_tA_r/(λd)².

  State the conditions: free space; each antenna in the other's far field; matched loads; matched polarisation; G including efficiency.
  - *Units*: dimensionless ✓.
  - *Energy*: P_r/P_t ≤ 1 is not built in. Section 7 shows this is exactly where Friis stops being true.
- **The checkpoint.** 1 W at 2.4 GHz, isotropic antennas, 1 km: λ = 0.1249 m, P_r = (0.1249/(4π × 1000))² W = **9.88 × 10⁻¹¹ W, about 0.1 nW**, or −70.1 dBm. The path gain is −100.1 dB. The isotropic A_e is λ²/4π = 12.4 cm². Say that "isotropic" is used here as a reference (G = 1), not as a description of any antenna in the example; make no further claim about it.
- **The running example's link** (all values assumed):
  - The base station: 20 W, G_t = 17 dBi = 50.1, so A_e = 0.0622 m². Its effective isotropic radiated power is 1002 W. At 1 km, S = 7.98 × 10⁻⁵ W/m² (E_rms = 0.173 V/m); a number only, with no exposure comparison.
  - To the phone's half-wave dipole (G = 1.64, A_e = 20.4 cm²): **1.62 × 10⁻⁷ W (−37.9 dBm)**. To an isotropic phone: 9.90 × 10⁻⁸ W (−40.0 dBm).
  - Uplink: the phone at an assumed 0.2 W reaches the base station with 1.62 × 10⁻⁹ W (−57.9 dBm). It is the same path gain, by reciprocity.
- Real links receive less (ground, buildings, the body); name the causes and give no figures.

**7. Why beaming power is hard.**
- **The phone, by Friis.** Unit 15's pad delivers 5 W across 5 mm at η ≈ 0.95. The base station delivers 1.62 × 10⁻⁷ W at 1 km, short of 5 W by a factor **3.1 × 10⁷**.
  - Friis would put 5 W at d = 0.18 m. There the base station's own aperture, A_e = 0.062 m², about 25 cm across, is not small against √(λd) ≈ 15 cm, so Friis no longer applies. The question becomes the next point.
- **When Friis fails, derived.**
  - In aperture form, P_r/P_t = A_tA_r/(λd)², which exceeds 1 once √(A_tA_r) > λd. That is impossible, so the formula's hypothesis (the far field) must have failed first.
  - *Why*, by spot size. An aperture of width D_t sends a beam spreading at an angle of order λ/D_t. That is the width–bandwidth reciprocity of MR 20's Fourier transform: the far-field pattern is the Fourier transform of the aperture illumination. Cite Balanis's aperture chapter, *confirm the chapter number*, or keep it at "of order".
  - So at distance d the spot is of order λd/D_t wide. A receiver much smaller than the spot collects the fraction Friis gives. A receiver as large as the spot, D_tD_r ≳ λd, collects most of the beam, and Friis's d⁻² law no longer describes it.
  - The point-receiver version is the Fraunhofer distance 2D²/λ (Balanis ch. 2, confirm). The path difference across an aperture seen from distance d is D²/8d, kept below λ/16. For equal apertures at that distance, the Friis ratio is (πD²/4)²/(2D²)² = π²/64 = 0.154. So Friis never predicts more than about 15% for equal apertures inside its own region of validity.
- **From geostationary orbit** (Glaser 1968 proposed it; Brown 1984 is the history).
  - d = 35 786 km (Kepler, with the stated constants). At an assumed 2.45 GHz, λ = 0.1224 m and λd = 4.38 × 10⁶ m².
  - Equal circular apertures reach Friis's ratio of 1 at A = λd, so **D = 2.36 km each**.
  - An assumed 1 km transmitter and 10 km receiver give a Friis ratio of **3.2 > 1**. Friis does not apply: the receiver is in the beam's Fresnel region. The spot from a 1 km aperture is of order λd/D_t = 4.4 km.
  - The lesson: collecting most of the power from orbit requires apertures whose product is of order λd, kilometres at microwave frequencies. Raising the frequency shrinks them as 1/√f (state the scaling only).
  - No real proposal's sizes, power densities or efficiencies are stated. 2.45 GHz is labelled assumed, and Brown's 1970 ground test at 2450 MHz may be mentioned as the confirmed use of that frequency (Brown 1980).
- **The rectenna** (Brown 1980, confirmed details only): an array of half-wave dipoles each feeding a rectifier, the first built in 1963 at Purdue for Raytheon. A helicopter powered by microwaves flew on 1 July 1964, and Brown's photograph of it 18.28 m above its transmitting antenna is dated October 1964. The 1970 test (Marshall, September 1970) gave 26.5% DC-to-DC overall. The rectenna is section 5's receiving antenna, with a rectifier as its load. If Goubau–Schwering's τ curve is confirmed, it can show how near 100% an aperture pair can come at τ ≳ 2; otherwise say "most of the power" with no curve.
