The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: the kettle cable. A kettle rated 2.3 kW at 230 V draws
\(I = 10\) A through copper conductors of cross-section \(A = 2.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\),
10 m from the fuse box. Its electrons drift at 0.29 mm/s, its copper carries a
field of 0.067 V/m, each metre of conductor has \(6.72\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\) and
makes 0.672 W of heat, and the kettle learns that the switch has closed at
neither the drift speed nor the Fermi speed, but at the speed at which the field
rearranges along the cable.

* What moves

- RMS value :: For a sinusoidal current \(i(t) = \sqrt2\,I\cos\omega t\), the
  number \(I = \sqrt{\overline{i^2}}\): the steady current that heats a wire at
  the same mean rate. Mains "230 V" and "10 A" are RMS values. /Slide: The
  kettle cable, from nothing./
- current density :: The vector field \(\vec J\), in \(\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\),
  whose flux through a surface is the current through it: \(I = \int_S\vec
  J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\). Met in MR 23. /Slide: Current density, from
  carriers./
- drift velocity :: The mean velocity \(\vec v_{\mathrm d}\) of the mobile
  charges. Carriers of charge \(q\) and number density \(n\) give \(\vec J =
  nq\vec v_{\mathrm d}\); the random part of their motion carries no net charge.
  In a metal \(q = -e\), so the electrons drift against \(\vec J\).
- conduction electron :: An electron free to move through a metal; copper has
  one per atom, \(n = 8.47\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\) (Ashcroft & Mermin,
  table 1.1), which the handbook density and molar mass reproduce to 0.24%.
  /Slide: Worked: the drift speed in the kettle cable./

* The Drude model

- Drude model :: Drude's 1900 model of a metal: classical electrons moving
  freely between instantaneous collisions, which occur with probability
  \(\mathrm dt/\tau\) in each interval \(\mathrm dt\) and leave each electron
  with a random velocity of mean zero. /Slide: Drude's hypotheses./
- relaxation time :: The constant \(\tau\) of the Drude model: the mean time
  between collisions. Not predicted by the model; read off the measured
  resistivity, \(\tau = m/ne^2\rho = 2.49\times10^{-14}\) s for copper at 20 °C.
- relaxation-time equation :: \(\mathrm d\vec p/\mathrm dt = -\vec p/\tau +
  \vec f\) for the mean momentum per electron under a force \(\vec f\):
  collisions act on the mean motion as a friction of rate \(1/\tau\). /Slide:
  The relaxation-time equation./
- Ohm's law :: In local form, \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\): the steady state of the
  relaxation-time equation, with \(\sigma = ne^2\tau/m\). Its integrated form for
  a conductor between two electrodes is \(V = IR\). /Slide: Ohm's law, out of
  the model./
- conductivity :: \(\sigma\) in \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\), in siemens per
  metre (\(\mathrm S = \mathrm{A\,V^{-1}}\)). Copper at 20 °C: \(5.95\times10^7\
  \mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}\). Not to be confused with MR 23's surface charge density,
  written \(\Sigma\) in this course.
- resistivity :: \(\rho = 1/\sigma\), in \(\Omega\,\mathrm m\). Copper at 20
  °C: \(1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\) (CRC). In this course volume
  charge density is written \(\varrho\) to keep the two apart.
- linear response :: A response proportional to the push that causes it. Ohm's
  law is one because the drift is a tiny shift of the electrons' velocity
  distribution: in the kettle cable \(v_{\mathrm d}/v_F = 1.9\times10^{-10}\).
  /Slide: A linear response to a gentle push./
- Fermi speed :: The speed \(v_F\) of the fastest conduction electrons, set by
  the Pauli principle — a quantum result (Sommerfeld, 1928), cited from
  Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 2.
  For copper \(1.57\times10^6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (their table 2.1).

* Joule heating

- Joule heating :: The work the field does on the carriers, \(\vec J\cdot\vec
  E\) per unit volume. In the steady state all of it passes to the lattice
  through collisions, as heat. /Slide: The field's work is J·E./
- resistance :: \(R = V/I\) for a conductor between two electrodes, in ohms
  (\(\Omega = \mathrm{V\,A^{-1}}\)). For a uniform wire \(R = \rho L/A\); the
  heat in it is \(VI = I^2R\). /Slide: A uniform wire: I²R./
- current-carrying capacity :: The current a cable may carry continuously,
  tabulated in IEC 60364-5-52 by conductor size and installation method so
  that the conductor stays below its insulation's limit: 70 °C for PVC, 90 °C
  for XLPE. A thermal limit, set by \(I^2R\). /Slide: Why cables are rated by
  temperature./

* Resistance as a boundary-value problem

- mixed boundary-value problem :: Steady current in a conductor:
  \(\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\varphi) = 0\) inside, with \(\varphi\) given on the
  electrodes (Dirichlet) and \(\partial_n\varphi = 0\) on insulated surfaces
  (Neumann). Its solution is unique, by the energy method, so \(R\) depends only
  on shape and material. /Slide: Steady current is a Laplace problem./
- electrode :: A patch of a conductor's surface joined to a much better
  conductor, and so held at a single potential.

* Surface charge

- surface charge :: Charge on the surface of a current-carrying wire, graded
  along it, with extra charge at bends and junctions. Its three roles (Jackson,
  1996): it maintains the potential around the circuit, provides the field
  outside the conductors, and confines the current to the wire. /Slide: Surface
  charge steers the field./
- junction charge :: The sheet of charge \(\Sigma = \varepsilon_0J_n(\rho_2 -
  \rho_1)\) at an interface where current passes from resistivity \(\rho_1\) into
  \(\rho_2\): positive where it enters a worse conductor. /Slide: Charge at a
  junction./

* How fast does interior charge vanish?

- charge relaxation time :: \(t_c = \varepsilon_0/\sigma\), the decay time of
  interior charge if \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) held at every instant. For copper
  \(1.5\times10^{-19}\) s — far shorter than \(\tau\), so meaningless there;
  correct only for a poor conductor, as the overdamped limit of the charge
  oscillator. /Slide: The naive answer, and why it is meaningless./
- plasma frequency :: \(\omega_p = \sqrt{ne^2/\varepsilon_0m}\), the natural
  frequency of the charge oscillator \(\ddot\varrho + \dot\varrho/\tau +
  \omega_p^2\varrho = 0\). For copper \(1.64\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\),
  with quality factor \(\omega_p\tau = 410\). /Slide: Done properly: the charge
  is an oscillator./

* What Drude gets wrong

- mean free path :: \(\ell = v\tau\), the distance travelled between
  collisions. With the classical speed, 2.9 nm in copper; with the Fermi speed,
  39 nm, about 150 nearest-neighbour distances. /Slide: Two speeds, two mean free
  paths./
- Hall effect :: The transverse field \(E_y\) that appears across a conductor
  carrying current \(J_x\) in a magnetic field \(B_z\), from charge pushed to
  one edge by the magnetic force. /Slide: The Hall effect, and a wrong sign./
- Hall coefficient :: \(R_H = E_y/J_xB\); in the Drude model \(-1/ne\), so it
  measures the carrier density and the carriers' sign. For some metals it is
  measured positive, which classical electrons cannot explain (Ashcroft &
  Mermin, ch. 1).
- Wiedemann–Franz law :: For many metals \(\kappa/\sigma T\) is nearly the same.
  Drude's classical value \(\tfrac32(k_B/e)^2 = 1.11\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}\)
  came close only through two compensating errors; Sommerfeld's is
  \(\tfrac{\pi^2}{3}(k_B/e)^2 = 2.44\times10^{-8}\). /Slide: Wiedemann–Franz: right
  for the wrong reasons./
