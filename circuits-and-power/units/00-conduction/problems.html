Conduction: Ohm's law from a model, and what the model gets wrong

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You derive \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) from the Drude model, compute the kettle cable's drift velocity and per-metre dissipation — 0.29 mm/s and 0.67 W/m — and say why neither the drift speed nor the Fermi speed is the speed at which the kettle learns that the switch has closed.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Thirteen problems, about three and a half hours; P13 is optional. Conventions as in the deck: \(\sigma\) is conductivity and \(\rho = 1/\sigma\) resistivity; volume and surface charge densities are \(\varrho\) and \(\Sigma\); electrons carry charge \(-e\) with \(e \gt 0\); a complex amplitude goes with \(e^{+i\omega t}\).

Constants: \(e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\) C, \(m = 9.109\times10^{-31}\) kg, \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}\), \(k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\), \(N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}\) (CODATA). Copper at 20 °C: \(\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\), aluminium \(2.65\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\) (CRC); copper's conduction-electron density \(n = 8.47\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\) (Ashcroft & Mermin, table 1.1) and Fermi speed \(v_F = 1.57\times10^6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (their table 2.1).

Carriers and the Drude model

P1 The kettle cable, and a thinner one routine

exercises: \(J = nq v_{\mathrm d}\), \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\), \(R = \rho L/A\), Joule heating, units

A kettle draws a steady 10 A (treat the mains current as steady at its RMS value).

  1. Through copper conductors of \(2.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\): find \(J\), the field \(E\) in the copper, the drift speed, the resistance and the heat per metre of conductor, and — for a 10 m run, out on one conductor and back on the other — the total heat in the cable and the potential lost along it.
  2. Repeat for a 15 m run of \(1.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\) conductors (an assumption for the problem), and give the lost potential as a percentage of 230 V.
  3. Going from \(2.5\) to \(1.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\) at the same current, by what factor does the heat per metre rise? And the heat per unit volume of copper? Why are the two factors different?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything here is a number with units, found from one of four formulas: \(J = I/A\), \(E = \rho J\), \(v_{\mathrm d} = J/ne\), \(R' = \rho/A\). The rest is multiplication.

Rung 2

Convert \(\mathrm{mm^2}\) to \(\mathrm{m^2}\) first: \(1\ \mathrm{mm^2} = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}\). A run of length \(L\) is \(2L\) of conductor. Heat per metre is \(I^2R'\); per unit volume it is \(\vec J\cdot\vec E = \rho J^2\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(J = 10/(2.5\times10^{-6}) = 4.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\). For part 3, write each heat as a power of \(A\) at fixed \(I\): \(I^2\rho/A\) and \(\rho I^2/A^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. The deck's running example, done once more by hand, then varied. The four formulas are all local consequences of \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) and \(\vec J = -ne\vec v_{\mathrm d}\), plus the uniform-wire result.

1. \(J = 4.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\). \(E = \rho J = 1.68\times10^{-8}\times4.0\times10^6 = 0.0672\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\). \(ne = 8.47\times10^{28}\times1.602\times10^{-19} = 1.357\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{C\,m^{-3}}\), so \(v_{\mathrm d} = 4.0\times10^6/1.357\times10^{10} = 2.95\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), 0.29 mm/s. \(R' = \rho/A = 6.72\times10^{-3}\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), and \(I^2R' = 0.672\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}}\). Twenty metres of conductor: \(R = 0.134\ \Omega\), heat \(13.4\) W, lost potential \(IR = 1.34\) V.

2. \(A = 1.5\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^2}\): \(J = 6.67\times10^6\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\), \(E = 0.112\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\), \(v_{\mathrm d} = 4.91\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) (0.49 mm/s), \(R' = 0.0112\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(I^2R' = 1.12\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}}\). Thirty metres of conductor: \(R = 0.336\ \Omega\), heat \(33.6\) W, lost potential \(3.36\) V, which is \(1.46\%\) of 230 V.

3. At fixed \(I\), heat per metre \(I^2\rho/A\) goes as \(1/A\): factor \(2.5/1.5 = 1.67\) (and indeed \(1.12/0.672 = 1.67\)). Heat per unit volume \(\rho J^2 = \rho I^2/A^2\) goes as \(1/A^2\): factor \(2.78\) (\(2.69\times10^5\) to \(7.47\times10^5\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\)). They differ because a metre of the thinner conductor also holds less copper: the heat per metre is the heat per volume times \(A\). The thinner cable runs hotter for both reasons — more heat per metre, and less metal and less surface to carry it away — which is why a smaller conductor is rated for less current.

Check. Units: \(\Omega\,\mathrm m/\mathrm m^2 = \Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}\); \(\mathrm A^2\cdot\Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-1}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}}\) ✓. Energy: \(JE\cdot A = 6.67\times10^6\times0.112\times1.5\times10^{-6} = 1.12\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-1}}\), agreeing with \(I^2R'\) ✓.

P2 Counting electrons routine

exercises: carrier density from handbook data, the drift speed, \(\tau\) from \(\rho\)

  1. From copper's density \(8.96\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) and molar mass \(63.55\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\) (CRC), with one conduction electron per atom, find \(n\) and compare with Ashcroft & Mermin's \(8.47\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\).
  2. Aluminium: density \(2.70\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\), molar mass \(26.98\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\), three conduction electrons per atom. Find \(n\).
  3. An aluminium conductor of \(2.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\) carries 10 A. Find the drift speed, and say why it is slower than in copper although aluminium conducts worse.
  4. Find \(\tau\) for aluminium from its resistivity, and compare with copper's \(2.49\times10^{-14}\) s.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four numbers. The first two are a unit conversion and Avogadro's number; the last two use \(v_{\mathrm d} = J/ne\) and \(\tau = m/ne^2\rho\).

Rung 2

Moles per cubic metre are (density in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\))/(molar mass in \(\mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}\)). A density of \(8.96\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) is \(8.96\times10^3\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\).

Rung 3

First line: \(8.96\times10^3/0.06355 = 1.410\times10^5\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}\). For part 3, notice that \(v_{\mathrm d}\) depends on \(n\) only, not on \(\rho\).

Worked solution

Idea. Atoms per volume from mass per volume; carriers from atoms; then the two Drude relations, one of which knows about collisions and one of which does not.

1. \(1.410\times10^5\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}\times6.022\times10^{23} = 8.49\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), 0.24% above \(8.47\times10^{28}\). The small difference is in the handbook data's last digits.

2. \(2.70\times10^3/0.02698 = 1.001\times10^5\ \mathrm{mol\,m^{-3}}\); times \(N_A\) and times 3: \(n = 1.81\times10^{29}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\), a little over twice copper's.

3. \(J = 4.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\) as before, so \(v_{\mathrm d} = 4.0\times10^6/(1.808\times10^{29}\times1.602\times10^{-19}) = 1.38\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), 0.14 mm/s. The drift speed is fixed by the current and the number of carriers sharing it: \(v_{\mathrm d} = J/ne\). Aluminium has more carriers, so each moves more slowly. How hard it is to push them — the resistivity — decides the field needed, not the speed.

4. \(\tau = m/(ne^2\rho) = 9.109\times10^{-31}/(1.808\times10^{29}\times2.566\times10^{-38}\times2.65\times10^{-8}) = 7.41\times10^{-15}\) s, 0.30 of copper's. Aluminium's electrons are more numerous but collide over three times as often, and the second wins: \(\sigma\) is lower.

Check. Units of \(\tau\): \(\mathrm{kg}/(\mathrm{m^{-3}\,C^2\,\Omega\,m}) = \mathrm s\) ✓ (the deck's second worked example). Consistency: \(\sigma_{\mathrm{Al}}/\sigma_{\mathrm{Cu}} = (n\tau)_{\mathrm{Al}}/(n\tau)_{\mathrm{Cu}} = (1.808/0.847)\times0.297 = 0.634\), and \(1.68/2.65 = 0.634\) ✓.

P3 τ and the mean free path medium

exercises: reading \(\tau\) off \(\rho\), equipartition, what the Fermi speed changes

  1. Find \(\tau\) for copper at 20 °C.
  2. With Drude's classical speed \(\sqrt{3k_BT/m}\) at 293 K — from classical equipartition, \(\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac32k_BT\), which Drude took from the kinetic theory of gases (cited, as in the deck) — find the mean free path \(\ell = v\tau\), and express it in nearest-neighbour distances (0.256 nm in copper, a handbook value).
  3. Do the same with the Fermi speed.
  4. Which picture of a collision — an electron hitting the next ion along — is compatible with which \(\ell\)? Why does the change of speed not change \(\sigma\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three lengths and an argument. The same \(\tau\) is used for both speeds: it is fixed by the measured resistivity, not by the speed.

Rung 2

For part 2 the speed is given; only the arithmetic is yours. For part 4, look at what the derivation of \(\sigma = ne^2\tau/m\) used about the electrons' velocities.

Rung 3

First line: \(\tau = m/ne^2\rho = 9.109\times10^{-31}/3.652\times10^{-17}\) s.

Worked solution

Idea. \(\tau\) comes from experiment; the mean free path is \(\tau\) times a speed, and the speed is where classical and quantum physics disagree.

1. \(ne^2 = 8.47\times10^{28}\times2.566\times10^{-38} = 2.174\times10^{-9}\); times \(\rho\): \(3.652\times10^{-17}\); \(\tau = 2.49\times10^{-14}\) s.

2. \(v = \sqrt{3\times1.381\times10^{-23}\times293/9.109\times10^{-31}} = 1.15\times10^5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\); \(\ell = 1.154\times10^5\times2.494\times10^{-14} = 2.9\times10^{-9}\) m, about 11 nearest-neighbour distances.

3. \(\ell = 1.57\times10^6\times2.494\times10^{-14} = 3.9\times10^{-8}\) m = 39 nm, about 153 distances — \(v_F/v = 13.6\) times the classical value.

4. Drude's picture of an electron bouncing off ions wants \(\ell\) of the order of the spacing between ions. The classical estimate is within an order of magnitude of that; the quantum one is not: the electron passes some 150 ions between collisions, so collisions are not with the ions as such (why a perfect lattice does not scatter is quantum mechanics, Ashcroft & Mermin, later chapters). But the derivation of \(\sigma = ne^2\tau/m\) used only that collisions happen at rate \(1/\tau\) and leave a mean velocity of zero; the random speed never entered. So \(\sigma\) is untouched, and \(\tau\) — defined by the measured \(\rho\) — is the same number. What changes is the story told about it.

Check. Units: \(\sqrt{\mathrm{J}/\mathrm{kg}} = \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) ✓; \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm s = \mathrm m\) ✓. Consistency: the ratio of the two mean free paths is \(v_F/v = 13.6\), whatever \(\tau\) is, and \(39.2/2.88 = 13.6\) ✓.

P4 Switching on, switching off routine

exercises: a first-order linear ODE (MR 00, MR 01), the relaxation-time equation

  1. A field \(\vec E\) is switched on at \(t = 0\) in copper whose electrons have \(\vec p(0) = \vec 0\). Solve \(\dot{\vec p} = -\vec p/\tau - e\vec E\), and find the time at which the drift reaches 99% of its final value.
  2. Check the solution's behaviour for \(t \ll \tau\) and \(t \gg \tau\) against what the model says should happen.
  3. Now the field, having been on for a long time, is switched off. How far does the electrons' mean position move after that, for the kettle cable's drift \(2.95\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is a function of time and a time; to part 3, a length. The equation is linear with constant coefficients, one component at a time.

Rung 2

Shift the unknown: \(q = p + eE\tau\) obeys \(\dot q = -q/\tau\). For part 3 the drift decays as \(v_{\mathrm d}e^{-t/\tau}\); integrate it from 0 to \(\infty\).

Rung 3

First line: \(q(t) = q(0)e^{-t/\tau}\) with \(q(0) = eE\tau\), so \(p(t) = -eE\tau + eE\tau\,e^{-t/\tau}\).

Worked solution

Idea. A relaxation equation relaxes exponentially to its fixed point; everything follows from that.

1. \(\vec p(t) = -e\tau\vec E\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\), unique by MR 01 (the right side is Lipschitz with constant \(1/\tau\)). 99% when \(e^{-t/\tau} = 0.01\): \(t = \tau\ln100 = 4.61\tau = 1.15\times10^{-13}\) s for copper.

2. For \(t \ll \tau\), \(1 - e^{-t/\tau} \approx t/\tau\) and \(\vec p \approx -e\vec E\,t\): free acceleration under the force \(-e\vec E\), as it must be before any electron has had time to collide. For \(t \gg \tau\), \(\vec p\to-e\tau\vec E\), the steady drift of Ohm's law.

3. After switch-off, \(\dot{\vec p} = -\vec p/\tau\), so the mean velocity is \(v_{\mathrm d}e^{-t/\tau}\) and the mean position moves a further \(\int_0^\infty v_{\mathrm d}e^{-t/\tau}\,\mathrm dt = v_{\mathrm d}\tau = 2.95\times10^{-4}\times2.49\times10^{-14} = 7.4\times10^{-18}\) m, about \(3\times10^{-8}\) of copper's nearest-neighbour distance. The current stops, for every practical purpose, the instant the field does.

Check. Units: \(e\tau E\) is \(\mathrm{C\cdot s\cdot V\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{J\,s\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\), a momentum ✓. The solution satisfies the equation: \(\dot p = -eE\,e^{-t/\tau}\) and \(-p/\tau - eE = eE(1 - e^{-t/\tau}) - eE = -eE\,e^{-t/\tau}\) ✓.

P5 AC conductivity medium

exercises: the complex-drive trick with \(e^{+i\omega t}\) (MR 06), limits, small-parameter expansions

  1. With \(\vec E(t) = \mathrm{Re}(\tilde{\vec E}e^{i\omega t})\), derive \(\tilde{\vec J} = \sigma(\omega)\tilde{\vec E}\) with \(\sigma(\omega) = \sigma_0/(1 + i\omega\tau)\). Say why the real part of your complex solution is a solution.
  2. For copper, evaluate \(1 - |\sigma(\omega)|/\sigma_0\) at 50 Hz and at 2.4 GHz, and the phase of \(\sigma\) at 2.4 GHz.
  3. At what frequency is \(|\sigma| = \sigma_0/\sqrt2\)?
  4. Find the limits \(\omega\to0\) and \(\omega\to\infty\) of \(\sigma(\omega)\), and say what each means physically.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is a complex function of \(\omega\); to parts 2–3, small numbers and a frequency. Part 2 is an exercise in not subtracting two nearly equal numbers.

Rung 2

Try \(\vec p = \mathrm{Re}(\tilde{\vec p}e^{i\omega t})\) and replace \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dt\) by \(i\omega\). For part 2, expand \((1 + x)^{-1/2} = 1 - x/2 + O(x^2)\) with \(x = \omega^2\tau^2\).

Rung 3

First line: \(i\omega\tilde{\vec p} = -\tilde{\vec p}/\tau - e\tilde{\vec E}\), so \(\tilde{\vec p}(1 + i\omega\tau) = -e\tau\tilde{\vec E}\).

Worked solution

Idea. A linear equation with real coefficients, driven sinusoidally, responds at the drive frequency with a complex gain; the gain here is a low-pass filter with corner \(1/\tau\).

1. The equation \(\dot{\vec p} = -\vec p/\tau - e\vec E\) is linear with real coefficients, so if a complex \(\vec p\) solves it with the complex drive, its real part solves it with the real drive (take real parts of both sides). With \(\vec p = \tilde{\vec p}e^{i\omega t}\): \(\tilde{\vec p} = -e\tau\tilde{\vec E}/(1 + i\omega\tau)\). Then \(\tilde{\vec J} = -ne\tilde{\vec p}/m = (ne^2\tau/m)\tilde{\vec E}/(1 + i\omega\tau) = \sigma_0\tilde{\vec E}/(1 + i\omega\tau)\).

2. \(|\sigma|/\sigma_0 = (1 + \omega^2\tau^2)^{-1/2}\), so \(1 - |\sigma|/\sigma_0 = \tfrac12\omega^2\tau^2 + O(\omega^4\tau^4)\). At 2.4 GHz, \(\omega\tau = 2\pi\times2.4\times10^9\times2.494\times10^{-14} = 3.76\times10^{-4}\), and the difference is \(7.07\times10^{-8}\). The phase is \(-\arctan\omega\tau = -3.76\times10^{-4}\) rad \(= -0.0216^\circ\): the current lags the field by a fiftieth of a degree. At 50 Hz, \(\omega\tau = 7.8\times10^{-12}\) and the difference is utterly negligible — \(\tfrac12\omega^2\tau^2\) is below \(10^{-22}\). (Computing \(1 - (1 + x)^{-1/2}\) directly on a calculator for such \(x\) returns 0: the expansion is the honest way.)

3. \(\omega\tau = 1\): \(f = 1/2\pi\tau = 6.4\times10^{12}\) Hz, 6.4 THz.

4. \(\omega\to0\): \(\sigma\to\sigma_0\), the DC law. \(\omega\to\infty\): \(\sigma\approx\sigma_0/(i\omega\tau) = -i\,ne^2/(m\omega)\): purely imaginary, so current and field are \(90^\circ\) apart, and \(\tau\) has dropped out — the electrons are shaken faster than they collide and respond as free particles, \(m\dot{\vec v} = -e\vec E\).

Check. Dimensions: \(\omega\tau\) is a pure number, so \(\sigma(\omega)\) has the units of \(\sigma_0\) ✓. Energy: the mean of \(\vec J\cdot\vec E\) over a cycle is proportional to \(\mathrm{Re}\,\sigma = \sigma_0/(1 + \omega^2\tau^2) \gt 0\), so the field always loses energy to the copper, and less of it at high frequency, where collisions are rare in a cycle ✓.

P6 Proof: the power balance medium

exercises: energy from an equation of motion, Joule heating, a proof

  1. From the relaxation-time equation, prove that \[ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Big(\frac{n|\vec p|^2}{2m}\Big) = \vec J\cdot\vec E - \frac{n|\vec p|^2}{m\tau}, \] and deduce that in the steady state \(\vec J\cdot\vec E = \sigma E^2\), all of it lost to collisions.
  2. Integrate the identity over the switch-on of P4, from \(t = 0\) to \(\infty\). Show that the total by which the field's work exceeds the collisional loss is exactly the drift energy per unit volume at the end, \(\tfrac12\sigma E^2\tau\).
  3. Using Drude's full hypothesis 3 — a collision leaves an electron with the lattice's thermal energy \(K_0\) — show that in the steady state the electrons' mean kinetic energy exceeds \(K_0\) by \(mv_{\mathrm d}^2\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an identity: one dot product. Part 2 is two integrals of exponentials. Part 3 is a balance of two rates.

Rung 2

Dot \(\dot{\vec p} = -\vec p/\tau - e\vec E\) with \(\vec p/m\), and use \(\vec J = -ne\vec p/m\). For part 2 substitute \(p(t) = -p_\infty(1 - e^{-t/\tau})\) with \(p_\infty = eE\tau\). For part 3, between collisions the field changes the mean kinetic energy per electron at the rate \(-e\vec E\cdot\vec v_{\mathrm d}\); collisions remove \((K - K_0)/\tau\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\frac{\vec p}{m}\cdot\dot{\vec p} = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac{|\vec p|^2}{2m} = -\frac{|\vec p|^2}{m\tau} - e\vec E\cdot\frac{\vec p}{m}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Energy statements come from dotting an equation of motion with a velocity — the work–energy theorem of MR 04, for the mean motion.

1. The first line gives the identity per electron; multiply by \(n\) and note \(-ne\vec E\cdot\vec p/m = \vec E\cdot(-ne\vec p/m) = \vec J\cdot\vec E\). In the steady state the left side vanishes and \(\vec p = -e\tau\vec E\), so \(\vec J\cdot\vec E = n|\vec p|^2/m\tau = ne^2\tau E^2/m = \sigma E^2\). The collision term is the only sink in the equation, so all the field's work leaves through it. \(\square\)

2. Write \(u = e^{-t/\tau}\). From P4, \(\vec p = -e\tau\vec E(1 - u)\), so \(\vec J = -ne\vec p/m = \sigma\vec E(1 - u)\) and \[ \vec J\cdot\vec E = \sigma E^2(1 - u),\qquad \frac{n|\vec p|^2}{m\tau} = \sigma E^2(1 - u)^2,\qquad \frac{n|\vec p|^2}{2m} = \tfrac12\sigma E^2\tau(1 - u)^2 . \] The field's work exceeds the loss at the rate \(\sigma E^2(1 - u)\big(1 - (1 - u)\big) = \sigma E^2u(1 - u)\), and \(\int_0^\infty u(1 - u)\,\mathrm dt = \tau - \tfrac12\tau = \tfrac12\tau\). So the total excess is \(\tfrac12\sigma E^2\tau\), which is the final drift energy \(\tfrac12\sigma E^2\tau(1 - 0)^2\) ✓. Both the field's work and the collisional loss grow without bound, at the steady rate \(\sigma E^2\); their difference is finite and is exactly what the drift stores.

3. Steady state: \((K - K_0)/\tau = -e\vec E\cdot\vec v_{\mathrm d} = e^2\tau E^2/m = m v_{\mathrm d}^2/\tau\), using \(v_{\mathrm d} = e\tau E/m\). So \(K - K_0 = mv_{\mathrm d}^2\): the drift's own \(\tfrac12mv_{\mathrm d}^2\), and as much again in extra random motion. The power into the lattice, \(n(K - K_0)/\tau = nmv_{\mathrm d}^2/\tau = \sigma E^2\), is again \(\vec J\cdot\vec E\) ✓.

Check. Units: \(n|\vec p|^2/m\tau\) is \(\mathrm{m^{-3}\cdot kg^2\,m^2\,s^{-2}/(kg\,s)} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\) ✓. For the cable, \(mv_{\mathrm d}^2 = 9.1\times10^{-31}\times(2.95\times10^{-4})^2 \approx 8\times10^{-38}\) J: the electrons' excess heat is invisible, though the lattice's 0.67 W per metre is not.

Resistance as a boundary-value problem

P7 Proof: uniqueness with mixed data medium

exercises: the energy method of MR 23, Dirichlet and Neumann data, interfaces, a proof

A bounded connected conductor \(D\) has electrodes \(S_D\) (\(\varphi\) given) and insulated surface \(S_N\).

  1. Prove that at most one \(\varphi\) satisfies \(\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\varphi) = 0\) in \(D\), the electrode data, and \(\sigma\partial_n\varphi = 0\) on \(S_N\), when \(\sigma \gt 0\) is smooth and \(S_D\) is non-empty.
  2. Now \(D\) is two metals, \(\sigma_1\) and \(\sigma_2\), joined across an interface \(\Gamma\). State the two conditions that hold across \(\Gamma\) and show the proof survives.
  3. What fails if \(S_D\) is empty, and what does that mean physically?
  4. With two electrodes at \(V\) and \(0\), deduce that \(R = V/I\) is positive and independent of \(V\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each part is a short argument. The tool is one integral identity, applied to the difference of two solutions.

Rung 2

For the difference \(\psi\): \(\nabla\cdot(\psi\sigma\nabla\psi) = \sigma|\nabla\psi|^2 + \psi\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\psi)\). Integrate and use the divergence theorem. At an interface, ask what is continuous: the potential, and the normal current.

Rung 3

First line: \(\int_D\sigma|\nabla\psi|^2\,\mathrm dV = \oint_{\partial D}\psi\,\sigma\partial_n\psi\,\mathrm dA\), and each piece of the boundary contributes zero.

Worked solution

Idea. The energy method: the dissipated power of the difference of two solutions is a boundary term, which the data kill; a field that dissipates nothing is zero.

1. As in the deck (Uniqueness with mixed data): \(\psi = \varphi_1 - \varphi_2\) satisfies \(\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\psi) = 0\), \(\psi = 0\) on \(S_D\), \(\sigma\partial_n\psi = 0\) on \(S_N\). Integrating the product rule over \(D\) and applying the divergence theorem gives \(\int_D\sigma|\nabla\psi|^2 = \int_{S_D}\psi\sigma\partial_n\psi + \int_{S_N}\psi\sigma\partial_n\psi = 0 + 0\). The integrand is continuous and \(\ge 0\) with \(\sigma \gt 0\), so \(\nabla\psi \equiv \vec 0\), \(\psi\) is constant on the connected \(D\), and it is zero on \(S_D\). \(\square\)

2. Across \(\Gamma\): \(\varphi\) is continuous (a jump would be an infinite field), and the normal current \(\sigma\partial_n\varphi\) is continuous (steady state: no charge accumulates, \(\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0\) on a pillbox). Apply the identity in each metal separately. Each produces a term on \(\Gamma\): \(\int_\Gamma\psi\,\sigma_1\partial_{n_1}\psi\) and \(\int_\Gamma\psi\,\sigma_2\partial_{n_2}\psi\), with \(n_1 = -n_2\). Since \(\psi\) and \(\sigma\partial_n\psi\) are continuous, the two terms are equal and opposite and cancel. What remains is \(\int_{D_1}\sigma_1|\nabla\psi|^2 + \int_{D_2}\sigma_2|\nabla\psi|^2 = 0\), and the argument concludes as before.

3. With no Dirichlet data the argument still gives \(\nabla\psi = \vec 0\), but nothing fixes the constant: \(\varphi\) is determined only up to an additive constant. Physically, an insulated conductor with no electrodes carries no steady current at all — apply the energy identity to \(\varphi\) itself: \(\int\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2 = 0\), so \(\vec E = \vec 0\) — and its potential "floats", set by whatever charge it holds (MR 23's conductor data).

4. Linearity: \((V'/V)\varphi\) satisfies the data for \(V'\), so by part 1 it is the solution, and the current, a linear functional of \(\varphi\), scales by \(V'/V\). So \(V/I\) does not depend on \(V\). Positivity: the identity applied to \(\varphi\) gives \(\int_D\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2 = VI\), and the left side is positive because \(\varphi\) takes two different values. So \(VI \gt 0\) and \(R \gt 0\). \(\square\)

Check. For the uniform wire, \(\int\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2 = \sigma(V/L)^2AL = V^2\sigma A/L = V^2/R\) with \(R = \rho L/A\) ✓, and the units of the identity are \(\mathrm{W}\) on both sides ✓.

P8 Spheres, cones, and an earth electrode medium

exercises: radial solutions of Laplace's equation, uniqueness, limits, a number

  1. Without looking at the deck, show that the part of a cone of solid angle \(\Omega\) between spherical caps of radii \(r_1 \lt r_2\), with the caps as electrodes, has \(R = \rho(1/r_1 - 1/r_2)/\Omega\).
  2. Specialise to \(\Omega = 4\pi\): the shell between two concentric spherical electrodes.
  3. A hemispherical metal electrode of radius \(r_1 = 0.5\) m is set, flat face up, into level ground of uniform resistivity \(100\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\) (both assumed for the problem). Find its resistance to "remote earth", \(r_2\to\infty\). What fraction of it lies within \(2r_1\) of the centre? Within \(10r_1\)?
  4. Show that the thin-cone limit is \(\rho L/\pi ab\) and that it reduces to \(\rho L/A\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a boundary-value problem with radial symmetry: guess a potential depending on \(r\) only, check every condition, invoke uniqueness. The rest are special cases of the formula.

Rung 2

\(\nabla^2\varphi(r) = r^{-2}(r^2\varphi')'\). The ground's surface is insulating (air above), and the hemisphere is half of the \(\Omega = 4\pi\) case: \(\Omega = 2\pi\). The resistance between radii \(r_1\) and \(r\) is the formula with \(r_2 = r\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\varphi = a + C/r\), with \(C(1/r_1 - 1/r_2) = V\); the current through any cap is \(\sigma C\Omega\).

Worked solution

Idea. Radial current between spherical electrodes; the cone's side and the ground's surface are both surfaces through the centre, so the radial field is already tangent to them.

1. \(\varphi = a + C/r\) is harmonic; the data give \(C = V/(1/r_1 - 1/r_2)\); the conical side contains \(\hat r\), so \(\partial_n\varphi = 0\) there. By uniqueness this is the solution. \(\vec J = \sigma C\hat r/r^2\), and through the cap of area \(\Omega r^2\) the current is \(I = \sigma C\Omega\). So \(R = V/I = (1/r_1 - 1/r_2)/(\sigma\Omega)\).

2. \(R = \dfrac{\rho}{4\pi}\Big(\dfrac1{r_1} - \dfrac1{r_2}\Big)\).

3. The ground fills a half-space; the vertical planes through the centre are irrelevant, and the flat surface of the ground contains \(\hat r\), so the insulating air is a Neumann boundary satisfied by the radial solution: \(\Omega = 2\pi\). With \(r_2\to\infty\), \(R = \rho/(2\pi r_1) = 100/(2\pi\times0.5) = 31.8\ \Omega\). The part within radius \(r\) is \(\frac{\rho}{2\pi}(1/r_1 - 1/r)\), a fraction \(1 - r_1/r\) of the whole: \(\tfrac12\) within \(2r_1\), \(0.9\) within \(10r_1\). Most of an earth electrode's resistance is in the soil right next to it, which is why its size, and the soil there, matter so much.

4. With half-angle \(\theta\), \(\Omega = 2\pi(1 - \cos\theta) = \pi\theta^2(1 + O(\theta^2))\); the cap radii are \(a = r_1\sin\theta\), \(b = r_2\sin\theta\); and \(1/r_1 - 1/r_2 = L/(r_1r_2)\). So \(R = \rho L/(\Omega r_1r_2) = \rho L/(\pi ab)\,(1 + O(\theta^2))\). With \(a = b\): \(\rho L/(\pi a^2) = \rho L/A\).

Check. Units: \(\Omega\,\mathrm m/\mathrm m = \Omega\) (the solid angle is a pure number) ✓. Limit: \(r_2\to r_1\) gives \(R\to0\) ✓. Energy: \(VI = \int\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2\,\mathrm dV = \int_{r_1}^{r_2}\sigma C^2r^{-4}\,\Omega r^2\,\mathrm dr = \sigma C^2\Omega(1/r_1 - 1/r_2) = VI\) ✓.

P9 A washer, current flowing outward routine

exercises: a radial Laplace problem in cylindrical symmetry, a limit, a number

  1. A flat washer of thickness \(t\), inner radius \(a\), outer radius \(b\), has electrodes on its inner and outer rims; its flat faces are insulated. Show that \(R = \rho\ln(b/a)/(2\pi t)\).
  2. Check the limit of a thin ring, \(b = a + w\) with \(w \ll a\).
  3. Evaluate for a copper washer with \(a = 5\) mm, \(b = 10\) mm, \(t = 1\) mm.
  4. Remark: a long coaxial cable's insulation, of resistivity \(\rho_{\mathrm{ins}}\), between conductor radius \(a\) and outer radius \(b\), is the same geometry rolled along its length. What is the leakage resistance of a length \(\ell\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A formula, a limit, a number. The potential depends on the distance \(s\) from the axis only.

Rung 2

In cylindrical coordinates, \(\nabla^2\varphi(s) = s^{-1}(s\varphi')'\). The current through a cylinder of radius \(s\) and height \(t\) is \(J\cdot2\pi st\).

Rung 3

First line: \(s\varphi' = -C\), so \(\varphi = a_0 - C\ln s\).

Worked solution

Idea. The two-dimensional version of P8: the radial harmonic function in the plane is the logarithm (MR 23, Worked: the constant, in one, two and three dimensions).

1. \(\varphi = a_0 - C\ln s\); with \(\varphi(a) = V\), \(\varphi(b) = 0\): \(C = V/\ln(b/a)\). The flat faces have normal \(\pm\hat z\), perpendicular to \(\nabla\varphi\), so the Neumann condition holds; uniqueness. \(J = \sigma C/s\), and \(I = (\sigma C/s)\cdot2\pi st = 2\pi\sigma Ct\), independent of \(s\) ✓. So \(R = \ln(b/a)/(2\pi\sigma t)\).

2. \(\ln(1 + w/a) = w/a + O(w^2/a^2)\), so \(R \approx \rho w/(2\pi at)\): a strip of length \(w\) (the current's path) and cross-section \(2\pi a\cdot t\), i.e. \(\rho L/A\) ✓.

3. \(R = 1.68\times10^{-8}\times\ln2/(2\pi\times10^{-3}) = 1.85\times10^{-6}\ \Omega\), under two micro-ohms.

4. Replace \(t\) by \(\ell\) and \(\rho\) by \(\rho_{\mathrm{ins}}\): \(R_{\mathrm{leak}} = \rho_{\mathrm{ins}}\ln(b/a)/(2\pi\ell)\). It falls as the cable gets longer — more insulation in parallel — so leakage is quoted as a resistance times a length. No insulation data are needed for the point, and none are quoted.

Check. Units: \(\Omega\,\mathrm m/\mathrm m = \Omega\) ✓. Energy: \(\int\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2 = \int_a^b\sigma C^2s^{-2}\,2\pi st\,\mathrm ds = 2\pi\sigma tC^2\ln(b/a) = V^2/R\) ✓.

P10 The charge at the element medium

exercises: jump conditions (MR 23), continuity of \(J_n\), the sign of surface charge, a number

Inside the kettle, a copper lead (\(\rho_1 = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\)) joins the element's resistance wire. Take \(\rho_2 = 1.10\times10^{-6}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\) — a representative value for a nickel–chromium resistance alloy, assumed for the problem — and suppose the current density crossing the interface is the cable's, \(J = 4.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\).

  1. Derive the surface charge on the interface and evaluate it.
  2. How many electron charges is that per square millimetre? Compare with the number of atoms on a square millimetre of copper surface, roughly \(n^{2/3}\).
  3. What sign is it where the current enters the element, and where it leaves?
  4. Check your \(\Sigma\) against the field jump directly.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A surface charge density, in \(\mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}\), from two pillbox arguments: one for \(\vec J\), one for \(\vec E\).

Rung 2

In the steady state \(J_n\) is the same on both sides; \(E_n = \rho J_n\) is not. MR 23's jump condition turns the jump in \(E_n\) into \(\Sigma\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\Sigma = \varepsilon_0(E_{n,2} - E_{n,1}) = \varepsilon_0J(\rho_2 - \rho_1)\), with \(\hat n\) pointing from copper into the element.

Worked solution

Idea. Equal current through unequal resistivities needs unequal fields, and a jump in the normal field is a sheet of charge.

1. \(\Sigma = 8.854\times10^{-12}\times4.0\times10^6\times(1.10\times10^{-6} - 1.68\times10^{-8}) = 3.8\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}\).

2. \(3.836\times10^{-11}/1.602\times10^{-19} = 2.39\times10^8\) per square metre, i.e. about 240 per square millimetre. A square millimetre of copper surface has about \(n^{2/3}\times10^{-6} = 1.9\times10^{13}\) atoms, so the surface charge is one electron's worth for every \(8\times10^{10}\) atoms: an imperceptible rearrangement does the steering.

3. Where the current enters the element (\(\rho\) rises along \(\vec J\)), \(\Sigma \gt 0\); where it leaves back into copper, \(\rho_2 - \rho_1\) changes sign relative to the direction of flow and \(\Sigma \lt 0\), equal in size if the same current density crosses both junctions: the two sheets are equal and opposite.

4. \(E_1 = \rho_1J = 0.0672\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\), \(E_2 = \rho_2J = 4.40\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\); \(\Sigma/\varepsilon_0 = 4.333\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}} = E_2 - E_1\) ✓.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}\cdot A\,m^{-2}\cdot\Omega\,m} = \mathrm{(F\,\Omega)\,A\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{s\,A\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}\) ✓. Limit: \(\rho_1 = \rho_2\) gives \(\Sigma = 0\) ✓ — no interface, no charge. On 50 Hz mains the sheet reverses with the current; P11 shows it can follow far faster than that.

P11 Charge relaxation as an oscillator hard

exercises: continuity and Gauss with the Drude equation, MR 06's damped oscillator, dominant balance in a limit

  1. Derive \(\ddot\varrho + \dot\varrho/\tau + \omega_p^2\varrho = 0\), \(\omega_p^2 = ne^2/\varepsilon_0m\), for a small excess charge density inside a metal, stating each approximation.
  2. Evaluate \(\omega_p\) and \(Q = \omega_p\tau\) for copper, and describe the motion.
  3. The naive calculation with \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) gives decay in \(t_c = \varepsilon_0/\sigma\). Show that \(t_c/\tau = 1/Q^2\), and explain why the naive answer is meaningless for copper.
  4. Show that when \(Q \ll \tfrac12\) the slower root of the characteristic equation is \(-1/t_c\) to leading order: the naive answer is the overdamped limit. What does that say about when \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) may be used?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a derivation ending in a second-order linear ODE; parts 2–4 are about its characteristic roots.

Rung 2

Turn the relaxation-time equation into one for \(\vec J\) by multiplying by \(-ne/m\). Then take a divergence: continuity converts \(\nabla\cdot\vec J\) into \(-\dot\varrho\), and Gauss converts \(\nabla\cdot\vec E\) into \(\varrho/\varepsilon_0\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\partial_t\vec J = -\vec J/\tau + (ne^2/m)\vec E\); its divergence is \(-\ddot\varrho = \dot\varrho/\tau + (ne^2/\varepsilon_0m)\varrho\).

Worked solution

Idea. Replace Ohm's law by the equation it is the steady state of. The electrons then have inertia as well as friction, and a charge imbalance pulls them back with a force proportional to the imbalance: a damped oscillator.

1. Approximations: (a) the excess \(\varrho\) is small beside \(ne\), so \(\vec J = -ne\vec p/m\) to first order (the current \(\varrho\vec p/m\) carried by the excess is second order); (b) the disturbance varies slowly over a mean free path, so the relaxation-time equation holds locally; (c) the electrons' pressure is neglected. Then \(\partial_t\vec J = -\vec J/\tau + (ne^2/m)\vec E\); its divergence, with \(\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial_t\varrho\) and \(\nabla\cdot\vec E = \varrho/\varepsilon_0\), is \(-\ddot\varrho = \dot\varrho/\tau + \omega_p^2\varrho\). \(\square\)

2. \(\omega_p = \sqrt{2.174\times10^{-9}/8.065\times10^{-42}} = 1.64\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\); \(Q = 1.642\times10^{16}\times2.494\times10^{-14} = 410\). Strongly underdamped (MR 06): the charge oscillates at almost exactly \(\omega_p\), about \(2.6\times10^{15}\) times a second, inside an envelope \(e^{-t/2\tau}\), falling by \(e\) every \(2\tau = 5.0\times10^{-14}\) s, about 130 oscillations.

3. \(t_c = \varepsilon_0/\sigma = \varepsilon_0m/(ne^2\tau) = 1/(\omega_p^2\tau)\), so \(t_c/\tau = 1/(\omega_p\tau)^2 = 1/Q^2 = 6.0\times10^{-6}\); \(t_c = 1.5\times10^{-19}\) s. Ohm's law is the relaxation-time equation's steady state, reached only after a few \(\tau\) (P4); a decay over \(10^{-5}\tau\) is entirely inside the transient, where \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) is false. The calculation contradicts its own premise.

4. Roots of \(s^2 + s/\tau + \omega_p^2 = 0\): \(s_\pm = \frac{1}{2\tau}\big(-1 \pm \sqrt{1 - 4Q^2}\big)\). For \(Q \ll \tfrac12\), \(\sqrt{1 - 4Q^2} = 1 - 2Q^2 + O(Q^4)\), so \(s_+ = -Q^2/\tau\,(1 + O(Q^2)) = -\omega_p^2\tau = -\sigma/\varepsilon_0 = -1/t_c\), while \(s_- \approx -1/\tau\). The slow mode decays in \(t_c = \tau/Q^2 \gg \tau\): slow beside the collision time, so Ohm's law has time to hold, and the naive answer is right. The rule: \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) may be used for processes slow compared with \(\tau\), and never otherwise.

Check. Units: \(\omega_p^2\) is \(\mathrm{m^{-3}C^2}/(\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}\,kg}) = \mathrm{C^2}/(\mathrm{F\,kg\,m^2}) = \mathrm{C\,V}/(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}) = \mathrm{s^{-2}}\) ✓ (\(\mathrm{C\,V} = \mathrm J = \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}}\)). Limit \(\tau\to\infty\): an undamped oscillation at \(\omega_p\), the collisionless plasma ✓.

P12 The Hall effect in the Drude model medium

exercises: the relaxation-time equation with a magnetic force, a steady state with a constraint, a number

A strip carries current density \(J_x\) in a uniform \(\vec B = B\hat z\); no current can leave its edges, so \(J_y = 0\).

  1. Including the force \(-e\vec v\times\vec B\), write the relaxation-time equation for the mean momentum and find its steady state. Show that \(E_y = R_HJ_xB\) with \(R_H = -1/ne\), and that \(E_x = J_x/\sigma_0\) is unchanged by \(B\).
  2. A copper strip 0.1 mm thick carries 10 A in a field of 1 T perpendicular to it. Find the Hall voltage across it, and show it does not depend on the strip's width.
  3. Explain why measuring \(R_H\) measures the carriers' sign while measuring \(\sigma\) cannot, and state what some metals do that this model cannot explain.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a pair of linear equations for the steady components; part 2 is a voltage, a few microvolts.

Rung 2

Averaging \(-e\vec v\times\vec B\) gives \(-(e/m)\vec p\times\vec B\). With \(J_y = 0\), \(p_y = 0\). The Hall voltage across a strip of width \(w\) is \(E_yw\), and \(J_x = I/(wt)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(0 = -eE_x - \omega_cp_y - p_x/\tau\) and \(0 = -eE_y + \omega_cp_x - p_y/\tau\), with \(\omega_c = eB/m\).

Worked solution

Idea. The magnetic force on the drift pushes the carriers sideways until the edges' charge makes a field that cancels it; in the steady state the sideways current is zero.

1. \(\dot{\vec p} = -e(\vec E + \vec p\times\vec B/m) - \vec p/\tau\). With \(\vec B = B\hat z\), \(\vec p\times\hat z = (p_y, -p_x, 0)\), so in the steady state, writing \(\omega_c = eB/m\): \[ 0 = -eE_x - \omega_cp_y - p_x/\tau,\qquad 0 = -eE_y + \omega_cp_x - p_y/\tau . \] \(J_y = 0\) means \(p_y = 0\). The first equation gives \(p_x = -e\tau E_x\), i.e. \(J_x = \sigma_0E_x\), unchanged by \(B\). The second gives \(eE_y = \omega_cp_x\), so \(E_y = (B/m)p_x = -(B/ne)J_x\) using \(J_x = -nep_x/m\). Hence \(R_H = E_y/(J_xB) = -1/ne\).

2. \(V_H = E_yw = (1/ne)\,(I/wt)\,B\,w = IB/(net)\): the width cancels. Numerically \(10\times1/(1.357\times10^{10}\times10^{-4}) = 7.4\times10^{-6}\) V, 7.4 μV. For copper \(R_H = -7.37\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^3\,C^{-1}}\).

3. \(\sigma = nq^2\tau/m\) contains the charge squared, so it is blind to the sign; \(R_H = 1/(nq)\) contains it to the first power. Positive carriers moving with the current are pushed to the same edge as negative carriers moving against it, but charge that edge with the opposite sign, reversing \(E_y\). For some metals the measured \(R_H\) is positive (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1), which classical electrons cannot produce; band theory explains it and is outside this course.

Check. Units: \(IB/(net)\) is \(\mathrm{A\,T}/(\mathrm{C\,m^{-3}\cdot m}) = \mathrm{A\,T\,m^2/C} = \mathrm{T\,m^2\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm V\) ✓ (a tesla is \(\mathrm{V\,s\,m^{-2}}\)). Limit \(B\to0\): \(E_y\to0\) ✓. That \(E_x\) does not depend on \(B\) — no magnetoresistance — is itself a prediction of this model.

P13 Wiedemann–Franz, and two errors that cancel medium

exercises: dimensional bookkeeping, a cited formula used carefully, why a right answer can be luck

Take as given (kinetic theory; Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1) that a gas of carriers with heat capacity per unit volume \(c_v\), mean square speed \(\langle v^2\rangle\) and collision time \(\tau\) conducts heat with \(\kappa = \tfrac13c_v\langle v^2\rangle\tau\).

  1. With Drude's classical values \(c_v = \tfrac32nk_B\) and \(\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac32k_BT\), show \(\kappa/(\sigma T) = \tfrac32(k_B/e)^2\) and evaluate it. Drude's own calculation, through a factor-2 error (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1), gave twice this; evaluate that too.
  2. Sommerfeld's theory (Zeitschrift für Physik 47, 1, 1928) replaces \(\langle v^2\rangle\) by \(v_F^2\) and \(c_v\) by \(\tfrac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{k_BT}{E_F}nk_B\) with \(E_F = \tfrac12mv_F^2\) (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 2, cited). For copper at 293 K, by what factor is each classical ingredient wrong?
  3. Show that the product of the two corrections is exactly \(2\pi^2/9\), whatever \(T\) and \(v_F\), and hence find Sommerfeld's ratio.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A ratio of two transport coefficients, in which \(\tau\) and \(n\) cancel; then two correction factors and their product.

Rung 2

Divide \(\kappa\) by \(\sigma T = ne^2\tau T/m\); \(\tau\) goes. In part 3 write both corrections in terms of \(k_BT/E_F\) and watch it cancel.

Rung 3

First line: \(\dfrac{\kappa}{\sigma T} = \dfrac{\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac32nk_B\cdot\tfrac{3k_BT}{m}\cdot\tau}{ne^2\tau T/m}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The ratio of the heat and charge conductivities removes everything the model does not know (\(\tau\)) and leaves the product \(c_v\langle v^2\rangle\) — which the classical theory gets wrong twice, in opposite directions.

1. The first line simplifies to \(\tfrac32k_B^2/e^2\). With \(k_B/e = 8.618\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{V\,K^{-1}}\): \(\tfrac32\times7.427\times10^{-9} = 1.11\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}\); twice that is \(2.23\times10^{-8}\).

2. Speeds: \((v_F)^2/(3k_BT/m) = (1.57\times10^6/1.154\times10^5)^2 = 185\): the classical \(\langle v^2\rangle\) is 185 times too small. Heat capacity: \(E_F = \tfrac12\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times(1.57\times10^6)^2 = 1.12\times10^{-18}\) J \(= 7.0\) eV, and \(k_BT = 0.0253\) eV at 293 K; the ratio of Sommerfeld's \(c_v\) to the classical one is \(\frac{\pi^2}{3}\frac{k_BT}{E_F} = 0.0119\): the classical \(c_v\) is 84 times too large.

3. The product of corrections is \(\frac{\pi^2}{3}\frac{k_BT}{E_F}\cdot\frac{v_F^2}{3k_BT/m} = \frac{\pi^2}{3}\frac{k_BT}{E_F}\cdot\frac{2E_F}{3k_BT} = \frac{2\pi^2}{9} = 2.19\), independent of \(T\) and \(v_F\) (numerically \(185/84.35 = 2.19\) ✓). Sommerfeld's ratio is \(\tfrac32(k_B/e)^2\times\tfrac{2\pi^2}9 = \tfrac{\pi^2}3(k_B/e)^2 = 2.44\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}\). Two errors of about a hundred cancel to within a factor of about two, and Drude's factor-of-two slip happened to close most of the remaining gap: right for the wrong reasons.

Check. Units: \((k_B/e)^2\) is \(\mathrm{(J\,K^{-1}/C)^2} = \mathrm{V^2\,K^{-2}}\), and \(\mathrm{W\,\Omega} = \mathrm{V^2}\) ✓. The \(T\)-independence of the product is what makes the law a law: both theories predict a ratio that is the same for all metals and temperatures, which is the regularity the Wiedemann–Franz law states (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1).
