Conduction: Ohm's law from a model, and what the model gets wrong

A wire carrying a current is a conductor out of equilibrium. Classical electrons with random collisions give Ohm's law as a linear response, Joule heating as the field's work, resistance as a Laplace problem, and the surface charge that steers the field — and the same model says honestly where it fails.

needs MR 23, MR 24~2 h slides + 3½ h problemsproblems: ./cap problems 00

Mechanics to Relativity left you with the electrostatics of conductors: in equilibrium the field inside a conductor is zero, the charge sits on its surface, and the potential is constant over it (MR 23, Conductors). A wire carrying a current is not in equilibrium — charges move, so there is a field inside — and nobody has yet told you what happens then. This unit does. It is the first unit of the course, and everything later rests on two of its results: the local law $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$, and the fact that the field which drives a current is held in place by charge on the conductors' surfaces.

One cable, and a puzzle about speed

00 · what moves

The kettle cable, from nothing

A kettle heats water with an element: a coil of resistance wire, sealed in a metal sheath, which grows hot when a current passes through it. It reaches the mains through a cable: two copper conductors, each wrapped in plastic insulation, inside an outer sheath. Current goes out along one conductor (the live), through the element, and back along the other (the neutral).

European mains alternates at 50 Hz (IEC 60038), and "230 V" and "10 A" are RMS values:

RMS value

For $i(t) = \sqrt2\,I\cos\omega t$, the mean of $i^2$ over a period is $I^2$, because the mean of $\cos^2$ is $\tfrac12$. Heating goes as $i^2$ (section 3), so $I$ is the steady current that heats a wire at the same mean rate.

Until unit 06 develops AC power, treat the 10 A as steady; section 2 shows that at 50 Hz the copper cannot tell the difference.

The cable in section, and the run it makes. A sketch, not a circuit diagram: those start in unit 02.

Assumed for the example. The kettle is rated 2.3 kW at 230 V and draws 10 A; its cable has solid copper conductors of $2.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}$ (a stranded conductor would change nothing here but the cross-section's shape); the run from the fuse box is 10 m. These are representative values, not a product's specification. That $2.3\ \mathrm{kW} = 230\ \mathrm V\times 10\ \mathrm A$ is the statement $p = vi$, which unit 01 proves; here the three numbers are simply taken as given and consistent. The supply itself — 230 V between live and neutral, alternating at 50 Hz — is one of the nominal values listed in IEC 60038, IEC standard voltages, as 230/400 V for 50 Hz systems.

The third conductor. Many cables carry a third conductor, the protective conductor of IEC 60364-5-54 (Earthing arrangements and protective conductors). It is there for faults, not for the kettle's current, and plays no part in this unit.

Why only one line about AC. The mains voltage alternates, so the current does too: $i(t) = \sqrt2\cdot10\ \mathrm A\cdot\cos(2\pi\cdot50\ \mathrm{Hz}\cdot t)$, peaking at 14.1 A. Every result in this unit is about how copper responds to a field, and section 2 proves (Alternating fields: σ(ω)) that the response at 50 Hz is the DC response to about one part in $10^{11}$: the current lags the field by $\omega\tau = 7.8\times10^{-12}$ rad, and its size differs by under one part in $10^{22}$. So each DC result holds instant by instant, and a mean over a cycle replaces $i^2$ by $I^2$.

The mean of $\cos^2$. $\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 + \cos2\theta)$, and $\cos2\theta$ averages to zero over a period. So $\overline{i^2} = 2I^2\cdot\tfrac12 = I^2$: the RMS ("root mean square") value is $\sqrt{\overline{i^2}} = I$.

00 · what moves

Current density, from carriers

current density (MR 23)

$\vec J$, in $\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$: the current through a surface $S$ is $I = \int_S\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A$.

carriers make a current

If the mobile charges have charge $q$, number density $n$ and mean velocity $\vec v_{\mathrm d}$ — the drift velocity — then

$$\vec J = nq\,\vec v_{\mathrm d} .$$
proof

Take carriers of one velocity $\vec v$. Those crossing a patch $\mathrm d\vec A$ in time $\mathrm dt$ are the ones in the slanted cylinder of volume $\vec v\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\,\mathrm dt$ behind it (the figure). Their charge is $q\,n_{\vec v}\,\vec v\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\,\mathrm dt$. Sum over velocities: $\sum n_{\vec v}\vec v = n\vec v_{\mathrm d}$. Divide by $\mathrm dt$. $\square$

Dimensions: $\mathrm{m^{-3}\cdot C\cdot m\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{C\,s^{-1}\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$ ✓.

Everything inside the slanted cylinder crosses the patch within $\mathrm dt$; nothing outside it does.

Why only the mean velocity counts. The carriers in a metal move fast and in every direction (section 7 puts a number on it). Group them by velocity: $n_{\vec v}$ carriers per unit volume with velocity near $\vec v$. Each group contributes $q\,n_{\vec v}\vec v$ to $\vec J$, and the total is $q\sum n_{\vec v}\vec v = qn\langle\vec v\rangle$. The random part of the motion has mean zero, so it carries charge across a surface equally often in both directions and contributes nothing net. What is left is the drift: $\vec v_{\mathrm d} := \langle\vec v\rangle$.

The slanted cylinder. Its base is the patch, its side is $\vec v\,\mathrm dt$, so its volume is base times height, $|\mathrm d\vec A|\,(\vec v\cdot\hat n)\,\mathrm dt = \vec v\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\,\mathrm dt$. The sign takes care of carriers crossing backwards. This is the same argument as the flux of a fluid through a surface in MR 05.

Two kinds of carrier. If several species are present (in an electrolyte, positive and negative ions), the contributions add: $\vec J = \sum_k n_kq_k\vec v_{\mathrm d,k}$. In a metal only the conduction electrons move; the ions are fixed in the lattice.

Worked: the drift speed in the kettle cable

the kettle cable: $I = 10$ A, $A = 2.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}$

1. Atoms per cubic metre. Copper: density $8.96\times10^3\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, molar mass $0.06355\ \mathrm{kg\,mol^{-1}}$ (CRC). So $1\ \mathrm{m^3}$ holds $8.96\times10^3/0.06355 = 1.410\times10^5$ mol, and the number of atoms in it is

$$1.410\times10^5\times6.022\times10^{23} = 8.49\times10^{28}.$$

2. One conduction electron per atom, copper's single outermost electron: $n \approx 8.49\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$. Ashcroft & Mermin's table 1.1 gives $8.47\times10^{28}$, within 0.24%. We use $8.47\times10^{28}$.

3. Current density. $J = I/A = 10/(2.5\times10^{-6}) = 4.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$.

4. Drift speed. Electrons have $q = -e$, so $|v_{\mathrm d}| = J/ne$, with $ne = 8.47\times10^{28}\times1.602\times10^{-19} = 1.357\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{C\,m^{-3}}$:

$$v_{\mathrm d} = \frac{4.0\times10^6}{1.357\times10^{10}} = 2.95\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.$$

About 0.29 mm/s. Units: $\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}/(\mathrm{m^{-3}\,C}) = \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ ✓.

5. An hour per metre. $1/v_{\mathrm d} = 3392\ \mathrm s = 57$ min.

Check. The value of $ne$ means each cubic metre of copper holds 13.6 billion coulombs of mobile charge. With that much to move, a tiny speed is enough for 10 A.

Every step. $8.96\times10^3/0.06355 = 1.4099\times10^5$ mol per cubic metre; times Avogadro's number $N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ (CODATA; exact in the SI) gives $8.491\times10^{28}$ atoms per cubic metre. The ratio to Ashcroft & Mermin's $8.47\times10^{28}$ is $1.0024$. Then $ne = 8.47\times10^{28}\times1.602\times10^{-19} = 1.3569\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{C\,m^{-3}}$ and $4.0\times10^6/1.3569\times10^{10} = 2.948\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.

"One conduction electron per atom". Copper's atom has one electron outside closed shells, and Ashcroft & Mermin's table 1.1 lists copper's valence as 1 and computes $n$ exactly as in steps 1–2. That this one electron is free to move through the metal while the rest stay bound is the model's assumption, which section 7 revisits; the Hall effect (problem P12) measures the carrier density independently.

The density's source. Copper's density $8.96\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}$ and molar mass $63.55\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}$ are the CRC Handbook's values at room temperature.

A misreading to avoid. "0.29 mm/s" is the mean velocity of the electrons, not their speed: each one is moving far faster, in a random direction (section 7). The drift is a tiny bias on a fast random motion.

Electrons drift against the current

the sign

$\vec J$ points the way positive charge would move. A metal's carriers are electrons, $q = -e$, so

$$\vec J = -ne\,\vec v_{\mathrm d}:$$

the electrons drift against the current, and against the field that drives it. Nothing physical depends on the convention; every sign in this course follows it.

on 50 Hz mains: they do not even arrive

With $i(t) = \sqrt2\,I\cos\omega t$ the drift is $\sqrt2\,v_{\mathrm d}\cos\omega t$, and an electron's mean position moves by

$$\frac{\sqrt2\,v_{\mathrm d}}{\omega} = \frac{4.17\times10^{-4}}{2\pi\times50} = 1.3\ \mu\mathrm m$$

either way, then comes back. The electrons in the element were already in the element.

The field and the current point one way; the electrons drift the other. Each electron's own motion is fast and random; only its mean is drawn.

The convention. The direction of a current is defined as the direction in which positive charge would move, whatever the carriers actually are, and the sign is put in the carriers: $q = -e$ with $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ C > 0. The equation $\vec J = -ne\vec v_{\mathrm d}$ is then exact, and the field, the current density and the force on a positive test charge all point the same way inside a wire.

The 50 Hz shuffle. The drift velocity follows the current instant by instant (section 2 proves this): $v(t) = \sqrt2\,v_{\mathrm d}\cos\omega t$ with $v_{\mathrm d} = 2.948\times10^{-4}$ m/s, so the peak drift is $4.169\times10^{-4}$ m/s. Integrating, the mean position is $x(t) = (\sqrt2\,v_{\mathrm d}/\omega)\sin\omega t$, of amplitude $4.169\times10^{-4}/314.16 = 1.327\times10^{-6}$ m. So the electron cloud oscillates through about 2.7 μm peak to peak, some ten thousand nearest-neighbour distances (0.256 nm, section 7), and goes nowhere. The puzzle is sharper on AC than on DC: the energy reaches the kettle, and no electron does.

00 · Drude

Drude's hypotheses

the Drude model (Drude, 1900)
  1. The conduction electrons are classical point particles of charge $-e$ and mass $m$. Between collisions each moves freely under the applied field alone.
  2. Collisions are instantaneous. In any interval $\mathrm dt$ an electron collides with probability $\mathrm dt/\tau$, whatever its past. The constant $\tau$ is the relaxation time.
  3. Just after a collision an electron's velocity is random, with mean zero.

Nothing here says what the electrons collide with, or how fast they move between collisions. The first of those Drude guessed wrong (section 7). Neither enters Ohm's law.

The free time between collisions has mean $\tau$: the probability of none in time $t$ obeys $P' = -P/\tau$, so $P = e^{-t/\tau}$.

One electron, the same random collisions (dots) twice; the force is hugely exaggerated. With the field each free flight is a parabola bending against $\vec E$, and the walk drifts left.

History. Drude published the model in 1900, in Annalen der Physik, by applying the kinetic theory of gases to a gas of electrons moving through a lattice of heavy, fixed positive ions (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1, whose account this section follows). He pictured the collisions as electrons bouncing off the ions. That picture is wrong, as section 7 shows; the mathematics built on it is not.

The independence assumption in hypothesis 2 — a collision probability $\mathrm dt/\tau$ per interval, whatever happened before — makes collisions a Poisson process. Let $P(t)$ be the probability of no collision in $[0, t]$. Then $P(t + \mathrm dt) = P(t)(1 - \mathrm dt/\tau)$, so $P' = -P/\tau$ and $P = e^{-t/\tau}$ (MR 00's simplest ODE). The free time is exponentially distributed with density $e^{-t/\tau}/\tau$, whose mean is $\int_0^\infty t\,e^{-t/\tau}\,\mathrm dt/\tau = \tau$. So $\tau$ is the mean free time, and also the mean time back to the last collision from any moment.

Hypothesis 3, more fully. Drude assumed that collisions keep the electrons in thermal equilibrium with the lattice: an electron emerges with a random direction and a speed appropriate to the local temperature. Only the "mean zero" part is used for the current; the temperature part is used for the energy budget on The field's work is J·E.

The figure is a simulation: 16 free flights with exponentially distributed durations and random directions at fixed speed, drawn once with no field and once with a constant acceleration added to the left. The acceleration was chosen huge so that the drift shows in a dozen flights; in the cable the drift per flight is about $10^{-10}$ of the flight.

The relaxation-time equation

the relaxation-time equation

Let $\vec p(t)$ be the mean momentum per electron and $\vec f = -e\vec E$ the force on each. Then

$$\frac{\mathrm d\vec p}{\mathrm dt} = -\frac{\vec p}{\tau} + \vec f .$$
proof, in one line

In $\mathrm dt$, a fraction $1 - \mathrm dt/\tau$ of the electrons do not collide and each gains $\vec f\,\mathrm dt$; the fraction $\mathrm dt/\tau$ that do collide restart with mean momentum zero, plus at most $\vec f\,\mathrm dt$. So $\vec p(t + \mathrm dt) = (1 - \mathrm dt/\tau)(\vec p + \vec f\,\mathrm dt) + O(\mathrm dt^2)$. Full proof in the notes.

Collisions act as a friction on the mean motion, with rate $1/\tau$, though each collision is random. Units: $\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}/\mathrm s = \mathrm N$ ✓. Check: $\vec E = \vec 0$ gives $\vec p \propto e^{-t/\tau}$ — collisions erase any net momentum.

The field is switched on at $t = 0$. One electron's momentum is a ragged sawtooth; the mean over many is smooth and obeys the relaxation-time equation.

Full proof

Fix a time $t$ and a small $\mathrm dt$. Split the electrons into those that collide in $(t, t + \mathrm dt]$ and those that do not. By hypothesis 2 the first group is a fraction $\mathrm dt/\tau$ of the whole, chosen independently of the electrons' momenta, so the mean momentum of each group at time $t$ is $\vec p(t)$.

  • An electron that does not collide moves freely, so its momentum changes by $\int_t^{t+\mathrm dt}\vec f = \vec f\,\mathrm dt + O(\mathrm dt^2)$. This group's mean momentum at $t + \mathrm dt$ is $\vec p(t) + \vec f\,\mathrm dt + O(\mathrm dt^2)$.
  • An electron that does collide, at some $t' \in (t, t + \mathrm dt]$, leaves with a random momentum of mean zero (hypothesis 3), then moves freely for $t + \mathrm dt - t' \le \mathrm dt$, gaining at most $|\vec f|\,\mathrm dt$. This group's mean momentum at $t + \mathrm dt$ is $O(\mathrm dt)$.

Weighting by the fractions: $\vec p(t + \mathrm dt) = (1 - \tfrac{\mathrm dt}\tau)\big(\vec p(t) + \vec f\,\mathrm dt\big) + \tfrac{\mathrm dt}\tau\,O(\mathrm dt) + O(\mathrm dt^2) = \vec p(t) - \tfrac{\mathrm dt}\tau\vec p(t) + \vec f\,\mathrm dt + O(\mathrm dt^2)$. Subtract $\vec p(t)$, divide by $\mathrm dt$, let $\mathrm dt\to0$. $\square$ This is Ashcroft & Mermin's derivation in ch. 1, written out.

What was assumed about the force. Only that it is the same for every electron near a point and bounded over $\mathrm dt$. So the equation holds for any field that varies slowly on the scale of the distance an electron moves between collisions, including a time-dependent one: that is what lets the next slides use it at 50 Hz and at terahertz. A magnetic force $-e\vec v\times\vec B$ depends on each electron's velocity, and averaging gives $-e\langle\vec v\rangle\times\vec B = -(e/m)\vec p\times\vec B$ — problem P12 uses that for the Hall effect.

The heavy curve is the exact mean, $-eE\tau(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, derived on Switching on.

Ohm's law, out of the model

Steady state, $\mathrm d\vec p/\mathrm dt = 0$: $\ \vec p = -e\tau\vec E$, so $\vec v_{\mathrm d} = -\dfrac{e\tau}{m}\vec E$ and $\vec J = -ne\vec v_{\mathrm d}$.

Ohm's law, local form $$\vec J = \sigma\vec E,\qquad \sigma = \frac{ne^2\tau}{m}.$$

Units: $\mathrm{m^{-3}\,C^2\,s\,kg^{-1}}$. And $\mathrm{A\,V^{-1}\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{C\,s^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}\,m^{-2}\,s^2}\cdot\mathrm{m^{-1}}$, the same ✓ (a volt is $\mathrm{J\,C^{-1}} = \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}\,C^{-1}}$).

conductivity and resistivity

$\sigma$ in $\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$, where the siemens is $\mathrm S = \mathrm{A\,V^{-1}}$; resistivity $\rho = 1/\sigma$ in $\Omega\,\mathrm m$, where the ohm is $\Omega = \mathrm{V\,A^{-1}}$. Copper at 20 °C: $\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$ (CRC), $\ \sigma = 5.95\times10^7\ \mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}$.

notation, changed from MR 23

MR 23 wrote $\sigma$ for a surface charge density and $\rho$ for a charge density. In this course, as in Griffiths (§7.1) and Ashcroft & Mermin, $\sigma$ is conductivity and $\rho$ resistivity. Charge densities become

$$\varrho\ \ (\mathrm{C\,m^{-3}}),\qquad \Sigma\ \ (\mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}).$$
what the formula says

More carriers, longer between collisions, lighter carriers: more current for the same field. The charge enters squared — once in the force, once in the current — so the sign of the carriers cannot be read off $\sigma$ (the Hall effect can: section 7).

$\tau$ is not predicted. Measure $\rho$, and the model returns $\tau = m/ne^2\rho$.

The steady state of the relaxation-time equation with constant $\vec E$ is its fixed point: $0 = -\vec p/\tau - e\vec E$. Every solution approaches it (Switching on), so "steady" is not an extra assumption, only a wait of a few $\tau$.

Why "local". $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ relates two fields at one point. The familiar $V = IR$ is its integrated form for a whole conductor, and needs a boundary-value problem to get from one to the other: section 4. Griffiths, §7.1, makes the same distinction and calls $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ Ohm's law.

The dimensional check, slowly. $[ne^2\tau/m] = \mathrm{m^{-3}}\cdot\mathrm{C^2}\cdot\mathrm s/\mathrm{kg}$. A siemens per metre is an ampere per volt per metre. An ampere is $\mathrm{C\,s^{-1}}$; one over a volt is $\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}\,m^{-2}\,s^2}$; times $\mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Multiply: $\mathrm C^2\,\mathrm s\,\mathrm{kg^{-1}}\,\mathrm m^{-3}$. Equal.

Is the response linear in a real metal? In the model it is linear exactly, because the equation of motion is. What makes the linearity believable beyond the model is how small the push is: the drift is a minute bias on a fast random motion (next slide), so whatever the true response is, its first term in the small push is all that can be seen. This is what linear response means.

Resistivity varies with temperature, rising as copper warms (the CRC Handbook tabulates it). All values in this unit are at 20 °C.

00 · Drude

A linear response to a gentle push

Picture every electron's velocity as a point in velocity space. With no field the cloud of points is centred on the origin. A field shifts the whole cloud by $\vec v_{\mathrm d}$, and the current is the shift.

linear response

A response proportional to the push that causes it. Ohm's law is linear because the shift is tiny against the cloud's width: only the first term of any expansion in the push survives.

How tiny? The cloud's width is set by the electrons' random speed. In a metal that is the Fermi speed, a quantum result: for copper $v_F = 1.57\times10^6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ (Ashcroft & Mermin, table 2.1). In the cable,

$$\frac{v_{\mathrm d}}{v_F} = \frac{2.95\times10^{-4}}{1.57\times10^6} = 1.9\times10^{-10}.$$

The shift is drawn some $2\times10^9$ times too large. At true scale, on circles a kilometre in radius, it would be 0.19 μm.

What the figure claims. In the steady state, the distribution of electron velocities is the no-field distribution displaced by the drift. In the Drude model that is exactly true of the mean (it is the definition of $\vec v_{\mathrm d}$); that the whole cloud moves rigidly is the simplest picture consistent with it. Nothing below depends on more than the mean.

The Fermi speed is cited, not derived. Electrons obey the Pauli exclusion principle: no two can occupy the same quantum state, so even at absolute zero most of them must move fast. Sommerfeld worked out the consequences for metals in 1928 (Zeitschrift für Physik 47, 1; the theory is Ashcroft & Mermin's ch. 2). The speed of the fastest electrons, $v_F$, is $1.57\times10^8\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}$ for copper in their table 2.1. Section 7 shows why it does not change $\sigma = ne^2\tau/m$ but does change what $\tau$ means.

The exaggeration. The drawn shift is $34/92 = 0.37$ of the radius; the true one is $1.9\times10^{-10}$ of it, so the drawing exaggerates by about $2\times10^9$. A kilometre times $1.9\times10^{-10}$ is 0.19 μm, under a thousand atomic spacings: no drawing could show it.

Worked: τ for copper, and the field in the cable

the cable's copper at 20 °C

1. τ from the measured resistivity. Invert $\sigma = ne^2\tau/m$: $\ \tau = m/ne^2\rho$. Here $ne^2 = 8.47\times10^{28}\times(1.602\times10^{-19})^2 = 2.174\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C^2\,m^{-3}}$, and times $\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}$ it is $3.652\times10^{-17}$. So

$$\tau = \frac{9.109\times10^{-31}}{3.652\times10^{-17}} = 2.49\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm s .$$

Units: $\mathrm{kg}/(\mathrm{m^{-3}\,C^2\cdot\Omega\,m})$, and $\Omega = \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}\,C^{-2}}$, so $\mathrm{kg}/(\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}) = \mathrm s$ ✓.

2. The field in the copper. $E = \rho J = 1.68\times10^{-8}\times4.0\times10^6 = 0.0672\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$: 67 mV per metre of conductor.

3. The drift again, from the model. $e\tau E = 1.602\times10^{-19}\times2.494\times10^{-14}\times0.0672 = 2.685\times10^{-34}$, so

$$v_{\mathrm d} = \frac{e\tau E}{m} = 2.95\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},$$

agreeing with the count of the first worked example ✓ — as it must, since $\tau$ was fitted to the same $\rho$.

4. How gentle, how fast. $v_{\mathrm d}/v_F = 1.9\times10^{-10}$; and the drift reaches 99% of its steady value within $\tau\ln100 = 1.1\times10^{-13}$ s of the field appearing (next slide).

The arithmetic. $e^2 = 2.5664\times10^{-38}\ \mathrm{C^2}$; $ne^2 = 8.47\times10^{28}\times2.5664\times10^{-38} = 2.1737\times10^{-9}$; times $1.68\times10^{-8}$ is $3.6519\times10^{-17}$; and $9.109\times10^{-31}/3.6519\times10^{-17} = 2.4943\times10^{-14}$ s. The ohm in base units. $\Omega = \mathrm{V\,A^{-1}} = (\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}\,C^{-1}})(\mathrm{s\,C^{-1}}) = \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}\,C^{-2}}$. Then $\mathrm{m^{-3}\,C^2}\cdot\Omega\,\mathrm m = \mathrm{m^{-3}\,C^2\cdot kg\,m^3\,s^{-1}\,C^{-2}} = \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}$.

Switching on

Switch on a constant $\vec E$ at $t = 0$, with $\vec p(0) = \vec 0$. The relaxation-time equation is first-order linear with constant coefficients (MR 00; unique by MR 01), and its solution is

$$\vec p(t) = -e\tau\vec E\,\big(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\big).$$
check

$\dot{\vec p} = -e\vec E\,e^{-t/\tau}$, and $-\vec p/\tau - e\vec E = e\vec E(1 - e^{-t/\tau}) - e\vec E = -e\vec E\,e^{-t/\tau}$ ✓; $\ \vec p(0) = \vec 0$ ✓.

99% of the steady drift when $e^{-t/\tau} = 0.01$:

$$t_{99} = \tau\ln100 = 4.61\,\tau = 1.1\times10^{-13}\ \mathrm s .$$

Limits. $t \ll \tau$: $\vec p \approx -e\vec E\,t$, free acceleration — no collisions yet. $\ t \gg \tau$: the steady state ✓.

The approach to the steady drift: $1 - e^{-t/\tau}$. The tangent at $t = 0$ is free acceleration; it would reach the steady value at $t = \tau$.

Solving it. Componentwise, $\dot p = -p/\tau - eE$ is MR 00's case of a force depending on velocity only, separable: $\mathrm dp/(p + eE\tau) = -\mathrm dt/\tau$, so $p + eE\tau = (p(0) + eE\tau)e^{-t/\tau}$. With $p(0) = 0$ this is the boxed solution. MR 01's Picard–Lindelöf theorem makes it the only solution: the right-hand side is Lipschitz in $p$ with constant $1/\tau$.

The number. $\ln100 = 4.6052$; $4.6052\times2.4943\times10^{-14} = 1.149\times10^{-13}$ s.

Switching off is the same equation with $E = 0$: the drift decays as $e^{-t/\tau}$, gone to 1% in the same $1.1\times10^{-13}$ s. So the current in a wire responds to a change of field within a tenth of a picosecond. If the current through a wire takes longer than that to change — and in every circuit it does — the delay is not in the copper's electrons. Units 02 and 05 find it in the fields the circuit stores.

What "the field is switched on" hides. The field inside the wire is produced by charges, and they too must move into place (section 5). This slide asks only how the electrons respond to a given field; how the field itself gets there is the closing question.

Alternating fields: σ(ω)

Drive with $\vec E(t) = \mathrm{Re}\big(\tilde{\vec E}e^{i\omega t}\big)$ — MR 06's complex-drive trick, with the course's $e^{+i\omega t}$. Try $\vec p = \mathrm{Re}(\tilde{\vec p}\,e^{i\omega t})$: $\ i\omega\tilde{\vec p} = -\tilde{\vec p}/\tau - e\tilde{\vec E}$, so $\tilde{\vec p} = -e\tau\tilde{\vec E}/(1 + i\omega\tau)$, and $\tilde{\vec J} = \sigma(\omega)\tilde{\vec E}$ with

AC conductivity $$\sigma(\omega) = \frac{\sigma_0}{1 + i\omega\tau},\qquad |\sigma| = \frac{\sigma_0}{\sqrt{1 + \omega^2\tau^2}} .$$

$\sigma_0 = ne^2\tau/m$; $\omega\tau$ is a pure number ✓. Limits: $\omega\to0$ gives $\sigma_0$ ✓; $\omega\to\infty$ gives $ne^2/(im\omega)$, free electrons, current $90^\circ$ behind the field and no dissipation ✓.

$|\sigma| = \sigma_0/\sqrt2$ at $\omega\tau = 1$: $\ f = 1/2\pi\tau = 6.4$ THz. At 2.4 GHz, $\omega\tau = 3.8\times10^{-4}$ and $1 - |\sigma|/\sigma_0 = 7\times10^{-8}$. At 50 Hz the difference is utterly negligible.

consequence

At every frequency this course uses, the copper's electrons obey the DC law. (What changes in a wire at high frequency is where the field goes — the skin effect, branch 10 — not the law.)

$|\sigma(\omega)|/\sigma_0$ for copper, $\tau = 2.49\times10^{-14}$ s. Flat to within $10^{-7}$ up to 2.4 GHz.

Why the trick works (MR 06): the equation $\dot{\vec p} = -\vec p/\tau - e\vec E$ is linear with real coefficients, so the real part of a complex solution solves it with the real part of the complex drive. The complex amplitudes $\tilde{\vec E}$, $\tilde{\vec J}$ here are MR 06's, with peak values; the RMS phasors of the course arrive in unit 05, and since $\sigma(\omega)$ is a ratio it is the same in either.

The numbers. $f = 1/(2\pi\times2.4943\times10^{-14}) = 6.381\times10^{12}$ Hz. At 2.4 GHz, $\omega\tau = 2\pi\times2.4\times10^9\times2.4943\times10^{-14} = 3.761\times10^{-4}$, and $1 - (1 + \omega^2\tau^2)^{-1/2} \approx \omega^2\tau^2/2 = 7.07\times10^{-8}$. At 50 Hz, $\omega\tau = 7.8\times10^{-12}$ and the difference, $\omega^2\tau^2/2$, is below $10^{-22}$ — too small even to represent as a difference from 1 in a computer's ordinary arithmetic. The phase of $\sigma$ at 2.4 GHz is $-\arctan\omega\tau = -0.0216^\circ$: the current lags the field by that much.

Why the high-frequency limit dissipates nothing. For $\omega\tau \gg 1$, $\sigma \approx -i\,ne^2/(m\omega)$ is almost purely imaginary: current and field are $90^\circ$ apart, so $\vec J\cdot\vec E$ averages to zero over a cycle. The electrons are shaken faster than they collide and simply oscillate, storing and returning kinetic energy. Section 6 meets the same physics as the plasma oscillation.

What is neglected. A real AC field comes with a magnetic field, and the force $-e\vec v\times\vec B$; its average is $-e\vec v_{\mathrm d}\times\vec B$, smaller than $e\vec E$ by roughly $v_{\mathrm d}/c$ for fields of the size a wave carries. And the field must be nearly uniform over a mean free path. Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1, discuss both conditions.

00 · Joule heating

The field's work is J·E

Joule heating

The field does work on the carriers at the rate $\vec J\cdot\vec E$ per unit volume. In the steady state all of it passes to the lattice through collisions.

proof

Rate: mean power per electron $-e\vec E\cdot\vec v_{\mathrm d}$ (magnetic force does no work, MR 23); times $n$, $\vec J\cdot\vec E$. Destination: dot the relaxation-time equation with $n\vec p/m$:

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Big(\frac{n|\vec p|^2}{2m}\Big) = \vec J\cdot\vec E - \frac{n|\vec p|^2}{m\tau}.$$

Drift energy changes at the rate the field supplies it minus the rate collisions remove it; in the steady state, zero. $\square$

Check: $nm v_{\mathrm d}^2/\tau = nm(e\tau E/m)^2/\tau = \sigma E^2 = \vec J\cdot\vec E$ ✓. Units: $\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}\cdot V\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}$ ✓.

The energy budget per unit volume. The drift is a conduit, not a store: its energy is constant while the current is.

Full proof of the balance

The relaxation-time equation is $\dot{\vec p} = -\vec p/\tau - e\vec E$. Take the dot product with $\vec p/m$: $\ \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac{|\vec p|^2}{2m} = -\frac{|\vec p|^2}{m\tau} - e\vec E\cdot\frac{\vec p}m$. Since $\vec p/m = \vec v_{\mathrm d}$, the last term is $-e\vec E\cdot\vec v_{\mathrm d}$, and multiplying by $n$ makes it $\vec J\cdot\vec E$. That is the boxed identity. In the steady state $\vec p$ is constant, so $\vec J\cdot\vec E = n|\vec p|^2/m\tau = nmv_{\mathrm d}^2/\tau > 0$ whenever a current flows.

The whole budget, not only the drift

The identity above follows the energy of the mean motion. Follow the full mean kinetic energy per electron, $K = \langle\tfrac12m|\vec v|^2\rangle$, instead. Between collisions the field changes it at the mean rate $-e\vec E\cdot\vec v_{\mathrm d}$. At a collision, Drude's hypothesis 3 in full says the electron leaves with the thermal energy $K_0$ of the lattice's temperature, so collisions remove energy at the rate $(K - K_0)/\tau$ and deliver it to the lattice. In the steady state $K - K_0 = -e\tau\vec E\cdot\vec v_{\mathrm d} = mv_{\mathrm d}^2$, and the power into the lattice per unit volume is $n(K - K_0)/\tau = \vec J\cdot\vec E$ — the same answer, now with the destination named. Notice that the electrons run hotter than the lattice by $mv_{\mathrm d}^2$ each: half of it is the drift's kinetic energy $\tfrac12mv_{\mathrm d}^2$, and half extra random energy. That is why the loss term in the box is $|\vec p|^2/m\tau$, twice the drift energy over $\tau$.

How big. In the cable, $mv_{\mathrm d}^2 = 9.1\times10^{-31}\times(2.95\times10^{-4})^2 \approx 8\times10^{-38}$ J, against a thermal energy of order $10^{-21}$ J. The heating of the electrons is invisible; the heating of the lattice, 0.67 W in every metre, is not.

Name. Griffiths, §7.1, calls $P = VI = I^2R$ the Joule heating law, after Joule. The derivation above is Ashcroft & Mermin's picture, made into an energy balance.

A uniform wire: I²R

A straight wire, length $L$, cross-section $A$, uniform $\vec J$ along it (section 4 proves it). Then $I = JA$, and the potential difference is $V = \varphi(0) - \varphi(L) = EL$.

resistance

$R = V/I$, in ohms. With $E = \rho J$, $\ V = \rho LI/A$, so

$$R = \frac{\rho L}{A} .$$

Units: $\Omega\,\mathrm m\cdot\mathrm m/\mathrm m^2 = \Omega$ ✓.

the heat in the wire $$P = \int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = \frac IA\cdot\frac VL\cdot AL$$ $$= VI = I^2R = V^2/R .$$

Units: $\mathrm{V\cdot A} = \mathrm W$ ✓. Check: $R\to0$ at fixed $I$ heats nothing ✓.

The uniform wire. The potential falls linearly along it, from $V$ to $0$.

Potential difference. In a static situation $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ (MR 23), so the integral of $\vec E$ along any path from one end to the other is $\varphi(\text{start}) - \varphi(\text{end})$, independent of the path. That number, in volts, is what "the voltage across the wire" means here. Unit 02 shows when, and why, it stops being path-independent.

The power, slowly. $\vec J\cdot\vec E = JE$ is uniform, so $\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = JE\cdot AL$. With $J = I/A$ and $E = V/L$ this is $VI$. Using $V = IR$: $I^2R$; using $I = V/R$: $V^2/R$.

What this does not yet say. That $VI$ is also the power the source delivers — and by what route the energy reaches the wire — is the content of unit 01, which proves $p = vi$ from Poynting's theorem. Here $VI$ is only the Joule heat inside, computed from fields we have assumed. The next section's energy identity makes the same statement for any shape.

The resistor. A component made to have a chosen $R$ is a resistor, drawn in circuit diagrams from unit 02 on. The kettle's element is one: $R = V/I = 230/10 = 23\ \Omega$.

Why cables are rated by temperature

The 0.672 W in each metre is made in the copper and must leave through the insulation. In the steady state the conductor settles at the temperature at which heat leaves exactly as fast as $I^2R'$ makes it. Raise the current and the heat goes up as $I^2$: the current a cable may carry is a thermal limit.

current-carrying capacity (IEC 60364-5-52)

The standard tabulates, for each conductor size and each way of installing the cable, the current that keeps the conductor below the insulation's limit: 70 °C for PVC, 90 °C for XLPE (cross-linked polyethylene).

The installation method enters because it decides how easily heat escapes: a cable in open air sheds it more easily than one surrounded by thermal insulation. The current rating is a thermal limit; what voltage the insulation withstands is a separate question.

The heat is made in the core and leaves radially. How fast it can leave sets the current the cable may carry.

What the standard does, and what this slide does not do. IEC 60364-5-52 (Low-voltage electrical installations — Selection and erection of electrical equipment — Wiring systems) gives tables of current-carrying capacity organised by installation method, chosen so that in continuous service the conductor does not exceed the maximum temperature its insulation tolerates: 70 °C for PVC and 90 °C for XLPE. The tables' values depend on the installation method and the conditions of service, and are not quoted here — a learner with the standard can look them up, and a number quoted without its conditions is worse than none.

Why the limit is the insulation's. Copper is in no danger at 70 °C. The two limits the standard names are those of the insulating materials, and the rating exists to protect the insulation — which is what keeps the 230 V where it belongs.

The thermal problem is a cousin of the next section's. Steady heat flow obeys a diffusion law of the same form as $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ (heat current proportional to minus the temperature gradient), and the radial flow through a ring of insulation is solved exactly like problem P9's washer. That is a model assumption about heat conduction, not developed here; it is why a temperature rise roughly proportional to the heat, hence to $I^2$, is the right first picture.

A feedback, mentioned. Copper's resistivity rises with temperature, so a hot conductor makes a little more heat at the same current. This unit does not follow that feedback further.

Resistance as a boundary-value problem

Steady current in a uniform conductor is MR 23's Laplace problem with new boundary data. Uniqueness makes resistance a property of shape and material alone.

00 · boundary-value problem

Steady current is a Laplace problem

Steady means nothing changes in time. Then

  • continuity (MR 23, MR 24), $\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial_t\varrho = 0$;
  • Faraday (MR 24), $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t\vec B = \vec 0$, so $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$;
  • Ohm, $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$.
$$\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\varphi) = 0,$$

and for uniform $\sigma$, Laplace's equation $\nabla^2\varphi = 0$.

the boundary data: a mixed boundary-value problem

On each electrode — a patch of surface joined to a much better conductor — $\varphi$ is given: Dirichlet. On an insulated surface no current crosses, $\vec J\cdot\hat n = 0$, so $\partial_n\varphi = 0$: Neumann (MR 23).

Electrodes (thick) fix $\varphi$; everywhere else the surface is insulated and the current runs parallel to it.

Why a good conductor is an electrode. The copper leads attached to a resistor carry the same current through a far smaller $\rho$, so the potential barely changes across them: to the accuracy of the problem, each lead's contact patch is an equipotential. That is an approximation with a stated hypothesis — the lead's resistivity is negligible against the conductor's — and it is the same one that lets the kettle's copper cable be treated as an electrode of the element.

Why $\partial_n\varphi = 0$. Just inside an insulated surface, $\vec J\cdot\hat n = 0$ (no charge leaves the conductor) and $\vec J = -\sigma\nabla\varphi$, so $\partial_n\varphi = \nabla\varphi\cdot\hat n = 0$. This is a statement about the field just inside. Just outside, in air, the field may have a normal component — and section 5 shows that it does, and what that implies.

Non-uniform σ. If $\sigma$ varies with position (a conductor of graded composition, or one that is hotter in some places) the equation is $\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\varphi) = 0$, not Laplace's. Everything on the next two slides holds for it, with the same proofs.

Faraday's law with $\partial_t\vec B = 0$. In a steady state the current is constant, so the magnetic field it makes is constant, and $\nabla\times\vec E = 0$ exactly. Unit 02 is about what happens when that fails.

00 · boundary-value problem

Uniqueness with mixed data

uniqueness theorem, mixed data

Let the conductor $D$ be bounded and connected, with $\sigma \gt 0$, and let its boundary be $S_D\cup S_N$ with $S_D$ non-empty. There is at most one $\varphi$ with $\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\varphi) = 0$ in $D$, $\ \varphi$ given on $S_D$, and $\sigma\partial_n\varphi = 0$ on $S_N$.

proof, by energy (MR 23)

Let $\psi$ be the difference of two solutions: $\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\psi) = 0$, $\psi = 0$ on $S_D$, $\sigma\partial_n\psi = 0$ on $S_N$. The product rule gives $\nabla\cdot(\psi\sigma\nabla\psi) = \sigma|\nabla\psi|^2$. Divergence theorem:

$$\int_D\sigma|\nabla\psi|^2\,\mathrm dV = \oint_{\partial D}\psi\,\sigma\partial_n\psi\,\mathrm dA = 0,$$

each piece of the boundary contributing zero. So $\nabla\psi = \vec 0$, $\psi$ is constant, and it vanishes on $S_D$. $\square$

uniform σ: MR 23 exactly

Divide by $\sigma$: Green's first identity with $u = v = \psi$, as in MR 23's proof by energy, with Dirichlet and Neumann pieces of boundary instead of conductors of given charge.

the hypotheses, each needed

No electrode ($S_D$ empty): $\varphi$ is fixed only up to a constant — and no current flows.
A piece with no electrode: its potential floats; apply the theorem to each connected piece.
Existence is not proved here: we assume a steady current exists, as experiment says, and prove there is only one.

Units: $\sigma|\nabla\psi|^2$ is $\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}\cdot V^2\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}$ — a power density, which is why this is called the energy method.

Full proof

Suppose $\varphi_1$ and $\varphi_2$ are both solutions, $C^2$ in $D$ and $C^1$ up to its boundary, and put $\psi = \varphi_1 - \varphi_2$. The equation is linear, so $\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\psi) = 0$ in $D$; the data subtract to $\psi = 0$ on $S_D$ and $\sigma\partial_n\psi = 0$ on $S_N$. For any $C^1$ scalar $u$ and vector field $\vec F$, $\nabla\cdot(u\vec F) = \nabla u\cdot\vec F + u\nabla\cdot\vec F$; with $u = \psi$ and $\vec F = \sigma\nabla\psi$, $\nabla\cdot(\psi\sigma\nabla\psi) = \sigma|\nabla\psi|^2 + \psi\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\psi) = \sigma|\nabla\psi|^2$. Integrate over $D$ and apply the divergence theorem (MR 05): $\int_D\sigma|\nabla\psi|^2 = \oint_{\partial D}\psi\sigma\nabla\psi\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dA$. On $S_D$ the integrand vanishes because $\psi = 0$; on $S_N$ because $\sigma\partial_n\psi = 0$. So $\int_D\sigma|\nabla\psi|^2 = 0$. The integrand is continuous and non-negative and $\sigma \gt 0$, so $\nabla\psi = \vec 0$ throughout $D$. $D$ is connected, so $\psi$ is a constant; by continuity up to $S_D$, where $\psi = 0$, the constant is $0$. $\square$

Comparison with MR 23. MR 23 proved Dirichlet uniqueness by the maximum principle and "conductor data" uniqueness by this energy argument, whose boundary sum vanishes term by term. Here it vanishes term by term too: on Dirichlet pieces because $\psi = 0$, on Neumann pieces because $\partial_n\psi = 0$. The only new thing here is the weight $\sigma$ inside the integral.

Where $\sigma$ jumps. For two different metals joined at an interface, $\sigma$ is discontinuous. Apply the argument in each metal separately: the boundary terms on the shared interface cancel because $\psi$ and $\sigma\partial_n\psi$ (the normal current) are both continuous across it. The theorem survives.

00 · boundary-value problem

Resistance is a property of shape and material

Two electrodes: $\varphi = V$ on $S_1$, $\varphi = 0$ on $S_2$. The current entering at $S_1$ is $I = \int_{S_1}\sigma\partial_n\varphi\,\mathrm dA$ ($\hat n$ outward).

R is well defined, and positive
  1. Linearity. If $\varphi$ solves for $V$, then $(V'/V)\varphi$ solves for $V'$, and by uniqueness it is the solution. So $I \propto V$, and $R = V/I$ depends only on $\sigma$ and the shape.
  2. Energy. The identity of the last slide, applied to $\varphi$ itself:
$$\int_D\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = \int_D\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2\,\mathrm dV = VI .$$

So $VI \gt 0$, $\ R \gt 0$, and $R = V^2\big/\!\int_D\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2$.

scaling

Enlarge the conductor by a factor $\lambda$, same material: $R\to R/\lambda$.

proof

$\varphi_\lambda(\vec x) = \varphi(\vec x/\lambda)$ solves the enlarged problem with the same data. Its gradient is $1/\lambda$ times as large, the electrode's area $\lambda^2$ times, so $I_\lambda = \lambda I$. $\square$

Check on $\rho L/A$: $L\to\lambda L$, $A\to\lambda^2A$, $R\to R/\lambda$ ✓.

energy, for any shape

The Joule heat inside any two-electrode conductor is $VI$. Unit 01 shows the same $VI$ arriving through the space around it.

The energy identity, in detail. As on the last slide but with $\psi$ replaced by $\varphi$: $\int_D\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2 = \oint\varphi\,\sigma\partial_n\varphi\,\mathrm dA$. On $S_N$ the integrand vanishes; on $S_2$, $\varphi = 0$; on $S_1$, $\varphi = V$ is constant and comes out: $V\int_{S_1}\sigma\partial_n\varphi\,\mathrm dA$. The current density is $\vec J = -\sigma\nabla\varphi$, so the current flowing into $D$ through $S_1$ is $\int_{S_1}\vec J\cdot(-\hat n)\,\mathrm dA = \int_{S_1}\sigma\partial_n\varphi\,\mathrm dA = I$. Hence the boundary integral is $VI$, and the left side is $\int\vec J\cdot\vec E$ since $\vec J\cdot\vec E = \sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2$.

Why $I \gt 0$ for $V \gt 0$. The identity gives $VI = \int\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2 \ge 0$, with equality only if $\varphi$ is constant — impossible, since it takes the two values $V$ and $0$. So current flows from the higher potential to the lower, as it must if the field does positive work.

Current conservation. The current leaving through $S_2$ equals $I$: integrate $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ over $D$, and the only boundary pieces with flux are the two electrodes.

What scaling says about real cables. Halving every dimension of a conductor doubles its resistance. A cable's resistance per metre goes as $1/A$, i.e. as the inverse square of its width, and each metre is a separate copy, so the total goes as $L/A$ — the uniform-wire formula, again.

00 · boundary-value problem

The uniform wire, proved

A straight conductor of length $L$ along $z$, any cross-section of area $A$, electrodes covering its two flat ends ($\varphi = V$ at $z = 0$, $\varphi = 0$ at $z = L$), side insulated.

guess, verify, and uniqueness does the rest

Try $\varphi = V(1 - z/L)$. It is harmonic ($\nabla^2\varphi = 0$) ✓; it takes the electrode values ✓; on the side, $\hat n\perp\hat z$, so $\partial_n\varphi = 0$ ✓. By uniqueness it is the solution. Then $\vec J = \sigma(V/L)\hat z$, uniform; $I = \sigma VA/L$; and

$$R = \frac{\rho L}{A},$$

exactly, for any shape of cross-section.

The cable's $6.72\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$ per metre is this, with $L = 1$ m. Section 3 assumed the flow uniform; now it is proved — for a straight wire whose ends are electrodes.

Equipotentials are flat cross-sections, equally spaced; the current runs straight. Guessed, checked, and made unique by the theorem.

Why the shape of the cross-section does not matter. The guess depends on $z$ alone, so it never notices the cross-section; the only place the shape could enter is the Neumann condition, and that holds on any side wall parallel to $\hat z$. This is the strength of "guess and invoke uniqueness" from MR 23: a solution found by any means, if it meets every condition, is the solution.

When the ends are not electrodes. If the current enters through a small contact rather than the whole end face, the flow near the contact is not uniform, and $R$ is larger than $\rho L/A$. The disturbance stays near the contact: The kettle cable round a bend shows how fast it dies away.

Griffiths, §7.1, derives $R = \rho L/A$ the same way, and computes the resistance of a coaxial cylinder (the washer of problem P9, rolled up) as a second example.

Worked: a cone cut by two spheres

the part of a cone of solid angle $\Omega$ between spheres of radii $r_1 \lt r_2$; electrodes on the two caps

1. Guess $\varphi = \varphi(r)$. Then $\nabla^2\varphi = r^{-2}(r^2\varphi')' = 0$, so $\varphi = a + C/r$ (MR 23's radial solutions).

2. Data. $\varphi(r_1) = V$, $\varphi(r_2) = 0$: $\ C = V\big/(1/r_1 - 1/r_2)$. On the cone's side the normal is perpendicular to $\hat r$, so $\partial_n\varphi = 0$ ✓. Uniqueness: this is the solution.

3. Current. $\vec J = -\sigma\varphi'\hat r = \sigma C\hat r/r^2$. Through the cap at radius $r$, of area $\Omega r^2$: $\ I = \sigma C\,\Omega$, the same at every $r$ ✓ ($\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$).

4. Resistance.

$$R = \frac VI = \frac{\rho}{\Omega}\Big(\frac1{r_1} - \frac1{r_2}\Big).$$

Caps are electrodes; the conical side is insulated. The current is radial.

5. Thin cone. Half-angle $\theta$: $\Omega = 2\pi(1 - \cos\theta) \approx \pi\theta^2$; end radii $a \approx r_1\theta$, $b \approx r_2\theta$; length $L = r_2 - r_1$. So $R = \rho L/(\Omega r_1r_2) \approx \rho L/\pi ab$.

6. Checks. $a = b$: $\rho L/\pi a^2 = \rho L/A$ ✓. Units: $\Omega$ here is a solid angle, a pure number, so $\rho/r$ is in ohms ✓.

Step 1, the Laplacian. For a function of $r$ alone in spherical coordinates, $\nabla^2\varphi = r^{-2}\,\mathrm d(r^2\varphi')/\mathrm dr$ (MR 23, Radial solutions, and where the dimension enters). Setting it to zero: $r^2\varphi' = -C$, $\varphi = a + C/r$.

Step 2, the constants. $a + C/r_1 = V$ and $a + C/r_2 = 0$; subtract: $C(1/r_1 - 1/r_2) = V$.

Step 3, the sign. $\varphi' = -C/r^2$, so $\vec J = -\sigma\nabla\varphi = \sigma C r^{-2}\hat r$, outward for $V \gt 0$: from the higher potential to the lower ✓.

Step 5, the limit. $1 - \cos\theta = \theta^2/2 + O(\theta^4)$, so $\Omega = \pi\theta^2(1 + O(\theta^2))$. The end radii are the caps' radii $r_k\sin\theta = r_k\theta(1 + O(\theta^2))$. And $1/r_1 - 1/r_2 = (r_2 - r_1)/r_1r_2$. So $R = \rho L/(\pi\theta^2r_1r_2)\cdot(1 + O(\theta^2)) = \rho L/(\pi ab)\cdot(1 + O(\theta^2))$.

A caution about "a truncated cone". The exact result is for electrodes on spherical caps. A truncated cone with flat end electrodes is a different problem whose potential is not purely radial. For a thin cone the caps are nearly flat, and the two answers agree to the order shown; the next slide finds $\rho L/\pi ab$ a third way.

Two special cases worth knowing, both in problem P8: $\Omega = 4\pi$ is the region between two concentric spheres; $\Omega = 2\pi$ with $r_2\to\infty$ is a hemispherical electrode set into the ground, $R = \rho/(2\pi r_1)$ — the basic formula of earthing.

When the cross-section varies

A wire whose area $A(x)$ changes slowly along it. Suppose the current is nearly uniform over each cross-section. Then $I = J(x)A(x)$ is the same at every $x$ (no charge piles up), $E(x) = \rho I/A(x)$, and $V = \int E\,\mathrm dx$:

slowly varying cross-section (an approximation) $$R \approx \rho\int_0^L\frac{\mathrm dx}{A(x)} .$$

Units: $\Omega\,\mathrm m\cdot\mathrm m/\mathrm m^2 = \Omega$ ✓. Exact when $A$ is constant.

checked on the cone

Radius $a + (b - a)x/L$: $\ \displaystyle\int_0^L\frac{\mathrm dx}{\pi(a + (b - a)x/L)^2} = \frac{L}{\pi(b - a)}\Big(\frac1a - \frac1b\Big) = \frac{L}{\pi ab}$ ✓ — the thin-cone limit of the exact solution.

Slices in series. The approximation fails where $A$ changes over a length comparable to the wire's width.

A joint in the cable. A narrowing to half the area over 2 mm adds roughly $\rho\cdot2\ \mathrm{mm}/A = 13\ \mu\Omega$ — as much as 2 mm more cable. At a joint the real hazard is different: the current crosses from one metal to the other only where they touch, and if that area is small, so is the denominator.

The approximation, stated honestly. The step "$J$ is uniform over each cross-section" is not exact for any non-uniform wire: where the wall slopes, the Neumann condition makes the current follow it, so $\vec J$ has a radial part. The approximation is good when the slope $|\mathrm dr/\mathrm dx|$ of the wall is small, which is when the solution of Laplace's equation is close to one that depends on $x$ alone. The cone shows how good: the exact answer for spherical caps and the slice formula agree to $O(\theta^2)$, where $\theta$ is the wall's slope.

The cone integral. Put $r(x) = a + (b - a)x/L$, so $\mathrm dr = (b - a)\,\mathrm dx/L$ and $\int\mathrm dx/(\pi r^2) = \frac{L}{\pi(b - a)}\int_a^b\mathrm dr/r^2 = \frac{L}{\pi(b - a)}\big(\frac1a - \frac1b\big)$. And $\frac1a - \frac1b = \frac{b - a}{ab}$, giving $L/\pi ab$. (For $a = b$ the integral is $L/\pi a^2$ directly.)

The joint's number. Over the narrowed 2 mm the resistance is $\rho\ell/(A/2) = 2\rho\ell/A$ instead of $\rho\ell/A$, an extra $\rho\ell/A = 1.68\times10^{-8}\times2\times10^{-3}/2.5\times10^{-6} = 1.34\times10^{-5}\ \Omega$. A narrowing that is short costs little. A 2 mm narrowing is about as long as the wire is wide, so the slice formula is only a rough guide here — enough for the order of magnitude, which is the point.

Contact. A crimped joint is made by squeezing a metal sleeve hard onto the conductor's end, so that the two metals touch over as much area as possible. The formula's moral is that what matters is the area the current actually passes through; at a joint that is the area of true metal-to-metal contact, and the heat $I^2R$ of a poor joint is made in a small volume. Ratings protect cables; a bad joint is a separate failure, and this slide says only why it is one.

The kettle cable round a bend

At a bend the uniform solution fails: the current crowds towards the inside, where the path is shorter. How far does the disturbance reach?

disturbances die within a radius

In a straight round conductor of radius $a$ with insulated wall, every steady flow whose current density stays bounded along it is the uniform flow plus terms decaying at least as fast as $e^{-1.841\,z/a}$.

proof sketch (in the notes, with completeness cited)

Separate in cylindrical coordinates (MR 23): $J_m(kr)\cos m\theta\,e^{\pm kz}$. The insulated wall needs $J_m'(ka) = 0$. Apart from $k = 0$ — the uniform flow — the smallest root is $ka = 1.841$, at $m = 1$.

The cable: $a = \sqrt{A/\pi} = 0.892$ mm, so the disturbance falls by $e$ every 0.48 mm. A few millimetres from a bend the flow is uniform again, and $\rho L/A$ holds.

A sketch: current lines and equipotentials (dashed) round a right-angle bend. Within four e-folding lengths the disturbance is down to 2%.

Full proof

Take the straight part of the conductor beyond the bend as the half-cylinder $r \lt a$, $z \gt 0$, with $\partial_r\varphi = 0$ at $r = a$. Separating $\varphi = R(r)\Theta(\theta)Z(z)$ in Laplace's equation (MR 23, Cylindrical coordinates: the same method, other special functions) gives $\Theta = \cos m\theta$ or $\sin m\theta$ with integer $m$, $Z = e^{\pm kz}$, and Bessel's equation for $R$, whose solutions regular at $r = 0$ are $J_m(kr)$ (MR 09 built them). The wall condition is $kJ_m'(ka) = 0$. For $k = 0$ the solutions with $\partial_r\varphi = 0$ at the wall are constants and $z$ itself (MR 23's $k = 0$ row: $s^{\pm m}$ fails the wall condition unless $m = 0$, and $\log s$ is unbounded on the axis): $\varphi = c_0 + c_1z$, the uniform flow. For $k \gt 0$, $ka$ must be a zero of $J_m'$. The smallest positive zeros are $j'_{1,1} = 1.8412$ for $m = 1$, then $j'_{2,1} = 3.0542$, then $j'_{0,1} = 3.8317$ (for $m = 0$, $J_0' = -J_1$). Any solution whose current density $-\sigma\nabla\varphi$ stays bounded as $z\to\infty$ has no $e^{+kz}$ terms, so it is a superposition of the uniform flow and terms $e^{-kz}$ with these $k$; the slowest decays as $e^{-1.8412z/a}$, and so does its current. (That the separated solutions are complete on the disc — every cross-section's data expand in them — is cited: it is the expansion theorem for Dini series, Watson, A Treatise on the Theory of Bessel Functions, ch. XVIII, the chapter MR 19 cites for the clamped-rim case.)

The numbers. $a = \sqrt{2.5\times10^{-6}/\pi} = 8.921\times10^{-4}$ m; $a/1.8412 = 4.845\times10^{-4}$ m; $e^{-4} = 0.018$, and four lengths is 1.94 mm.

What it does to R. By the scaling theorem, a bend changes the resistance by $(\rho/a)\,g$, with $g$ a pure number fixed by the bend's shape (its angle, and its radius over $a$). This unit does not compute $g$. What it does establish is that the change is made within a couple of millimetres of the bend, so a 10 m cable with a few bends is still, to good accuracy, $\rho L/A$.

section 5

Surface charge

Inside the copper there is no charge, yet the field follows every bend. What holds it there is charge on the surface — a little of it, graded along the wire.

00 · surface charge

Neutral inside, and still a field

no charge inside

In a steady current through a conductor of uniform $\sigma$, the charge density in the interior is zero.

proof

Gauss: $\varrho = \varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = \varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot(\vec J/\sigma) = (\varepsilon_0/\sigma)\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$. $\square$

With non-uniform resistivity, $\varrho = \varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec J) = \varepsilon_0\vec J\cdot\nabla\rho$: charge sits where $\rho$ changes along the current. Keep that for the junction.

the puzzle

The field is static, so it is the Coulomb field of all the charges there are (MR 23). None are inside the wire. The source alone cannot make a field that follows the wire: route the wire through a point $P$ in another direction, and the field at $P$ must turn, while the source has not changed.

A sketch. The source's field near the wire points wherever the source's geometry says; the field inside the wire must point along the wire.

The proof uses three things: Gauss's law (MR 23), $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$, and $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ in a steady state. With $\sigma$ constant it comes out of the divergence. With $\rho = 1/\sigma$ varying, use the product rule: $\nabla\cdot(\rho\vec J) = \vec J\cdot\nabla\rho + \rho\nabla\cdot\vec J = \vec J\cdot\nabla\rho$.

Compare MR 23. There, a conductor in equilibrium had $\vec E = 0$ inside and all its charge on the surface. Here $\vec E \ne 0$ inside, and still the interior is neutral; the charge is still on the surface, but now it is not the same everywhere.

The puzzle, sharpened. In a steady state $\vec E$ is the Coulomb field of every charge in the universe, at its present position. Inside a uniform wire there are none. So the field at a point $P$ inside the wire is produced by charges elsewhere: in the source and on surfaces. Now keep the source and change the wire: run it through $P$ horizontally, then (another day) vertically. The field at $P$ must lie along the wire both times, because $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ and the current follows the wire. If only the source's charges contributed, the field at $P$ would be the same both times. So charges whose arrangement depends on the wire's route must contribute — and the only place left is the surface of the wire itself.

The figure is a sketch of field lines of a dipole-like source, not a computed field.

00 · surface charge

Surface charge steers the field

The answer: surface charge on the wire, graded along its length. Positive near the source's + end, falling steadily, negative near its − end; with extra charge at every bend and every change of material.

why an insulated surface carries charge

Just inside, $\partial_n\varphi = 0$: the field is tangential. Just outside, the field is whatever all the charges make, and in general it has a normal part. By MR 23's jump condition,

$$\Sigma = \varepsilon_0\,(\vec E_{\text{out}} - \vec E_{\text{in}})\cdot\hat n = \varepsilon_0\,\vec E_{\text{out}}\cdot\hat n .$$
three roles (Jackson, 1996)

1. It maintains the potential around the circuit. 2. It provides the field outside the conductors — unit 01's subject. 3. It confines the current to the wire, keeping the field inside along it.

A qualitative sketch of the picture Jackson (1996) and Chabay & Sherwood describe: signs and grading only, not a computed charge density.

How the charge gets there. When the circuit is first connected, the field inside the wire does not yet follow it, and current runs into the wire's surface wherever the field has a component normal to it. Charge accumulates there, and that charge pushes the field round until the normal component inside is zero. The process stops exactly when $\partial_n\varphi = 0$ just inside — which is the steady state. Chabay & Sherwood's Matter and Interactions teaches circuits through exactly this feedback. The sketch is drawn to the rules this slide states — grading along the wire, extra charge where the field must turn — and is not copied from any computed figure: Jackson's computed examples use a different, coaxial geometry.

The bends. At the first bend the field must turn from rightward to downward. A surplus of positive charge on the outside corner pushes the field away from it, downward and back; a deficit on the inside corner pulls it in. Both turn it. "Surplus" and "deficit" are relative to the steady grading along the wire, which is why the second bend, in a negatively charged stretch, shows extra negative charge on the inside instead.

How much. Very little. Problem P10 computes one example: at a junction carrying the cable's current density, about 240 electron charges per square millimetre, against some $2\times10^{13}$ atoms on that square millimetre of surface. The distribution depends on the whole circuit's geometry, not on the wire alone, so there is no general formula; Jackson (1996) works out examples and stresses that the charges vary greatly with where the rest of the circuit lies.

Jackson's second role is the reason unit 01 can exist. Outside the wire, the surface charge makes an electric field with a component along the wire and one across it; together with the magnetic field of the current, it carries energy in the space around the conductors. Where the energy actually travels is that unit's question.

Charge at a junction

Two conductors in series, resistivities $\rho_1$ then $\rho_2$ along the current, with unit normal $\hat n$ from 1 to 2 across their interface.

junction charge $$\Sigma = \varepsilon_0\,J_n\,(\rho_2 - \rho_1).$$

Units: $\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}\cdot\Omega\,\mathrm m = (\mathrm{F\cdot\Omega})\,\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{s\cdot A\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}$ ✓.

proof

$J_n$ is continuous: $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ on a thin pillbox across the interface. So $E_n = \rho_1J_n$ on side 1 and $\rho_2J_n$ on side 2, and MR 23's jump condition gives $\Sigma = \varepsilon_0(E_{n,2} - E_{n,1})$. $\square$

In the kettle, current passes from copper into the element's far more resistive wire: positive charge where it enters, negative where it leaves. Problem P10 puts numbers in. Check: $\rho_1 = \rho_2$ gives $\Sigma = 0$ ✓.

Equal current, unequal field: the jump in the normal field is a sheet of charge. The pillbox (dashed) is MR 23's.

The pillbox, twice. For $J_n$: integrate $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ over a pillbox of face area $\delta A$ and vanishing height; the side contributes nothing in the limit, so $(J_{n,2} - J_{n,1})\,\delta A = 0$. For $E_n$: MR 23's jump condition, $(\vec E_2 - \vec E_1)\cdot\hat n = \Sigma/\varepsilon_0$, proved by Gauss's law on the same pillbox.

Units, slowly. A farad is a coulomb per volt and an ohm a volt per ampere, so $\mathrm F\cdot\Omega = \mathrm{C\,A^{-1}} = \mathrm s$. Then $\varepsilon_0\rho$ is in seconds — it is the naive time of the next section — and seconds times $\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$ is $\mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}$.

The sign, as a feedback. Suppose for a moment that $\Sigma = 0$. Then $E_n$ would be the same on both sides, and the more resistive side would carry less current: charge would arrive at the interface faster than it left, piling up positive. The pile-up raises the field ahead of it and lowers the field behind it, until the currents match. That is the junction charge, and why it is positive at the entrance to a worse conductor.

The continuous version is the formula of Neutral inside, and still a field, $\varrho = \varepsilon_0\vec J\cdot\nabla\rho$: integrate it across a thin layer in which $\rho$ climbs from $\rho_1$ to $\rho_2$, and it gives $\varepsilon_0J_n(\rho_2 - \rho_1)$ per unit area, the boxed result.

On 50 Hz mains the current reverses, and the junction charge with it, 100 times a second. It follows instantly — the next section shows how fast "instantly" is.

section 6

How fast does interior charge vanish?

The textbook answer for copper is $1.5\times10^{-19}$ s, and it is meaningless, because it uses Ohm's law where Ohm's law cannot hold. Done properly, it is MR 06's oscillator.

00 · interior charge

The naive answer, and why it is meaningless

Put some charge $\varrho$ inside the cable's copper. Continuity, Ohm and Gauss:

$$\partial_t\varrho = -\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\sigma\nabla\cdot\vec E = -\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}\,\varrho,$$

so $\varrho(t) = \varrho(0)\,e^{-t/t_c}$ with the charge relaxation time

$$t_c = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{\sigma} = \varepsilon_0\rho = 1.5\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm s$$

($8.854\times10^{-12}\times1.68\times10^{-8}$; units $\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}\cdot\Omega\,m} = \mathrm s$ ✓).

why it is meaningless

$\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ is the steady state of the relaxation-time equation, reached only after a few $\tau$. Here $t_c = 6\times10^{-6}\,\tau$: the calculation uses Ohm's law on a timescale a hundred thousand times too short for Ohm's law to hold.

Copper's timescales, on a logarithmic axis. The naive $t_c$ lies more than five decades below $\tau$, and nearly six below the time Ohm's law needs to hold.

The derivation is valid algebra on a false premise. Taking the divergence of $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ and using $\varepsilon_0\nabla\cdot\vec E = \varrho$ gives $\nabla\cdot\vec J = (\sigma/\varepsilon_0)\varrho$; continuity turns it into the decay law. Every step is correct if $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ holds at each instant. But Switching on showed that after a change of field the current takes about $4.6\tau$ to settle to $\sigma\vec E$. A process that claims to be over in $10^{-19}$ s is far inside that window.

The numbers. $8.854\times10^{-12}\times1.68\times10^{-8} = 1.4875\times10^{-19}$ s. $t_c/\tau = 1.4875\times10^{-19}/2.4943\times10^{-14} = 5.96\times10^{-6}$; its inverse is $1.68\times10^5$.

Why the textbook answer is still taught — and where it is right — comes out of the proper calculation on the next slide: it is the slow decay of an overdamped oscillator, which is what a poor conductor is. Copper is not one.

A useful identity. With $\omega_p^2 := ne^2/\varepsilon_0m$ (next slide), $t_c = \varepsilon_0m/(ne^2\tau) = 1/(\omega_p^2\tau)$, so $t_c/\tau = 1/(\omega_p\tau)^2$. The whole comparison is one dimensionless number.

00 · interior charge

Done properly: the charge is an oscillator

Keep the relaxation-time equation instead of its steady state. With $\vec J = -ne\vec p/m$, multiply it by $-ne/m$:

$$\partial_t\vec J = -\frac{\vec J}{\tau} + \frac{ne^2}{m}\vec E .$$

Take the divergence; use $\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial_t\varrho$ and $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \varrho/\varepsilon_0$:

the charge oscillator $$\ddot\varrho + \frac1\tau\,\dot\varrho + \omega_p^2\,\varrho = 0,\qquad \omega_p^2 = \frac{ne^2}{\varepsilon_0m}.$$

Units of $\omega_p^2$: $\mathrm{m^{-3}\,C^2}/(\mathrm{C^2\,J^{-1}\,m^{-1}\cdot kg}) = \mathrm{J\,m^{-2}\,kg^{-1}} = \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ ✓.

This is MR 06's damped oscillator, $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = 0$, with $\gamma = 1/\tau$, $\omega_0 = \omega_p$ the plasma frequency, and quality factor $Q = \omega_p\tau$.

the naive answer is a limit of this one

Roots: $s = -\tfrac1{2\tau} \pm \sqrt{\tfrac1{4\tau^2} - \omega_p^2}$. If $\omega_p\tau \ll \tfrac12$ (overdamped, a poor conductor), the slow root is

$$s \approx -\omega_p^2\tau = -\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0}:$$

exactly the naive decay. It is right when the charge's decay is slow beside $\tau$, so Ohm's law has time to hold. For a good conductor, $\omega_p\tau \gg 1$, and the charge rings instead.

linearised

The excess charge is assumed small beside the background $ne$, so the current it carries itself, $\varrho\,\vec v$, is dropped.

Full proof

Write the relaxation-time equation as $\partial_t\vec p = -\vec p/\tau - e\vec E$, with $\vec p$ now the mean momentum of the electrons near each point. The current density is $\vec J = -ne\vec p/m$, to first order in the disturbance (the term $\varrho\vec p/m$ from the excess charge is second order and dropped). Multiply the equation by $-ne/m$: $\ \partial_t\vec J = -\vec J/\tau + (ne^2/m)\vec E$. Take the divergence: $\partial_t(\nabla\cdot\vec J) = -(\nabla\cdot\vec J)/\tau + (ne^2/m)\nabla\cdot\vec E$. Continuity says $\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial_t\varrho$, and Gauss says $\nabla\cdot\vec E = \varrho/\varepsilon_0$. So $-\ddot\varrho = \dot\varrho/\tau + (ne^2/\varepsilon_0m)\varrho$. $\square$

What else was assumed. That the electrons' pressure — their random motion pushing them from where they are dense to where they are sparse — is negligible. For a disturbance spread over many mean free paths it is; Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1, derive the plasma oscillation this way, as a uniform displacement of the electron gas against the ions.

The overdamped limit. The characteristic equation is $s^2 + s/\tau + \omega_p^2 = 0$ (MR 06's The characteristic polynomial). For $\omega_p\tau \lt \tfrac12$ both roots are real and negative. The one nearer zero is $s_+ = -\tfrac1{2\tau}\big(1 - \sqrt{1 - 4\omega_p^2\tau^2}\big) = -\omega_p^2\tau\big(1 + O(\omega_p^2\tau^2)\big)$, by $\sqrt{1 - x} = 1 - x/2 + O(x^2)$. And $\omega_p^2\tau = ne^2\tau/(\varepsilon_0m) = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$. So in a poor conductor the long-lived part of the charge decays at exactly the naive rate, and the derivation that assumed Ohm's law is vindicated there — because there the decay is slow beside $\tau$.

interior charge in the cable's copper: $n = 8.47\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $\tau = 2.49\times10^{-14}$ s

Every number. $ne^2 = 2.1737\times10^{-9}$; $\varepsilon_0m = 8.854\times10^{-12}\times9.109\times10^{-31} = 8.0651\times10^{-42}$; the ratio is $2.6952\times10^{32}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$ and its root $1.6417\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}$, i.e. $f_p = 2.61\times10^{15}$ Hz. $Q = 1.6417\times10^{16}\times2.4943\times10^{-14} = 409.5$. $1 - \sqrt{1 - 1/4Q^2} \approx 1/8Q^2 = 7.45\times10^{-7}$. Step 3, from MR 06. The underdamped solution is $e^{-\gamma t/2}(A\cos\omega_1t + B\sin\omega_1t)$ with $\omega_1 = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$. $\varrho(0) = \varrho_0$ gives $A = \varrho_0$; $\dot\varrho(0) = 0$ gives $-\gamma A/2 + \omega_1B = 0$, so $B = \varrho_0\gamma/2\omega_1 = \varrho_0/(2\omega_1\tau)$. After a few times $2\tau$ — a tenth of a picosecond — it is gone. That is the honest answer, and it is still unimaginably fast; but it is $10^5$ times slower than the naive one.

What Drude gets wrong

The formula $\sigma = ne^2\tau/m$ survives. The picture behind $\tau$ does not, and two of the model's predictions fail outright.

00 · what Drude gets wrong

Two speeds, two mean free paths

mean free path

$\ell = v\tau$: how far an electron moving at its typical speed $v$ goes between collisions.

Drude's speed: classical equipartition, $\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac32k_BT$ — kinetic theory of gases, cited (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1). At 293 K, with $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$,

$$v = \sqrt{3k_BT/m} = 1.15\times10^5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}},$$

so $\ell = v\tau = 2.9$ nm.

The real speed is the Fermi speed, $v_F = 1.57\times10^6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ — a quantum result (Sommerfeld, Z. Phys. 47, 1, 1928; Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 2; cited). Then

$$\ell = v_F\tau = 39\ \mathrm{nm},$$

about 150 nearest-neighbour distances (0.256 nm in copper, CRC). An electron passes some 150 ions between collisions, so whatever it collides with, it is not each ion it meets.

The same $\tau$, two speeds. With the true speed an electron travels some 150 atomic spacings between collisions.

Numbers. $\sqrt{3\times1.380649\times10^{-23}\times293/9.109\times10^{-31}} = 1.154\times10^5$ m/s, and $\ell = 1.154\times10^5\times2.494\times10^{-14} = 2.88\times10^{-9}$ m, which is $11.2$ spacings of 0.256 nm. $v_F\tau = 1.57\times10^6\times2.494\times10^{-14} = 3.92\times10^{-8}$ m, which is $153$ spacings. Boltzmann's constant is exact in the SI (CODATA): $1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$.

What Drude believed. He pictured the collisions as electrons bouncing off the ions (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1). With the classical speed the mean free path comes out, as above, at about ten nearest-neighbour distances — near enough to the spacing of the ions for that picture to look plausible. The quantum speed multiplies $\ell$ by $v_F/v = 13.6$ and breaks it.

Equipartition — a mean kinetic energy $\tfrac12k_BT$ per direction of motion — is a result of classical statistical mechanics, not derived in this course or in MR 00–24; it is used here only as the input Drude took from the kinetic theory of gases.

Why the speed is so high. Cited, not derived: electrons are fermions, at most one in each quantum state, so filling $8.47\times10^{28}$ of them per cubic metre forces most into states of high momentum, even at absolute zero. The top of the filled states has speed $v_F$. The derivation is Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 2, and needs quantum mechanics beyond this course.

Why an electron passes ions without colliding is a further quantum result — a wave in a perfectly periodic lattice is not scattered by it at all — which Ashcroft & Mermin develop in later chapters. What scatters electrons is departure from perfect periodicity. None of that is used here: τ is read off the measured resistivity and nothing else.

00 · what Drude gets wrong

What survives, and what does not

why σ = ne²τ/m survives

Look back at what the derivation used: collisions at rate $1/\tau$, and a mean velocity of zero just after one. The electrons' random speed never entered. So the relation between $\sigma$ and $\tau$ is untouched by the quantum correction (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1–2); only what $\tau$ means changes.

trust
  • $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$, with $\sigma$ measured;
  • Joule heating $\vec J\cdot\vec E$, and $I^2R$;
  • $\sigma(\omega) = \sigma_0/(1 + i\omega\tau)$ at every frequency this course uses;
  • resistance as a boundary-value problem — it needs only $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$;
  • surface charge, and the charge oscillator.
do not trust
  • the microscopic picture of $\tau$: collisions with ions;
  • the classical speed of the electrons, and the mean free path built on it;
  • the sign of the Hall coefficient, for some metals;
  • Drude's agreement with the Wiedemann–Franz law, which was luck (next slides).

And keep the small parameter in view: in the cable, $v_{\mathrm d}/v_F = 1.9\times10^{-10}$. Linear response is not an approximation anyone will ever see fail in a wire.

The test of the claim. Go through the derivation of the relaxation-time equation (The relaxation-time equation, Full proof) line by line: the uncollided electrons gain $\vec f\,\mathrm dt$ whatever their velocity, and the collided ones restart with mean momentum zero whatever their speed. No property of the velocity distribution beyond its mean enters. The same is true of every result in the "trust" list. The Sommerfeld model keeps Drude's relaxation-time picture with the electrons' distribution replaced by the quantum one (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 2), and returns $\sigma = ne^2\tau/m$ unchanged.

The last line, defended. A drift $2\times10^{-10}$ of the random speed is the cable at 10 A. To make $v_{\mathrm d}$ even $10^{-3}$ of $v_F$ in copper would need a current density $5.3\times10^6$ times larger, $2.1\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$, whose Joule heating, $\rho J^2 = 7.6\times10^{18}\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}$, would vaporise the wire long before. Linear response fails in a metal only under conditions the metal does not survive.

The Hall effect, and a wrong sign

Current along $x$, magnetic field $B$ along $z$. The magnetic force pushes the carriers to one edge, where charge builds until a transverse field $E_y$ — the Hall effect — cancels the push. In the Drude model (problem P12):

the Hall coefficient $$R_H := \frac{E_y}{J_xB} = -\frac{1}{ne}.$$

Units: $\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}/(\mathrm{A\,m^{-2}\cdot T}) = \mathrm{m^3\,C^{-1}}$, and so is $1/ne$ ✓.

Unlike $\sigma$, $R_H$ has $q$ to the first power: it measures $n$ and the carriers' sign.

the failure

For some metals the measured $R_H$ is positive, as if the carriers were positive (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1). Classical electrons cannot do that; band theory explains it, not this course.

Electrons drifting against the current are pushed to the lower edge; the edges charge until the Hall field balances the push.

The direction, checked. Electrons drift along $-\hat x$ with speed $v$; the magnetic force on one is $-e(-v\hat x)\times B\hat z = evB(\hat x\times\hat z) = -evB\,\hat y$: towards the lower edge. The lower edge charges negative and the upper positive, so the Hall field points along $-\hat y$, and it grows until $-e(E_y + (\vec v\times\vec B)_y) = 0$, i.e. $E_y = -vB$. With $J_x = nev$: $E_y/(J_xB) = -1/ne$. Positive carriers moving along $+\hat x$ would be pushed to the same lower edge, which would then charge positive: the sign of $E_y$ distinguishes the two cases, which is the whole point.

Copper's number. $-1/ne = -1/(1.357\times10^{10}) = -7.37\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^3\,C^{-1}}$. Problem P12 turns it into the Hall voltage across a copper strip: about 7 μV for 10 A in 1 T through a strip 0.1 mm thick — small, but readily measured.

The failure, stated and not quantified. Ashcroft & Mermin's ch. 1 tabulates measured Hall coefficients against $-1/ne$: agreement for the alkali metals, and for some other metals a positive sign. The resolution — that electrons in a nearly full band respond to fields as positive "holes" — is band theory (Ashcroft & Mermin, later chapters), and is outside this course. No measured values are quoted here.

Copper. Its measured Hall coefficient is negative, the sign of electrons. How close it comes to $-1/ne$ with one electron per atom is in Ashcroft & Mermin's ch. 1 table and is not quoted here.

The name is E. H. Hall's, who discovered the effect (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1).

Wiedemann–Franz: right for the wrong reasons

Good conductors of electricity are good conductors of heat. The Wiedemann–Franz law: for many metals the ratio of thermal conductivity $\kappa$ to $\sigma T$ is nearly the same (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1).

Drude's classical model gives (problem P13)

$$\frac{\kappa}{\sigma T} = \frac32\Big(\frac{k_B}{e}\Big)^2 = 1.11\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}.$$

Drude's own calculation contained a factor-2 error and gave twice this, $3(k_B/e)^2 = 2.23\times10^{-8}$, close to experiment (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1). Sommerfeld's quantum value is $\tfrac{\pi^2}{3}(k_B/e)^2 = 2.44\times10^{-8}$ (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1–2, cited).

two compensating errors

The classical $\langle v^2\rangle$ is $(v_F/v)^2 = 185$ times too small, the classical heat capacity 84 times too large (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 2). In $\kappa$ they nearly cancel, leaving $2\pi^2/9 = 2.19$ (P13).

The ratio three ways. Experiment is not plotted: no measured values are quoted here.

The numbers. $k_B/e = 1.380649\times10^{-23}/1.602\times10^{-19} = 8.618\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{V\,K^{-1}}$, whose square is $7.427\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{V^2\,K^{-2}}$. Times $\tfrac32$: $1.114\times10^{-8}$; times 3: $2.228\times10^{-8}$; times $\pi^2/3$: $2.444\times10^{-8}$. Units: $\mathrm{V^2\,K^{-2}} = \mathrm{W\,\Omega\,K^{-2}}$, since $\mathrm{W\,\Omega} = \mathrm{V^2}$ ✓.

The story, as Ashcroft & Mermin tell it (ch. 1). The ratio $\kappa/\sigma T$ is the Lorenz number. In the classical kinetic theory the electrons' thermal conductivity is $\kappa = \tfrac13c_v\langle v^2\rangle\tau$, with $c_v = \tfrac32nk_B$ and $\tfrac12m\langle v^2\rangle = \tfrac32k_BT$; dividing by $\sigma T$ cancels $\tau$ and $n$ and leaves $\tfrac32(k_B/e)^2$. Drude's own value was twice that, through a factor-of-two slip in his calculation, and agreed remarkably well with experiment.

Why the cancellation. In the quantum theory $\langle v^2\rangle$ is of order $v_F^2$, and $(v_F/v_{\text{classical}})^2 = (1.57\times10^6/1.154\times10^5)^2 = 185$ at 293 K. Meanwhile only electrons within about $k_BT$ of the top of the filled states can absorb heat, so the electronic heat capacity is smaller than the classical $\tfrac32nk_B$: Sommerfeld's value is $\tfrac{\pi^2}{2}(k_BT/E_F)\,nk_B$, smaller by the factor $\tfrac{\pi^2}{3}k_BT/E_F = 1/84$ for copper at 293 K, with $E_F = \tfrac12mv_F^2 = 7.0$ eV (Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 2, cited; problem P13 does the arithmetic). The two errors very nearly cancel in $\kappa$, so the classical ratio is off only by the numerical factor $\tfrac32$ against $\pi^2/3$.

The moral for this course: a model can get a number right for the wrong reason. Check its pieces, not only its answers.

Three speeds, and the kettle

Three speeds in the kettle cable, on a logarithmic axis spanning thirteen decades.

Drift, 0.29 mm/s: an hour a metre — and on AC the electrons only shuffle 1.3 μm. They do not carry the news.
Fermi, $1.57\times10^6$ m/s: fast, but random, with no net direction. It carries nothing anywhere.
The field: when the switch closes, the surface charge must rearrange along the whole cable, and the field inside the copper with it. That rearrangement travels at a large fraction of $c$, set by the insulation — unit 08 computes it. Ten metres take at least 33 ns.

the puzzle, answered

The kettle learns that the switch has closed at the third speed. The electrons in its element were already there; what reaches them is a change in the field, made by surface charge moving into place along the cable.

And what carries the 2.3 kW from the fuse box to the kettle, if not the electrons? That is unit 01's question — and its answer is not in the copper at all.

the cable, closed

$n = 8.47\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $J = 4.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$, $v_{\mathrm d} = 0.29$ mm/s, $\tau = 2.49\times10^{-14}$ s, $E = 0.067\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$, $6.72\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$ and $0.672$ W per metre, 13.4 W in the 10 m run.

The bound. Nothing travels faster than $c$ (MR 24's retarded potentials: a change in the charges is felt at distance $r$ no sooner than $r/c$ later). So the far end of a 10 m cable learns of a change at the near end no sooner than $10/2.998\times10^8 = 33.4$ ns later. That the actual speed is a large fraction of $c$, fixed by the insulation between the conductors, is unit 08's result, from the telegrapher's equations; here it is only quoted ahead, with no number attached.

Why "the field" and not "a signal in the electrons". A change at one end of a long wire is a change in the surface charge there, and its field reaches along the cable at the speed of fields; the electrons everywhere then respond within $10^{-13}$ s (section 2). So the delay is set by the field, never by the electrons.

The positions on the axis. $\log_{10}$ of $2.95\times10^{-4}$, $1.154\times10^5$, $1.57\times10^6$ and $2.998\times10^8$ are $-3.53$, $5.06$, $6.20$ and $8.48$. The band for "the field" is drawn from $10^8$ m/s to $c$ only to say "a large fraction of $c$"; its left end is not a computed value.

What carries the energy is the subject of unit 01, which follows Poynting's vector (MR 24) around a battery, a coaxial cable and a resistor, and proves that the power entering any component is $vi$.
