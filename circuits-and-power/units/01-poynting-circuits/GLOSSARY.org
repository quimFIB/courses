The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: a 12 V battery drives a \(6\ \Omega\) resistor through a long
coaxial cable — copper inner conductor of radius \(a = 0.5\) mm, outer conductor
at \(b = 2.0\) mm, vacuum between. \(I = 2\) A and 24 W. In the gap \(\vec E\) is
radial and \(\vec B\) azimuthal, so \(\vec S\) runs along the cable and its flux
is exactly \(VI = 24\) W whatever the radii; the copper takes its own
\(I^2R' = 85.6\) mW per metre through its surface, and the resistor takes the
24 W through its side. Unit 00's kettle cable returns for the real-world half:
its electrons' kinetic energy carries about \(10^{-21}\) of the 2.3 kW.

* The puzzle and the tool

- Poynting flux :: The flux \(\int\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\) of the Poynting
  vector \(\vec S = \vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0\) through a surface: the power
  carried across it by the field, in watts. Through a closed surface it is
  fixed by Poynting's theorem (MR 24); through an open one it depends on the
  conventional choice of \(\vec S\). /Slide: Poynting's theorem, recalled./

* Sources

- electromotive force (EMF) :: For a loop, \(\mathcal E = \oint\vec f\cdot\mathrm
  d\vec l\), where \(\vec f = \vec f_s + \vec E\) is the total force per unit
  charge and \(\vec f_s\) the non-electrostatic force acting inside a source
  (chemical in a battery). Since \(\oint\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = 0\) for a
  static field, \(\mathcal E = \int\vec f_s\cdot\mathrm d\vec l\) through the
  source. In volts; despite its name, a work per unit charge (Griffiths §7.1).
  /Slide: What a battery adds: a force that is not electrostatic./
- ideal source :: A source with no internal resistance (\(\sigma\to\infty\)
  inside), so that \(\vec E = -\vec f_s\) there and its terminals differ in
  potential by exactly \(\mathcal E\). Inside it \(\vec J\cdot\vec E \lt 0\): it
  gives the field energy at the rate \(\mathcal EI\). /Slide: An ideal source
  hands the field energy at the rate ℰI./
- internal resistance :: The resistance \(r_i\) of a real source's interior.
  The source then emits \(\mathcal EI - I^2r_i\) through its surface, and its
  terminal potentials differ by \(\mathcal E - Ir_i\).

* The coaxial cable

- coaxial cable :: Two conductors sharing an axis: a wire (the inner
  conductor) inside a metal tube (the outer conductor), insulated from each
  other; current goes out along one and back along the other. Between them
  \(\vec E = V\hat r/(r\ln(b/a))\) and \(\vec B = \mu_0I\hat\theta/2\pi r\); outside
  both vanish. /Slide: The running example, from nothing./
- inner conductor :: The coaxial cable's central wire, radius \(a\); in the
  running example copper, 0.5 mm, carrying charge \(+\lambda\) per metre on its
  surface and current \(I\) out.
- outer conductor :: The coaxial cable's tube, inner radius \(b\), carrying
  \(-\lambda\) per metre on its inner surface and the return current \(-I\).

* p = vi, proved

- two-terminal component :: A component joined to the rest of a circuit by two
  leads only. The power flowing into it through any closed surface crossed
  only by its leads is \(vi\), if the fields on that surface are quasi-static
  and the leads cross it at equipotential cross-sections. /Slide: The
  statement./
- lead :: A conductor joining a component to the rest of the circuit. In a
  steady state a cut square to a straight lead is an equipotential.
- terminal voltage :: For a two-terminal component, \(v = \varphi_A -
  \varphi_B\), the potential difference between its two leads — unit 00's
  meaning. Unit 02 says when "voltage" needs more care.
- quasi-static :: Of the fields on a surface: \(\partial_t\vec B\) and the
  displacement current \(\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\) are negligible there, so
  that \(\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0\) and \(\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J\)
  hold as in statics. Exact in a steady state; approximate for slowly varying
  currents, which is unit 02's subject.
- thin-wire picture :: The approximation that treats each conductor of a cable
  as a line at its centre carrying its charge per length and its current. For a
  pair with \((\pm\lambda, \pm I)\) it gives \(\vec S = (\varepsilon_0I/\lambda)E^2\hat
  z\): along the cable everywhere, falling as \(1/r^4\) far away. /Slide: The
  kettle's two-wire cable, computed./

* What the electrons do

- conveyor-belt picture :: The account of a circuit in which each charge
  carries energy \(qV\) from source to load: \(I\) coulombs per second times
  \(V\) joules per coulomb. Exact for totals, by the theorem \(p = vi\); wrong
  about where the energy is, since the electrons' own kinetic energy carries
  about \(10^{-21}\) of the power and their "\(qV\)" is field energy. /Slide: The
  conveyor belt, stated fairly./
- interaction energy :: The part of the field energy of two charge
  distributions that depends on their relative position,
  \(\varepsilon_0\int\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2\,\mathrm dV = q\varphi_2(\vec x_1) =
  Q\varphi_1(\vec x_2)\): it belongs to the pair, not to either charge. /Slide:
  Where qV lives./

* How fast can energy move?

- energy-transport speed :: For a cable, the delivered power over the field
  energy stored per metre, \(VI/W'\). In vacuum \(|\vec S| \le cu\) bounds it by
  \(c\); for the running example it is \(4.31\times10^7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} =
  0.144\,c\), and it would equal \(c\) for a load of 83 Ω, where the electric and
  magnetic energies are equal. /Slide: |S| ≤ cu: energy never outruns light./
