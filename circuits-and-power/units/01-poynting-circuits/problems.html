Part A · unit 01 · problems

Where the energy flows: the Poynting vector around a circuit, and \(p = vi\) as a theorem

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You compute the Poynting flux in the insulation of a coaxial cable carrying 12 V and 2 A and get 24 W without using the radii, show that the flux into the inner conductor per metre is its own \(I^2R'\), and prove that the flux into any quasi-static two-terminal component is \(vi\) — and, for the kettle cable, show that the electrons' own kinetic energy carries about \(10^{-21}\) of the 2.3 kW, name the three things the electrons do instead, and say why the conveyor-belt picture still gets every total right.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Thirteen problems, about three and a half hours. Conventions as in the deck: cylindrical coordinates \((r, \theta, z)\) with \(\hat r\times\hat\theta = \hat z\); \(\varphi\) is the potential, \(\varrho\) and \(\Sigma\) volume and surface charge density, \(\rho\) resistivity; \(\vec S = \vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0\); the EMF is \(\mathcal E\); all insulation is vacuum. The running example: a 12 V battery, a \(6\ \Omega\) resistor, a coaxial cable with copper inner conductor of radius \(a = 0.5\) mm and outer conductor at \(b = 2.0\) mm, \(I = 2\) A.

Constants: \(\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}\), \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}\), \(e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\) C, \(m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}\) kg (CODATA); \(c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 2.998\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Copper at 20 °C: \(\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\), aluminium \(2.65\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\) (CRC); copper's conduction-electron density \(n = 8.47\times10^{28}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}\) (Ashcroft & Mermin, table 1.1).

The coaxial cable

P1 The coax by numbers routine

exercises: the fields in the gap, the direction and size of \(\vec S\), its flux, units

  1. For the running example (12 V, 2 A, \(a = 0.5\) mm, \(b = 2.0\) mm), find \(E\), \(B\) and \(|\vec S|\) at \(r = a\), \(r = 1.0\) mm and \(r = b\), and give the direction of \(\vec S\).
  2. How much of the 24 W flows between \(r = a\) and \(r = 1.0\) mm?
  3. The same cable now carries 10 A at 230 V. Find \(E\) and \(B\) at \(r = a\) and \(r = b\), \(|\vec S|\) at \(r = a\), and the total flux.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each answer is a number with units from three formulas: \(E = V/(r\ln(b/a))\), \(B = \mu_0I/2\pi r\), \(S = EB/\mu_0\).

Rung 2

For part 2 integrate \(S\) over rings of area \(2\pi r\,\mathrm dr\); the integrand is \(\propto 1/r\), so the answer is a ratio of logarithms.

Rung 3

First line: \(\ln(b/a) = \ln4 = 1.3863\), so \(E(a) = 12/(5\times10^{-4}\times1.3863) = 1.731\times10^4\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Laplace in the annulus gives \(E\), Ampère gives \(B\); both fall as \(1/r\), so \(S\) falls as \(1/r^2\) and every ring of equal ratio of radii carries the same power.

1. \(V/\ln4 = 8.656\) V and \(\mu_0I/2\pi = 4\times10^{-7}\) T·m. At \(r = 0.5\) mm: \(E = 17.3\ \mathrm{kV\,m^{-1}}\), \(B = 0.80\) mT, \(S = EB/\mu_0 = 1.10\times10^7\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). At 1.0 mm: \(8.66\ \mathrm{kV\,m^{-1}}\), 0.40 mT, \(2.76\times10^6\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). At 2.0 mm: \(4.33\ \mathrm{kV\,m^{-1}}\), 0.20 mT, \(6.89\times10^5\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). Direction: \(\hat r\times\hat\theta = \hat z\), along the cable from battery to resistor.

2. \(P = VI\ln(r/a)/\ln(b/a) = 24\times\ln2/\ln4 = 12\) W: half, since 1 mm \(= \sqrt{ab}\).

3. \(V/\ln4 = 165.9\) V, \(\mu_0I/2\pi = 2\times10^{-6}\) T·m. At \(a\): \(E = 332\ \mathrm{kV\,m^{-1}}\), \(B = 4.0\) mT, \(S = 1.06\times10^9\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). At \(b\): \(E = 83.0\ \mathrm{kV\,m^{-1}}\), \(B = 1.0\) mT. Total flux \(VI = 2.3\) kW.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\times\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). Scaling: \(E\) scales with \(V\) (×19.2), \(B\) with \(I\) (×5), so \(S\) by 95.8 — and \(1.10\times10^7\times95.8 = 1.06\times10^9\) ✓. Energy: the flux equals \(VI\) in both cases, the power the load takes ✓.

P2 Into a resistor through its side routine

exercises: continuity of tangential \(\vec E\), Ampère's law, the flux through a side, \(I^2R\)

A uniform cylindrical resistor of radius \(r_0\) and length \(L\), resistance \(R\), carries a steady current \(I\) along its axis.

  1. Find the axial component of \(\vec E\) and the field \(\vec B\) just outside its curved surface, and the radial component of \(\vec S\) there.
  2. Show that the flux of \(\vec S\) inward through the curved surface is \(I^2R\), whatever \(r_0\) and \(L\).
  3. For the running example's resistor (\(r_0 = 0.5\) mm, \(L = 10\) mm, \(6\ \Omega\), 2 A) give the numbers. Why does the radial \(\vec E\) outside the resistor, made by its surface charge, not enter the answer?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a flux: a component of \(\vec S\) normal to the side, times the side's area. Only the axial \(E_z\) and the azimuthal \(B\) make a radial \(\vec S\).

Rung 2

Inside, \(E_z = V/L\) with \(V = IR\), and tangential \(\vec E\) is continuous across the surface. Outside, Ampère on a circle of radius \(r_0\) gives \(B\).

Rung 3

First line: just outside, \(E_z = IR/L\) and \(B = \mu_0I/2\pi r_0\), so \(S_r = -E_zB/\mu_0\), using \(\hat z\times\hat\theta = -\hat r\).

Worked solution

Idea. The resistor's axial field leaks into the space just outside it by continuity; with the current's \(\vec B\) it points \(\vec S\) inward. The area grows with \(r_0L\) while \(E_zB\) falls as \(1/(r_0L)\).

1. \(E_z = IR/L\) (inside and, by continuity, just outside). \(\vec B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r_0}\hat\theta\). The part of \(\vec S\) made by these two is \(\frac1{\mu_0}E_z\hat z\times B\hat\theta = -\frac{E_zB}{\mu_0}\hat r = -\frac{I^2R}{2\pi r_0L}\hat r\): inward.

2. Inward flux \(= 2\pi r_0L\cdot\frac{I^2R}{2\pi r_0L} = I^2R\).

3. \(E_z = 12/0.01 = 1200\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\); \(B = 0.80\) mT; \(|S_r| = 1200\times8.0\times10^{-4}/1.2566\times10^{-6} = 7.64\times10^5\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\); area \(2\pi(5\times10^{-4})(0.01) = 3.14\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2}\); flux 24.0 W \(= I^2R = 4\times6\). The radial \(E_r\) made by the surface charge gives \(\vec S\) a component \(\frac1{\mu_0}E_r\hat r\times B\hat\theta\), along \(\hat z\): parallel to the curved surface, so it crosses it nowhere. It carries energy past the resistor, not into it.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{A^2\,\Omega} = \mathrm W\). Energy: by Poynting's theorem in a steady state the inward flux must equal \(\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = (I/\pi r_0^2)(IR/L)\pi r_0^2L = I^2R\) ✓. Limit: \(R\to0\) gives no flux, as for a perfect conductor ✓. MR 24's problem P4 did the same for copper.

P3 Into the copper routine

exercises: \(\vec S\) inside a conductor, its divergence, the copper's \(I^2R'\)

  1. Inside a long straight wire of radius \(a\) and resistivity \(\rho\) carrying a uniform current \(I\), find \(\vec S(r)\) for \(r \lt a\). Show that its inward flux through a coaxial cylinder of radius \(r\) and unit length equals the Joule heat made inside that cylinder, and that at \(r = a\) it is \(I^2R'\) with \(R' = \rho/\pi a^2\).
  2. Check the local form: compute \(\nabla\cdot\vec S\) and compare with \(-\vec J\cdot\vec E\).
  3. Numbers for the running example's inner conductor, and for the same conductor made of aluminium.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A vector field inside the wire, then two integrals that must agree. \(\vec E\) is axial and uniform, \(\vec B\) azimuthal and growing linearly with \(r\).

Rung 2

Ampère on a circle of radius \(r \lt a\) encloses \(Ir^2/a^2\). In cylindrical coordinates a radial field \(F_r(r)\hat r\) has divergence \(\frac1r\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dr}(rF_r)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(E_z = \rho I/\pi a^2 = IR'\), \(B = \mu_0Ir/2\pi a^2\), so \(\vec S = -\frac{IR'\,Ir}{2\pi a^2}\hat r\).

Worked solution

Idea. The deck's Inside the inner conductor slide, checked two ways: globally on every cylinder, and locally at every point.

1. \(\vec S = \frac1{\mu_0}E_z\hat z\times B\hat\theta = -\frac{I^2R'r}{2\pi a^2}\hat r\). Inward flux through the cylinder of radius \(r\), per metre: \(2\pi r\cdot\frac{I^2R'r}{2\pi a^2} = I^2R'\frac{r^2}{a^2}\). Joule heat inside it: \(\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\pi r^2 = \frac{I}{\pi a^2}IR'\pi r^2 = I^2R'\frac{r^2}{a^2}\) — equal. At \(r = a\): \(I^2R'\).

2. With \(S_r = -kr\), \(k = I^2R'/2\pi a^2\): \(\nabla\cdot\vec S = \frac1r(r\cdot(-kr))' = -2k = -I^2R'/\pi a^2\), and \(\vec J\cdot\vec E = I^2R'/\pi a^2\). So \(\nabla\cdot\vec S = -\vec J\cdot\vec E\), Poynting's theorem with \(\partial_tu = 0\) ✓.

3. \(\pi a^2 = 7.854\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}\). Copper: \(R' = 21.4\ \mathrm{m\Omega\,m^{-1}}\), \(E_z = 42.8\ \mathrm{mV\,m^{-1}}\), \(I^2R' = 85.6\ \mathrm{mW\,m^{-1}}\), surface \(|\vec S| = 27.2\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). Aluminium: \(R' = 33.7\ \mathrm{m\Omega\,m^{-1}}\), \(E_z = 67.5\ \mathrm{mV\,m^{-1}}\), \(I^2R' = 135\ \mathrm{mW\,m^{-1}}\), surface \(43.0\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\).

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{A^2\,\Omega\,m^{-1}\cdot m/m^2} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). The ratio aluminium/copper is \(2.65/1.68 = 1.58\) in every quantity, as it must be at fixed current and geometry ✓. Against the 24 W flowing past, copper takes 0.36% per metre.

The theorem

P4 Proof: \(p = vi\) medium

exercises: the curl product rule, the flux of a curl through a closed surface, naming hypotheses

  1. Prove: if a closed surface \(\Sigma\) is crossed only by two leads A and B, and on \(\Sigma\) (H1) \(\partial_t\vec B = 0\), (H2) the displacement current is negligible, (H3) each lead crosses at an equipotential, then the power flowing in through \(\Sigma\) is \((\varphi_A - \varphi_B)i\), with \(i\) the current entering at A. Say where each hypothesis is used.
  2. Show from the same hypotheses that the current leaving by B equals the current entering by A.
  3. A capacitor is charging. A surface \(\Sigma'\) encloses one plate and its lead and cuts through the gap. Which hypothesis fails on \(\Sigma'\), and what term does the proof then miss?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A proof by one identity. Write \(\vec S\) in terms of \(\varphi\) and \(\vec B\), and look for a curl that integrates to zero over a closed surface.

Rung 2

\(\nabla\times(\varphi\vec B) = \nabla\varphi\times\vec B + \varphi\nabla\times\vec B\). The flux of a curl through a closed surface vanishes: cut it into two caps and use Stokes.

Rung 3

First line: by H1, \(\vec S = -\frac1{\mu_0}\nabla\varphi\times\vec B = -\frac1{\mu_0}\big[\nabla\times(\varphi\vec B) - \varphi\nabla\times\vec B\big]\) on \(\Sigma\).

Worked solution

Idea. Move the derivative off \(\varphi\) onto \(\vec B\), where Ampère's law turns \(\nabla\times\vec B\) into the current, which lives only in the leads.

1. H1: \(\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t\vec B = 0\) in a shell round \(\Sigma\); the shell is simply connected, so \(\vec E = -\nabla\varphi\) there (MR 04). Then \(-\oint\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \frac1{\mu_0}\oint\nabla\times(\varphi\vec B)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A - \frac1{\mu_0}\oint\varphi(\nabla\times\vec B)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\). The first term is zero: split \(\Sigma\) along a curve \(C\) into two caps; by Stokes each cap's flux is the circulation of \(\varphi\vec B\) round \(C\), in opposite senses, so they cancel (no hypothesis used). H2: \(\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E \approx \mu_0\vec J\) on \(\Sigma\). So the power in is \(-\oint\varphi\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\). \(\vec J\) is non-zero on \(\Sigma\) only in the leads; H3 makes \(\varphi\) constant on each crossing; outward fluxes of \(\vec J\) are \(-i\) at A and \(+i_B\) at B. Using part 2, \(i_B = i\), so the power in is \(-(-\varphi_Ai + \varphi_Bi) = (\varphi_A - \varphi_B)i\). \(\square\)

2. By H2, \(\oint\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \frac1{\mu_0}\oint(\nabla\times\vec B)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = 0\) (the flux of a curl again), so \(-i + i_B = 0\).

3. H2 fails: in the gap \(\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\) is exactly as large as the current density in the lead would be across the same area (MR 24's displacement current), and \(\Sigma'\) cuts it. The proof misses \(-\oint_{\Sigma'}\varphi\,\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\); also part 2 fails — current enters by the lead and none leaves by another, the balance being made by displacement current through the gap. There is no second lead, so "\(v\)" has no meaning for \(\Sigma'\). The cure is to enclose the whole capacitor (P11).

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{V\cdot A} = \mathrm W\). A limit: \(\varphi_A = \varphi_B\) gives no flux, as for a perfect wire passing straight through \(\Sigma\) ✓. A case: for a steady resistor, Poynting's theorem gives the power in as \(\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = I^2R\), and \(\varphi_A - \varphi_B = IR\) ✓.

P5 Proof: any two-conductor cable medium

exercises: the vector potential in two dimensions, Green's first identity, the theorem's planar version

Two long, straight, perfect conductors of arbitrary cross-sections \(C_1\), \(C_2\), at potentials \(V\) and 0, carry currents \(+I\) and \(-I\) along \(\hat z\). The fields outside are static and independent of \(z\); in the plane, \(\vec E = -\nabla\varphi\) and \(\vec B = \nabla\times(A\hat z)\) for a function \(A(x, y)\).

  1. Show \(\vec B = \nabla A\times\hat z\) and \(S_z = \nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A/\mu_0\).
  2. Show that \(\nabla^2A = 0\) outside the conductors, and that \(\oint_{\partial C_k}\nabla A\cdot\hat n_k\,\mathrm dl = \mp\mu_0I_k\) for an appropriate sign, \(\hat n_k\) the outward normal of \(C_k\).
  3. Prove that the flux of \(S_z\) through the plane outside the conductors is \(VI\). (Assume the fields fall off fast enough that the circle at infinity contributes nothing; say why that is reasonable.)
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A proof: the same move as P4, one dimension down. The integrand \(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A\) is the kind Green's first identity handles.

Rung 2

\(\nabla\times(f\hat z) = \nabla f\times\hat z\). For part 2, the tangential component of \(\nabla A\times\hat z\) along a curve with tangent \(\hat t\) is \(\nabla A\cdot(\hat z\times\hat t)\), and \(\hat z\times\hat t\) is a normal.

Rung 3

First line of part 3: \(\int_D\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A\,\mathrm dA = \oint_{\partial D}\varphi\,\partial_\nu A\,\mathrm dl - \int_D\varphi\nabla^2A\,\mathrm dA\), with \(\nu\) the outward normal of the region \(D\) outside the conductors.

Worked solution

Idea. \(S_z\) is a dot product of two gradients; Green's identity turns its integral into boundary terms, and on each conductor the potential is constant and the boundary term is its current.

1. \(\nabla\times(A\hat z) = \nabla A\times\hat z\) (the product rule with a constant vector). Then \((\vec E\times\vec B)_z = -[\nabla\varphi\times(\nabla A\times\hat z)]_z = -[\nabla A(\nabla\varphi\cdot\hat z) - \hat z(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A)]_z = \nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A\), since \(\nabla\varphi\perp\hat z\).

2. \(\nabla\times\vec B = \nabla\times(\nabla\times A\hat z) = -\nabla^2A\,\hat z\) (as \(\nabla\cdot(A\hat z) = 0\)), and Ampère gives \(\mu_0J_z\hat z\), which is zero outside. Round \(\partial C_k\) anticlockwise with tangent \(\hat t\), the outward normal is \(\hat n_k = \hat t\times\hat z\), and \(\vec B\cdot\hat t = (\nabla A\times\hat z)\cdot\hat t = \nabla A\cdot(\hat z\times\hat t) = -\nabla A\cdot\hat n_k\). Ampère: \(\oint\vec B\cdot\hat t\,\mathrm dl = \mu_0I_k\), so \(\oint\partial_{n_k}A\,\mathrm dl = -\mu_0I_k\).

3. On \(D\), \(\nabla^2A = 0\), and the outward normal of \(D\) on \(\partial C_k\) is \(\nu = -\hat n_k\). So \(\int_DS_z\,\mathrm dA = \frac1{\mu_0}\sum_k\varphi_k\oint\partial_\nu A = \frac1{\mu_0}\sum_k\varphi_k\mu_0I_k = V\cdot I + 0\cdot(-I) = VI\). \(\square\) The circle at infinity: with net charge and net current zero, \(\varphi\) and \(A\) fall off at least as \(1/R\) and their gradients as \(1/R^2\), so \(\oint\varphi\,\partial_\nu A\sim(1/R)(1/R^2)R\to0\).

Check. Coaxial case: \(\varphi = V\ln(b/r)/\ln(b/a)\), \(A = -\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\ln r\) gives \(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A/\mu_0 = \frac{V}{r\ln(b/a)}\frac{I}{2\pi r}\), the deck's \(S\) ✓, and \(B = -A' = \mu_0I/2\pi r\) ✓. Units: \([\varphi][A]/\mu_0\) per \(\mathrm m^2\): \(\mathrm{V\cdot T\,m/(H\,m^{-1})}\cdot\mathrm m^{-2}\) — and \(\mathrm{T\,m^2/H} = \mathrm A\), so \(\mathrm{V\,A\,m^{-2}}\) ✓.

P6 Two parallel wires medium

exercises: superposing line charges and line currents, the thin-wire picture, far-field decay

In the thin-wire picture the kettle cable is two lines at \((\mp h, 0)\) carrying \((+\lambda, +I)\) and \((-\lambda, -I)\), currents along \(\pm\hat z\).

  1. Find \(\vec E\), \(\vec B\) and \(\vec S\) on the plane of symmetry \(x = 0\) as functions of \(y\).
  2. Show that far away \(|\vec S|\) falls as \(1/y^4\), and explain the exponent from how \(E\) and \(B\) fall.
  3. With \(h = 2\) mm (the deck's assumed spacing), by what factor is \(|\vec S|\) at \(y = 2\) cm smaller than at the midpoint?
  4. Show that everywhere in the plane \(|\vec S|\propto1/(r_1r_2)^2\), where \(r_1\), \(r_2\) are the distances to the two lines.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two superpositions, each of two known fields: a line charge \(\vec E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec\rho}{\rho^2}\) and a line current \(\vec B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\frac{\hat z\times\vec\rho}{\rho^2}\), with \(\vec\rho\) the planar vector from the line.

Rung 2

On \(x = 0\) both lines are at distance \(\sqrt{h^2 + y^2}\); the \(y\)-components of \(\vec E\) cancel and the \(x\)-components of \(\vec B\) cancel. For part 4 use the deck's relation \(\vec B = (\mu_0\varepsilon_0I/\lambda)\hat z\times\vec E\).

Rung 3

First line: from \(+\lambda\) at \((-h, 0)\), \(\vec E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(h, y)}{h^2 + y^2}\); from \(-\lambda\) at \((h, 0)\), \(-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{(-h, y)}{h^2 + y^2}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Each wire alone has \(\vec E\perp\vec B\) with \(\vec E\times\vec B\) along \(\hat z\); for the pair, the same proportionality holds, so \(\vec S\) is along the cable and goes as \(E^2\).

1. Summing: \(\vec E = \frac{\lambda h}{\pi\varepsilon_0(h^2 + y^2)}\hat x\). For \(\vec B\): from \(+I\) at \((-h, 0)\), \(\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\frac{\hat z\times(h, y)}{h^2 + y^2} = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\frac{(-y, h)}{h^2 + y^2}\); from \(-I\) at \((h, 0)\), \(-\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\frac{(-y, -h)}{h^2 + y^2}\). Sum: \(\vec B = \frac{\mu_0Ih}{\pi(h^2 + y^2)}\hat y\). So \(\vec S = \frac{\lambda Ih^2}{\pi^2\varepsilon_0(h^2 + y^2)^2}\hat z\), along \(+\hat z\), the direction of the \(+\lambda\) wire's current.

2. For \(y\gg h\), \(S\approx\lambda Ih^2/(\pi^2\varepsilon_0y^4)\). Each field is that of a pair of opposite sources a distance \(2h\) apart — a two-dimensional dipole — and falls as \(1/y^2\) instead of a single line's \(1/y\); the product falls as \(1/y^4\).

3. \(\big(h^2/(h^2 + y^2)\big)^2 = (4/404)^2 = 9.80\times10^{-5}\): about ten thousand times weaker 2 cm away.

4. For each wire alone \(\vec B = (\mu_0\varepsilon_0I/\lambda)\hat z\times\vec E\) (same ratio, same sign for both wires), so for the sum \(\vec S = (\varepsilon_0I/\lambda)E^2\hat z\). And \(\vec E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\big(\frac{\vec\rho_1}{\rho_1^2} - \frac{\vec\rho_2}{\rho_2^2}\big)\), whose length is \(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{|\vec\rho_1 - \vec\rho_2|}{\rho_1\rho_2}\) (inversion \(\vec\rho\mapsto\vec\rho/\rho^2\) scales distances by \(1/(\rho_1\rho_2)\)), with \(|\vec\rho_1 - \vec\rho_2| = 2h\). So \(|\vec S|\propto1/(r_1r_2)^2\); its contours \(r_1r_2 = \) const are Cassini ovals.

Check. Part 4 at \(x = 0\): \(r_1 = r_2 = \sqrt{h^2 + y^2}\) and \(E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2h}{h^2 + y^2}\), agreeing with part 1 ✓. Units of \(\lambda Ih^2/\varepsilon_0y^4\): \(\mathrm{C\,m^{-1}\cdot A\cdot m^2/(F\,m^{-1}\cdot m^4)} = \mathrm{V\,A\,m^{-2}}\) ✓. The identity in the inversion step: \(|\vec u/u^2 - \vec w/w^2|^2 = 1/u^2 - 2\vec u\cdot\vec w/(u^2w^2) + 1/w^2 = |\vec u - \vec w|^2/(u^2w^2)\).

P7 The battery medium

exercises: the EMF, \(\vec J = \sigma(\vec E + \vec f_s)\), the energy balance of a real source

A battery of EMF \(\mathcal E\) has internal resistance \(r_i\): inside it \(\vec J = \sigma(\vec E + \vec f_s)\) with finite \(\sigma\). Model it as a uniform cylinder, length \(\ell\), cross-section \(A\), with \(\vec f_s\) along its axis and \(r_i = \ell/\sigma A\).

  1. Show that with current \(I\) the potential rises across it by \(\mathcal E - Ir_i\).
  2. Show that \(\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV\) over the battery is \(I^2r_i - \mathcal EI\), and hence that in a steady state it emits \(\mathcal EI - I^2r_i\) through any closed surface round it. Where does the \(I^2r_i\) go?
  3. Check against section 5's theorem.
  4. Numbers: \(\mathcal E = 12\) V, \(r_i = 0.1\ \Omega\) (an assumption for the problem), load \(6\ \Omega\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

An energy balance: three powers that must add up. Solve Ohm's law with a source term for \(\vec E\).

Rung 2

\(\vec E = \vec J/\sigma - \vec f_s\). The potential rise from − to + is \(-\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l\) along the axis.

Rung 3

First line: \(\varphi_+ - \varphi_- = \int_0^\ell(f_s - J/\sigma)\,\mathrm dl = \mathcal E - \frac{I\ell}{\sigma A}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Inside a real source two things happen at once: the source force gives the field energy at \(\mathcal EI\), and the field gives some back to the charges as heat in the internal resistance.

1. \(\varphi_+ - \varphi_- = \mathcal E - Ir_i\).

2. \(\vec J\cdot\vec E = J^2/\sigma - \vec J\cdot\vec f_s\). Integrated: \(\frac{I^2}{\sigma A^2}A\ell - \frac IA f_sA\ell = I^2r_i - \mathcal EI\). Steady state: \(-\oint\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \int\vec J\cdot\vec E\), so the outflow is \(\mathcal EI - I^2r_i\). The \(I^2r_i\) is Joule heat inside the battery: the battery gets warm.

3. The theorem round the battery: current enters at its − terminal, so power in \(= (\varphi_- - \varphi_+)I = -(\mathcal E - Ir_i)I\), an outflow of \(\mathcal EI - I^2r_i\) ✓.

4. \(I = 12/6.1 = 1.967\) A. \(\mathcal EI = 23.61\) W; \(I^2r_i = 0.387\) W; emitted 23.22 W; terminal voltage 11.80 V; the load takes \(I^2R = 1.967^2\times6 = 23.22\) W ✓.

Check. Limit \(r_i\to0\): the ideal source of the deck, \(\mathcal EI\) out ✓. Units: \(\mathrm{A^2\,\Omega} = \mathrm W\). Energy: what the source force supplies (23.61 W) = internal heat (0.39 W) + load (23.22 W) ✓. Which load takes the most power is unit 04's question.

exercises: the electrons' kinetic energy and its flow, a ratio of 21 powers of ten, interaction energy

  1.   2. In which direction does that kinetic energy flow in the live conductor, relative to the kettle?

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is numbers: electrons per metre times kinetic energy each, then times the speed. Part 3 is an identity.

Rung 2

Electrons per metre: \(nA\). \(v_{\mathrm d} = I/(neA)\), so the flow \(nA\cdot\frac12m_ev_{\mathrm d}^3\) goes as \(A\cdot A^{-3}\). For part 3 use Green's first identity and Poisson's equation.

Rung 3

First line: \(v_{\mathrm d} = 10/(8.47\times10^{28}\times1.602\times10^{-19}\times2.5\times10^{-6}) = 2.95\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Worked solution

Idea. If the electrons carried the energy as their own, their kinetic energy flux would be the power. It is not within twenty orders of magnitude; and the "potential energy" they are said to carry belongs to the field.

1. \(2.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\): \(v_{\mathrm d} = 2.95\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\); \(nA = 2.12\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\); \(K' = nA\cdot\frac12m_ev_{\mathrm d}^2 = 8.38\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}\); flow \(K'v_{\mathrm d} = 2.47\times10^{-18}\) W; ratio \(1.07\times10^{-21}\). \(1.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\): \(v_{\mathrm d} = 4.91\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\); \(K' = 1.40\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}\); flow \(6.86\times10^{-18}\) W; ratio \(2.98\times10^{-21}\). The flow is \(\frac12m_e I^3/(n^2e^3A^2)\), so it goes as \(1/A^2\): \((2.5/1.5)^2 = 2.78\), and indeed \(6.86/2.47 = 2.78\).

2. Electrons drift against \(\vec J\); in the live conductor \(\vec J\) points towards the kettle, so the electrons and their kinetic energy move away from it. In the neutral they move towards it; the two cancel.

3. \(q\varphi_Q = qQ/4\pi\varepsilon_0r\) changes with \(r\), i.e. when \(Q\) moves. With \(\vec E_q = -\nabla\varphi_q\), \(\varepsilon_0\int\nabla\varphi_q\cdot\nabla\varphi_Q = -\varepsilon_0\int\varphi_q\nabla^2\varphi_Q = \int\varphi_q\varrho_Q = Q\varphi_q(\vec x_Q)\), and by symmetry also \(q\varphi_Q(\vec x_q)\) (the boundary term at infinity vanishes as \(1/R\)). Check. Units: \(\mathrm{m^{-1}\cdot kg\,m^2s^{-2}} = \mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}\); \(\times\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm W\). The bookkeeping is still right: \(10\ \mathrm{C\,s^{-1}}\times 230\ \mathrm{J\,C^{-1}} = 2.3\) kW, which by the theorem of the deck's section 5 is the Poynting flux into the kettle ✓.

exercises: the AM–GM inequality, equality cases, integrating a pointwise bound

  1. Prove that in vacuum \(|\vec S| \le cu\) at every point, and that equality holds exactly when \(\vec E\perp\vec B\) and \(E = cB\).
  2. Show that MR 24's plane wave is an equality case.
  3. Deduce that for any cable with vacuum insulation, \(VI \le cW'\), where \(W'\) is the field energy per metre. Is the static field of a single charge an equality case of part 1?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A proof by two inequalities, each with its own equality case.

Rung 2

Write \(x = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0}E\), \(y = B/\sqrt{\mu_0}\); then \(u = \frac12(x^2 + y^2)\), and \(xy\) is \(EB\) times a constant.

Rung 3

First line: \(|\vec S| = EB\sin\alpha/\mu_0 \le EB/\mu_0 = c\,xy\), since \(xy = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0/\mu_0}\,EB\) and \(c\sqrt{\varepsilon_0/\mu_0} = 1/\mu_0\).

Worked solution

Idea. The flux is a product of the two field strengths; the energy is a sum of their squares; AM–GM compares them.

1. \(|\vec S| \le c\,xy \le \frac c2(x^2 + y^2) = cu\), the last by \((x - y)^2 \ge 0\). Equality in the first needs \(\sin\alpha = 1\), \(\vec E\perp\vec B\) (or one field zero); in the second \(x = y\), \(\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}E = B/\sqrt{\mu_0}\), i.e. \(E = cB\). Both: \(\vec E\perp\vec B\) and \(E = cB\) (which excludes one field zero unless both are). \(\square\)

2. In MR 24's plane wave \(\vec B = \hat k\times\vec E/c\), so \(\vec B\perp\vec E\) and \(B = E/c\) ✓: there \(|\vec S| = cu\), energy moving at \(c\).

3. \(VI = \int S_z\,\mathrm dA \le \int|\vec S|\,\mathrm dA \le c\int u\,\mathrm dA = cW'\). A single static charge has \(\vec B = 0\), so \(\vec S = 0\) while \(u \gt 0\): far from equality — its energy goes nowhere.

Check. Units: \([cu] = \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}\cdot J\,m^{-3}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\) ✓. P10 shows the integrated bound is attained for one load.

exercises: field energy per unit length, optimising a ratio, the equality case of P9

  1. For the coaxial gap, show \(W'_E = \pi\varepsilon_0V^2/\ln(b/a)\) and \(W'_B = \mu_0I^2\ln(b/a)/4\pi\), and give their values for the running example.
  2. With a load \(R\) at the end (\(V = IR\)), show \(VI/W' = c\cdot2x/(1 + x^2)\) with \(x = R/R_*\), and find \(R_*\) for \(b/a = 4\).

Rung 1

Two integrals over the annulus, then a function of one variable to maximise.

Rung 2

Both integrands go as \(1/r^2\) times \(2\pi r\,\mathrm dr\). Divide numerator and denominator by \(I^2\); the two terms of the denominator are equal at the optimum.

Rung 3

First line: \(W'_E = \int_a^b\frac{\varepsilon_0}2\frac{V^2}{r^2\ln^2(b/a)}2\pi r\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\pi\varepsilon_0V^2}{\ln(b/a)}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The flux is fixed at \(VI\); the stored energy is a sum of an electric part \(\propto V^2\) and a magnetic part \(\propto I^2\). For given power, the ratio is best when neither dominates.

1. \(W'_B = \int_a^b\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\frac{\mu_0^2I^2}{4\pi^2r^2}2\pi r\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\mu_0I^2\ln(b/a)}{4\pi}\). Running example: \(W'_E = 2.89\ \mathrm{nJ\,m^{-1}}\), \(W'_B = 0.555\ \mu\mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}\).

2. \(VI/W' = R/(\pi\varepsilon_0R^2/L + \mu_0L/4\pi)\), \(L = \ln(b/a)\). The two terms are equal at \(R_*^2 = \mu_0L^2/(4\pi^2\varepsilon_0)\), i.e. \(R_* = \frac{L}{2\pi}\sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0}\); writing \(K = \mu_0L/4\pi\) the ratio is \(\frac{R_*x}{K(1 + x^2)}\), and \(R_*/K = 2/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 2c\). P11 The charging capacitor, revisited medium

exercises: Poynting's theorem with stored energy, choosing \(\Sigma\), the hypotheses H1–H3

  1. Take the region \(V\) between the plates. Write Poynting's theorem for it as an energy balance, and check it with numbers at the moment \(V = 100\) V.
  2. Now take a closed surface \(\Sigma\) round the whole capacitor, crossed by its two leads. Which of H1–H3 hold on \(\Sigma\), and what does the theorem then give? Compare with \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac12CV^2\).
  3. Why does the region of part 1 — whose surface is crossed by no lead at all — still receive \(VI\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

An energy balance twice: once for a region with no current inside, once for a closed surface crossed by leads.

Rung 2

Between the plates \(\vec J = 0\), so the balance is inflow \(= \mathrm dU/\mathrm dt\). On \(\Sigma\) away from the plates the field of an ideal parallel-plate capacitor is negligible; \(B\) is steady because \(I\) is.

Rung 3

First line: at the rim \(E = V/d = 1.00\times10^5\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) and \(B = \mu_0I/2\pi a = 4.00\ \mu\)T, so \(|\vec S| = EB/\mu_0\), inward.

Worked solution

Idea. The changing field inside a capacitor is where H2 fails, so the theorem applies to surfaces that keep the gap inside them.

1. With \(\vec J = 0\): \(-\oint\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \mathrm dU/\mathrm dt\). \(|\vec S| = (10^5)(4.00\times10^{-6})/1.2566\times10^{-6} = 3.18\times10^5\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\) through the rim, area \(2\pi ad = 3.14\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}\): 100 W in. Stored energy \(\frac12CV^2\) grows at \(CV\dot V = VI = 100\) W ✓ (MR 24's worked example).

2. On \(\Sigma\): H1 holds (\(I\) steady, so \(\vec B\) static); H2 holds where \(\Sigma\) runs outside the plates, where \(\vec E\) and its rate of change are negligible; H3 holds at the lead crossings. So power in \(= (\varphi_A - \varphi_B)I = VI\), and Poynting's theorem for the enclosed region, with no Joule heat (ideal leads), says it all goes into \(\mathrm d(\frac12CV^2)/\mathrm dt = VI\) ✓. At 100 V: 100 W.

3. Its surface — the plates' inner faces and the rim — is crossed by no conduction current, but it is crossed by displacement current: \(\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\) runs across the gap and so through both plate faces, a total \(I\) in through the face of the plate at \(V\) and out through the face of the plate at 0 (none through the rim, where \(\vec E\) is tangential). So H2 fails on this surface, and the term the proof dropped in P4.3 is the whole answer: with \(\vec J = 0\) on the surface, step 4 of the proof becomes \(-\oint\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = -\oint\varphi\,\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\), and with \(\varphi\) constant on each plate face this is \(-(V(-I) + 0\cdot I) = VI\). P12 The ambiguity medium

exercises: the flux of a curl, divergence-free flows, what a closed-surface theorem can and cannot fix

  1. Show that \(\vec S' = \vec S + \nabla\times\vec F\) obeys Poynting's local law whenever \(\vec S\) does, and has the same flux through every closed surface. Give an open surface through which the two fluxes can differ.
  2. Feynman (Lectures, vol. II, ch. 27) places a static charge beside a bar magnet. Show that \(\nabla\cdot\vec S = 0\) everywhere outside the magnet, and conclude what the flow \(\vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0\) can and cannot do there.
  3. Which numbers in the deck are independent of the choice of \(\vec S\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A proof by \(\nabla\cdot\nabla\times = 0\), then an argument about a divergence-free field.

Rung 2

The flux of a curl through a closed surface vanishes (Stokes on two caps). In the static case \(\partial_tu = 0\), and outside the magnet \(\vec J = 0\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\partial_tu + \nabla\cdot\vec S' = \partial_tu + \nabla\cdot\vec S + \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec F) = -\vec J\cdot\vec E\).

Worked solution

Idea. The local law constrains only the divergence of \(\vec S\), so any divergence-free addition is invisible to it and to every closed-surface balance.

1. The local law follows from \(\nabla\cdot\nabla\times\vec F = 0\); closed fluxes agree because \(\oint(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = 0\). Open surfaces: take \(\vec F = \frac12f\,(-y, x, 0)\) for a smooth bump function \(f\) equal to 1 near the origin, so \(\nabla\times\vec F = \hat z\) there; the flux through a small disc in the \(xy\)-plane at the origin changes by the disc's area.

2. Static: \(\partial_tu = 0\). Outside the magnet no current flows, so \(\vec J\cdot\vec E = 0\) (the charge itself has no current). Hence \(\nabla\cdot\vec S = 0\): through every closed surface the net flux is zero, so \(\vec S\) cannot carry energy from one place to another; it can only circulate, which is Feynman's picture of energy going round in loops, accumulating nowhere.

3. Every closed-surface flux: the 24 W into the resistor, \(-24\) W at the battery, 85.6 mW into a metre of copper, \(vi\) for any component. The 24 W through the gap's cross-section too, with one mild condition: close that cross-section with a cap round the battery end running outside the cable, where the fields vanish. The closed surface's flux is fixed, and any energy flux built from the local fields (as \(\vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0\) is) puts nothing through the cap; part 1's bare \(\nabla\times\vec F\) carries no such guarantee, which is why only closed surfaces are physics without conditions. The streamline shapes are the conventional choice.

Check. The deck's figures obey part 1's moral: every computed figure is labelled with a closed-surface total, and the sketches are labelled sketches. MR 24 notes that relativity singles out \(\vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0\); that is cited, not proved here.

Peak amplitudes are written with a hat in this problem: \(v(t) = \hat V\cos\omega t\). Treat the fields as quasi-static, so the flux into a component at each instant is \(v(t)i(t)\).

  1. Across a resistor \(R\), \(i = v/R\). Show the flux into it is never negative. For the kettle (23 Ω on 230 V RMS, \(\hat V = \sqrt2\cdot230\) V) give its peak and its mean.
  2. Across an ideal capacitor, \(Q = Cv\) (MR 24) and \(i = \mathrm dQ/\mathrm dt\). Show the flux into it averages zero over a cycle, and give its amplitude for \(C = 10\ \mu\)F (assumed) on the same supply at 50 Hz.

Rung 1

Two functions of time, \(v(t)i(t)\), and their means over a period.

Rung 2
