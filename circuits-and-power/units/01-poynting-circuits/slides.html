01

Where the energy flows: the Poynting vector around a circuit, and $p = vi$ as a theorem

The energy a circuit delivers travels through the space round its wires, not through the copper. A coaxial cable shows it in numbers, and one identity proves the flux into any component is $vi$ — which is why the picture of charges carrying energy gets every total right and the location wrong.

needs 00, MR 23, MR 24~2 h slides + 3½ h problemsproblems: ./cap problems 01

Unit 00 ended on a question. The electrons in the kettle cable drift at 0.29 mm/s, and on mains they go nowhere at all; yet 2.3 kW reaches the kettle. What carries it? Mechanics to Relativity already holds the answer, in MR 24's Poynting's theorem: energy lives in the field, at density $u = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2E^2 + \tfrac1{2\mu_0}B^2$, and flows with flux density $\vec S = \vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0$. This unit applies that theorem to circuits, where the fields are steady or slowly varying, and finds the energy flowing through the insulation and the air, guided by the wires.

It also proves the statement circuit theory rests on: the power delivered to a component is $p = vi$, the potential difference across its two leads times the current through them. Unit 00 used $VI$ as the Joule heat inside a resistor; here it becomes a theorem about the energy crossing any closed surface round any component, with its hypotheses stated. Unit 02 turns those hypotheses into the lumped approximation.

The puzzle and the tool

Unit 00 left 2.3 kW arriving at a kettle through electrons that crawl. MR 24 already has the theorem that says where energy is and how it moves.

01 · the puzzle

Unit 00's question

The kettle cable: two copper conductors, 10 m long, carrying 10 A out to a kettle and back. The kettle turns $230\ \mathrm V\times10\ \mathrm A = 2.3$ kW into heat.

Unit 00 found the electrons in the copper drifting at 0.29 mm/s. On 50 Hz mains they reverse 100 times a second and shuffle about 1.3 μm either way; none of them ever travels from the fuse box to the kettle.

the puzzle

Energy arrives at the kettle at 2.3 kW, steadily. Whatever carries it must cross the 10 m — and the electrons do not.

Two candidate answers will be tested: the electrons' own kinetic energy (section 6: it is $10^{-21}$ of what is needed), and the field (this unit's answer).

The run from unit 00, as a sketch. The energy arrives steadily; the electrons stay where they are.

The numbers, from unit 00. $J = 10/(2.5\times10^{-6}) = 4.0\times10^6\ \mathrm{A\,m^{-2}}$ and $v_{\mathrm d} = J/ne = 2.95\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. At that speed 10 m take $10/2.95\times10^{-4} = 3.39\times10^4$ s, over 9 hours. On mains the drift velocity alternates at 50 Hz with peak $\sqrt2\,v_{\mathrm d}$, so an electron's excursion is $\sqrt2\,v_{\mathrm d}/\omega = 1.3\ \mu$m either side.

The steady-state simplification. As in unit 00, the 230 V and 10 A are RMS values and the cable is treated as if it carried a steady 10 A at 230 V. At 50 Hz the fields round the cable follow the current instant by instant (unit 02 makes "follow" precise); the closing slide says what changes when the sign alternates.

Why "what crosses the 10 m" is the right question. Energy is conserved locally, not only globally: MR 24's Poynting's theorem says that energy cannot vanish at the fuse box and appear at the kettle; it must cross every surface in between. So something crosses the plane that cuts the cable halfway, at 2.3 kW. The next slide recalls the theorem that says what.

01 · the tool

Poynting's theorem, recalled

Poynting's theorem (MR 24, proved there) $$u = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 + \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2,\qquad \vec S = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\vec E\times\vec B,$$ $$\partial_tu + \nabla\cdot\vec S = -\vec J\cdot\vec E .$$

For a fixed region $V$, by the divergence theorem (MR 05):

$$-\oint_{\partial V}\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_Vu\,\mathrm dV + \int_V\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV .$$

Power flowing in through the boundary = rate of increase of field energy inside + work done on the charges inside. Its flux through a surface is the Poynting flux.

Units: $[EB/\mu_0] = \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}\cdot T\cdot A^2N^{-1}} = \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}\cdot A\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ ✓, since $\mathrm T = \mathrm{N\,A^{-1}m^{-1}}$.

A sketch of the balance. Where $\vec J\cdot\vec E \gt 0$ the field gives energy to the charges; where $\vec J\cdot\vec E \lt 0$ it takes energy from them.

$\vec J\cdot\vec E \gt 0$ in a resistor is unit 00's Joule heat. A place with $\vec J\cdot\vec E \lt 0$ — current flowing against the field — is where something else pushes the charges: a source. Section 2.

Recalled, not re-derived. MR 24 proves the local law by dotting Faraday's law with $\vec B/\mu_0$ and the Ampère–Maxwell law with $\vec E/\mu_0$ and using $\nabla\cdot(\vec E\times\vec B) = \vec B\cdot\nabla\times\vec E - \vec E\cdot\nabla\times\vec B$. The integral form follows by integrating over a fixed region and applying the divergence theorem to $\nabla\cdot\vec S$.

In a steady state nothing changes, $\partial_tu = 0$, and the balance reads: power in through the boundary = $\int_V\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV$. That is the form used for most of this unit.

Notation. MR 24 writes $\vec S = \vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0$ with no $\vec H$, and so does this course: in vacuum $\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0$, and the insulation here is treated as vacuum (section 3). Volume charge density is $\varrho$ and surface charge density $\Sigma$, as in unit 00, because $\rho$ and $\sigma$ are resistivity and conductivity.

The units, slowly. From $\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B$, a tesla is $\mathrm{N\,s\,C^{-1}m^{-1}} = \mathrm{N\,A^{-1}m^{-1}}$; $\mu_0$ is in $\mathrm{N\,A^{-2}}$. So $B/\mu_0$ is in $\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}}$, and $E\cdot B/\mu_0$ in $\mathrm{V\,A\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$.

History. MR 24 records the theorem as Poynting's, 1884, found independently by Heaviside, and notes that Poynting's own first example was a current-carrying wire with its heat entering through the side — section 4's subject.

section 2

Sources: the battery as an electromotive force

A static field cannot drive a current round a closed loop. Something else must push the charges somewhere — and there the field gains energy instead of losing it.

01 · sources

What a battery adds: a force that is not electrostatic

A static $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ has $\oint\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = 0$ round any loop (MR 04). A steady current round a loop through resistance needs net work done on the charges each lap. So somewhere a non-electrostatic force acts.

source force, and electromotive force (Griffiths §7.1)

In a source, a force per unit charge $\vec f_s$ — chemical in a battery — acts besides $\vec E$, and Ohm's law becomes $\vec J = \sigma(\vec E + \vec f_s)$. With $\vec f = \vec f_s + \vec E$ the total force per unit charge, the EMF round a loop is

$$\mathcal E = \oint\vec f\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \int_{\text{source}}\vec f_s\cdot\mathrm d\vec l .$$

Units: $\vec f$ is force per charge, $\mathrm{N\,C^{-1}} = \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$; so $\mathcal E$ is in volts ✓. "Force" in the name is historical: it is work per unit charge.

The second equality: $\oint\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = 0$, and $\vec f_s = 0$ outside the source.

A sketch, not a circuit diagram (those start in unit 02). Inside the battery the source force drives the current up, against the field.

Why a source is unavoidable. In a steady state the Joule heat $\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV$ in the resistor is positive (unit 00). By Poynting's theorem with $\partial_tu = 0$, the integral of $\vec J\cdot\vec E$ over all space is minus the flux of $\vec S$ through a sphere at infinity, which vanishes for static fields of a bounded circuit ($E$ falls off at least as $1/r^2$, $B$ as a magnetic dipole's $1/r^3$, so the flux through a sphere of radius $r$ goes as $r^2\cdot r^{-5}\to0$). So $\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = 0$ over all space, and somewhere $\vec J\cdot\vec E \lt 0$: current flows against the field. With $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ alone that is impossible, since $\sigma\vec E\cdot\vec E \ge 0$. Hence a force other than $\vec E$.

The source of the definition. Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics, §7.1 (its subsection on electromotive force) writes the force per unit charge as $\vec f = \vec f_s + \vec E$ and defines the EMF as $\oint\vec f\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$. This course writes the EMF as $\mathcal E$, as Griffiths does, to keep the letter $\varepsilon$ for $\varepsilon_0$.

What $\vec f_s$ is in a battery — electrochemistry at the electrodes — is outside this course; all that matters here is that it is a force on the charges that is not the gradient of the electrostatic potential. In a generator it is the magnetic force on moving charges, or an induced electric field; units 02 and 07 meet those.

The two signs in the figure. Outside the battery, current flows from the + terminal through the leads and the resistor to the − terminal, down the potential. Inside the battery it flows from − to +, up the potential, pushed by $\vec f_s$. The field $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ points from + to − everywhere, including inside the battery; so there $\vec J$ and $\vec E$ are opposed.

01 · sources

An ideal source hands the field energy at the rate ℰI

ideal source

If $\sigma\to\infty$ inside the source (no internal resistance), then $\vec E = -\vec f_s$ there, the potential rises by $\mathcal E$ from its − to its + terminal, and

$$\int_{\text{source}}\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = -\mathcal EI .$$
proof

$\vec J = \sigma(\vec E + \vec f_s)$ stays finite as $\sigma\to\infty$ only if $\vec E + \vec f_s\to\vec 0$. Then $\varphi_+ - \varphi_- = -\int_-^+\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \int_-^+\vec f_s\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \mathcal E$. For a source of length $\ell$ and cross-section $A$ carrying a uniform current along $\vec f_s$: $\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = -(I/A)f_s\,A\,\mathrm dl$, so the integral is $-I\int f_s\,\mathrm dl = -\mathcal EI$. $\square$

The potential once round the loop, ideal battery and ideal leads. The rise and the fall cancel, as $\oint\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = 0$ requires: $\mathcal E = V$.

So $\vec J\cdot\vec E \lt 0$: the source gives the field energy at the rate $\mathcal EI$, and by Poynting's theorem, in a steady state, $\mathcal EI$ flows out through any surface round it. With an internal resistance $r_i$ the outflow is $\mathcal EI - I^2r_i$ (problem P7).

Units: $\mathrm{V\cdot A} = \mathrm W$ ✓. Check: $I = 0$ gives no power ✓.

Full proof, for any shape of source

Let the source occupy a region $D$ whose surface is insulated except for two terminal faces, the − face $T_-$ and the + face $T_+$, each an equipotential. In the ideal limit $\vec E = -\vec f_s$ in $D$, and $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$, so $\int_D\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = -\int_D\vec J\cdot\nabla\varphi\,\mathrm dV$. Since $\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, $\vec J\cdot\nabla\varphi = \nabla\cdot(\varphi\vec J)$, and the divergence theorem gives $-\oint_{\partial D}\varphi\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A$. Only the terminal faces carry current: $I$ enters through $T_-$ (outward flux $-I$) and leaves through $T_+$ (outward flux $+I$). So the integral is $-(\varphi_+I - \varphi_-I) = -(\varphi_+ - \varphi_-)I = -\mathcal EI$. The slide's version is the special case of a uniform straight source. The same manipulation — $\vec J\cdot\nabla\varphi = \nabla\cdot(\varphi\vec J)$ — is the heart of section 5's theorem.

The running example's battery. Ideal, $\mathcal E = 12$ V; it supplies $\mathcal EI = 24$ W, all of which is dissipated in the 6 Ω resistor (less the copper's small share, section 4). The figure takes the leads as ideal too; with real leads the flat stretches tilt down very slightly, by $IR' = 42.8$ mV per metre of the cable's inner conductor.

With internal resistance. $\vec E = \vec J/\sigma - \vec f_s$, so $\int\vec J\cdot\vec E = \int J^2/\sigma - \mathcal EI = I^2r_i - \mathcal EI$, and the potential rise across the source is $\mathcal E - Ir_i$. Problem P7 does this in full. Unit 04 asks what load takes the most power from such a source.

section 3

The coaxial cable

Two fields, each from a theorem MR 23 already proved: a radial $\vec E$ from the surface charge, an azimuthal $\vec B$ from the current. Their cross product points along the cable, through the gap — and integrates to exactly $VI$.

01 · the coaxial cable

The running example, from nothing

coaxial cable

A wire, the inner conductor, along the axis of a metal tube, the outer conductor, with insulation between. Current goes out along one and back along the other.

The example. Inner radius $a = 0.5$ mm (copper), outer conductor from $b = 2.0$ mm, length many times $b$. The battery and the resistor are each a short cylinder of radius $a$ in series with the inner conductor, one at each end; metal plates close both ends, joining inner to outer. The outer conductor is taken as perfect.

the insulation is vacuum

Throughout, the gap holds vacuum ($\varepsilon_0$, $\mu_0$). A real, non-magnetic insulator would change none of the results here — $\vec E$ is fixed by the potential difference, $\vec B$ by the current, the flux by both — but it would change the stored energy. Unit 02 brings in dielectrics.

The running example as a sketch. Battery and resistor sit in the inner conductor; the outer conductor and the end plates carry the return current.

Why this arrangement. Putting both the battery and the resistor in series with the inner conductor, with the ends closed by plates, makes the whole circuit symmetric about the axis. Every field then depends on $r$ and $z$ only, $\vec B$ is purely azimuthal everywhere, and the long middle stretch of the cable is exactly the infinite coaxial line of MR 23's methods. Near each end the fields are more complicated, but the general theorem of section 5 needs only closed surfaces, so nothing depends on the details there.

Coordinates. Cylindrical $(r, \theta, z)$ with $z$ along the cable from battery to resistor, as unit 00 wrote them; MR 23 and MR 24 write $s$ and $\hat\phi$ for the same things, but here $\varphi$ is the potential. The orientation used throughout: $\hat r\times\hat\theta = \hat z$, $\hat\theta\times\hat z = \hat r$, $\hat z\times\hat r = \hat\theta$.

About the vacuum. In a uniform insulator of permittivity $\varepsilon$ the potential in the gap still solves Laplace's equation with the same boundary values, so $\vec E$ is unchanged for the same $V$; the surface charge on the conductors is then larger by $\varepsilon/\varepsilon_0$, but the free charge is not what the energy flow depends on. A non-magnetic insulator leaves $\vec B$ unchanged too. So $\vec S = \vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0$ and its flux $VI$ are unchanged, while the electric energy density, $\tfrac12\varepsilon E^2$ there, is larger. Unit 02 states the dielectric facts this relies on, with a source; this unit uses none of them.

The battery as a cylinder is a modelling choice: any ideal source in that position does the same, since only the EMF and the current enter the results.

01 · the coaxial cable

The electric field: Laplace in an annulus

In the long middle stretch, inner conductor at potential $V = 12$ V, outer at 0. Off the ends, $\varphi = \varphi(r)$, and MR 23's radial solution in two dimensions is $\alpha + \beta\ln r$. Fitting $\varphi(a) = V$, $\varphi(b) = 0$:

the field in the gap $$\varphi = V\frac{\ln(b/r)}{\ln(b/a)},\qquad \vec E = \frac{V}{r\ln(b/a)}\,\hat r .$$

Units: $\mathrm V/\mathrm m$ ✓. Check: $\int_a^bE\,\mathrm dr = V$ ✓.

Who makes it. Gauss's law on a cylinder of radius $r$: the inner conductor carries charge per length

$$\lambda = 2\pi\varepsilon_0rE = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0V}{\ln(b/a)} = 4.82\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{C\,m^{-1}},$$

on its surface, and the outer conductor $-\lambda$ on its inner surface. This is Jackson's second role of surface charge (unit 00): it provides the field outside the conductors.

Cross-section, radii to scale. A thin layer of charge on each conductor; between them, $E \propto 1/r$.

The radial solution. MR 23, Radial solutions, and where the dimension enters: in $\mathbb R^2$ a harmonic function of $r$ alone is $\alpha + \beta\ln r$. Here the cable's long middle stretch is translation-invariant in $z$ and the potential is constant on each conductor, so $\varphi$ depends on $r$ only. $\varphi(b) = 0$ gives $\alpha = -\beta\ln b$; $\varphi(a) = V$ gives $\beta = -V/\ln(b/a)$; so $\varphi = V\ln(b/r)/\ln(b/a)$ and $E_r = -\varphi' = V/(r\ln(b/a))$. Uniqueness (MR 23) says this is the solution.

"Constant on each conductor". Not exactly: the inner conductor's potential falls by $IR' = 42.8$ mV per metre along it (section 4). Against 12 V that is a correction of 0.36% per metre, ignored on this slide and restored on Near the copper, the flow tilts.

The numbers. $\ln4 = 1.3863$. $\lambda = 2\pi(8.854\times10^{-12})(12)/1.3863 = 4.816\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{C\,m^{-1}}$, about $3.0\times10^9$ electron charges per metre. As a surface density on the inner conductor, $\Sigma = \lambda/2\pi a = 1.53\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}$, about $9.6\times10^5$ electron charges per square millimetre — a tiny excess, as unit 00 promised.

Jackson's three roles, in the order unit 00 gave them from Jackson (1996): the surface charges maintain the potential around the circuit, provide the electric field in the space outside the conductors, and keep the current confined to the wire. The second is what this unit needs.

01 · the coaxial cable

The magnetic field: Ampère, three times

Azimuthal, by MR 23's argument for a long wire. Ampère's law on a circle of radius $r$ counts the current inside it:

$\vec B = B(r)\,\hat\theta$ $$B = \begin{cases}\mu_0Ir/2\pi a^2, & r \lt a,\\ \mu_0I/2\pi r, & a \lt r \lt b,\\ 0, & \text{outside the cable.}\end{cases}$$

Inside, the current is uniform; outside, the net current is $I - I = 0$. Units: $\mathrm{N\,A^{-2}\cdot A/m} = \mathrm T$ ✓. Continuous at $r = a$ ✓.

In the gap: $B(a) = 0.80$ mT, $B(b) = 0.20$ mT. $E(a) = 17.3$ kV/m, $E(b) = 4.33$ kV/m. Both fall as $1/r$; so their product falls as $1/r^2$.

Outside the cable both fields vanish: no energy flows outside a coaxial cable.

Both fields against $r$, computed. Shaded: the conductors. The outer conductor's thickness (0.2 mm) is assumed for drawing only; across it $B$ falls to zero.

Inside the inner conductor the current is uniform over the cross-section (unit 00 proved that a steady current in a uniform straight wire is uniform), so a circle of radius $r \lt a$ encloses $I r^2/a^2$ and $2\pi rB = \mu_0Ir^2/a^2$.

Outside the outer conductor a circle encloses $+I$ out along the inner conductor and $-I$ back along the outer, so $B = 0$. Similarly $E = 0$ outside, by Gauss: the enclosed charge per length is $\lambda - \lambda = 0$. (Both statements are for the long middle stretch and a perfect outer conductor.)

Inside the outer conductor, for the figure only, a wall from $b = 2.0$ to $2.2$ mm carrying the return current uniformly: the enclosed current is $I(c^2 - r^2)/(c^2 - b^2)$ with $c = 2.2$ mm.

The numbers. $\mu_0I/2\pi = 2\times10^{-7}\times2 = 4\times10^{-7}$ T·m; at $r = 5\times10^{-4}$ m, $8.0\times10^{-4}$ T; at $2\times10^{-3}$ m, $2.0\times10^{-4}$ T. $V/\ln4 = 8.656$ V; divided by $5\times10^{-4}$ m, $1.731\times10^4\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$; by $2\times10^{-3}$ m, $4.33\times10^3$. For scale: the 0.8 mT at the surface of the 2 A inner conductor is some sixteen times the Earth's field, which MR 23 gives as about $5\times10^{-5}$ T.

worked example 1 · the running example: $V = 12$ V, $I = 2$ A, $a = 0.5$ mm, $b = 2.0$ mm

At $r = a$: $1.10\times10^7\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$; at $r = b$: $6.89\times10^5\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. Units: $\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ ✓.

$$\int_a^bS\,2\pi r\,\mathrm dr = \frac{VI}{\ln(b/a)}\int_a^b\frac{\mathrm dr}r = VI .$$

4. Number. $12\times2 = 24$ W — whatever the radii. Check: equals the resistor's $I^2R = 4\times6 = 24$ W ✓.

Looking along the cable from the battery: $\vec S$ into the page everywhere in the gap, shaded by its size ($\propto 1/r^2$, computed). None inside the copper's cross-section, none outside.

Where the 24 W is. Not in the copper: through the cross-section of the inner conductor, $\vec S$ has no axial component at all (section 4 shows it is radial there). Not outside: both fields vanish. All of it crosses the gap, the vacuum between the conductors. That is the answer to the overview's question for the middle of the cable: the energy travels through the insulation, guided by the conductors.

The numbers. $VI/(2\pi\ln4) = 24/8.7103 = 2.7554$ W; divided by $a^2 = 2.5\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, $1.102\times10^7\ \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$; by $b^2 = 4\times10^{-6}$, $6.89\times10^5$. The step that made the radii drop out. $S \propto 1/r^2$ while the ring's area grows as $r$, so equal ratios of radius carry equal power, and $\int_a^b\mathrm dr/r = \ln(b/a)$ cancels the $\ln(b/a)$ in $E$. The next slide draws that, and section 5 shows it is no accident: the flux is $VI$ for every cross-section shape.

Whatever the radii — and where in the gap

The power inside radius $r$:

$$P(r) = VI\,\frac{\ln(r/a)}{\ln(b/a)} .$$

Each doubling of radius carries the same share. In the example $b/a = 4$, two doublings: half the 24 W flows within 1 mm of the axis, at $r = \sqrt{ab}$, in the inner 20% of the gap's area.

section 5

$p = vi$, proved

Everything in section 4 was one geometry. One identity — the curl of $\varphi\vec B$ — makes it a theorem about any component inside any closed surface, and names the hypotheses it needs.

01 · p = vi

The statement

the flux into a component is $vi$

Let a closed surface $\Sigma$ enclose the component, crossed only by its two leads, and suppose on $\Sigma$:

H1 $\ \partial_t\vec B = 0$, so $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ near $\Sigma$;
H2 $\ $the displacement current $\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E$ crossing $\Sigma$ is negligible;
H3 $\ $each lead crosses $\Sigma$ in a cross-section of constant potential, $\varphi_A$ or $\varphi_B$.

(H1 and H2: the fields on $\Sigma$ are quasi-static.) If current $i$ enters by lead A, the power flowing in through $\Sigma$ is

$$-\oint_\Sigma\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = (\varphi_A - \varphi_B)\,i = vi .$$

Units: $\mathrm{V\cdot A} = \mathrm W$ ✓.

two-terminal component, leads, terminal voltage

A component joined to the rest of the world by two conducting leads, A and B. Its terminal voltage is $v = \varphi_A - \varphi_B$, the potential difference between the leads — unit 00's meaning.

The set-up. Only the flux through the closed surface is claimed; how it is spread over $\Sigma$ depends on the fields, the total does not.

What the theorem buys. Nothing is assumed about what is inside $\Sigma$: a resistor, a battery, a motor, a capacitor, the kettle, or a metre of copper. The fields inside can be as complicated as they like, and can be changing. Only the surface is constrained, and only by H1–H3, which are statements about the fields outside the component.

The word quasi-static. H1 and H2 together say that on $\Sigma$ the fields obey the equations of statics, $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$ and $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$, even if they change in time. In a steady state they hold exactly. For slowly varying currents they hold approximately, and unit 02 finds out how slowly "slowly" must be.

H3 in practice. A lead is a good conductor carrying a modest current: the potential varies along it by $IR'$ per metre (42.8 mV/m in the example) and across it by nothing at all in a steady state (the field inside is axial). So any flat cross-section square to a straight lead is an equipotential.

The sign convention hidden here — current entering at A, power counted inward, $v = \varphi_A - \varphi_B$ — is the one unit 02 will name and adopt for every component. Here it is simply what the algebra produces.

01 · p = vi

The proof

proof

1. By H1, $\vec S = -\nabla\varphi\times\vec B/\mu_0$ on $\Sigma$. The product rule for the curl (MR 04):

$$\nabla\times(\varphi\vec B) = \nabla\varphi\times\vec B + \varphi\,\nabla\times\vec B .$$

2. So $\ -\oint_\Sigma\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \frac1{\mu_0}\oint_\Sigma\nabla\times(\varphi\vec B)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A - \frac1{\mu_0}\oint_\Sigma\varphi\,(\nabla\times\vec B)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A$.

3. The flux of a curl through a closed surface is zero (Stokes, MR 04–05).

4. By H2, $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ on $\Sigma$. So the power in is $-\oint_\Sigma\varphi\,\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A$.

5. $\vec J \ne 0$ on $\Sigma$ only in the two leads, where by H3 $\varphi$ is constant. Outward flux of $\vec J$: $-i$ at A, $+i$ at B. So the power in is $-(\varphi_A(-i) + \varphi_B\,i) = (\varphi_A - \varphi_B)\,i$. $\square$

A bonus from step 4: $\oint_\Sigma\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \frac1{\mu_0}\oint_\Sigma(\nabla\times\vec B)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = 0$ — the current leaving by B is the current that entered by A.

check: a steady resistor

Poynting's theorem with $\partial_tu = 0$: power in through $\Sigma$ $= \int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV$, the Joule heat, which unit 00 found to be $VI$ for a uniform wire. The theorem gives $vi$. Same number, from opposite ends ✓.

check: limits

$i = 0$ (open leads): no flux ✓. $\varphi_A = \varphi_B$ (a perfect wire inside $\Sigma$): no flux ✓ — a perfect conductor takes no energy, though energy may pass by it.

With MR 24's energy balance this is the whole statement circuit theory needs: the power delivered to a component is $vi$, and it goes into heat, into stored field energy, or (for a source) comes out of one.

Full proof

Step 1. H1: if $\partial_t\vec B = 0$ in an open shell $N$ containing $\Sigma$, then $\nabla\times\vec E = 0$ in $N$ (Faraday). A shell round a closed surface is simply connected — every loop in it can be shrunk to a point within it — so by MR 04's criterion $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ in $N$ for a single-valued $\varphi$. (A component threaded by changing flux, such as a transformer core poking through $\Sigma$, violates H1; see the next slide.)

Step 1, the identity, in components: $[\nabla\times(\varphi\vec B)]_i = \epsilon_{ijk}\partial_j(\varphi B_k) = \epsilon_{ijk}(\partial_j\varphi)B_k + \varphi\,\epsilon_{ijk}\partial_jB_k$.

Step 3. Cut $\Sigma$ along a closed curve $C$ into two caps. Stokes' theorem turns the flux of $\nabla\times(\varphi\vec B)$ through each cap into the circulation of $\varphi\vec B$ round $C$, traversed in opposite senses for the two caps (their outward normals induce opposite orientations on $C$). The two cancel. Only $N$ is needed, where $\varphi\vec B$ is defined and smooth; no knowledge of the inside of $\Sigma$ is used.

Step 2's sign. $\vec S = \frac1{\mu_0}\vec E\times\vec B = -\frac1{\mu_0}\nabla\varphi\times\vec B = -\frac1{\mu_0}[\nabla\times(\varphi\vec B) - \varphi\nabla\times\vec B]$. So $-\oint\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \frac1{\mu_0}\oint\nabla\times(\varphi\vec B)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A - \frac1{\mu_0}\oint\varphi(\nabla\times\vec B)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A$.

Step 4. Ampère–Maxwell (MR 24): $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E$. H2 drops the second term on $\Sigma$.

Step 5. $\mathrm d\vec A$ points outward. Where lead A crosses, current flows in, so $\int_A\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = -i$; at B it flows out, $+i$. Elsewhere $\Sigma$ runs through insulator, where $\vec J = 0$. With $\varphi$ constant on each cross-section, $\oint\varphi\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \varphi_A(-i) + \varphi_B(i)$, and the power in is minus this.

Compare section 2's full proof, which turned $\int\vec J\cdot\vec E$ inside a source into terminal potentials by $\vec J\cdot\nabla\varphi = \nabla\cdot(\varphi\vec J)$. Here the same bookkeeping is done on the surface, from the fields outside. That is why the theorem knows nothing about what is inside.

The same theorem for a whole cable

the flux along any two-conductor cable

For static fields round two long parallel conductors of any cross-sections, at potentials differing by $V$ and carrying $\pm I$, the Poynting flux through a cross-section of the cable is

$$\int_{\text{plane}}S_z\,\mathrm dA = VI .$$

Why. In the plane, $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ and $\vec B = \nabla\times(A\hat z) = \nabla A\times\hat z$ (MR 23's vector potential, here along $\hat z$). Then

$$S_z = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A ,$$

and Green's first identity (MR 23) turns its integral into $\varphi$ times the circulation of $\vec B$ round each conductor: $V\cdot\mu_0I/\mu_0$ (problem P5). So the coaxial result was not luck: any cable carries $VI$ round its conductors.

A cross-section of an arbitrary two-conductor cable. The shaded plane outside the conductors carries exactly $VI$; inside perfect conductors $S_z = 0$.

The two-dimensional identity

With $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ and $\vec B = \nabla A\times\hat z$, both in the plane: $(\vec E\times\vec B)_z = -[\nabla\varphi\times(\nabla A\times\hat z)]_z$. The BAC–CAB rule (MR 04) gives $\nabla\varphi\times(\nabla A\times\hat z) = \nabla A\,(\nabla\varphi\cdot\hat z) - \hat z\,(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A) = -\hat z\,(\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A)$, since $\nabla\varphi\perp\hat z$. So $S_z = \nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A/\mu_0$. Outside the conductors $\nabla^2A = -\mu_0J_z = 0$, so Green's first identity on the region $D$ outside them gives $\int_D\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla A = \oint_{\partial D}\varphi\,\partial_nA$. On each conductor's boundary $\varphi$ is constant, and $\oint\partial_nA$ is, up to sign, the circulation of $\vec B$ round it, $\mu_0$ times its current; the circle at infinity contributes nothing because the fields of a pair carrying $\pm\lambda$ and $\pm I$ fall off fast enough. Signs and the fall-off are problem P5.

Static fields, perfect conductors. With resistive conductors there is a small axial $\vec E$ too, and $S_z$ is slightly reduced near the conductors as energy peels into them — the coaxial case already showed the size of that.

The kettle's two-wire cable, computed

The kettle cable in the thin-wire picture: each conductor's field as if from a line at its centre — charge $\pm\lambda$, current $\pm I$. A line charge gives $\vec E\propto\hat r/r$, a line current $\vec B\propto\hat\theta/r$, so for the pair

$$\vec B = \frac{\mu_0\varepsilon_0I}{\lambda}\,\hat z\times\vec E,\qquad \vec S = \frac{\varepsilon_0I}{\lambda}\,E^2\,\hat z .$$

$\vec S$ is along the cable everywhere, and largest where $E$ is: between and beside the wires. Its total is $\frac{I}{\lambda}\int\varepsilon_0E^2\,\mathrm dA = \frac I\lambda\cdot\lambda V = VI$, since $\varepsilon_0\int E^2\,\mathrm dA = \lambda V$ (Green's first identity, MR 23 — notes).

Far away $E$ and $B$ each fall as $1/r^2$, so $S$ falls as $1/r^4$ (problem P6). Through this cross-section pass 2.3 kW: in the air and the insulation, not in the copper, which takes only 0.672 W per metre of each conductor.

Computed in the thin-wire picture, centres 4 mm apart (assumed), conductors to scale; seen from the kettle. $\vec S$ points out of the page; shading shows $|\vec S|$, whose contours are Cassini ovals, $r_1r_2 =$ const.

The relation between $\vec B$ and $\vec E$. For one line charge $\lambda$ at the origin $\vec E = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}\hat r$, and for a line current $I$ there, $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\hat\theta = \frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi r}\hat z\times\hat r$. So $\vec B = \frac{\mu_0\varepsilon_0I}{\lambda}\hat z\times\vec E$ for each wire alone; the live wire has $(+\lambda, +I)$ and the neutral $(-\lambda, -I)$, the same ratio, so the relation holds for the sum. Then $\vec E\times(\hat z\times\vec E) = \hat z\,E^2 - \vec E\,(\vec E\cdot\hat z) = \hat zE^2$, giving the slide's $\vec S$.

The total. Green's first identity (MR 23) on the plane $D$ outside the two conductors, where $\nabla^2\varphi = 0$: $\int_D|\nabla\varphi|^2\,\mathrm dA = \oint_{\partial D}\varphi\,\partial_\nu\varphi\,\mathrm dl$, with $\nu$ the outward normal of $D$, which points into each conductor. On conductor $k$, $-\varepsilon_0\partial_\nu\varphi$ is the outward normal component of $\vec E$, and its integral round the conductor is $\lambda_k$ (Gauss, MR 23); the circle at infinity contributes nothing, since $\varphi\sim1/R$ and $\nabla\varphi\sim1/R^2$. With $\varphi$ constant on each conductor, $\varepsilon_0\int_DE^2\,\mathrm dA = \sum_k\varphi_k\lambda_k = \lambda\varphi_{\text{live}} - \lambda\varphi_{\text{neutral}} = \lambda V$. (Half of it, $\tfrac12\lambda V$, is the electric field energy per metre.) So $\int S_z = \frac{I}{\lambda}\cdot\lambda V = VI$ — the whole-cable theorem again, in one line for this case. In the thin-wire picture $\varphi$ is constant on each conductor only approximately, so this one-line version inherits that approximation; problem P5's version does not.

The approximation. Real conductors of radius 0.892 mm with centres 4 mm apart are not thin: the charge on each crowds towards the other, so the electric field is that of line charges slightly displaced from the centres, while at DC the current is uniform and the magnetic field outside is exactly that of lines at the centres. The figure's pattern is therefore approximate near the wires; the total $VI$ is not approximate, by the whole-cable theorem. The 4 mm spacing is an assumption for the figure, not a product's dimension.

Reading the figure. Electric field lines of two opposite line charges are circles through both wires; magnetic field lines are the equipotentials of that electric field — circles round each wire — because $\vec B\propto\hat z\times\vec E$. Since $|\vec E| \propto 1/(r_1r_2)$, with $r_1$, $r_2$ the distances to the two wires, $|\vec S| \propto 1/(r_1r_2)^2$. Seen from the kettle, the live current comes towards you and $\vec S = \vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0$ points towards you too: towards the kettle.

Two pictures, one number: what the electrons actually do

"The electrons flow through the copper and carry the energy." As bookkeeping it is exact. As a mechanism it is wrong by twenty-one powers of ten. Both statements are theorems.

01 · what the electrons do

The conveyor belt, stated fairly

the conveyor-belt picture

Charges circulate like a belt. Each coulomb leaving the fuse box carries 230 J of "potential energy" and gives it up in the kettle; 10 C pass each second; so $10\ \mathrm{C\,s^{-1}}\times230\ \mathrm{J\,C^{-1}} = 2.3$ kW. Each charge $q$ carries $qV$.

as bookkeeping, it is exact

In a steady state the heat made in the load is $\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = VI$ (unit 00), and the theorem of section 5 says $VI$ is also what crosses any closed surface round it. "Coulombs per second times joules per coulomb" is that number.

Circuit theory needs only totals — how much power each component takes — and for totals the picture never fails. That is why it survives, and why it is taught.

The question here is narrower: is it what happens? Three tests follow.

A cartoon, labelled for what it is: bookkeeping. The total it gives is right; the next slides test whether anything moves as drawn.

Why "fairly". The picture is not a careless mistake. It is the integral statement $P = VI$ read as a story: $I$ coulombs per second each "dropping" through $V$ volts. As long as the question is "how much power does the load take?", the story and the field theory give the same answer, and section 5 proved that they must. The trouble starts only when the story is asked where the energy is at a given moment, or how it gets there.

Where qV lives

The belt says each charge "carries" $qV$. Test it on two point charges, $q$ at $\vec x_1$ and $Q$ at $\vec x_2$, fields $\vec E_1$ and $\vec E_2$. The field energy of the pair, less what each has alone, is their interaction energy:

the energy of a pair belongs to the pair $$\begin{aligned}W_{\text{int}} &= \varepsilon_0\!\int\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2\,\mathrm dV\\ &= q\,\varphi_2(\vec x_1) = Q\,\varphi_1(\vec x_2) .\end{aligned}$$
proof

$\vec E_1 = -\nabla\varphi_1$; Green's first identity (MR 23) moves the gradient: $\varepsilon_0\int\nabla\varphi_1\cdot\nabla\varphi_2 = -\varepsilon_0\int\varphi_1\nabla^2\varphi_2 = \int\varphi_1\varrho_2$, which is $Q\varphi_1(\vec x_2)$. Symmetric in 1 and 2. $\square$

A sketch. The number "$q\varphi$" depends on where the other charges are; it is a property of the configuration, not of $q$.

So "$q$'s potential energy" $q\varphi_2(\vec x_1)$ is equally "$Q$'s". Move $Q$ and it changes while $q$ sits still. It is not in either charge: it is in the field, spread over all space where $\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2 \ne 0$.

Full proof

The total field is $\vec E_1 + \vec E_2$, so $\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2\int|\vec E_1 + \vec E_2|^2 = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2\int E_1^2 + \tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2\int E_2^2 + \varepsilon_0\int\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2$. For point charges the first two terms are infinite, but they do not depend on where the charges are; the cross term does, and it is the part that changes when either charge moves. Green's first identity on all space (the surface term at infinity vanishes, as $\varphi_1\nabla\varphi_2 \sim 1/r^3$): $\int\nabla\varphi_1\cdot\nabla\varphi_2 = -\int\varphi_1\nabla^2\varphi_2$. Poisson's equation $\nabla^2\varphi_2 = -\varrho_2/\varepsilon_0$ (MR 23) then gives $\varepsilon_0\int\vec E_1\cdot\vec E_2 = \int\varphi_1\varrho_2\,\mathrm dV = Q\varphi_1(\vec x_2)$. By the same steps with 1 and 2 exchanged, it equals $q\varphi_2(\vec x_1)$. Both equal $qQ/4\pi\varepsilon_0|\vec x_1 - \vec x_2|$.

In the cable. The "230 J per coulomb" is the potential of the live conductor relative to the neutral, and that potential is made by the surface charges on both conductors and on everything nearby (unit 00, section 5). Change the routing of the neutral and the surface charges rearrange; the potential at a given electron changes; the electron has not. The $qV$ of the conveyor belt is real energy, but it is the field's.

Three jobs, none of them carrying

If not carrying the energy, what are the electrons for? Everything the field needs from them:

1 · moving, they make B

Their drift is the current, which Ampère's law turns into $\vec B$ round the wire. No current, no $\vec B$, no $\vec S$.

2 · displaced, they make E

A tiny fraction, displaced to the surfaces, are unit 00's surface charge: the field inside that drives the current, and the field outside.

3 · colliding, they make heat

In the load the field does work $\vec J\cdot\vec E$ on them, and collisions hand it to the lattice (unit 00): they touch the energy where it is dissipated, not where it travels.

The electrons' three jobs. The wire is a guide that shapes the field; the field carries the energy; the electrons in the load turn it into heat.

Job 2 in numbers. On the coaxial example's inner conductor the surface charge is $4.8\times10^{-10}$ C per metre, about $3\times10^9$ electrons' worth; the conduction electrons in that metre number $nA = 6.7\times10^{22}$. So the surface charge is a displacement of about one conduction electron in $2\times10^{13}$.

Jackson's roles. Unit 00 listed them from Jackson (1996): to maintain the potential round the circuit, to provide the field outside the conductors, and to keep the current confined. Job 2 here is all three, seen from the energy's side: the field inside drives the current and keeps it in the wire, the field outside — with the $\vec B$ of job 1 — is the energy flow.

Job 3 is where the two pictures meet. In the load, energy flows in from the field ($-\nabla\cdot\vec S = \vec J\cdot\vec E$) and the electrons pass it to the lattice by collisions. The conveyor belt is right that the electrons are where the energy is converted; it is wrong that they brought it.

The guide. Change the route of the wires and the energy's route changes with it, because the surface charges and currents follow the wires and make the fields there. In that sense the wires do steer the energy — from outside.

How fast can energy move?

A one-line inequality bounds the energy flux by $c$ times the energy density. Applied to the cable it compares what is stored with what is delivered.

01 · how fast

|S| ≤ cu: energy never outruns light

the bound, pointwise in vacuum $$|\vec S| \le c\,u,\qquad c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0},$$

with equality exactly when $\vec E\perp\vec B$ and $E = cB$.

proof

$|\vec E\times\vec B| \le EB$. Write $EB/\mu_0 = c\,(\sqrt{\varepsilon_0}\,E)(B/\sqrt{\mu_0})$, using $c\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0} = 1$. For reals $xy \le \tfrac12(x^2 + y^2)$ (AM–GM), so

$$\frac{EB}{\mu_0} \le c\Big(\frac{\varepsilon_0E^2}{2} + \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\Big) = cu .$$

Equality needs both steps tight: $\sin = 1$, and $x = y$, i.e. $E = cB$. $\square$

Units: $[cu] = \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}\cdot J\,m^{-3}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ ✓. Check: MR 24's plane wave has $E = cB$, $\vec E\perp\vec B$ — the equality case ✓.

Read it as a speed. $|\vec S|/u$ is the speed at which the energy at a point would have to move to make the flux. It never exceeds $c$.

Integrated over the cable's cross-section, with $W' = \int u\,\mathrm dA$ the field energy per metre:

$$VI = \int S_z\,\mathrm dA \le c\int u\,\mathrm dA = cW' .$$

So the energy-transport speed $VI/W'$ — delivered power over stored energy per metre — is at most $c$, for every cable with vacuum insulation.

The inequality's two steps. First $|\vec E\times\vec B| = EB\sin\alpha \le EB$. Second, with $x = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0}E$ and $y = B/\sqrt{\mu_0}$, $\ 0 \le (x - y)^2$ gives $xy \le \tfrac12(x^2 + y^2) = u$. And $xy = \sqrt{\varepsilon_0/\mu_0}\,EB$, so $EB/\mu_0 = xy/\sqrt{\varepsilon_0\mu_0} = c\,xy$. Equality in the second step is $x = y$: $\varepsilon_0E^2 = B^2/\mu_0$, i.e. $E = cB$, which says the electric and magnetic energy densities are equal.

The integrated form. Integrate $S_z \le |\vec S| \le cu$ over the gap. The bound uses the field energy in the gap only; the magnetic field inside the copper stores a little more, $\mu_0I^2/16\pi$ per metre, which only makes $VI/W'$ smaller if counted.

With an insulator the same argument gives a bound by the speed of light in the insulator, using its permittivity; unit 08 meets that speed as the speed of signals on a cable. Here the insulation is vacuum and the bound is $c$.

Read as a speed, carefully. $|\vec S|/u$ is not the velocity of anything one can follow, since energy has no labels. It is the speed the local energy would need if it all moved together to produce the flux; the bound says that even that speed is at most $c$.

Only closed-surface fluxes are fixed by the physics; the streamlines are a convention that agrees with every one of them. Then back to the kettle.

01 · close

Honesty about S

what is physics, and what is a choice

MR 24: Poynting's theorem fixes only $\nabla\cdot\vec S$. Any $\vec S + \nabla\times\vec F$ obeys the same local law, and through every closed surface it has the same flux, since $\oint(\nabla\times\vec F)\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = 0$ (problem P12).

So: the fluxes through closed surfaces — every result of section 5 — are physics. The streamlines drawn in this deck are the standard choice $\vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0$; what justifies drawing them is that they agree with every closed-surface balance, and that MR 24 notes the choice is the one relativity confirms.

Feynman's example (vol. II, ch. 27): a charge at rest beside a bar magnet. Nothing changes, nothing is heated, yet $\vec E\times\vec B \ne 0$: the standard $\vec S$ circulates in closed loops, and through every closed surface its net flux is zero.

A qualitative sketch after Feynman's charge-and-magnet example. The loop's shape and direction are illustrative, not computed.

Why the circulation is harmless. In a static situation $\partial_tu = 0$ and, away from currents, $\vec J\cdot\vec E = 0$; so $\nabla\cdot\vec S = 0$ everywhere. A divergence-free flow has zero flux through every closed surface: whatever enters a region leaves it. It can only circulate. Feynman's point (vol. II, ch. 27) is that the standard formula nevertheless says energy is moving round in loops, which looks absurd and does no harm: no energy accumulates anywhere, and no closed-surface balance is affected. The rest of his chapter goes on to the momentum the field carries; that part is not used here.

What this means for the deck's figures. Every streamline sketch here — the coaxial gap, the copper, the resistor, the kettle cable — shows the standard $\vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0$. A different choice could add circulating loops to any of them without changing a single closed-surface flux, and so without changing any number this unit computed through section 5's theorem. The coaxial gap's 24 W through a cross-section is fixed too, under one mild condition: close the cross-section with a cap round the battery end that runs outside the cable, where $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ vanish. The closed surface's flux is fixed by section 5; and any choice of energy flux that, like $\vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0$, is built from the local fields and so vanishes where they do, puts nothing through the cap. (A bare $\nabla\times\vec F$ with no such condition could shift flux between cap and cross-section — which is exactly why only closed surfaces are unconditionally physics.)

MR 24's remark is that the choice $\vec E\times\vec B/\mu_0$ is the one relativity singles out, when $u$ and $\vec S$ become components of one symmetric energy–momentum tensor. That is cited here from MR 24, not proved.

The AC remark, in one line. $\vec E$ is proportional to the live conductor's potential $v(t)$ and $\vec B$ to the current $i(t)$; for a resistor $i = v/R$, so $\vec S\propto v(t)i(t) = v(t)^2/R \ge 0$ at every instant (problem P13). For an ideal capacitor the current leads the voltage by a quarter cycle, and $v\,i$ is positive half the time and negative the other half, averaging zero. Unit 06 develops this.

Into the element. As at the coaxial cable's resistor, the element's axial field is far larger than the copper's — it drops 230 V along its length — so the flow turns into it entirely. By section 5's theorem, with a surface round the element, the total is $vi = 2.3$ kW whatever the element's shape.

What unit 02 does with this. H1 says the changing magnetic flux stays away from the surfaces we draw; H2 that displacement current does; H3 that leads are equipotential across. Unit 02 restates them for a whole circuit — charge accumulates only inside capacitors, changing flux stays inside inductors, the circuit is small against a wavelength — and from them gets Kirchhoff's laws.
