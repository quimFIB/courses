The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: Lewin's ring. Two resistors, \(R_1 = 100\ \Omega\) and
\(R_2 = 900\ \Omega\), joined in a ring round a long solenoid whose growing flux
makes an EMF \(\mathcal E = 1\) V round the ring, so \(I = 1\) mA circulates. Two
voltmeters on the same two junctions P and Q read \(-0.1\) V and \(+0.9\) V.
Both are right: a voltmeter reads \(\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l\) along its own
leads, and with changing flux outside every element that integral depends on
the path. The first hypothesis, (H1), has failed. The configuration is Lewin's
lecture demonstration (MIT 8.02, 2002, lecture 16); the values are chosen here.

* What a circuit is

- lumped element :: A region of space with a boundary drawn round it, crossed
  by exactly two wires, whose behaviour is summarised by one voltage across it
  and one current through it, related by its element law. Agarwal & Lang's
  term (§1.2–1.3). /Slide: From fields to a list of numbers./
- lumped circuit :: A set of lumped elements joined by ideal wires. Its state
  at each instant is a finite list of numbers, one \(v\) and one \(i\) per
  element.
- terminal :: One of the two places where a wire crosses an element's
  boundary.
- ideal wire :: A conductor with \(\sigma\to\infty\): the field along it
  vanishes, so it is at one potential throughout. A real wire whose resistance
  matters is drawn as a resistor.
- node :: A set of terminals joined by ideal wires; one point of the circuit,
  at one potential, whatever its physical shape.
- circuit diagram :: A drawing of a lumped circuit: each element as a symbol,
  each wire as a line. Only what is joined to what is meaningful; lengths,
  positions and areas are not. This course draws a resistor as a rectangle
  (IEC 60617); American texts use a zigzag (IEEE Std 315-1975 gives the two
  as equal alternatives). /Slide: The symbols./
- passive sign convention :: Mark one terminal of each element +, and draw its
  current arrow into the + terminal. Then \(p = vi\) is the power the element
  absorbs: positive absorbing, negative delivering — so a source delivering
  power shows \(p \lt 0\). Unit 01's theorem is what makes \(vi\) the power.
  /Slide: The passive sign convention./

* Three hypotheses, and Kirchhoff's laws

- lumped matter discipline :: The three hypotheses under which a field problem
  becomes a circuit (Agarwal & Lang, §1.3): (H1) no changing magnetic flux
  through any closed curve outside the elements; (H2) no changing net charge on
  any element, wire or node; (H3) the circuit is small against the wavelength
  \(c/f\), so retardation is negligible. /Slide: Three hypotheses./
- Kirchhoff's current law :: At every node the currents leaving through the
  wires sum to zero. Proved from the continuity equation and (H2), with a
  closed surface round the node cutting only wires. /Slide: The current law./
- Kirchhoff's voltage law :: Round every closed loop of elements the signed
  sum of the element voltages is zero. Proved from Faraday's law and (H1):
  \(\vec E\) has a potential outside the elements, and the sum telescopes.
  /Slide: The voltage law./
- stray inductance :: The flux linked by a circuit's own loops outside its
  inductors — what (H1) neglects. For a circuit of size \(\ell\) with
  resistances \(R\) its relative effect is of order
  \(2\pi(\ell/\lambda)(\mu_0c/R)\), with \(\mu_0c = 376.7\ \Omega\). /Slide:
  What each hypothesis neglects./
- stray capacitance :: The changing charge on a circuit's wires and nodes —
  what (H2) neglects. Its relative effect is of order
  \(2\pi(\ell/\lambda)(R/\mu_0c)\).

* The element laws, from fields

- element law :: The relation between an element's own voltage and current,
  which is all the circuit needs to know about what is inside its boundary.
  The resistor's is \(v = Ri\). /Slide: The resistor./
- capacitance :: For two conductors with charges \(\pm q\), the constant
  \(C = q/v\); it exists because Laplace's equation is linear and its solution
  unique for given charges, and it is positive because the field energy
  \(\tfrac12Cv^2 = \int\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2E^2\) is. In farads. Parallel
  plates: \(\varepsilon_0A/d\), fringing neglected; coaxial:
  \(2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(b/a)\) per metre. /Slide: Capacitance from
  linearity and uniqueness./
- capacitor :: An element with law \(q = Cv\), hence \(i = C\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm
  dt\) under the passive convention. It stores \(\tfrac12Cv^2\) and dissipates
  nothing. /Slide: The capacitor's energy, and i = C dv/dt./
- linear dielectric :: An insulator whose molecules polarise in proportion to
  the field. Filling the space between two conductors with one multiplies their
  capacitance by its relative permittivity (cited, Griffiths, ch. 4). /Slide: A
  dielectric multiplies C by \(\varepsilon_r\)./
- relative permittivity :: \(\varepsilon_r\), the factor by which a linear
  dielectric multiplies a capacitance. Polyethylene is taken as \(2.25\) in the
  unit's example, an assumption to be checked against Pozar's tables.
- inductance :: For a closed loop carrying \(i\), the constant \(L =
  \Lambda/i\), where \(\Lambda\) is the flux of the loop's own field through it;
  it exists because Biot–Savart is linear, and it is positive because the field
  energy \(\tfrac12Li^2 = \int B^2/2\mu_0\) is. In henries. For a thick
  conductor \(L\) is defined by the energy. /Slide: Inductance from linearity./
- flux linkage :: \(\Lambda\), the flux through the surface bounded by a
  circuit; for a coil of \(N\) turns each turn's flux counts once, so
  \(\Lambda \approx N\Phi_{\text{turn}}\). Faraday's law applies to it.
- inductor :: An element with law \(v = L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt\) under the
  passive convention, derived from Faraday's law round a loop through the coil
  and back outside it. It stores \(\tfrac12Li^2\) and dissipates nothing.
  /Slide: Worked: v = L di/dt, sign and energy./
- internal inductance :: The part of a conductor's inductance from the field
  inside the conductor itself: \(\mu_0/8\pi = 50\ \mathrm{nH/m}\) for a round
  wire with uniform current, whatever its radius. It shrinks at high frequency,
  where the current is not uniform. /Slide: Inside the wire, and a divergence./
- mutual inductance :: For two loops, the constant \(M\) with \(\Lambda_{12} =
  Mi_2\): the flux of loop 2's field through loop 1 per unit current in loop 2.
  The same in both directions. /Slide: Mutual inductance: Neumann's formula./
- Neumann's formula :: \(M = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint\oint\frac{\mathrm d\vec
  l_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2}{|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|}\), from Stokes and the
  Coulomb-gauge vector potential; symmetric in the two loops.
- inductance matrix :: \(\begin{pmatrix}L_1 & M\\ M & L_2\end{pmatrix}\), whose
  quadratic form is the magnetic field energy. Positive semi-definite because
  that energy cannot be negative, so \(M^2 \le L_1L_2\). /Slide: The inductance
  matrix is positive semi-definite./
- ideal voltage source :: An element that fixes \(v\) whatever \(i\): unit
  01's EMF with no internal resistance. /Slide: Ideal sources./
- ideal current source :: An element that fixes \(i\) whatever \(v\); a
  modelling idealisation, the dual of the voltage source.
- internal resistance :: The resistance \(r_i\) in series with an ideal source
  in a model of a real battery (unit 01's notation); with the passive marking
  \(v = \mathcal E + r_ii\), so a delivering battery shows less than its EMF.

* The ring, modelled properly

- voltmeter :: A large resistance with ideal leads that displays \(R_m\) times
  its own current. It reads \(\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l\) along its own
  leads and body, from the + lead to the − lead; under (H1) that is the
  potential difference, and without it two meters on the same points differ by
  the EMF round the loop their paths make. /Slide: A voltmeter reads
  \(\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l\) along its own leads./
