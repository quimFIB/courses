The lumped approximation: three hypotheses under which Kirchhoff's laws are true

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You derive both of Kirchhoff's laws from Maxwell's equations under three stated hypotheses, and in Lewin's circuit predict the two voltmeter readings — 0.1 V and 0.9 V, of opposite sign — and name the hypothesis that failed.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about four hours. Conventions as in the deck: the passive sign convention (current arrow into the + terminal, so \(p = vi\) is power absorbed); (H1) no changing flux through any loop outside the elements, (H2) no changing net charge on any element, wire or node, (H3) circuit small against \(\lambda = c/f\); potential \(\varphi\), flux \(\Phi\), flux linkage \(\Lambda\), line element \(\mathrm d\vec l\).

Constants: \(\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}\), \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}\), \(c = 2.998\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(\mu_0c = 376.7\ \Omega\). Copper at 20 °C: \(\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m\) (CRC). Polyethylene is taken as \(\varepsilon_r = 2.25\), an assumption for these problems. Lewin's ring: \(R_1 = 100\ \Omega\) on the left, \(R_2 = 900\ \Omega\) on the right, junctions P (top) and Q (bottom), solenoid flux out of the page and growing, so that the clockwise EMF is \(\mathcal E = 1\) V; values chosen for the course.

The hypotheses and the laws

P1 When is it lumped? routine

exercises: (H3), \(\lambda = c/f\), the order-of-magnitude estimates for what (H1) and (H2) neglect

  1. Find the vacuum wavelength at 50 Hz, 100 kHz and 2.4 GHz.
  2. For each system, find \(\ell/\lambda\) and say whether it is lumped: a house's wiring, \(\ell = 100\) m, at 50 Hz; a wireless charging pad a few centimetres across (take 5 cm) at 100 kHz; a phone's circuit board, 10 cm, at 2.4 GHz. (The sizes and the pad's frequency are assumptions for the problem.)
  3. The board's conductors run on an insulator; take its relative permittivity to be 4 (an assumption), so that waves in it travel at \(c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r} = c/2\). What does that do to the board's \(\ell/\lambda\)?
  4. For the house with element resistances of order \(23\ \Omega\) (the kettle), estimate the relative errors from stray inductance and stray capacitance using the deck's formulas. Which is larger, and why?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every answer is a dimensionless ratio, \(\ell/\lambda\), or a wavelength in metres. "Lumped" means the ratio is much less than 1.

Rung 2

In an insulator the wavelength is the speed there divided by the frequency. The two error estimates are \(2\pi(\ell/\lambda)(\mu_0c/R)\) and \(2\pi(\ell/\lambda)(R/\mu_0c)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\lambda_{50\,\mathrm{Hz}} = 2.998\times10^8/50 = 5.996\times10^6\) m, about 6000 km.

Worked solution

Idea. (H3) compares two lengths; everything else is arithmetic, and the error estimates say which of (H1) or (H2) gives way first.

1. \(\lambda = c/f\): \(5.996\times10^6\) m (6000 km) at 50 Hz; \(2998\) m (3 km) at 100 kHz; \(0.1249\) m (12.5 cm) at 2.4 GHz.

2. House: \(100/5.996\times10^6 = 1.67\times10^{-5}\), lumped by almost five orders of magnitude. Pad: \(0.05/2998 = 1.67\times10^{-5}\), lumped just as well — a pad is small and its frequency is low. Board: \(0.10/0.1249 = 0.80\): the board is most of a wavelength across, and is not lumped.

3. At half the speed the wavelength halves, to 6.2 cm, and \(\ell/\lambda = 1.6\): the board is more than a wavelength across. An insulator makes things less lumped, not more.

4. \(2\pi\times1.67\times10^{-5} = 1.05\times10^{-4}\). Stray inductance: \(1.05\times10^{-4}\times376.7/23 = 1.7\times10^{-3}\). Stray capacitance: \(1.05\times10^{-4}\times23/376.7 = 6.4\times10^{-6}\). Inductance wins by the factor \((\mu_0c/R)^2 = 268\): a low-resistance circuit carries large currents for its voltage, and it is current that makes flux.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}/\mathrm{s^{-1}} = \mathrm m\) ✓; both error ratios dimensionless (\(\Omega/\Omega\)) ✓. Limit: as \(f\to0\), \(\lambda\to\infty\) and every circuit is lumped — DC circuit theory is exact in the static limit ✓. These are orders of magnitude: a factor of a few either way is not meaningful.

P2 Sign convention drill routine

exercises: the passive sign convention, power absorbed and delivered, the sum of powers (checked, not proved)

A 12 V battery has its + terminal at node a and its − terminal at node g. A \(2\ \Omega\) resistor joins a to b; two resistors, \(6\ \Omega\) and \(3\ \Omega\), each join b to g. Someone has marked the four elements as below, not always with the passive convention, and measured the values shown (each \(v\) is the potential of the terminal marked + minus that of the other; each \(i\) is the current in the direction of the arrow).

elementterminalsmarked +arrow\(v\)\(i\)
batterya, gafrom outside into a12 V−3 A
\(2\ \Omega\)a, baa → b6 V3 A
\(6\ \Omega\)b, ggb → g−6 V1 A
\(3\ \Omega\)b, gbg → b6 V−2 A
  1. Which of the four markings follow the passive convention?
  2. Find the power each element absorbs, and say which absorb and which deliver.
  3. Check that the absorbed powers sum to zero, and that the currents satisfy the current law at b.
  4. Re-mark the \(6\ \Omega\) and \(3\ \Omega\) resistors with the passive convention and the + on b; give their new \(v\) and \(i\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

For each element, ask one question: does the arrow enter the terminal marked +? If so, the absorbed power is \(vi\); if not, it is \(-vi\).

Rung 2

A marking that is reversed in both \(v\) and \(i\) flips both signs and leaves \(vi\) unchanged. A marking reversed in only one of them flips the sign of \(vi\).

Rung 3

First line: the battery's arrow enters a, which is +, so it absorbs \(vi = 12\times(-3) = -36\) W.

Worked solution

Idea. The passive convention is a rule about the arrow relative to the + sign. When it is broken, the absorbed power is \(-vi\) instead of \(vi\); the physics does not care how the arrows were drawn.

1. Battery: arrow into a, the + terminal — passive. \(2\ \Omega\): arrow from a (+) to b — passive. \(6\ \Omega\): + on g, arrow enters at b, the − terminal — not passive. \(3\ \Omega\): + on b, arrow enters at g, the − terminal — not passive.

2. Battery \(12\times(-3) = -36\) W: delivers 36 W. \(2\ \Omega\): \(6\times3 = +18\) W, absorbs. \(6\ \Omega\): \(-vi = -(-6)(1) = +6\) W, absorbs. \(3\ \Omega\): \(-vi = -(6)(-2) = +12\) W, absorbs.

3. \(-36 + 18 + 6 + 12 = 0\) ✓. At b: 3 A arrives from the \(2\ \Omega\) resistor; the \(6\ \Omega\) carries 1 A from b to g; the \(3\ \Omega\) carries \(-2\) A from g to b, i.e. 2 A from b to g. Out of b: \(1 + 2 = 3\) A, in: 3 A ✓.

4. With + on b and the arrow into b, going b → g: the \(6\ \Omega\) has \(v = 6\) V, \(i = 1\) A; the \(3\ \Omega\) has \(v = 6\) V, \(i = 2\) A. Absorbed powers \(6\) W and \(12\) W, as before ✓.

Check. Each resistor's absorbed power should be \(v^2/R\) with its true voltage: \(6^2/2 = 18\), \(6^2/6 = 6\), \(6^2/3 = 12\) ✓ — all positive, as a resistor's must be. The source delivers what the resistors absorb, \(12\ \mathrm V\times3\ \mathrm A = 36\) W. That the sum is zero in every circuit is Tellegen's theorem, proved in unit 03; here it was only checked.

P3 A surface through a capacitor medium

exercises: the proof of the current law, why (H2) is stated per element, MR 24's displacement current

A parallel-plate capacitor (circular plates of radius \(a\), gap \(d\), vacuum between, fringing neglected) is being charged through a wire by a current \(i(t)\). The plates carry \(\pm q(t)\).

  1. Take a closed surface \(S_1\) round the whole capacitor, crossed by both its wires. Show that the current law holds for it.
  2. Take a closed surface \(S_2\) round the + plate only, crossed by the incoming wire and by the gap. Compute \(\oint_{S_2}\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\) and the flux of \(\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\) through \(S_2\), and show that their sum vanishes.
  3. Explain in two sentences why (H2) is phrased "each element's total charge is constant" and not "no charge anywhere changes".
  4. For MR 24's capacitor (\(a = 5\) cm, \(d = 1\) mm) charged so that \(v\) reaches 100 V after 6.954 ns at steady current, find \(C\), \(i\), and the displacement current through the gap.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answers to 1 and 2 are fluxes through closed surfaces, each a sum of a conduction part and a displacement part. The tool is continuity integrated over a volume: \(\oint\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = -\mathrm dQ_{\text{inside}}/\mathrm dt\).

Rung 2

Gauss's law gives the flux of \(\varepsilon_0\vec E\) through a closed surface as the enclosed charge. Differentiate it in time. In the gap the field is uniform, \(E = q/\varepsilon_0\pi a^2\).

Rung 3

First line for 2: the only conduction current through \(S_2\) is the wire's, entering, so \(\oint_{S_2}\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = -i\).

Worked solution

Idea. Continuity holds for every closed surface; the current law is continuity plus "the enclosed charge does not change". That extra clause is true for \(S_1\) and false for \(S_2\).

1. \(S_1\) encloses \(+q - q = 0\) at all times, so \(\oint_{S_1}\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = 0\): current \(i\) in through one wire, \(i\) out through the other. That is the current law for the element, and it is why a capacitor has one current.

2. \(S_2\) encloses \(q(t)\). Conduction: \(\oint_{S_2}\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = -i\) (entering), and continuity says this equals \(-\dot q\), so \(i = \dot q\). Displacement: by Gauss \(\oint_{S_2}\varepsilon_0\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = q\), so the flux of \(\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\) is \(\dot q = i\), all of it crossing the gap, outward from the + plate towards the − plate. Sum: \(-i + i = 0\) ✓. This is MR 24's statement that \(\vec J + \varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\) is divergence-free.

3. Inside a capacitor charge must change — that is what it does — so a ban on all changing charge would forbid capacitors. (H2) allows it as long as it is equal and opposite inside one boundary, so that every surface the lumped picture uses (round a node, or round a whole element) encloses constant charge.

4. \(C = \varepsilon_0\pi a^2/d = 8.854\times10^{-12}\times\pi\times0.05^2/10^{-3} = 69.54\) pF, MR 24's value. At steady current \(q = it\), so \(i = Cv/t = 69.54\times10^{-12}\times100/6.954\times10^{-9} = 1.00\) A. The displacement current through the gap equals \(\dot q = 1.00\) A.

Check. Units: \(\varepsilon_0\,\partial_tE\cdot\text{area}\) is \(\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm m^2 = \mathrm{C\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm A\) ✓. Limit: \(i\to0\) — nothing changes and both surfaces satisfy the current law ✓.

P4 The kettle, lumped routine

exercises: drawing a circuit, the voltage law, element laws, the energy audit

An ideal 230 V source feeds a 23 Ω kettle element through a two-conductor cable (out on one conductor, back on the other). Treat the mains as steady at its RMS value.

  1. With 10 m of \(2.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\) copper each way, find the current, the voltage at the element, the power in the element, the power lost in the cable, and the power from the source. Show that the absorbed powers, with the passive convention, sum to zero.
  2. Repeat for 15 m of \(1.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\) copper each way (an assumed, poorer installation).
  3. In case 2, what fraction of the source's power does the cable waste, and by how much does the element's power fall short of its 2.3 kW rating?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The circuit is one loop of four elements: the source and three resistors in series. One current; the voltage law shares out 230 V.

Rung 2

Each conductor's resistance is \(\rho L/A\). Convert \(\mathrm{mm^2}\) to \(\mathrm{m^2}\). The source's own current, in the passive marking, is minus the loop current.

Rung 3

First line: \(R_w = 1.68\times10^{-8}\times10/2.5\times10^{-6} = 0.0672\ \Omega\), so \(i = 230/(23 + 2\times0.0672)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Series resistors share one current; the voltage law makes the source's voltage the sum of the three drops; the energy audit is the same sum multiplied by \(i\).

1. \(R_w = 0.0672\ \Omega\) each. \(i = 230/23.1344 = 9.942\) A. Element: \(23\times9.942 = 228.7\) V, power \(9.942^2\times23 = 2273\) W. Cable: \(9.942^2\times0.1344 = 13.3\) W. Source: \(230\times(-9.942) = -2287\) W in the passive marking. Sum \(2273.4 + 13.3 - 2286.6 = 0\) ✓.

2. \(R_w = 1.68\times10^{-8}\times15/1.5\times10^{-6} = 0.168\ \Omega\) each, \(0.336\ \Omega\) together. \(i = 230/23.336 = 9.856\) A. Element: \(226.7\) V, \(9.856^2\times23 = 2234\) W. Cable: \(9.856^2\times0.336 = 32.6\) W. Source: \(230\times9.856 = 2267\) W delivered. \(2234.2 + 32.6 = 2266.9\) ✓.

3. The cable wastes \(32.6/2267 = 1.44\%\) of the source's power (equal to its share of the loop's resistance, \(0.336/23.336\)). The element gets 2234 W, 66 W (2.9%) short of 2300 W: it loses twice over, from the lower current and the lower voltage, \(P \propto v^2\).

Check. Units: \(\Omega\,\mathrm m\cdot\mathrm m/\mathrm m^2 = \Omega\) ✓. Limit \(R_w\to0\): \(i = 10\) A and 2300 W, unit 00's figures ✓. The wasted fraction equals the cable's resistance fraction because the same current flows through everything in a series loop.

P5 Proof: the voltmeter's path medium

exercises: Ohm's law as a local law, Faraday's law in integral form, what (H1) buys — a proof

Model a voltmeter as a uniform resistor \(R_m\) (conductivity \(\sigma\), cross-section \(A\), length \(\ell\)) between two ideal leads, displaying \(R_mi_m\) where \(i_m\) is the current through it from the + lead to the − lead.

  1. Prove that it displays \(\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l\) taken from the end of its + lead to the end of its − lead along the leads and through the resistor — with no assumption that \(\vec E\) is a gradient.
  2. Two such meters have their lead ends at the same points P and Q, along paths \(\pi_1\) and \(\pi_2\). Prove that \(V_2 - V_1 = -\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_S\vec B\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\), where \(S\) is a surface bounded by "\(\pi_2\) from P to Q, then \(\pi_1\) back from Q to P", oriented by that direction.
  3. Deduce that if (H1) holds for the region the leads run through, every voltmeter on P and Q reads the same, namely \(\varphi_{\text P} - \varphi_{\text Q}\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a line integral split into pieces: two leads and one resistor. Part 2 is a difference of two line integrals with the same ends, which is one closed line integral.

Rung 2

In an ideal lead \(\vec E = \vec 0\) (why?). In the resistor, \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) holds pointwise whatever produced \(\vec E\). For part 2, reversing a path reverses the sign of its integral.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: inside the resistor \(\vec E = \vec J/\sigma\), with \(|\vec J| = i_m/A\) along the path, so \(\int_{\text{resistor}}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = i_m\ell/\sigma A\).

Worked solution

Idea. A meter measures a current; Ohm's local law converts it into a line integral of \(\vec E\) through the meter; the leads add nothing; and the difference between two meters is a closed loop, which Faraday's law evaluates.

1. In a lead, \(\vec E = \vec J/\sigma\) with \(\sigma\to\infty\) and \(\vec J\) finite, so \(\vec E = \vec 0\) and the lead contributes nothing. In the meter's resistor the current \(i_m\) flows uniformly from + to −, \(\vec J = (i_m/A)\hat t\) along the path's tangent \(\hat t\), and \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) — a local law, valid for any \(\vec E\) — gives \(\vec E\cdot\hat t = i_m/\sigma A\). Integrating over the length: \(i_m\ell/\sigma A = i_mR_m\), with \(R_m = \ell/\sigma A\) (unit 00). So the display equals \(\int_{\pi}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l\) from P to Q. \(\square\)

2. \(V_2 - V_1 = \int_{\pi_2,\,\text P\to\text Q}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l - \int_{\pi_1,\,\text P\to\text Q}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \int_{\pi_2,\,\text P\to\text Q}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l + \int_{\pi_1,\,\text Q\to\text P}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \oint_{\partial S}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l\), and Faraday's law (MR 24) for the fixed surface \(S\) makes this \(-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_S\vec B\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\). \(\square\)

3. Under (H1), the closed curve of part 2 encloses no changing flux (each meter is a small element whose own resistor links none), so \(V_2 = V_1\) for every pair of meters. For the common value: by the deck's The voltage law, \(\vec E = -\nabla\varphi\) outside the elements, and each meter's leads are ideal wires, at the potentials of P and Q. The meter is an element whose voltage is \(\varphi_{\text P} - \varphi_{\text Q}\) and whose law is \(v = R_mi_m\); its display \(R_mi_m\) is therefore \(\varphi_{\text P} - \varphi_{\text Q}\). \(\square\)

Check. Units: \(\mathrm A\cdot\Omega = \mathrm V\), and \(\mathrm{T\,m^2\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm V\) ✓. On Lewin's ring: meter 2's path out and meter 1's back go clockwise round the solenoid once; with the flux out of the page and growing, the clockwise circulation is \(+\dot\Phi = \mathcal E\), so \(V_2 - V_1 = 1\) V, as in the deck ✓. (Orienting \(S\) clockwise means its normal points into the page, so \(-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_S\vec B\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = -(-\dot\Phi) = +\dot\Phi\).)

P6 Lewin both ways medium

exercises: readings from \(\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l\) along each meter's path; winding number; the lumped model with a cut placed elsewhere

Lewin's ring as in the preamble, with general \(R_1, R_2\) and EMF \(\mathcal E\); voltmeters draw negligible current and all have their + lead on P.

  1. Find the readings \(V_1\) (leads beside \(R_1\), outside the ring on the left) and \(V_2\) (beside \(R_2\), on the right) in terms of \(R_1, R_2, \mathcal E\). Check against the deck's numbers.
  2. A third meter sits on the left like meter 1, but its + lead, on the way from P to the meter, is taken once round the outside of the whole ring, clockwise. What does it read? What if the loop is anticlockwise?
  3. Build the lumped model with the cut drawn from the solenoid to the left instead of the right. Find \(\varphi_{\text P} - \varphi_{\text Q}\) and both readings in that model, and compare with the deck's.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each reading is a number of volts fixed by one closed loop: the meter's path from P to Q, closed by going back from Q to P through one of the resistors. Faraday's law gives that loop's circulation as \(\mathcal E\) times how many times it winds clockwise.

Rung 2

Close every meter's path through \(R_1\) (upward, with the current, contributing \(+IR_1\)). Then \(V + IR_1 = k\mathcal E\), where \(k\) is the loop's clockwise winding number. For part 3, a cut on the left crosses \(R_1\)'s branch and meter 1's lead; there the clockwise direction is upward.

Rung 3

First line: \(I = \mathcal E/(R_1 + R_2)\), clockwise, and meter 1's loop does not encircle the solenoid, so \(V_1 + IR_1 = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. A meter's reading is decided by the loop it makes with the circuit and by how many times that loop winds round the changing flux. Where a lumped model puts its cut changes the node potentials but cannot change a reading.

1. \(I = \mathcal E/(R_1 + R_2)\). Meter 1's loop (path P→Q, back up through \(R_1\)) does not encircle the solenoid: \(V_1 + IR_1 = 0\), so \(V_1 = -\mathcal ER_1/(R_1 + R_2)\). Meter 2's path with \(R_1\) winds once clockwise: \(V_2 + IR_1 = \mathcal E\), so \(V_2 = \mathcal E - \mathcal ER_1/(R_1 + R_2) = \mathcal ER_2/(R_1 + R_2)\). Numbers: \(-1\times100/1000 = -0.1\) V and \(1\times900/1000 = 0.9\) V ✓. (The same as closing meter 2's path through \(R_2\), which does not wind: \(V_2 - IR_2 = 0\).)

2. The extra clockwise turn makes the loop (meter 3's path, back through \(R_1\)) wind once clockwise: \(V_3 + IR_1 = \mathcal E\), \(V_3 = \mathcal E - 0.1 = 0.9\) V — the same as meter 2, though the meter sits on the left. Anticlockwise: \(k = -1\), \(V_3 = -\mathcal E - 0.1 = -1.1\) V. In general \(V = -0.1 + k\) volts.

3. Cut to the left: sources of EMF \(\mathcal E\), pointing upward (clockwise on the left side), in \(R_1\)'s branch and in meter 1's lead. With \(\varphi_{\text Q} = 0\): up through \(R_1\)'s branch, \(\varphi_{\text P} = \varphi_{\text Q} + \mathcal E - IR_1 = 0.9\) V. Down \(R_2\): \(\varphi_{\text Q} = 0.9 - IR_2 = 0\) ✓. Meter 2 (no source) reads \(\varphi_{\text P} - \varphi_{\text Q} = +0.9\) V. Meter 1: from Q up through its source to the point X below the meter, \(\varphi_{\text X} = \mathcal E = 1\) V; it reads \(\varphi_{\text P} - \varphi_{\text X} = -0.1\) V. The node potential difference is 0.9 V here, \(-0.1\) V with the cut on the right; the readings are the same ✓.

Check. \(V_2 - V_1 = \mathcal E\) in every case ✓. Limit \(R_1\to0\): \(V_1\to0\) and \(V_2\to\mathcal E\) — meter 1 is across a short circuit, and meter 2's loop with the ring's left wire takes the whole EMF ✓. Units: volts throughout ✓.

P7 Parallel plates routine

exercises: \(C = \varepsilon_0A/d\), stored energy two ways, the dielectric factor

Two square plates of area \(1\ \mathrm{cm^2}\), 1 mm apart, in air (treat as vacuum), fringing neglected.

  1. Find \(C\).
  2. At 100 V: find \(q\), the field \(E\) between the plates, and the stored energy, both as \(\tfrac12Cv^2\) and as \(\int\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2E^2\,\mathrm dV\).
  3. Fill the gap with polyethylene (\(\varepsilon_r = 2.25\), assumed) and keep 100 V. What are \(C\) and the stored energy now?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answers are a capacitance in picofarads and energies in nanojoules; one formula each.

Rung 2

\(A = 10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2}\), \(d = 10^{-3}\) m. The field is \(v/d\); the volume is \(Ad\).

Rung 3

First line: \(C = 8.854\times10^{-12}\times10^{-4}/10^{-3} = 8.854\times10^{-13}\) F.

Worked solution

Idea. A one-line capacitance, then the energy checked from the field, then the dielectric factor.

1. \(C = 0.885\) pF.

2. \(q = Cv = 88.5\) pC. \(E = 100/10^{-3} = 1.00\times10^5\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\). \(\tfrac12Cv^2 = \tfrac12\times8.854\times10^{-13}\times10^4 = 4.43\) nJ. From the field: \(\tfrac12\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times10^{10}\times(10^{-4}\times10^{-3}) = 4.43\) nJ ✓.

3. \(C = 2.25\times0.885 = 1.99\) pF; at the same voltage the energy is \(2.25\times4.43 = 9.96\) nJ.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm m^2/\mathrm m = \mathrm F\) ✓; \(\mathrm F\,\mathrm V^2 = \mathrm J\) ✓. The approximation: the plates are 10 mm wide and 1 mm apart, only ten gaps across, so the field that bulges out at the edges is not obviously negligible; the 0.885 pF is the uniform-field value, and no size is claimed here for the correction. Tiny: the deck's stray-capacitance estimate \(\varepsilon_0\ell\) is \(0.89\) pF for conductors \(\ell = 10\) cm across — the same scale — which is why (H2)'s neglect is usually safe at low frequency.

P8 Coaxial \(L'\) and \(C'\) medium

exercises: capacitance and inductance from field energy, \(L'C' = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\), the dielectric factor

  1. For a coaxial cable of radii \(a \lt b\) with vacuum between, derive \(C'\) and the external \(L'\) per metre from the electric and magnetic field energies (not from \(q = Cv\) or from a flux).
  2. Show that \(L'C' = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\) for every \(b/a\).
  3. Evaluate both for \(b/a = 4\) and for \(b/a = e\).
  4. Fill the \(b/a = 4\) cable with polyethylene (\(\varepsilon_r = 2.25\), assumed). What are \(C'\), \(L'\) and \(L'C'\) now?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each answer is a per-metre constant, found by computing an energy per metre and matching it to \(\lambda^2/2C'\) or \(\tfrac12L'I^2\).

Rung 2

Gauss and Ampère on circles give \(E = \lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0r\) and \(B = \mu_0I/2\pi r\). The volume element per metre is \(2\pi r\,\mathrm dr\). A non-magnetic insulator leaves \(\vec B\) alone.

Rung 3

First line: \(W'_E = \int_a^b\frac{\varepsilon_0}2\frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi^2\varepsilon_0^2r^2}2\pi r\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac ba\).

Worked solution

Idea. Both constants are the coefficient of a quadratic energy; both fields fall as \(1/r\), so both energies carry the same \(\ln(b/a)\), which cancels in the product.

1. \(W'_E = \frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac ba = \frac{\lambda^2}{2C'}\), so \(C' = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}\). \(W'_B = \int_a^b\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\frac{\mu_0^2I^2}{4\pi^2r^2}2\pi r\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi}\ln\frac ba = \tfrac12L'I^2\), so \(L' = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba\).

2. \(L'C' = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac ba\cdot\frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)} = \mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1.113\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{s^2\,m^{-2}}\), and \(1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 2.998\times10^8\) m/s.

3. \(b/a = 4\): \(C' = 40.1\ \mathrm{pF/m}\), \(L' = 277\ \mathrm{nH/m}\). \(b/a = e\): \(\ln = 1\), so \(C' = 2\pi\varepsilon_0 = 55.6\ \mathrm{pF/m}\) and \(L' = \mu_0/2\pi = 200\ \mathrm{nH/m}\). Product \(1.113\times10^{-17}\) both times ✓.

4. \(C' = 2.25\times40.13 = 90.3\ \mathrm{pF/m}\); \(L' = 277\ \mathrm{nH/m}\) unchanged; \(L'C' = 2.25\,\mu_0\varepsilon_0\), and \(1/\sqrt{L'C'} = c/1.5 = 2.0\times10^8\) m/s — a speed unit 08 will interpret.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,F\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{s^2\,m^{-2}}\) ✓. Limits: \(b\to a\) gives \(C'\to\infty\), \(L'\to0\); \(b/a\to\infty\) gives \(C'\to0\), \(L'\to\infty\); the product is fixed throughout ✓.

P9 Internal inductance, two ways medium

exercises: inductance from energy; flux linkage for a thick conductor; Ampère's law inside a wire

A long straight round wire of radius \(a\) carries a current \(I\) spread uniformly over its cross-section.

  1. Find \(B(r)\) for \(r \lt a\), and the field energy per metre inside the wire. Deduce \(L'_{\text{int}}\).
  2. Now by flux linkage. Split the wire into thin tubes; the tube at radius \(r\) carries the fraction \(2r\,\mathrm dr/a^2\) of the current and links only the internal flux between \(r\) and \(a\) (per metre). Weight each tube's linked flux by its fraction of the current and add. Do you get the same \(L'_{\text{int}}\)?
  3. Give \(L'_{\text{int}}\) in nH/m, and say why it does not depend on \(a\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Both routes give one number per metre, in henries per metre, independent of the radius.

Rung 2

Ampère on a circle of radius \(r \lt a\) encloses \(Ir^2/a^2\). For part 2, the internal flux per metre between \(r\) and \(a\) is \(\int_r^aB(s)\,\mathrm ds\).

Rung 3

First line: \(B(r) = \mu_0Ir/2\pi a^2\), so the energy per metre is \(\int_0^a\frac{\mu_0I^2r^2}{8\pi^2a^4}2\pi r\,\mathrm dr\).

Worked solution

Idea. For a thick conductor "the flux through the circuit" is ambiguous, because different filaments enclose different flux. The energy definition avoids the question; the weighted flux linkage answers it, and the two agree.

1. \(2\pi rB = \mu_0Ir^2/a^2\), so \(B = \mu_0Ir/2\pi a^2\). Energy per metre: \(\frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi a^4}\int_0^ar^3\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{16\pi}\). Setting it to \(\tfrac12L'_{\text{int}}I^2\): \(L'_{\text{int}} = \mu_0/8\pi\).

2. Internal flux per metre between \(r\) and \(a\): \(\int_r^a\frac{\mu_0Is}{2\pi a^2}\mathrm ds = \frac{\mu_0I(a^2 - r^2)}{4\pi a^2}\). Weighted: \(\Lambda' = \int_0^a\frac{\mu_0I(a^2 - r^2)}{4\pi a^2}\cdot\frac{2r}{a^2}\mathrm dr = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi a^4}\Big[a^2r^2 - \tfrac{r^4}{2}\Big]_0^a = \frac{\mu_0I}{8\pi}\). So \(L'_{\text{int}} = \Lambda'/I = \mu_0/8\pi\) ✓, the same.

3. \(4\pi\times10^{-7}/8\pi = 5.0\times10^{-8}\) H/m \(= 50\ \mathrm{nH/m}\). Independent of \(a\) because \(B\) inside scales as \(1/a\) at the surface while the area scales as \(a^2\): energy density \(\propto 1/a^2\) times area \(\propto a^2\).

Check. Units: \(\mu_0\) in \(\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}\) ✓. At \(r = a\) the inside field \(\mu_0I/2\pi a\) matches the outside field — continuous ✓. At high frequency the current is not uniform (unit 10), so this is a low-frequency value.

P10 The diverging self-inductance medium

exercises: magnetic field energy near a line current; why a real inductance depends on wire radius

  1. A straight line current \(I\) (zero radius) has \(B = \mu_0I/2\pi r\). Find the field energy per metre between radii \(r_0\) and \(r_1\), and show it grows without bound as \(r_0\to0\). What does this say about the self-inductance of a loop of infinitely thin wire?
  2. Show that giving the wire a radius \(a\) with uniform current makes the energy inside finite, and find the total per metre out to \(r_1\).
  3. Unit 01's coaxial cable has \(b = 2\) mm. Find the external \(L'\) for inner radii \(a = 0.5\) mm, \(0.25\) mm and \(1\ \mu\mathrm m\). How fast does \(L'\) grow as \(a\) shrinks?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to 1 is a logarithm; the answer to 3 is three numbers that grow only slowly.

Rung 2

Energy per metre is \(\int\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}2\pi r\,\mathrm dr\); with \(B\propto1/r\) the integrand is \(\propto1/r\). For 2, use P9's internal energy.

Rung 3

First line: \(\int_{r_0}^{r_1}\frac{\mu_0I^2}{8\pi^2r^2}2\pi r\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi}\ln\frac{r_1}{r_0}\).

Worked solution

Idea. A \(1/r\) field has an energy density \(1/r^2\), which over the area element \(r\,\mathrm dr\) gives \(\mathrm dr/r\): logarithmically divergent at both ends. The wire's radius cuts off the inner end, a return conductor the outer one.

1. \(W' = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi}\ln\frac{r_1}{r_0}\to\infty\) as \(r_0\to0\). Since \(W = \tfrac12Li^2\), a loop of zero-radius wire has infinite self-inductance: "the inductance of a thin loop" is meaningless without the wire's radius. Neumann's formula with both points on one loop shows the same disease, since \(1/|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|\) is not integrable along a line when the two points meet.

2. Inside a wire of radius \(a\), P9 gives \(\mu_0I^2/16\pi\), finite. Total out to \(r_1\): \(\frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi}\big(\ln\frac{r_1}{a} + \tfrac14\big)\). (The outer end still needs cutting off; in a real circuit the return current does it, as the coax's outer conductor does at \(b\).)

3. \(L' = 2\times10^{-7}\ln(b/a)\): \(a = 0.5\) mm gives \(277\ \mathrm{nH/m}\); \(0.25\) mm gives \(416\ \mathrm{nH/m}\); \(1\ \mu\)m gives \(1.52\ \mu\mathrm{H/m}\). Halving \(a\) adds \(2\times10^{-7}\ln2 = 139\ \mathrm{nH/m}\) each time: every factor of \(e\) in radius adds 200 nH/m.

Check. Units: \(\mu_0I^2\) in \(\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,A^2} = \mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}\) ✓. The divergence is real but gentle: shrinking the wire by a factor of 500 (from 0.5 mm to 1 μm) multiplies \(L'\) by only 5.5.

P11 Proof: Neumann is symmetric, and \(M^2 \le L_1L_2\) medium

exercises: Stokes, the Coulomb-gauge vector potential, energy positivity, positive semi-definite forms — a proof

  1. For two thin closed loops, derive Neumann's formula for \(M_{12}\) (flux of loop 2's field through loop 1, per unit \(i_2\)) and deduce \(M_{12} = M_{21}\).
  2. Show that adding a gradient \(\nabla\chi\) to the vector potential does not change \(M_{12}\).
  3. Using \(W = \tfrac12L_1i_1^2 + Mi_1i_2 + \tfrac12L_2i_2^2 = \int|\vec B|^2/2\mu_0\,\mathrm dV\), prove \(M^2 \le L_1L_2\).
  4. Can \(M\) be negative? What would that mean physically, and does it affect the bound?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a change of variables in a flux: surface integral to line integral, then a formula for the integrand. Part 3 is a fact about quadratics that are never negative.

Rung 2

Stokes turns \(\int_{S_1}\nabla\times\vec A_2\cdot\mathrm d\vec A\) into \(\oint_{\gamma_1}\vec A_2\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_1\). MR 23 gives \(\vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\vec J/|\vec x - \vec y|\,\mathrm dV\), and a thin wire has \(\vec J\,\mathrm dV = i\,\mathrm d\vec l\). For part 3, divide by \(i_1^2\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: with \(i_2 = xi_1\), \(W/i_1^2 = \tfrac12L_2x^2 + Mx + \tfrac12L_1 \ge 0\) for every real \(x\).

Worked solution

Idea. Symmetry is visible once the flux is written as a double line integral; the bound is the statement that field energy, a quadratic form in the currents, never goes negative.

1. \(\Lambda_{12} = \int_{S_1}\vec B_2\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \oint_{\gamma_1}\vec A_2\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_1\) (Stokes, MR 04), and \(\vec A_2(\vec r_1) = \frac{\mu_0i_2}{4\pi}\oint_{\gamma_2}\frac{\mathrm d\vec l_2}{|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|}\) (MR 23, Coulomb gauge). So \(M_{12} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint_{\gamma_1}\oint_{\gamma_2}\frac{\mathrm d\vec l_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2}{|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|}\). Exchanging the labels 1 and 2 changes neither the dot product nor the distance, and the order of the two integrations does not matter (Fubini, for these continuous integrands on disjoint loops), so \(M_{21} = M_{12}\). \(\square\)

2. \(\oint_{\gamma_1}\nabla\chi\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_1 = 0\) for a closed loop and single-valued \(\chi\) (MR 04). So any gauge gives the same \(M_{12}\) — as it must, since \(M_{12}\) is a flux of \(\vec B\). \(\square\)

3. \(W \ge 0\) for all \(i_1, i_2\), being the integral of \(|\vec B|^2\). With \(i_1 = 0\), \(\tfrac12L_2i_2^2 \ge 0\), so \(L_2 \ge 0\); likewise \(L_1 \ge 0\). If \(L_2 = 0\), then \(Mx + \tfrac12L_1 \ge 0\) for all \(x\) forces \(M = 0\) and the bound holds. If \(L_2 \gt 0\), the minimum of \(q(x)\) at \(x = -M/L_2\) is \(\tfrac12L_1 - M^2/2L_2 \ge 0\), i.e. \(M^2 \le L_1L_2\). \(\square\)

4. Yes: reversing the orientation of one loop reverses \(M\)'s sign. Physically, a positive current in loop 2 then makes a negative flux through loop 1 as oriented. The bound involves \(M^2\), so it is unaffected.

Check. Units: \(\mu_0\) times \(\mathrm m\cdot\mathrm m/\mathrm m\) is henries ✓. A limit: two loops very far apart, distance \(D\): the integrand is about \(\mathrm d\vec l_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2/D\), and \(\oint\mathrm d\vec l_1 = \vec 0\) for a closed loop, so the leading term vanishes and \(M\) falls faster than \(1/D\) — unit 15 finds the rate ✓. The figure in the deck checks the bound for \(L_1 = L_2 = 1\), \(M = 0.6\).

P12 A hairpin of leads medium

exercises: inductance of a two-wire loop from flux; \(v = L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt\) as a number; why stray inductance matters for fast switching

Two parallel wires of radius \(r = 0.25\) mm, \(d = 2\) mm apart centre to centre, \(\ell = 1\) cm long, joined at one end: a hairpin. Approximate each wire as a straight segment of a long pair carrying \(\pm I\), count only the flux in the gap between the wires, and neglect the ends.

  1. Show that the flux through the gap is \(\Phi = \frac{\mu_0I\ell}{\pi}\ln\frac{d - r}{r}\), so \(L \approx \frac{\mu_0\ell}{\pi}\ln\frac{d - r}{r}\). Evaluate it.
  2. Add the two wires' internal inductances (P9). What is the total?
  3. A current of 10 A through the hairpin switches off in 10 ns, at a steady rate. What voltage appears across it?
  4. Is the approximation good here? Name what it neglects.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answers are a few nanohenries and a few volts. The flux is a sum of two equal pieces, one from each wire.

Rung 2

In the gap both wires' fields point the same way. Wire 1's field at distance \(s\) from its axis is \(\mu_0I/2\pi s\); integrate from its surface \(s = r\) to the other wire's surface \(s = d - r\), over a length \(\ell\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\Phi_1 = \ell\int_r^{d-r}\frac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s}\mathrm ds = \frac{\mu_0I\ell}{2\pi}\ln\frac{d - r}{r}\), and wire 2 gives the same.

Worked solution

Idea. A pair of leads is a one-turn loop of area about \(\ell d\); its inductance is a few nanohenries, and a fast enough change of current turns that into volts.

1. Each wire contributes \(\frac{\mu_0I\ell}{2\pi}\ln\frac{d - r}{r}\); together \(\Phi = \frac{\mu_0I\ell}{\pi}\ln\frac{d - r}{r}\), and \(L = \Phi/I\). Numbers: \(\mu_0\ell/\pi = 4\times10^{-7}\times0.01 = 4\times10^{-9}\) H, \(\ln(1.75/0.25) = \ln7 = 1.946\): \(L = 7.8\) nH.

2. Each wire adds \(\mu_0\ell/8\pi = 0.5\) nH, so 1 nH in all: \(L \approx 8.8\) nH.

3. \(|\mathrm di/\mathrm dt| = 10/10^{-8} = 10^9\ \mathrm{A\,s^{-1}}\). \(|v| = L|\mathrm di/\mathrm dt| = 7.8\) V from the gap flux, 8.8 V with the internal part: about 8 V, from one centimetre of wire. Its sign, by the passive convention, opposes the fall of the current: the hairpin tries to keep it flowing.

4. Rough. The wires are only 5 times longer than their spacing, so the ends — the joining bend, and wherever the other ends connect — carry field the long-pair formula ignores; the "long pair" itself assumes \(\ell \gg d\). The field outside the gap, beyond each wire, is not counted (the return current makes it small far away, but not zero nearby). And the 1 nH internal part is a low-frequency value; at the frequencies a 10 ns edge contains, the current crowds to the surface (unit 10) and it shrinks. Treat the answer as an estimate of the order of magnitude — enough to say "volts, not millivolts".

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm m = \mathrm H\); \(\mathrm H\cdot\mathrm{A\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm V\) ✓. With \(r \ll d\) the formula becomes \(\frac{\mu_0\ell}{\pi}\ln\frac dr = 8.3\) nH, close to 7.8 nH ✓. Energy stored at 10 A: \(\tfrac12\times8.8\times10^{-9}\times100 = 0.44\ \mu\)J, delivered in 10 ns — an average of 44 W, which is why fast switching circuits keep their current loops small.
