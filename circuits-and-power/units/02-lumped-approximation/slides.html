The lumped approximation: three hypotheses under which Kirchhoff's laws are true

Circuit theory replaces a field problem by one voltage and one current per component. Under three stated hypotheses Kirchhoff's laws are theorems of Maxwell's equations — and two voltmeters show what happens when the first one fails.

Units 00 and 01 worked with fields: a current density inside copper, surface charge on the wire, a Poynting vector in the space round it. 02 · overview

Two voltmeters, the same two points, two answers

the running example: Lewin's ring

Two resistors, $R_1 = 100\ \Omega$ and $R_2 = 900\ \Omega$, joined in a ring round a long solenoid. The solenoid's flux grows, and the EMF round the ring is $\mathcal E = 1$ V, so $I = 1$ mA flows. Two voltmeters are clipped to the same junctions P and Q, + lead on P.

The puzzle. They read $-0.1$ V and $+0.9$ V. Both meters work. Which is "the voltage between P and Q"?

P. How the EMF is made. A current ramped in the solenoid's winding makes a flux $\Phi(t)$ inside it that grows; outside a long solenoid $\vec B \approx \vec 0$ (MR 23, The solenoid: closed but not exact, again). The ring never touches the field, yet $\oint\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \pm\dot\Phi$ round it by Faraday's law. While the ramp lasts the EMF is steady, and so is $I$. The ring's own self-inductance is neglected throughout; McDonald's note shows why that is safe here.

What a circuit is

Elements, terminals, nodes and ideal wires; the symbols for drawing them; and one sign convention that keeps the energy bookkeeping straight.

02 · what a circuit is

From fields to a list of numbers

a lumped circuit

A set of lumped elements, each a region of space with two terminals, joined by ideal wires. Where wires meet is a node.

Each element $k$ carries one current $i_k$, through it from terminal to terminal, and has one voltage $v_k$ across it — numbers, functions of time only.

An ideal wire has no resistance: by unit 00's $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ with $\sigma\to\infty$, the field along it vanishes, so its whole length is at one potential. A node is then one point of the circuit, whatever its shape.

what is being claimed

That the fields can be forgotten: everything outside the elements is summarised by the $v_k$ and $i_k$, and every element by a law relating its own two. Section 2 says when this is true.

Four elements (dashed boundaries; the shaded blob is whatever is inside), four nodes (dots), wires between. Element $k$'s voltage is marked + and −, its current by an arrow.

The words. An element is a piece of the apparatus with a boundary drawn round it: a resistor, the plates of a capacitor, a coil, a battery. Its terminals are the two places where wires cross its boundary. A node is a set of terminals joined by wires. This is the vocabulary of Agarwal and Lang (§1.2–1.3), who call the whole construction the lumped circuit abstraction: what is inside each boundary is "lumped" into one law relating one voltage to one current.

Why an ideal wire is equipotential. In a conductor $\vec E = \vec J/\sigma$ (unit 00). A wire carrying finite current with $\sigma\to\infty$ has $\vec E\to\vec 0$ inside it, so $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$ along it vanishes. A real wire is a resistor; when its resistance matters it is drawn as one, as the kettle's wires are on The kettle, as the first diagram.

The analogy Agarwal and Lang draw: a lumped element is to a circuit what a point mass is to Newtonian mechanics (MR 00). Its size and shape are forgotten; only what crosses its boundary is kept.

Notation. MR 24 writes $\mathrm d\vec r$ for the line element; this course writes $\mathrm d\vec l$, as circuit texts do. Potential is $\varphi$, flux $\Phi$, volume and surface charge density $\varrho$ and $\Sigma$ (unit 00's choice, since $\rho$ and $\sigma$ are resistivity and conductivity).

02 · what a circuit is

The symbols

A circuit diagram draws each element as a symbol and each wire as a line; lengths and positions mean nothing. Only what is joined to what.

this course's symbols

Resistor: a rectangle, the IEC 60617 form. Capacitor: two plates. Inductor: a row of loops. Voltage source: a circle marked + and −. Current source: a circle with an arrow. A dot joins wires; lines crossing without one do not touch.

the zigzag

American texts draw a zigzag. IEEE Std 315-1975 (ANSI Y32.2-1975) gives the zigzag or the rectangle, as equal alternatives.

The symbols drawn once. Every later diagram in the course uses these.

The two resistor symbols, sourced. IEEE Std 315-1975, Graphic Symbols for Electrical and Electronics Diagrams, also published as ANSI Y32.2-1975, gives the resistor (its symbol 2.1.1) as a zigzag "or" a rectangle, the zigzag drawn first. It marks both as recognised by the IEC, and its introduction says that the order in which alternatives are drawn implies no preference. The IEC's own collection is IEC 60617, Graphical symbols for diagrams. This course uses the rectangle throughout.

The sources' symbols are a convention of this course: a circle with + and − for a source that fixes a voltage, and a circle with an arrow for one that fixes a current. Standards and textbooks differ in the details; what matters is that the diagram says which quantity the source fixes and in which sense. The elements themselves are defined in section 3.

What a diagram does not say. The physical layout: how long a wire is, how close two wires run, what area a loop encloses. Section 2 shows that forgetting these is exactly the lumped approximation, and section 4 shows a case in which the area enclosed by a loop is the whole story.

02 · what a circuit is

The passive sign convention

the passive sign convention

For each element, choose which terminal to mark +; then draw its current arrow into the + terminal. Here $v = \varphi_+ - \varphi_-$, and $i$ is the current through the element from + to −; either may come out negative. Then

$$p = v\,i$$

is the power the element absorbs: $p > 0$ absorbing, $p < 0$ delivering.

That $vi$ is the power is unit 01's theorem: the Poynting flux into a quasi-static two-terminal component, through any closed surface round it, is $vi$. Here it becomes bookkeeping.

Units: $\mathrm V\cdot\mathrm A = \mathrm{J\,C^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{C\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm W$ ✓.

The same marking on both: arrow into +. The battery's current really leaves by its + terminal, so its $i$ is negative and its $p$ is negative.

Why a convention is needed. Without one, "the power of an element" has no sign. With it, every element's $p$ is read the same way, and conservation of energy in a circuit becomes a statement with no exceptions: the sum of $p$ over all elements is zero (checked on the kettle in Worked: the kettle, one loop; unit 03 proves it for every circuit as Tellegen's theorem).

Unit 01's theorem, restated. Draw a closed surface round one element, crossed only by its two leads, on which the fields are quasi-static. Unit 01 proves that the Poynting flux into the surface is $(\varphi_A - \varphi_B)\,i$, where $i$ enters at lead A. With A the + terminal that is exactly $vi$ with the arrow into +. So the convention is not arbitrary: it is the orientation in which $vi$ is the energy flowing in.

Sources. A battery delivering energy has its current leaving by the + terminal, so with the passive marking $i < 0$ and $p < 0$. Some texts mark sources the other way round so that $p > 0$ means delivering; this course never does — one rule for every element, sources included.

The labels. The figure's $v$ and $i$ are the element's own variables. The arrow for the actual flow of positive charge in the battery is drawn separately, in the second colour, only to show why $i$ is negative.

section 2

Three hypotheses, and Kirchhoff's laws

Draw the element boundaries so that changing flux and changing charge stay inside them, and keep the whole circuit small against a wavelength. Then both of Kirchhoff's laws are theorems.

02 · three hypotheses

Three hypotheses

the lumped matter discipline (Agarwal & Lang, §1.3)

(H1) No changing magnetic flux through any closed curve drawn outside the elements: changing flux is confined to inductors.

(H2) No net charge accumulates on any element, wire or node: charge may separate inside an element (a capacitor's plates carry $\pm q$), but each element's total stays zero.

(H3) The circuit is small against the wavelength $c/f$ of anything happening in it, so retardation (MR 24) can be ignored and the fields follow the charges and currents instantaneously.

(H1) and (H2) are about where the boundaries are drawn; (H3) is about the physics, and is what lets the first two hold to good accuracy. The next four slides spend (H2) and (H1).

Dashed: element boundaries. The capacitor's charges and the inductor's field (both drawn in the accent colour) stay inside theirs. The dotted curve $\gamma$ runs entirely outside the elements.

The source, and what is ours. Agarwal and Lang (2005), §1.3, call these three constraints the lumped matter discipline: (1) $\partial\Phi_B/\partial t = 0$ through any closed path outside the elements; (2) $\partial q/\partial t = 0$ for the total charge within each element; (3) signal timescales much longer than the propagation delay of electromagnetic waves across the element — and, for a whole circuit, across the circuit, so that it is much smaller than the wavelength $c/f$ (300 km at 1 kHz, 300 m at 1 MHz, 300 mm at 1 GHz). Their appendix A develops the constraints from Maxwell's equations. The wording and numbering here are this course's; the content is theirs.

Not unit 01's H1–H3. Unit 01's theorem $p = vi$ had three hypotheses of its own, about the fields on one closed surface: no changing $\vec B$ there, no displacement current through it, and leads that cross it at one potential. (H1) here is its first made global — no changing flux round any loop outside the elements. (H2) is its second made global: by Gauss's law, an element whose total charge is constant has no net displacement current through its boundary. Its third is built into the ideal wire, at one potential throughout. And (H3) here is new: it is the answer to unit 01's question of how slowly "slowly varying" must be.

"Choose the boundaries". (H1) and (H2) are partly choices. Changing flux and accumulating charge happen in every real circuit; the discipline is to draw element boundaries round the places they happen, and call what is inside an inductor or a capacitor. When two coils' fields reach each other, the discipline's answer is to draw a single boundary round both and call the pair one element — which is exactly what section 4 does to Lewin's ring.

Why (H3) underpins the others. A changing current somewhere makes a changing $\vec B$ everywhere, and a changing voltage makes changing charge on every conductor. (H1) and (H2) can only be true to a good approximation, and What each hypothesis neglects shows that the size of what they neglect is set by the ratio of the circuit's size to the wavelength — which (H3) makes small.

02 · three hypotheses

The current law

Kirchhoff's current law

At every node, the currents leaving through the wires sum to zero:

$$\sum_{\text{wires at the node}} i_{\text{out}} = 0 .$$
proof

Enclose the node by a closed surface $S$ cutting only wires; let $Q$ be the charge inside. Continuity (MR 24) and the divergence theorem (MR 05) give

$$\oint_S\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = -\frac{\mathrm dQ}{\mathrm dt}.$$

Outside the wires $\vec J = \vec 0$, so the left side is $\sum i_{\text{out}}$. By (H2), $Q$ is constant. $\square$

Units: both sides in $\mathrm{C\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm A$ ✓.

The closed surface $S$ (dashed) meets the circuit only where it cuts the four wires. Inside it there is only wire, which by (H2) holds no changing net charge.

Full proof

The continuity equation $\partial_t\varrho + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ (MR 24) is exact: it is charge conservation, and it follows from Maxwell's equations by taking the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell law. Integrate it over the region $V$ inside $S$ and apply the divergence theorem (MR 05) to the second term: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_V\varrho\,\mathrm dV + \oint_S\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = 0$, with $\mathrm d\vec A$ outward. The surface meets conductors only where it cuts a wire; elsewhere it lies in insulator where $\vec J = \vec 0$. Over each cut the flux of $\vec J$ is, by definition, the current leaving through that wire. So $\sum i_{\text{out}} = -\mathrm dQ/\mathrm dt$.

Where (H2) entered. Only in the last step: the charge on the node's wires is constant. Charge may still sit there — unit 00's surface charge on a wire is exactly such charge — but it must not change. In a real circuit it does change a little whenever the voltage does, since a wire at potential $\varphi$ carries surface charge roughly proportional to $\varphi$; that is the stray capacitance of What each hypothesis neglects.

The displacement current. With $Q$ constant, Gauss's law makes the flux of $\varepsilon_0\vec E$ through $S$ constant too, so no net displacement current crosses $S$ either. Unit 03 rewrites this law as $A\,i = 0$ for the circuit's incidence matrix.

02 · three hypotheses

One current per element

An element has two terminals. Apply the same argument to a surface round the whole element: by (H2) its total charge is constant, so the current in at one terminal equals the current out at the other. That is why one number $i$ describes it.

why (H2) is per element

A capacitor's plates do accumulate charge: $+q(t)$ on one, $-q(t)$ on the other. A surface round both plates encloses $q - q = 0$, and the law holds. A surface through the gap, round one plate only, encloses $q(t)$, and $\sum i_{\text{out}} = -\dot q \ne 0$ there.

Across the gap no charge flows; what crosses it is MR 24's displacement current $\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t\vec E$, and its flux is exactly $\dot q$. The lumped picture hides it inside the element boundary. Problem P3 does the balance.

The same capacitor, two surfaces. $S_1$ is the element boundary: one wire in, one out, total charge zero, so the two currents are equal. $S_2$ cuts the gap: one wire, and a charge that changes.

The surface $S_2$ in full. It encloses the left plate, with charge $q(t)$, and cuts the incoming wire and the gap. Continuity: $\oint_{S_2}\vec J\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = -\dot q$; the only conduction current through $S_2$ is the wire's, entering, so $-i = -\dot q$: $i = \dot q$. Nothing in that is a failure of physics — it is the statement that the plate charges up. It is a failure of the current law applied to a surface the lumped picture forbids: the plate is not a node, because a node may not accumulate charge.

MR 24's repair. The flux of $\vec J + \varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E$ through any closed surface is zero (the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell law). Through $S_2$ the displacement term crossing the gap is $\varepsilon_0\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \dot q$ by Gauss, and it balances the wire's current exactly. This is the "Ampère's law meets a charging capacitor" slide of MR 24, read as a statement about where to draw a capacitor's boundary.

Agarwal and Lang's version (§1.3): choose element boundaries so that the total charge within each is constant, and the current into one terminal then equals the current out of the other.

02 · three hypotheses

The voltage law

Kirchhoff's voltage law

Round every closed loop of elements, with each $v_k$ counted $+$ when the loop runs from its $+$ terminal to its $-$ terminal and $-$ otherwise,

$$\sum_{\text{loop}} \pm v_k = 0 .$$
proof

Let $D$ be the space outside the elements. By (H1) and Faraday's law, $\oint_\gamma\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = -\dot\Phi_\gamma = 0$ for every closed $\gamma$ in $D$, so $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ on $D$ (MR 04: zero circulation round every loop gives a potential). Each node is a wire, so at one $\varphi$. Then $v_k = \varphi_{+} - \varphi_{-}$, and the signed sum round a loop of nodes telescopes to $0$. $\square$

So $v_k$ is well defined: $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$ from $+$ to $-$ is the same along every path in $D$.

Once a potential exists outside the elements, each voltage is a difference of node potentials, and any sum of differences round a closed chain of nodes cancels. Here $v_d = \varphi_4 - \varphi_1$.

Full proof

Step 1, a potential. Faraday's law (MR 24) for a fixed closed curve $\gamma$ bounding a surface: $\oint_\gamma\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = -\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int\vec B\cdot\mathrm d\vec A$. (H1) says the right side vanishes for every closed curve lying in $D$. MR 04 proved that a continuous field whose circulation round every closed curve in a connected region vanishes is a gradient there: fix a point $x_0$ and set $\varphi(x) = -\int_{x_0}^x\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$ along any path in $D$; the value does not depend on the path, because two paths together make a closed curve. Note that this needs zero circulation round every loop, not merely $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$: $D$ is not simply connected (it has the elements as holes), and section 4 is about a curl-free field that is not a gradient.

Step 2, nodes. Inside an ideal wire $\vec E = \vec 0$, so a wire and everything joined to it is at one potential. Each terminal sits where a wire meets an element boundary, so it too has a potential.

Step 3, telescoping. A loop of elements visits nodes $n_0, n_1, \dots, n_m = n_0$. Each step contributes $\pm v_k = \varphi_{n_{j}} - \varphi_{n_{j+1}}$, with the sign fixed by whether the loop meets the element's $+$ terminal first. The sum is $\varphi_{n_0} - \varphi_{n_m} = 0$.

What is not claimed. Nothing about the field inside an element: in an inductor $\vec E$ is far from a gradient. The voltage across an element is defined by a path outside it. Unit 03 rewrites this law as $v = A^{\mathsf T}\varphi$.

02 · three hypotheses · example

Worked: Kirchhoff's laws from Maxwell's equations

worked example 1 — the kettle's loop, every hypothesis named where it is used

1. Wires are nodes. In the four joining wires $\sigma\to\infty$, so $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside them (unit 00's $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$): four nodes, potentials $\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_4$, numbered clockwise from the source's + terminal.

2. One current. Round each element, continuity and (H2) give current in = current out. Round each node (two wires), continuity and (H2) give the same: one current $i$, clockwise, through all four.

3. A potential outside. (H1) and Faraday: $\oint\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = 0$ round every loop in the air, so $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ there.

4. Voltages. Live wire $v_{\mathrm{L}} = \varphi_1 - \varphi_2$, element $v_{\mathrm E} = \varphi_2 - \varphi_3$, neutral $v_{\mathrm N} = \varphi_3 - \varphi_4$, source $v_{\mathrm S} = \varphi_1 - \varphi_4$.

5. The law. Clockwise, the loop meets the three resistors $+$ first and the source $-$ first:

$$v_{\mathrm L} + v_{\mathrm E} + v_{\mathrm N} - v_{\mathrm S} = 0 .$$

6. Where (H3) went. Into steps 2 and 3: it is what makes the stray charge on the wires and the flux of the loop small enough to neglect — the next slides but one.

Check. Units: volts throughout ✓. Limit: with the wires' $R_w\to0$, $\varphi_1 = \varphi_2$ and $\varphi_3 = \varphi_4$, and the law says the element sees the full 230 V ✓.

Step 2, more slowly. A node here is the short wire joining two elements' terminals: a surface round it cuts two wires, so (H2) and continuity say the current out through one equals the current in through the other. A surface round an element cuts its two leads and says the same. Going round, every element carries the same current. That is the current law, used four times.

Step 5, the signs. Travel clockwise from node 1. The live wire is entered at its $+$ terminal (node 1): $+v_{\mathrm L}$. The element and the neutral wire likewise. The source is entered from node 4 at its $-$ terminal and left at node 1: $-v_{\mathrm S}$. Rearranged, $v_{\mathrm S} = v_{\mathrm L} + v_{\mathrm E} + v_{\mathrm N}$: the source's 230 V is shared out among the three resistors. Section 3 adds the element laws and finds the shares.

Why the example is honest. Nothing here used the elements' laws. Kirchhoff's laws are statements about the wires and the space between the elements, and they hold whatever is inside the boundaries — resistors, a kettle, a lamp, a transistor.

02 · three hypotheses · the ring

Where the first hypothesis fails

Draw element boundaries round the ring's two resistors. The loop through $R_1$ and $R_2$ runs outside both boundaries, and it encircles the solenoid:

$$\oint_{\text{ring}}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = -\dot\Phi = \mathcal E\ \text{(clockwise)} \ne 0 .$$
(H1) fails

The changing flux is inside the solenoid, and the solenoid is inside no element. So $\vec E$ outside the elements is not a gradient, the voltage across $R_1$ is not defined by "any path outside it", and the voltage law fails: $v_1 + v_2 = \mathcal E$, not $0$.

Each resistor still obeys Ohm's law, $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = iR$ through it (unit 00, a local law): $0.1$ V and $0.9$ V, summing to $\mathcal E = 1$ V.

The highlighted loop lies outside both element boundaries (dashed), yet it encircles changing flux. No choice of boundary round the resistors can fix that.

The orientation. Take the flux $\Phi$ out of the page, growing. Faraday's law with the anticlockwise orientation (normal out of the page) gives $\oint_{\circlearrowleft}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = -\dot\Phi < 0$, so the clockwise circulation is $+\dot\Phi = \mathcal E = 1$ V. A current $I = \mathcal E/(R_1 + R_2) = 1/1000 = 1$ mA flows clockwise: down through $R_2$, up through $R_1$.

Ohm's law still holds inside each resistor. Unit 00's $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ is a local law and does not care whether $\vec E$ is a gradient. Integrating it along a resistor in the direction of the current gives $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = iR$, as in unit 00's derivation of $R = \rho L/A$ for a uniform bar. In the ideal wires $\vec E = \vec 0$. So the clockwise circulation is $IR_1 + IR_2 = 0.1 + 0.9 = 1$ V, consistent with Faraday.

Where the field goes. Inside the wires the induced field $-\partial_t\vec A$ is exactly cancelled by the field of surface charge that piles up on the wires — the same mechanism unit 00 used to steer a current round a bend. Inside the resistors the two do not cancel, and what is left drives the current.

What fails, precisely. Not Ohm's law, not the current law (the ring's charge is constant, so (H2) holds), but the claim that a voltage across $R_1$ can be read off by a path outside it. Section 4 makes this exact.

02 · three hypotheses

What each hypothesis neglects

A circuit of size $\ell$ at angular frequency $\omega$, with element resistances of order $R$. Write $\omega/c = 2\pi/\lambda$ and $\mu_0c = 1/\varepsilon_0c = 376.7\ \Omega$.

(H1) neglects stray inductance. The loop links flux $\sim\mu_0\ell i$ (Biot–Savart), an EMF $\sim\omega\mu_0\ell i$ against $Ri$:

$$\frac{\text{error}}{\text{signal}} \sim 2\pi\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{\mu_0c}{R}.$$

(H2) neglects stray capacitance. Wires at voltage $v$ carry charge $\sim\varepsilon_0\ell v$ (Gauss), a current $\sim\omega\varepsilon_0\ell v$ against $v/R$:

$$\frac{\text{error}}{\text{signal}} \sim 2\pi\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\cdot\frac{R}{\mu_0c}.$$

(H3) neglects retardation (phase errors $2\pi\ell/\lambda$) and radiation, of relative order $(\ell/\lambda)^2\,\mu_0c/R$ — one power of $\ell/\lambda$ smaller still (notes).

The neglected pieces, drawn as the elements they would be. For the kettle ($\ell = 10$ m, $R = 23\ \Omega$, 50 Hz) the two ratios are $1.7\times10^{-4}$ and $6.4\times10^{-7}$.

the moral

Both errors carry $\ell/\lambda$: (H3) makes (H1) and (H2) good. The second factor says which fails first: low $R$ meets stray inductance, high $R$ meets stray capacitance.

The estimates, step by step. These are orders of magnitude, with logarithms and factors of order one dropped. Flux: a current $i$ round a loop of size $\ell$ makes $B\sim\mu_0i/\ell$ near it (Biot–Savart), over an area $\sim\ell^2$, so the loop links $\Phi\sim\mu_0\ell i$; at angular frequency $\omega$ the EMF is $\omega\Phi$. Divided by $Ri$: $\omega\mu_0\ell/R = (\omega\ell/c)(\mu_0c/R)$. Charge: a conductor of size $\ell$ at potential $v$ has $E\sim v/\ell$ near it over area $\ell^2$, so by Gauss a charge $\sim\varepsilon_0\ell v$; the current to change it is $\omega\varepsilon_0\ell v$. Divided by $v/R$: $(\omega\ell/c)(R/\mu_0c)$, using $\varepsilon_0c = 1/\mu_0c$.

The constant. $\mu_0c = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\times2.998\times10^8 = 376.7\ \Omega$, a resistance built from two constants; $1/\varepsilon_0c$ is the same number since $c^2 = 1/\mu_0\varepsilon_0$. It will reappear in unit 08.

The kettle's numbers. $\ell/\lambda = 1.668\times10^{-6}$; $2\pi\times1.668\times10^{-6}\times376.7/23 = 1.72\times10^{-4}$; $2\pi\times1.668\times10^{-6}\times23/376.7 = 6.40\times10^{-7}$. Small, but the first is not "six orders": the factor $\mu_0c/R = 16$ costs one. For a 10 cm board at 2 GHz with $R = 50\ \Omega$ (an assumed value), the ratios are 32 and 0.56: nothing is small.

Radiation. MR 24's Larmor formula for a short current element of length $\ell$ carrying $i = \hat\imath\cos\omega t$ (peak $\hat\imath$): the dipole moment changes as $\dot p = \ell i$, the radiated power is $\mu_0\ddot p^2/6\pi c$, and its mean is $\tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat\imath^2$ with $R_{\mathrm{rad}} = \mu_0\omega^2\ell^2/6\pi c = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}\mu_0c(\ell/\lambda)^2 \approx 789(\ell/\lambda)^2\ \Omega$. Against $R$ the ratio is $\tfrac{2\pi}{3}(\mu_0c/R)(\ell/\lambda)^2$ — for the kettle, $9.5\times10^{-11}$; a closed loop, whose opposite sides carry opposite currents, radiates less still. Unit 16 derives this as the radiation resistance.

When the estimate is too generous. A coil is designed to link a lot of flux and is drawn inside an inductor's boundary; a capacitor likewise. The estimates are for what is left outside the boundaries.

What lives inside the boundaries. Linearity and uniqueness give each element one number; the field energy fixes its sign and its meaning; Faraday's law fixes the sign of $v = L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$.

02 · element laws

The resistor

the resistor's element law $$v = R\,i,\qquad R > 0 .$$

Unit 00: integrate $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ along a conductor between two equipotential ends, and $R$ comes out of a Laplace problem — $\rho L/A$ for a uniform bar.

An element law is a relation between an element's own $v$ and $i$; it is what the boundary hides. With the passive convention the resistor absorbs

$$p = vi = Ri^2 \ge 0,$$

all of it turned to heat — unit 00's Joule heating $\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV$, and by unit 01 all of it arriving through the resistor's surface as Poynting flux.

Units: $\Omega\cdot\mathrm A^2 = \mathrm{V\,A} = \mathrm W$ ✓.

The resistor's law is a line through the origin, lying entirely in the shaded quadrants where $p = vi \ge 0$: a resistor can only absorb.

Why a law for each element is legitimate. (H1) and (H2) also stop elements interacting except through their terminals: changing flux and changing charge are kept inside each boundary, so no changing field from one element reaches another to change its behaviour. So each element's law can be found once, in isolation, and used in any circuit.

The resistor, from unit 00. A conductor between two equipotential electrodes at $\varphi_+$ and $\varphi_-$: inside, $\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla\varphi) = 0$, and the current through it is linear in $\varphi_+ - \varphi_-$ by linearity and uniqueness of that boundary-value problem. The constant is $1/R$. For a uniform bar $R = \rho L/A$ (unit 00, The uniform wire, proved).

Inside a changing field. $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ is local and holds whatever $\vec E$'s origin, so $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = iR$ along the resistor, in the direction of $i$, even when $\vec E$ is not a gradient (section 4 uses exactly this). Under (H1) that integral is also $\varphi_+ - \varphi_-$, and $v = Ri$.

02 · element laws

Capacitance from linearity and uniqueness

capacitance

Two conductors carry charges $+q$ and $-q$, with no other charge, in vacuum. Then the potential difference between them is proportional to $q$:

$$q = C\,v,\qquad v = \varphi_+ - \varphi_- ,$$

with $C$ depending only on the conductors' shapes and placement.

proof

Linearity: Laplace's equation outside the conductors, constant $\varphi$ on each, and the charge on each (a flux of $\vec E$, by Gauss) are all linear, so if $\varphi$ fits charge $q$, then $\lambda\varphi$ fits $\lambda q$. Uniqueness: given the charges, $\varphi$ is unique (MR 23's energy method; notes). So $v$ is a linear function of $q$. $\square$

Units: $C$ in farads, $\mathrm F = \mathrm{C\,V^{-1}}$ ✓. That $C > 0$ is the next slide.

Any two conductors with equal and opposite charges. The field lines run from $+q$ to $-q$; each conductor is an equipotential (MR 23).

Full proof of uniqueness for given charges

Let $\varphi_1, \varphi_2$ both be harmonic in the region $D$ outside the conductors, each constant on each conductor, each giving charge $+q$ on conductor A and $-q$ on B, and each tending to zero at infinity like $1/r$ or faster. Their difference $w$ is harmonic in $D$, constant ($w_A$, $w_B$) on each conductor, and the flux of $\nabla w$ through each conductor's surface is zero (the charges agree). Green's first identity (MR 23) on $D$, cut off by a large sphere: $\int_D|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm dV = \oint_{\partial D}w\,\partial_nw\,\mathrm dA$. On conductor A the boundary term is $w_A\oint_A\partial_nw\,\mathrm dA = 0$; likewise on B; on the large sphere $w\,\partial_nw = O(r^{-3})$ against area $O(r^2)$, which vanishes as $r\to\infty$. So $\nabla w = \vec 0$, $w$ is constant, and the constant is zero at infinity. This is MR 23's energy method with charges prescribed in place of potentials.

Existence is cited: MR 23 names the existence theory for the Dirichlet problem (Perron's method). Given it, the solutions with prescribed conductor potentials $(\varphi_A, \varphi_B)$, zero at infinity, form a two-dimensional space, and the map from $(\varphi_A, \varphi_B)$ to the charges $(q_A, q_B)$ is linear. By the uniqueness just proved it is injective, so, being a linear map of $\mathbb R^2$ to itself, it is onto: every pair of charges, $(q, -q)$ among them, is reached. Nothing in this course needs more.

What MR 23 left out is the general system: for $n$ conductors with arbitrary charges, potentials are linear in charges through a symmetric matrix. A two-terminal capacitor needs only the case $q_A = -q_B$, which (H2) guarantees: the element's total charge is zero.

Time-dependence. By (H3) the field at each instant is the electrostatic field of that instant's charges, so $q(t) = C\,v(t)$ holds at every instant with the same $C$.

The capacitor's energy, and $i = C\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt$

energy fixes the sign $$W = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}\int|\vec E|^2\,\mathrm dV = \tfrac12qv = \tfrac12Cv^2 = \frac{q^2}{2C}.$$

$W > 0$ for $q \ne 0$, so $C > 0$.

proof

Green's first identity (MR 23) turns $\int|\nabla\varphi|^2$ into one boundary term per conductor, its potential times its charge (notes). $\square$

the capacitor

An element with law $q = Cv$. Current into + lands on the + plate, $i = \mathrm dq/\mathrm dt$:

$$i = C\,\frac{\mathrm dv}{\mathrm dt}.$$

Marked passively: the arrow into + charges the + plate.

energy check

$p = vi = Cv\,\dot v = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\tfrac12Cv^2\big)$: all stored, returned when $v$ falls ✓. Units: $\mathrm F\,\mathrm V^2 = \mathrm J$ ✓.

Full proof of $W = \tfrac12qv$

Let $D$ be the region outside the conductors, cut off by a large sphere. Green's first identity (MR 23) with $u = v = \varphi$ harmonic: $\int_D|\nabla\varphi|^2\,\mathrm dV = \oint_{\partial D}\varphi\,\partial_n\varphi\,\mathrm dA$, with $n$ the normal pointing out of $D$ — that is, into each conductor. Just outside a conductor, $-\partial_n\varphi$ along the normal out of the conductor is $E_\perp = \Sigma/\varepsilon_0$ (MR 23's jump condition); along the normal into it the sign flips, so $\partial_n\varphi = \Sigma/\varepsilon_0$. With $\varphi$ constant on the conductor, its boundary term is $\varphi_k\oint\Sigma\,\mathrm dA/\varepsilon_0 = \varphi_kq_k/\varepsilon_0$. The large sphere contributes nothing in the limit (total charge zero, so $\varphi = O(r^{-2})$). Multiply by $\varepsilon_0/2$: $W = \tfrac12(q\varphi_+ + (-q)\varphi_-) = \tfrac12qv$.

The element law and (H2). Current $i$ enters the + terminal and charges the + plate at the rate $i$. By (H2) the element's total charge is constant, so the same $i$ leaves the − terminal, and the − plate's charge is $-q$ throughout. Then $i = \dot q = C\dot v$.

MR 24's capacitor. MR 24's running example charged a parallel-plate capacitor at a steady current and checked that the Poynting flux through its rim equals $\mathrm d(\tfrac12CV^2)/\mathrm dt$. That is this energy check, seen from outside: unit 01's theorem says the Poynting flux in is $vi$, and here $vi$ is the rate of storage.

The limits. DC ($\dot v = 0$): $i = 0$, the capacitor is an open circuit. A sudden jump in $v$ would need infinite current: a capacitor's voltage is continuous whenever its current is finite.

Two capacitances: plates and a coaxial cable

Parallel plates, area $A$, gap $d$. With the field taken uniform between the plates and zero outside, Gauss gives $E = q/\varepsilon_0A$, and $v = Ed$:

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0A}{d}.$$

This neglects the fringing field at the edges, which is small when $d$ is small against the plates' width. P7 puts in numbers.

Coaxial, inner radius $a$, outer $b$, charge $\lambda$ per metre. Gauss on a cylinder gives $E = \lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0r$; integrate from $a$ to $b$:

$$v = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac ba,\qquad C' = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}\ \text{per metre}.$$

Units: $\varepsilon_0$ in $\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$, so $\varepsilon_0A/d$ in F and $C'$ in $\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$ ✓.

Two shapes for which Laplace's equation is one-dimensional. The plates are an approximation (fringing neglected); the coaxial formula is exact for a long cable.

The plates. For infinite plates the field between them is uniform by symmetry and zero outside, with $E = \Sigma/\varepsilon_0 = q/\varepsilon_0A$ by MR 23's jump condition. For finite plates this is the approximation that ignores the field bulging out at the edges. That correction is small when $d$ is small against the plates' size; no figure for its size is quoted here. The energy check: $\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2E^2\cdot Ad = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0A}{2d}(Ed)^2 = \tfrac12Cv^2$ ✓.

The coax. For an infinitely long cable the field is radial by symmetry. Gauss's law on a cylinder of radius $r$ and length $\ell$ between the conductors encloses $\lambda\ell$, so $2\pi r\ell\,E = \lambda\ell/\varepsilon_0$. The inner conductor is the higher: $v = \int_a^bE\,\mathrm dr = (\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(b/a)$, and $C' = \lambda/v$. Outside the outer conductor $E = 0$ (the enclosed charge is zero). This is the same field unit 01 used, there fixed by the voltage rather than the charge. Worked: the coaxial cable's C′ and L′ puts in numbers and checks the energy.

Per metre. A primed quantity is per unit length: $C'$ in $\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}$, $L'$ in $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}}$. A 1 m length of cable is, to this approximation, a capacitor of $C'\times1$ m.

A dielectric multiplies $C$ by $\varepsilon_r$

cited · Griffiths, ch. 4

If the whole space between two conductors is filled with a linear dielectric of relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$, every capacitance is multiplied by $\varepsilon_r$:

$$C = \varepsilon_r\,C_{\text{vacuum}} .$$

Why, in one sentence: the field polarises the insulator's molecules, and the layers of their charge next to each conductor partly cancel the conductor's own, so for given $q$ the field and $v$ are smaller by $\varepsilon_r$.

02 · element laws

Inductance from linearity

inductance

A closed thin loop $\gamma$ carries a current $i$, with no magnetic material near. The flux of its own $\vec B$ through a surface $S$ bounded by $\gamma$ is proportional to $i$:

$$\Lambda = L\,i ,$$

with $L$ depending only on the loop's shape.

proof

Biot–Savart (MR 23) is linear in the current, so $\vec B = i\,\vec b(\vec x)$ with $\vec b$ fixed by the geometry. Then $\Lambda = i\int_S\vec b\cdot\mathrm d\vec A$, and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ makes the integral the same for every $S$ with boundary $\gamma$. $\square$

For a coil of $N$ turns the surface bounded by the wire winds round $N$ times, so each turn's flux counts once: the flux linkage $\Lambda \approx N\Phi_{\text{turn}}$.

Units: henry, $\mathrm H = \mathrm{Wb\,A^{-1}} = \mathrm{V\,s\,A^{-1}}$ ✓.

The loop's own field threads the surface it bounds. Double $i$ and every field line doubles: the flux is linear in $i$. (A sketch, not a computed field.)

Mechanics to Relativity never defined inductance; this slide and the next do, from Biot–Savart and the field energy. Griffiths, ch. 7, treats the same material (inductance, Neumann's formula, energy in magnetic fields) and is the textbook reference for this section.

Independence of the surface. Two surfaces with the same boundary together enclose a volume; by the divergence theorem the difference of their fluxes is $\int\nabla\cdot\vec B\,\mathrm dV = 0$. This is the same argument MR 23 used for the current through a loop.

The hypotheses hidden in "fixed by the geometry". (i) The current's distribution across the wire has a fixed shape, merely scaled by $i$. For a thin wire that is automatic; for a thick one it holds at low frequency, where unit 00's Laplace problem fixes it; at high frequency the current crowds to the surface (unit 10's skin effect) and $L$ changes. (ii) No magnetic material — iron would make $\vec B$ nonlinear in $i$. (iii) (H3): Biot–Savart is magnetostatics, valid at each instant only when retardation is negligible.

Flux linkage. For a helical coil the surface bounded by the wire is a spiral ramp, crossed by the field once per turn. If every turn encloses the same flux $\Phi_{\text{turn}}$ the total is $N\Phi_{\text{turn}}$; in general it is the integral over the ramp, which is what $\Lambda$ means. Faraday's law applies to $\Lambda$, since the wire is the boundary of the ramp.

The inductor's energy: $\tfrac12Li^2 = \int B^2/2\mu_0$

energy fixes the sign $$W = \int\frac{|\vec B|^2}{2\mu_0}\,\mathrm dV = \tfrac12\int\vec A\cdot\vec J\,\mathrm dV$$ $$= \tfrac12\,i\,\Lambda = \tfrac12Li^2 .$$

$W > 0$ for $i \ne 0$, so $L > 0$.

proof

$\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$ in Coulomb gauge (MR 23). The identity $\nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec B) = \vec B\cdot\nabla\times\vec A - \vec A\cdot\nabla\times\vec B$ and $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ give $|\vec B|^2 = \mu_0\vec A\cdot\vec J + \nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec B)$; the divergence integrates to zero. For a thin wire $\int\vec A\cdot\vec J\,\mathrm dV = i\oint_\gamma\vec A\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = i\Lambda$ by Stokes. $\square$

For a thick conductor, define $L$ by $W = \tfrac12Li^2$: the energy is the definition, and it never needs a "surface bounded by the wire".

Seen from above. In Coulomb gauge $\vec A$ runs alongside the current that makes it (Biot–Savart's integrand is $\vec J/r$), so $\vec A\cdot\vec J > 0$ on the wire and its circulation is the flux through the loop.

Full proof

The identity. $\nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec B) = \vec B\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) - \vec A\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B)$ is the same product-rule identity MR 24 used for Poynting's theorem, with $\vec A, \vec B$ for $\vec E, \vec B$. With $\nabla\times\vec A = \vec B$ and $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J$ (magnetostatics, MR 23; by (H3) the displacement current is negligible): $|\vec B|^2 = \mu_0\vec A\cdot\vec J + \nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec B)$.

The surface term. Integrate over a ball of radius $r$. For a localised current, the Coulomb-gauge $\vec A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\vec J/|\vec x - \vec y|$ falls as $r^{-2}$ when the net current through every plane vanishes (a closed loop), and $\vec B$ as $r^{-3}$; the flux of $\vec A\times\vec B$ through the sphere is $O(r^{-5}\cdot r^2)\to0$. So $\int|\vec B|^2 = \mu_0\int\vec A\cdot\vec J$.

The thin wire. $\vec J\,\mathrm dV = i\,\mathrm d\vec l$ along the wire, so $\int\vec A\cdot\vec J\,\mathrm dV = i\oint_\gamma\vec A\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$, and Stokes (MR 04) with $\nabla\times\vec A = \vec B$ makes the circulation the flux through $S$: $\Lambda$. Hence $W = \tfrac12i\Lambda = \tfrac12Li^2$.

The same answer from work. To raise the current from 0 to $i$ against the EMF $\mathrm d\Lambda/\mathrm dt$ that Faraday's law induces, a source must supply power $i\,\mathrm d\Lambda/\mathrm dt = Li\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$, a total of $\int_0^iLi'\,\mathrm di' = \tfrac12Li^2$. Worked: v = L di/dt, sign and energy does it with signs.

A thin wire is a warning, not a convenience: for an infinitely thin wire $W$ is infinite (Inside the wire, and a divergence), and the thin-wire formula must be read as the limit of a thick one.

Worked: $v = L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$, sign and energy

worked example 2 — the inductor's law, from Faraday, with the passive convention

1. Mark it. Terminal a is +, b is −; $i$ enters at a. $v = \varphi_a - \varphi_b$, defined by a path outside the element (H1).

2. The loop. $\gamma$: from a through the coil's wire to b (in the direction of $i$), then back from b to a outside the element. Orient the surface $S$ by $\gamma$; then $i$'s own flux through $S$ is $+\Lambda = +Li$.

3. Faraday: $\oint_\gamma\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = -\dot\Lambda = -L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$.

4. The left side. In the ideal wire of the coil $\vec E = \vec 0$. Outside, $\int_b^a\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \varphi_b - \varphi_a = -v$.

5. So $-v = -L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$:

$$v = L\,\frac{\mathrm di}{\mathrm dt}.$$
energy check

$p = vi = Li\,\dot i = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\tfrac12Li^2\big)$: power absorbed is the rate of storing field energy, so $\int vi\,\mathrm dt = \tfrac12Li^2$ from rest ✓. Units: $\mathrm H\cdot\mathrm{A\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm V$ ✓.

Step 2, the orientation. Stokes' convention (MR 04): the surface's normal and the loop's direction are related by the right-hand rule. $\gamma$ follows the current through the coil, and the current's own field threads the coil in the right-hand sense of its direction, so with this orientation the flux of the coil's own field through $S$ is $+Li$ with $L > 0$ (previous slide). The short return path across the outside contributes a sliver of surface that, by (H1), carries no changing flux.

Step 4, the field in the wire. The coil's wire is taken ideal: $\sigma\to\infty$, so $\vec E = \vec 0$ inside it, even though the induced field $-\partial_t\vec A$ is large there — surface charge on the coil's wire cancels it, exactly as in the ring's wires on Where the first hypothesis fails. The whole of $\oint\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$ is on the outside path. A real coil's resistance is drawn as a separate resistor in series.

Lenz, checked. If $i$ grows, $v > 0$: the + terminal is higher, so the coil pushes back against the current that is growing — the minus sign of MR 24's Faraday slide, now hidden inside the passive convention. The ordinary-language version "a back-EMF opposes the change" and the formula $v = +L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$ say the same thing.

The limits. DC ($\dot i = 0$): $v = 0$, the inductor is a short circuit. A sudden jump in $i$ would need infinite voltage: an inductor's current is continuous whenever its voltage is finite. Unit 05's ignition coil spends this.

Energy, integrated. From $i = 0$ at $t = 0$: $\int_0^tvi\,\mathrm dt' = \int_0^tLi\,\dot i\,\mathrm dt' = \int_0^{i(t)}Li'\,\mathrm di' = \tfrac12Li(t)^2$, the field energy of the previous slide. Nothing is dissipated: an ideal inductor stores and returns.

Inside the wire, and a divergence

Inside a solid inner conductor with uniform current, Ampère gives $B = \mu_0Ir/2\pi a^2$. Its energy per metre adds an internal inductance:

$$\int_0^a\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}2\pi r\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{16\pi},$$ $$L'_{\text{int}} = \frac{\mu_0}{8\pi} = 50\ \mathrm{nH/m},$$

whatever the radius (P9). For unit 01's cable at low frequency, $L' = 277 + 50 = 327\ \mathrm{nH/m}$. At high frequency the current is not uniform (unit 10) and this part shrinks.

the honest caveat

For an infinitely thin wire, $a\to0$: $\ln(b/a)\to\infty$. The field energy near a line current is infinite, so a thin loop's self-inductance diverges. A real $L$ depends on the wire's radius (P10).

$B$ rises linearly inside the copper (shaded), falls as $1/r$ in the gap, and vanishes outside the cable. The dashed curve is the thin-wire field continued inward: its energy, $\int B^2r\,\mathrm dr\sim\int\mathrm dr/r$, has no finite limit at $r = 0$.

The internal field. Ampère's law on a circle of radius $r < a$ encloses the fraction $r^2/a^2$ of the current (uniform $\vec J$, unit 00's DC distribution): $2\pi rB = \mu_0Ir^2/a^2$. Energy per metre: $\int_0^a\frac{1}{2\mu_0}\frac{\mu_0^2I^2r^2}{4\pi^2a^4}2\pi r\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi a^4}\cdot\frac{a^4}{4} = \frac{\mu_0I^2}{16\pi}$. Setting this to $\tfrac12L'_{\text{int}}I^2$: $L'_{\text{int}} = \mu_0/8\pi = 4\pi\times10^{-7}/8\pi = 5.0\times10^{-8}$ H/m. The radius has cancelled.

Why the energy route is needed here. Inside the wire there is no single "surface bounded by the current": different filaments of current enclose different flux. Defining $L$ by $W = \tfrac12Li^2$ sidesteps the question. (Problem P9 also does it by the flux-linkage route, weighting each filament.)

The divergence. Near any thin wire carrying $I$ the field is close to $\mu_0I/2\pi r$, and the energy per metre between radii $r_0$ and $r_1$ is $\frac{\mu_0I^2}{4\pi}\ln\frac{r_1}{r_0}$, which grows without bound as $r_0\to0$. A filament of zero radius therefore has infinite self-inductance. The real wire's radius $a$ cuts the integral off, and the inside contributes only the finite $\mu_0/8\pi$. Neumann's double integral on the next slide shows the same disease when both points run over one loop: $1/|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|$ blows up as they meet.

The skin effect (unit 10): at high frequency the current crowds into a layer near the conductor's surface, the field inside the copper shrinks, and $L'_{\text{int}}$ falls towards zero. The 50 nH/m is a low-frequency value.

Mutual inductance: Neumann's formula

mutual inductance and Neumann's formula

Two thin loops. The flux of loop 2's field through loop 1 is $\Lambda_{12} = M_{12}i_2$, with

$$M_{12} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint_{\gamma_1}\oint_{\gamma_2}\frac{\mathrm d\vec l_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2}{|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|} = M_{21}.$$
proof

Stokes: $\Lambda_{12} = \int_{S_1}\vec B_2\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \oint_{\gamma_1}\vec A_2\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_1$. Coulomb gauge for a thin wire (MR 23): $\vec A_2(\vec r_1) = \frac{\mu_0i_2}{4\pi}\oint_{\gamma_2}\frac{\mathrm d\vec l_2}{|\vec r_1-\vec r_2|}$. Substitute. The integrand is symmetric under $1\leftrightarrow2$. $\square$

The ring and the solenoid are two such loops: the ring's EMF is $M\,\mathrm di_s/\mathrm dt$. For MR 23's solenoid ($n = 2000\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, radius 2 cm) $M = \mu_0n\pi R^2 = 3.16\ \mu\mathrm H$, and 1 V needs $\mathrm di_s/\mathrm dt = 3.2\times10^5\ \mathrm{A\,s^{-1}}$.

The double integral runs over every pair of line elements, one on each loop, weighted by $1/\text{distance}$. Swapping the loops swaps nothing.

Step by step. (i) The flux of $\vec B_2$ through a surface $S_1$ bounded by $\gamma_1$ is the circulation of $\vec A_2$ round $\gamma_1$: Stokes (MR 04) with $\vec B_2 = \nabla\times\vec A_2$. (ii) MR 23's Biot–Savart slide gives the Coulomb-gauge potential of a current density as $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\vec J/|\vec x - \vec y|\,\mathrm dV$; for a thin wire $\vec J\,\mathrm dV = i_2\,\mathrm d\vec l_2$. (iii) Substituting gives the double integral, in which $\mathrm d\vec l_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2$ and $|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|$ are both symmetric. Gauge does not matter: adding $\nabla\chi$ to $\vec A_2$ adds $\oint\nabla\chi\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_1 = 0$.

Reciprocity. $M_{12} = M_{21}$ says: a current of 1 A in loop 2 links the same flux with loop 1 as 1 A in loop 1 links with loop 2, however different the loops are. Unit 03 meets reciprocity again as a theorem about whole networks. Neumann's name for the formula is the standard attribution (Griffiths, ch. 7).

Self-inductance by the same formula? Put $\gamma_1 = \gamma_2$ and the integrand blows up where the two points meet: the divergence of the previous slide again. For a thick wire one uses the energy definition instead.

The ring and the solenoid. A long solenoid of $n$ turns per metre and radius $R$ carrying $i_s$ has $B = \mu_0ni_s$ inside and $\approx 0$ outside (MR 23), so any loop encircling it once links $\Phi = \mu_0ni_s\pi R^2$: $M = \mu_0n\pi R^2$. With MR 23's numbers, $M = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\times2000\times\pi\times0.02^2 = 3.158\times10^{-6}$ H. An EMF of 1 V then needs $\mathrm di_s/\mathrm dt = 1/3.158\times10^{-6} = 3.17\times10^5$ A/s — for instance 3.2 A in 10 μs. The solenoid used here is MR 23's for illustration, not Lewin's apparatus. Note what $M$ does not depend on: the ring's shape, as long as it goes round once.

The inductance matrix is positive semi-definite

energy of two loops $$W = \tfrac12L_1i_1^2 + M\,i_1i_2 + \tfrac12L_2i_2^2$$ $$= \int\frac{|\vec B|^2}{2\mu_0}\,\mathrm dV \ \ge\ 0$$

for all $i_1, i_2$. So the inductance matrix $\begin{pmatrix}L_1 & M\\ M & L_2\end{pmatrix}$ is positive semi-definite, and

$$M^2 \le L_1L_2 .$$
proof

$\tfrac12\int\vec A\cdot\vec J = \tfrac12(i_1\Lambda_1 + i_2\Lambda_2)$ with $\Lambda_1 = L_1i_1 + Mi_2$, $\Lambda_2 = Mi_1 + L_2i_2$. Set $i_2 = xi_1$: $\tfrac12L_1 + Mx + \tfrac12L_2x^2 \ge 0$ for all $x$, so the discriminant $M^2 - L_1L_2 \le 0$. $\square$

Units: $\mathrm{H\,A^2} = \mathrm J$ ✓. Unit 07 names $M/\sqrt{L_1L_2}$; here only the bound.

$W/i_1^2$ against $x = i_2/i_1$, computed for $L_1 = L_2 = 1$. A parabola that must never enter the shaded region $W < 0$: that forces $M^2 \le L_1L_2$. With $M = 1.4$ it would.

Full proof

The energy. With both loops carrying current, $\vec J = \vec J_1 + \vec J_2$ and, by linearity, $\vec A = \vec A_1 + \vec A_2$ and $\vec B = \vec B_1 + \vec B_2$. The inductor's energy showed $\int|\vec B|^2/2\mu_0 = \tfrac12\int\vec A\cdot\vec J$ for any localised steady current. For thin loops $\int\vec A\cdot\vec J_1 = i_1\oint_{\gamma_1}\vec A\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_1 = i_1\Lambda_1$, the total flux through loop 1: its own $L_1i_1$ plus loop 2's $Mi_2$. Likewise for loop 2 with $M_{21} = M$. So $W = \tfrac12(L_1i_1^2 + Mi_1i_2 + Mi_1i_2 + L_2i_2^2)$.

The bound. $W$ is the integral of a square, so $W \ge 0$ for every choice of the two currents. If $i_1 = 0$ that says $L_2 \ge 0$; otherwise divide by $i_1^2$: the quadratic $q(x) = \tfrac12L_2x^2 + Mx + \tfrac12L_1$ is non-negative for all real $x$. With $L_2 > 0$ its minimum, at $x = -M/L_2$, is $\tfrac12L_1 - M^2/2L_2 \ge 0$, i.e. $M^2 \le L_1L_2$. In linear-algebra terms (assumed background): a symmetric matrix is positive semi-definite iff its quadratic form is non-negative, and for a $2\times2$ matrix that is non-negative diagonal entries and determinant $L_1L_2 - M^2 \ge 0$.

The figure's numbers. $L_1 = L_2 = 1$: with $M = 0.6$ the minimum is $\tfrac12 - 0.18 = 0.32$ at $x = -0.6$; with $M = 1.4$ it would be $\tfrac12 - 0.98 = -0.48$ at $x = -1.4$ — negative field energy, which no magnetic field has.

Equality $M^2 = L_1L_2$ would need a pair of currents with $\vec B = \vec 0$ everywhere — every field line of one loop linking the other perfectly. Real coils approach it but do not reach it; unit 07 takes the limit as the ideal transformer.

Ideal sources

two idealisations

An ideal voltage source fixes $v = V_s$ whatever $i$. It is unit 01's EMF with no internal resistance: the non-electrostatic push $\mathcal E = \int\vec f_s\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$ appears in full across the terminals.

An ideal current source fixes $i = I_s$ whatever $v$: the modelling dual, used by later units.

A real battery is an ideal source in series with a resistance $r_i$ — unit 01's internal resistance. With the passive marking, $v = \mathcal E + r_i\,i$: delivering ($i < 0$), its terminals show less than $\mathcal E$. Unit 04 analyses this.

ideal means "within limits"

Short an ideal voltage source with an ideal wire and the laws contradict each other ($v = V_s$, $v = 0$). The model holds only within the real source's limits.

Element laws as curves in the $(v, i)$ plane, with the passive convention. The battery's line crosses $i = 0$ at its EMF and tilts by its internal resistance; $r_i\to0$ turns it into the voltage source's vertical line.

Unit 01's battery. Inside a source a non-electrostatic force per unit charge $\vec f_s$ (chemical, in a battery) drives the charges, so $\vec J = \sigma(\vec E + \vec f_s)$, and the EMF is $\mathcal E = \int\vec f_s\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$ through the source. Integrate $\vec J/\sigma = \vec E + \vec f_s$ from the − terminal to the + terminal through the source, with the current $i$ entering at + (passive marking), so that the current inside runs from + to −, against the path: $-ir_i = \int_-^+\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l + \mathcal E = -(\varphi_+ - \varphi_-) + \mathcal E$, where $r_i = \int\mathrm dl/\sigma A$ is the internal resistance (unit 01's $r_i$). So $v = \mathcal E + r_ii$. With $r_i\to0$ (σ → ∞ inside) it is the ideal source.

Sign check. Delivering current to a load means current leaves by + : in passive terms $i < 0$, so $v = \mathcal E - r_i|i|$, less than the EMF. Charging the battery ($i > 0$) needs more than $\mathcal E$ across it. Power absorbed: $p = vi = \mathcal Ei + r_ii^2$ — the source's term $\mathcal Ei$ is negative when delivering, the internal loss $r_ii^2$ always positive. (Unit 01 wrote the same thing as an outflow $\mathcal EI - I^2r_i$ with $I = -i$.)

The current source. A device whose current is set by its internals regardless of what it drives is an idealisation of the same kind. It is defined now so that later units can use it; nothing in this unit needs one, and no device is claimed to be one.

The contradiction. Ideal elements can be joined into circuits whose laws have no solution (a voltage source shorted, two different voltage sources in parallel, a current source left open). These are not physics failing; they are a model asked a question outside its range. A real source has internal resistance that resolves it.

02 · the ring

A voltmeter reads $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$ along its own leads

what a voltmeter reads

A voltmeter is a large resistance $R_m$ with ideal leads; it displays $R_mi_m$, its own current times $R_m$. With its + lead at P and − lead at Q, it reads

$$V = \int_{\text{P}}^{\text{Q}}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$$

taken along the leads and through the meter.

proof

Along the ideal leads $\vec E = \vec 0$. Through the meter's resistor, in the direction of $i_m$ (from + to −), unit 00's local law $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ integrates to $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = i_mR_m$, whatever made $\vec E$. Add the pieces. $\square$

Under (H1) the path does not matter and $V = \varphi_{\text P} - \varphi_{\text Q}$. Without it, two meters on the same points differ by the EMF round the loop their two paths make.

The path that matters (shaded): from P along the + lead, through the meter, along the − lead to Q. The display is $i_mR_m$, which is $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$ along exactly that path.

The meter, modelled. A voltmeter of the kind meant here is a large known resistance with something that measures the current through it; the display is scaled to read $R_mi_m$. McDonald's note ("Lewin's Circuit Paradox", 2010) models it the same way and makes the point bluntly: voltmeters measure the current inside them. Take $R_m$ much larger than the circuit's resistances, so that the meter draws a negligible current and does not disturb what it measures.

Full proof

Parametrise the path from P to Q: + lead, then the meter's resistor from its + end to its − end, then the − lead. In the leads, $\sigma\to\infty$ and finite current force $\vec E = \vec 0$, so they contribute nothing. In the resistor, take it uniform of length $\ell$, cross-section $A$ and conductivity $\sigma$, carrying $i_m$ from + to −: $\vec E = \vec J/\sigma$ is along the path with $E = i_m/\sigma A$, so $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = i_m\ell/\sigma A = i_mR_m$. Nothing assumed that $\vec E$ is a gradient; $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ is a local law (unit 00).

Two meters. If meter 1's path $\pi_1$ and meter 2's path $\pi_2$ both run from P to Q, then $V_2 - V_1 = \int_{\pi_2}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l - \int_{\pi_1}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \oint_{\pi_2 - \pi_1}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$, the EMF round the closed loop "out along $\pi_2$, back along $\pi_1$", which by Faraday is minus the rate of change of the flux through it. Problem P5 asks for this as a proof.

The same statement in the literature. R. H. Romer, "What do 'voltmeters' measure? Faraday's law in a multiply connected region", Am. J. Phys. 50, 1089 (1982), is the standard reference on this situation (McDonald's note cites it); the derivation above is self-contained and does not depend on it.

02 · the ring

The ring, lumped properly

(H1) failed because the changing flux was inside no element. The repair is the discipline's own: put it inside elements. The solenoid and each conductor that goes round it are coupled loops, with mutual inductance $M$ (section 3), so each picks up an EMF $M\,\mathrm di_s/\mathrm dt = \mathcal E$.

the cut

Draw a line from the solenoid outward, to infinity. In every branch it crosses, insert an ideal source of EMF $\mathcal E$, pointing clockwise. Then a loop's sources add up to $\mathcal E$ times the number of times it winds round the solenoid — the same as Faraday's law.

Outside the sources, $\vec E$ is now a gradient on the plane with the cut removed — closed and exact there (MR 04). So the voltage law holds, round every loop, sources included.

The ring, lumped properly. The cut (dashed) crosses the right side of the ring and meter 2's lead; each gets a source $\mathcal E$. Meter 1's loop crosses nothing; the ring crosses once; meter 2 with the right side crosses twice, in opposite senses.

Why this is the mutual-inductance model. Each conductor that runs round the solenoid is, with the rest of its loop, one winding of a two-coil system whose other coil is the solenoid. By Neumann's formula the flux it links is $Mi_s$, and its EMF is $M\,\mathrm di_s/\mathrm dt$, located — in the lumped picture — inside an element boundary drawn round the coupling, as for any pair of coils whose fields reach each other. The ring's own self-inductance is neglected, as throughout.

Why the cut works. A closed loop in the plane encircles the solenoid $k$ times (its winding number). Crossing the cut downward on the right is going clockwise; a loop crosses it, net, $k$ times clockwise. So the sources round it add to $k\mathcal E$ — which is what Faraday's law says the circulation of $\vec E$ is. Every loop's EMF is reproduced, so every meter reading is.

The cut is a choice. Any line from the solenoid to infinity will do. Putting it to the left instead puts the sources in $R_1$'s branch and meter 1's lead; the node potentials change, the readings do not (problem P6). This is MR 04's observation that the vortex is a gradient of the polar angle once a ray is removed from the plane — the choice of ray is the choice of branch of the angle.

Each lead is a branch. The meters' leads must be drawn as branches of the circuit, not as "attached to nodes" with no geometry: the whole point is that their routing decides whether they cross the cut. A lumped model of this apparatus that omits the meters' leads cannot get both readings.

02 · the ring

What "the voltage between two points" means

the moral, stated

"The voltage between P and Q" means $\int_{\text P}^{\text Q}\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l$ along some path. It is a property of the two points alone exactly when every closed curve through them encloses no changing flux — which is (H1).

When (H1) fails, two things are still true and measurable: each resistor's $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = iR$ through it, and each meter's reading along its own leads. Kirchhoff's voltage law is rescued not by changing physics but by drawing the circuit so that (H1) holds: the changing flux goes inside elements, every lead that matters becomes a branch.

the lumped approximation, in one sentence

It is the decision to draw element boundaries so that changing flux and changing charge are inside them, on a circuit small enough against a wavelength that what is left outside can be neglected.

Every reading a meter on P and Q can give, against how many times the loop it makes with $R_1$ winds clockwise round the solenoid: $-0.1 + k$ volts. Meters 1 and 2 are $k = 0$ and $k = 1$.

The formula behind the figure. Any meter's path from P to Q, followed by the return Q→P up through $R_1$, is a closed loop; if it winds $k$ times clockwise round the solenoid, Faraday gives $V + IR_1 = k\mathcal E$, so $V = -0.1 + k$ volts. Meter 1's loop does not wind ($k = 0$); meter 2's path with $R_1$ winds once ($k = 1$). Problem P6 derives the third meter's case.

Is Kirchhoff wrong, then? The dispute over this demonstration has been loud; McDonald's note surveys it. The position of this course is the one Agarwal and Lang's discipline implies: Kirchhoff's voltage law is a theorem under (H1), and (H1) is something the modeller arranges by where the boundaries go. Lewin's ring, drawn without a boundary round the coupling, violates (H1) and the law fails; drawn with the coupling inside elements, the law holds and predicts both readings. Both statements are correct; they are about different diagrams.

Where this matters in practice. Any measurement whose leads form a loop near a changing magnetic field picks up the EMF of that loop. Problem P12 estimates the inductance of a 1 cm loop of leads, and the voltage it adds when a current switches fast — which is why careful measurements twist their leads together, making the loop's area, and so its flux, small.
