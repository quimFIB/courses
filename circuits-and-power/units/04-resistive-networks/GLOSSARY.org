The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: a car battery cranking a starter motor with the headlights
on. The battery is \(\mathcal E = 12.6\) V behind \(r_i = 10\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\)
(the bound a 540 A cold-cranking rating gives at −18 °C in a linear model);
the starter branch is cables \(R_c = 5\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\) and the stalled
starter \(R_s = 45\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\); the lamp branch is wiring \(R_w = 30\
\mathrm{m\Omega}\) and two 55 W headlamps, \(R_L = 1.309\ \Omega\). All values
are assumed. Lamps alone: 114.2 W. Cranking: the lamps see 10.5 V behind
38.33 mΩ and get 79.5 W. The starter gets 1984.5 W at 75% efficiency; a
matched 15 mΩ starter would get 2646 W at 50%.

* The running example

- starter motor :: The electric motor that turns a car's engine until it
  fires. At the instant it engages it is not yet turning, and then opposes
  current only by its windings' resistance, so it is modelled as a
  resistance; turning, it also develops a back-EMF (unit 12). /Slide: Why
  the headlights dim when the engine cranks./

* The Laplacian

- conductance :: The reciprocal of a resistance, \(G = 1/R\), in siemens
  (\(\mathrm S = \Omega^{-1}\)): the current an element passes per volt. Ohm's
  law on every edge is \(i = Gv\) with \(G = \operatorname{diag}(G_e)\).
  /Slide: Ohm's law on every edge./
- weighted graph Laplacian :: \(L = AGA^{\mathsf T}\) for a graph with incidence
  matrix \(A\) and edge conductances \(G_e > 0\). Diagonal entries: the sum of
  the conductances at a node; off-diagonal: \(-G_e\) for each edge joining two
  nodes; rows sum to zero. Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws together are
  \(L\varphi = s\). It is the matrix of nodal analysis. /Slide: The weighted
  graph Laplacian./
- grounding :: Fixing one node's potential (to zero) in each connected
  component. \(L\varphi = s\) is solvable iff the injections sum to zero on
  each component, and its solution is unique up to a constant on each
  component; grounding removes those constants. /Slide: When the potentials
  exist, and how unique they are./
- boundary node :: A node whose potential is given — a source's terminal, or
  ground. /Slide: The discrete Dirichlet problem./
- interior node :: A node at which no current is injected, so that Kirchhoff's
  current law holds there with zero right-hand side. /Slide: The discrete
  Dirichlet problem./
- discrete Dirichlet problem :: Given the potentials at the boundary nodes,
  find the interior potentials with \((L\varphi)_I = 0\). If every interior
  component is joined to the boundary, \(L_{II}\) is positive definite and
  the solution exists and is unique — MR 23's uniqueness theorem on a graph.
  /Slide: The discrete Dirichlet problem./
- harmonic :: Said of node potentials at an interior node: the potential there
  is the conductance-weighted average of its neighbours'. The graph version
  of the mean value property. /Slide: The maximum principle./
- maximum principle :: In a discrete Dirichlet problem whose interior
  components all touch the boundary, every potential lies between the least
  and greatest boundary potentials; an interior maximum forces its whole
  interior component to the maximum. /Slide: The maximum principle./

* Thomson's principle and effective resistance

- flow :: Any edge vector \(j\) obeying the current law with given injections,
  \(Aj = s\), whether or not it obeys Ohm's law. A unit flow from \(a\) to
  \(b\) has \(s = e_a - e_b\). /Slide: Thomson's principle./
- Thomson's principle :: Among all flows with the same injections, the
  dissipated power \(\sum_eR_ej_e^2\) is least for the actual currents, and
  only for them. Proved from Tellegen's theorem; Doyle and Snell §1.3.5.
  /Slide: Thomson's principle./
- effective resistance :: For a unit current in at \(a\) and out at \(b\):
  \(R_{\text{eff}}(a, b) = \varphi_a - \varphi_b = s^{\mathsf T}L^+s\), which is
  also the power that current dissipates, and by Thomson the least power of
  any unit flow from \(a\) to \(b\). /Slide: Effective resistance./
- pseudo-inverse :: For symmetric \(L = \sum_k\lambda_ku_ku_k^{\mathsf T}\),
  \(L^+ = \sum_{\lambda_k > 0}\lambda_k^{-1}u_ku_k^{\mathsf T}\). Then \(LL^+\) is
  the orthogonal projection onto \(\operatorname{im}L\), \(L^+LL^+ = L^+\), and
  \(L^+s\) solves \(L\varphi = s\) whenever \(s\perp\ker L\). /Slide: Effective
  resistance./
- Rayleigh's monotonicity law :: Raising any edge's resistance cannot lower any
  effective resistance. Proof: the new network's current is an admissible flow
  in the old one, at no lower power. /Slide: Rayleigh's monotonicity law./
- cutting :: Removing an edge (\(R_e\to\infty\)); by Rayleigh it can only
  raise effective resistances. /Slide: Rayleigh's monotonicity law./
- shorting :: Merging two nodes (\(R_e\to0\) on an edge between them); by
  Rayleigh it can only lower effective resistances, and changes nothing if the
  two nodes were at the same potential. /Slide: Rayleigh's monotonicity law./
- series :: Two edges meeting at a node of degree two with no injection act as
  one edge of resistance \(R_1 + R_2\). /Slide: Series and parallel, as
  theorems./
- parallel :: Two edges joining the same pair of nodes act as one edge of
  conductance \(G_1 + G_2\): \(R_1\parallel R_2 = R_1R_2/(R_1 + R_2)\).
  /Slide: Series and parallel, as theorems./

* Thévenin and Kron

- one-port :: A network with two terminals brought out, \(p\) and \(q\),
  marked by the passive convention: \(v = \varphi_p - \varphi_q\), and \(i\)
  enters at \(p\). /Slide: Thévenin's theorem./
- Thévenin's theorem :: A one-port of linear resistors and ideal sources,
  uniquely solvable when driven by a current at its port, obeys \(v =
  V_{\text{th}} + R_{\text{th}}i\): an EMF in series with a resistance. Helmholtz
  (1853) and Thévenin (1883), per Johnson, Proc. IEEE 91 (2003). /Slide:
  Thévenin's theorem./
- open-circuit voltage :: \(V_{\text{th}}\), the port voltage of a one-port with
  nothing attached (\(i = 0\)). /Slide: Thévenin's theorem./
- superposition :: The response of a uniquely solvable linear network is a
  linear function of all its sources jointly. /Slide: Proof: an affine map./
- Norton's form :: Thévenin's line solved for the current,
  \(i = v/R_{\text{th}} - I_{\text{N}}\): a current source \(I_{\text{N}} = V_{\text{th}}/R_{\text{th}}\) in
  parallel with \(R_{\text{th}}\). /Slide: Setting a source to zero, and
  Norton's form./
- short-circuit current :: \(I_{\text{N}}\), the current that flows out of
  terminal \(p\) when the port is joined by an ideal wire. /Slide: Setting a
  source to zero, and Norton's form./
- Kron reduction :: Eliminating the interior nodes of a network by block
  elimination: \(s_B = L_{\text{red}}\varphi_B\) with \(L_{\text{red}} = L_{BB} -
  L_{BI}L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}\). The result is again a Laplacian, of a network on
  the kept nodes that is indistinguishable from the original there. Named
  after Kron, Tensor Analysis of Networks (1939). /Slide: Kron reduction:
  eliminating interior nodes./
- Schur complement :: For a block matrix with invertible block \(L_{II}\), the
  matrix \(L_{BB} - L_{BI}L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}\) left after eliminating the \(I\)
  unknowns. /Slide: Kron reduction: eliminating interior nodes./
- star–delta transformation :: A node joined only to \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) by
  conductances \(G_a, G_b, G_c\) is equivalent at those three nodes to a
  triangle with \(G_{ab} = G_aG_b/(G_a + G_b + G_c)\) and cyclically: Kron
  reduction of one node of degree three. /Slide: Star–delta: one interior
  node of degree three./

* A metric, and Foster's theorem

- resistance distance :: Effective resistance as a metric on the nodes of a
  connected network; the triangle inequality follows from the maximum
  principle. Named by Klein and Randić (1993). /Slide: Effective resistance
  is a metric./
- Foster's theorem :: For a connected network with \(n\) nodes,
  \(\sum_eR_{\text{eff}}(e)/R_e = n - 1\), where \(R_{\text{eff}}(e)\) is the
  effective resistance between the ends of edge \(e\). Each term is the
  fraction of a unit current injected across \(e\) that goes through \(e\)
  itself. Proved as the trace of an orthogonal projection onto the
  (conductance-scaled) cut space. Foster (1949). /Slide: Foster's theorem./

* Maximum power, and its price

- maximum-power theorem :: A source \(V_{\text{th}}\) behind \(R_{\text{th}}\)
  delivers the most power to a load \(R = R_{\text{th}}\), namely
  \(V_{\text{th}}^2/4R_{\text{th}}\), and at that load the efficiency is exactly
  50%. /Slide: The maximum-power theorem./
- matched load :: The load equal to the source's (Thévenin) resistance, which
  receives the most power. /Slide: The maximum-power theorem./
- efficiency :: The fraction of the source EMF's output that reaches the load,
  \(\eta = R/(R_{\text{th}} + R)\); it rises to 1 as the load grows, at falling
  power. /Slide: The maximum-power theorem./
