Resistive networks: the graph Laplacian, Thomson's principle, Thévenin, and maximum power

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You prove the maximum-power theorem and its 50% efficiency, prove Foster's theorem, and compute the cube's corner-to-opposite-corner resistance, \(5R/6\), two ways: by symmetry, and by Kron reduction.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about four and a half hours. P1, P7 and P8 are the checkpoint's. Conventions as in the deck: a graph with \(n\) nodes, \(m\) edges and incidence matrix \(A\) (\(A_{ke} = +1\) if edge \(e\) leaves node \(k\), \(-1\) if it enters); conductances \(G = \operatorname{diag}(G_e)\), \(G_e = 1/R_e\); the Laplacian \(L = AGA^{\mathsf T}\); injected currents \(s\), so \(L\varphi = s\); \(R_{\text{eff}}(a, b) = (e_a - e_b)^{\mathsf T}L^+(e_a - e_b)\); a port marked by the passive convention, so a one-port obeys \(v = V_{\text{th}} + R_{\text{th}}i\).

Effective resistance, computed

P1 The cube, two ways and a third medium

exercises: symmetry and uniqueness, shorting equipotential nodes, Kron reduction, superposition — the checkpoint's computation

Twelve equal resistors \(R\) form the edges of a cube. Let \(a\) be one corner and \(b\) the opposite corner.

  1. Find \(R_{\text{eff}}(a, b)\) by symmetry: say which nodes are equipotential and why you may short them.
  2. Find it again by Kron reduction, keeping only \(a\) and \(b\), without using the symmetry of step 1.
  3. Now let \(b'\) be a corner adjacent to \(a\). Find \(R_{\text{eff}}(a, b')\) by superposition: first inject 1 A at \(a\) and extract \(\tfrac18\) A at every one of the eight corners; then do the mirror image at \(b'\); then add.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each answer is a resistance, a rational multiple of \(R\). For step 1 the tool is uniqueness: a symmetry that fixes the injections maps the solution to a solution, hence to itself.

Rung 2

Step 2: put \(\varphi_a = 1\), \(\varphi_b = 0\) and write the six interior equations; you only need the sums \(X\) of the potentials next to \(a\) and \(Y\) of those next to \(b\). Step 3: in the first experiment the net injection at \(a\) is \(1 - \tfrac18\), and the three edges at \(a\) are alike.

Rung 3

First line of step 3: by the symmetry fixing \(a\), each of \(a\)'s three edges carries \(\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac78 = \tfrac7{24}\) A away from \(a\).

Worked solution

Idea. Three different ways to avoid solving the \(8\times8\) system: a symmetry that collapses it to a chain, an elimination that needs only two sums, and a superposition of two symmetric experiments.

1. Symmetry. The rotation by \(120°\) about the diagonal \(ab\) permutes the three neighbours of \(a\) cyclically, and the three neighbours of \(b\), and fixes \(a\) and \(b\). It maps edges to edges, so if \(\varphi\) solves \(L\varphi = e_a - e_b\) then so does \(\varphi\) composed with the rotation; with \(b\) grounded the solution is unique, so they are equal. Hence the three nodes at distance 1 from \(a\) share a potential, and so do the three at distance 2. Shorting nodes at equal potential changes no current (Rayleigh with equality). The network becomes three bundles in series: 3 edges in parallel (\(R/3\)), the 6 middle edges in parallel (\(R/6\)), 3 in parallel (\(R/3\)): \[R_{\text{eff}}(a, b) = \tfrac R3 + \tfrac R6 + \tfrac R3 = \tfrac{5R}6 .\]

2. Kron reduction. Take \(R = 1\). Interior: \(x_1, x_2, x_3\) adjacent to \(a\), \(y_1, y_2, y_3\) adjacent to \(b\); each \(x\) has two \(y\) neighbours, each \(y\) two \(x\) neighbours. With \(\varphi_a = 1\), \(\varphi_b = 0\), the equations \((L\varphi)_I = 0\) are \(3x_k - (\text{its two } y) = 1\) and \(3y_k - (\text{its two } x) = 0\). Summing each set, every \(y\) is counted twice in the first and every \(x\) twice in the second: \[3X - 2Y = 3,\qquad 3Y - 2X = 0\quad\Rightarrow\quad X = \tfrac95,\ Y = \tfrac65 .\] The current injected at \(a\) is \(\sum_k(1 - x_k) = 3 - X = \tfrac65\), and at \(b\) it is \(\sum_k(0 - y_k) = -\tfrac65\). These are the first column of \(L_{\text{red}}\); with zero row sums and symmetry, \[L_{\text{red}} = \frac6{5R}\begin{pmatrix}1 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix},\] the Laplacian of one edge of conductance \(6/5R\): \(R_{\text{eff}} = 5R/6\).

3. Superposition. Experiment 1: \(+1\) A at \(a\), \(-\tfrac18\) A at each corner (total zero, so solvable). The symmetries fixing \(a\) permute its three edges, and the net \(\tfrac78\) A leaving \(a\) splits equally: \(\tfrac7{24}\) A along \(ab'\), from \(a\) to \(b'\). Experiment 2: \(+\tfrac18\) A at each corner, \(-1\) A at \(b'\): by the same argument at \(b'\), \(\tfrac7{24}\) A along \(ab'\) into \(b'\), again from \(a\) to \(b'\). Sum: injections \(+1\) at \(a\), \(-1\) at \(b'\), zero elsewhere — the unit current from \(a\) to \(b'\) — with \(\tfrac7{12}\) A in edge \(ab'\). So \[R_{\text{eff}}(a, b') = \varphi_a - \varphi_{b'} = R\cdot\tfrac7{12} = \tfrac{7R}{12}.\]

Check. Foster: all twelve edges are alike, \(12\cdot\tfrac7{12} = 7 = 8 - 1\) ✓. Thomson for step 1: the flow \(\tfrac13, \tfrac16, \tfrac13\) A has power \(3\cdot\tfrac19 + 6\cdot\tfrac1{36} + 3\cdot\tfrac19 = \tfrac56\) (times \(R\)) ✓. Doyle and Snell (§1.3.4) get \(7/12\) for the unit cube by symmetry and series–parallel ✓. Ordering: \(\tfrac7{12} \lt \tfrac34 \lt \tfrac56\) for adjacent, face-diagonal and opposite corners, increasing with distance as a metric should.

P2 The infinite square grid, adjacent nodes hard

exercises: superposition, Foster's theorem, Rayleigh's law, the difference between a heuristic and a proof

Every edge of the square lattice \(\mathbb Z^2\) is a resistor \(R\); \(a\) and \(b\) are adjacent nodes.

  1. The classic argument: inject 1 A at \(a\) "to infinity"; by symmetry \(\tfrac14\) A leaves along each edge at \(a\). Extract 1 A at \(b\) "from infinity"; \(\tfrac14\) A arrives along each edge at \(b\). Superpose: \(\tfrac12\) A in edge \(ab\), so \(R/2\). Show that the single-source experiment has infinite dissipated power, so that the argument is not yet about any network.
  2. Make it exact on the \(N\times N\) torus (the grid with opposite sides joined, \(N^2\) nodes, \(2N^2\) edges): show \(R_{\text{eff}}(a, b) = \tfrac R2\big(1 - \tfrac1{N^2}\big)\). Check it by Foster.
  3. For odd \(N\), let \(R_{\square}(N)\) be the resistance between the centre of an \(N\times N\) square of the grid and one of its neighbours. Show that \(R_{\square}(N)\ge R/2\) for every \(N\), and that it does not increase as the square grows by a ring at a time (\(N\to N + 2\)).
  4. Show that the same square with its whole outer ring of nodes shorted together has resistance \(\le R/2\). What would you need to conclude that \(R_{\square}(N)\to R/2\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an estimate of a divergent sum. Parts 2–4 are inequalities between effective resistances of networks that differ by cutting or shorting edges, plus one exact symmetric calculation.

Rung 2

On the torus the injections must sum to zero: extract the 1 A uniformly, \(1/N^2\) at every node. A square is a torus with its wrap-around edges cut. The shorted square is a smaller torus with one row and one column shorted.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: with \(+1\) at \(a\) and \(-1/N^2\) everywhere, the net \(1 - 1/N^2\) leaving \(a\) splits equally over its four edges, \(\tfrac14(1 - 1/N^2)\) each.

Worked solution

Idea. The superposition is right, but its two halves have no finite meaning in two dimensions. Put it on finite networks where it is exact (tori), and pinch the squares between tori and shorted tori with Rayleigh's law.

1. The divergence. Let \(\Pi_r\) be the set of edges leaving the square of side \(2r + 1\) centred at \(a\), each joining a node at lattice distance \(\max(|x|, |y|) = r\) from \(a\) to one at \(r + 1\); there are \(4(2r + 1) = 8r + 4\) of them. Any flow carrying 1 A from \(a\) out "to infinity" — the symmetric one or any other — sends a net 1 A across every \(\Pi_r\) (the current law summed over the square's nodes, where only \(a\) is fed). By Cauchy–Schwarz, \[\sum_{e\in\Pi_r}j_e^2 \ge \frac{\big(\sum_{e\in\Pi_r}|j_e|\big)^2}{8r + 4} \ge \frac1{8r + 4}.\] The \(\Pi_r\) are disjoint, so the power is at least \(R\sum_{r\ge0}1/(8r + 4) = \infty\), a harmonic series. No flow at all carries a unit current to infinity in the plane at finite power: the experiment describes no network's currents, and "the potential at infinity" is not defined. So the classic argument proves nothing yet. (Doyle and Snell, §2.2.3, reach the same sum by shorting the squares.)

2. The torus. Experiment 1: \(+1\) at \(a\), \(-1/N^2\) at every node (sum zero: solvable). The torus's symmetries fixing \(a\) (rotations by \(90°\) and reflections) permute \(a\)'s four edges, and the solution is unique up to a constant, so the currents in them are equal: \(\tfrac14(1 - 1/N^2)\) each, away from \(a\) — in particular along \(ab\). Experiment 2: \(+1/N^2\) everywhere, \(-1\) at \(b\): by symmetry at \(b\), \(\tfrac14(1 - 1/N^2)\) along \(ab\) into \(b\). Sum: the unit current from \(a\) to \(b\), with \(\tfrac12(1 - 1/N^2)\) A in \(ab\). So \(R_{\text{eff}} = \tfrac R2(1 - 1/N^2)\).

By Foster: the torus's translations and \(90°\) rotations carry any edge to any other, so all \(2N^2\) edges have the same \(R_{\text{eff}} = r\), and \(2N^2\cdot r/R = N^2 - 1\): \(r = \tfrac R2\big(1 - \tfrac1{N^2}\big)\) ✓.

3. Squares from above \(R/2\). For any \(M\ge N\), the \(N\times N\) square sits inside the \(M\times M\) square, which is the \(M\times M\) torus with its wrap-around edges cut. Cutting can only raise effective resistance (Rayleigh); so does deleting everything outside the \(N\times N\) square (also cutting). Hence \(R_{\square}(N)\ge\tfrac R2(1 - 1/M^2)\) for every \(M\ge N\), and letting \(M\to\infty\), \(R_{\square}(N)\ge R/2\). Growing the square by a ring keeps the central pair and only adds edges, which is the reverse of cutting: \(R_{\square}(N)\) does not increase.

4. Shorted squares from below. Take the \(N\times N\) square with its outer ring shorted into one node. Compare the \((N-1)\times(N-1)\) torus with row 0 and column 0 shorted into one node: its remaining nodes are the square's \((N-2)^2\) interior nodes, its edges into row 0 and column 0 are exactly the square's edges into the outer ring (from the first and the last interior row and column), and the edges inside row 0 and column 0 become loops that carry nothing. So the shorted square is a shorted torus, and shorting can only lower: \(R_{\text{short}}(N)\le\tfrac R2\big(1 - \tfrac1{(N-1)^2}\big)\lt\tfrac R2\). It does not decrease with \(N\) (the \(N\)-square shorted is the \((N+2)\)-square with its next ring shorted too).

So \(R_{\text{short}}(N)\le R/2\le R_{\square}(N)\), the first non-decreasing and the second non-increasing. Both have limits; that the two limits are equal is the missing step, and it is cited: R. Lyons and Y. Peres, Probability on Trees and Networks (Cambridge, 2016), §9.1, define the "free" and "wired" effective resistances of an infinite network as the limits over the finite pieces taken as they are and with everything outside them shorted to one node — exactly these two limits (the shorted \(N\)-square is the inner \((N - 2)\)-square with the rest of the lattice shorted) — and show that they coincide when free and wired currents agree (Proposition 9.3), which their Corollary 9.6, "Recurrence Yields Unique Currents" (§9.3), gives for every recurrent network. The square lattice is recurrent by Pólya's theorem (Doyle and Snell, §2.1.4; its electrical proof by shorting is §2.2.3). With that, both limits equal \(R/2\).

Check. \(N = 2\) torus: each node has neighbours \(\pm1\) in each direction, which coincide, so it is a 4-cycle of doubled edges (conductance \(2/R\) each); \(R_{\text{eff}}\) between adjacent nodes \(= \tfrac R2\parallel\tfrac{3R}2 = \tfrac{3R}8\), and the formula gives \(\tfrac R2\cdot\tfrac34 = \tfrac{3R}8\) ✓.

P3 Star–delta as a Kron reduction routine

exercises: the Schur complement on the smallest example, the inverse map, one bridge computation

  1. Kron-reduce a star — node 0 joined to \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) by \(G_a\), \(G_b\), \(G_c\) — and show \(G_{ab} = G_aG_b/(G_a + G_b + G_c)\).
  2. Invert: given a delta \(G_{ab}\), \(G_{bc}\), \(G_{ca}\), find the star that reduces to it. Give the answer in resistances too.
  3. Replace the star at node B of the bridge (\(R_1\), \(R_2\), \(R_g\) to A, C, D) by a delta, and find the current the 10 V source supplies. Unit 03 found \(29/605\) A.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a \(1\times1\) Schur complement. Part 2 is three equations in three unknowns that become easy with the right products.

Rung 2

Compute \(G_{ab}G_{ca}/G_{bc}\) and \(G_{ab}G_{bc} + G_{bc}G_{ca} + G_{ca}G_{ab}\) in terms of the star.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(G_{ab}G_{bc} + G_{bc}G_{ca} + G_{ca}G_{ab} = \dfrac{G_aG_bG_c(G_a + G_b + G_c)}{\Sigma^2} = \dfrac{G_aG_bG_c}\Sigma\).

Worked solution

Idea. The star–delta rule is not a separate fact to memorise; it is block elimination of one node.

1. \(B = \{a, b, c\}\), \(I = \{0\}\): \(L_{II} = \Sigma = G_a + G_b + G_c\), \(L_{BI} = -g\) with \(g = (G_a, G_b, G_c)^{\mathsf T}\), \(L_{BB} = \operatorname{diag}(g)\). So \(L_{\text{red}} = \operatorname{diag}(g) - gg^{\mathsf T}/\Sigma\); its \((a, b)\) entry is \(-G_aG_b/\Sigma\), so \(G_{ab} = G_aG_b/\Sigma\), and cyclically.

2. With \(P = G_{ab}G_{bc} + G_{bc}G_{ca} + G_{ca}G_{ab} = G_aG_bG_c/\Sigma\) (rung 3) and \(G_{bc} = G_bG_c/\Sigma\): \[G_a = \frac P{G_{bc}},\quad G_b = \frac P{G_{ca}},\quad G_c = \frac P{G_{ab}} .\] In resistances (\(R_a = 1/G_a\), and so on) this is \(R_a = \dfrac{R_{ab}R_{ca}}{R_{ab} + R_{bc} + R_{ca}}\): multiply numerator and denominator of \(G_{bc}/P\) by \(R_{ab}R_{bc}R_{ca}\).

3. The star at B: \(R_1 = 100\) to A, \(R_2 = 200\) to C, \(R_g = 50\) to D. In resistances the delta is \(R_{XY} = (R_XR_Y + R_YR_Z + R_ZR_X)/R_Z\) with \(R_Z\) the star arm at the third tip; the sum of products is \(100\cdot200 + 200\cdot50 + 50\cdot100 = 35\,000\ \Omega^2\). So \(R_{AC} = 35\,000/50 = 700\ \Omega\), \(R_{AD} = 35\,000/200 = 175\ \Omega\), \(R_{CD} = 35\,000/100 = 350\ \Omega\). Now everything is series–parallel: A–D is \(175\parallel300 = 2100/19\ \Omega\); D–C is \(350\parallel400 = 560/3\ \Omega\); in series \(16\,940/57\ \Omega\); in parallel with \(R_{AC} = 700\): \[R_{\text{eff}}(A, C) = \frac{700\cdot16\,940/57}{700 + 16\,940/57} = \frac{6050}{29}\ \Omega \approx 208.6\ \Omega,\] and the source supplies \(10/(6050/29) = 29/605\) A \(\approx 47.93\) mA ✓, unit 03's figure.

Check. Equal star \(G\): \(G_{ab} = G^2/3G = G/3\), and back: \(P = 3(G/3)^2 = G^2/3\), \(G_a = P/(G/3) = G\) ✓. Units: \(\mathrm S^2/\mathrm S = \mathrm S\) ✓. Power: \(10\times29/605 = 0.4793\) W, unit 03's \(58/121\) W ✓.

The Laplacian's theorems

P4 Thomson by Lagrange multipliers medium

exercises: Lagrange multipliers (MR 04) on a quadratic problem, convexity, reading a multiplier physically

Minimise \(P(j) = \sum_eR_ej_e^2\) over \(j\in\mathbb R^m\) subject to \(Aj = s\), for a connected network with \(\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}s = 0\).

  1. Show that at a stationary point the multipliers \(\lambda\in\mathbb R^n\) satisfy \(2R_ej_e = \lambda_{\text{tail}} - \lambda_{\text{head}}\), and read this as Ohm's law: what are the node potentials?
  2. Show that the stationary point is the global minimum and is unique. Why are the multipliers unique only up to a constant?
  3. For 1 A shared between \(1\ \Omega\) and \(2\ \Omega\) in parallel, find \(\lambda\) and the minimum.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is an identification \(\varphi = c\lambda\) for a constant \(c\). Part 2 uses that \(P\) is a strictly convex quadratic and the constraint set is an affine subspace.

Rung 2

The Lagrangian is \(\mathcal L = \sum_eR_ej_e^2 - \lambda^{\mathsf T}(Aj - s)\), and \(\lambda^{\mathsf T}Aj = (A^{\mathsf T}\lambda)^{\mathsf T}j\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial j_e = 2R_ej_e - (A^{\mathsf T}\lambda)_e = 2R_ej_e - (\lambda_{\text{tail}(e)} - \lambda_{\text{head}(e)}) = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. Minimising dissipation under the current law produces, as the price of the constraint, a potential — and the stationarity condition is Ohm's law.

1. \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial j_e = 2R_ej_e - (A^{\mathsf T}\lambda)_e = 0\), and \((A^{\mathsf T}\lambda)_e = \lambda_{\text{tail}} - \lambda_{\text{head}}\). With \(\varphi = \lambda/2\): \(j_e = (\varphi_{\text{tail}} - \varphi_{\text{head}})/R_e = v_e/R_e\). The multipliers are twice the node potentials, and stationarity is Ohm's law on every edge; the constraint is the current law. So the stationary point is the actual current distribution.

Why multipliers exist here. MR 04's rule asks for independent constraints, and the \(n\) rows of \(Aj = s\) are not independent (they sum to zero). Two ways round it: delete one row (ground one node), leaving \(n - 1\) independent constraints, since \(\operatorname{rank}A = n - 1\); or argue directly — at a constrained minimum the gradient of \(P\), with entries \(2R_ej_e\), is orthogonal to every admissible direction \(c\in\ker A\), so it lies in \((\ker A)^\perp = \operatorname{im}A^{\mathsf T}\), i.e. it is \(A^{\mathsf T}\lambda\) for some \(\lambda\). That is the multiplier equation, with no independence needed.

2. For any admissible \(j = i + c\) (\(Ac = 0\)): \(P(j) = P(i) + 2\sum R_ei_ec_e + \sum R_ec_e^2\), and \(2\sum R_ei_ec_e = (A^{\mathsf T}\lambda)^{\mathsf T}c = \lambda^{\mathsf T}Ac = 0\). So \(P(j) = P(i) + \sum R_ec_e^2 \gt P(i)\) unless \(c = 0\): global, unique. (This is the deck's Tellegen proof in Lagrange's language.) The multipliers: if \(\lambda\) works, so does \(\lambda + k\mathbf 1\), because \(A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1 = 0\) — the constraints \(Aj = s\) are dependent (their rows sum to \(\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}Aj = 0\)), and a dependent constraint's multiplier is not determined. Physically: potentials are defined up to a constant.

3. \(2\cdot1\cdot t = \lambda_a - \lambda_b = 2\cdot2\cdot(1 - t)\), so \(t = 2/3\), \(\lambda_a - \lambda_b = 4/3\), i.e. \(\varphi_a - \varphi_b = 2/3\) V; minimum \(P = 2/3\) W \(= R_{\text{eff}}\cdot1^2\) with \(R_{\text{eff}} = 1\parallel2 = 2/3\ \Omega\).

Check. Units: \(\lambda\) has units of \(\Omega\cdot\mathrm A = \mathrm V\) ✓. MR 11's catenary had its multiplier as the tension; here it is (twice) the potential — in both, the multiplier is the force that enforces the constraint.

P5 Proof: the interior block is invertible medium

exercises: positive definiteness from the energy form, and exactly when it fails

  1. Prove: \(L_{II}\) is positive definite if and only if every connected component of the subgraph on the interior nodes is joined by at least one edge to a boundary node.
  2. Give a small example where it fails, with at least one resistor on the isolated part, write \(L_{II}\), and describe its kernel physically.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The "if" is the deck's proof. The "only if" needs a vector in \(\ker L_{II}\) when some interior component is isolated from the boundary.

Rung 2

\(x^{\mathsf T}L_{II}x = \hat x^{\mathsf T}L\hat x\) with \(\hat x\) the extension of \(x\) by zeros on \(B\). For "only if", try the indicator of the isolated component.

Rung 3

First line of "only if": if the interior component \(C\) has no edge to \(B\), then \(\hat{\mathbf 1}_C\) is constant across every edge except those leaving \(C\) — and there are none.

Worked solution

Idea. \(x^{\mathsf T}L_{II}x\) is the power dissipated with the boundary grounded; it vanishes only for potentials that make no current flow, and a floating island can sit at any potential without making current flow.

If. \(x^{\mathsf T}L_{II}x = \hat x^{\mathsf T}L\hat x = \sum_eG_e(\Delta\hat x)^2\ge0\), and \(= 0\) forces \(\hat x\) constant on each component of the whole graph. Each interior node lies in an interior component joined to some boundary node, where \(\hat x = 0\); so \(x = 0\). Positive definite.

Only if. Let \(C\) be an interior component with no edge to \(B\). Edges at nodes of \(C\) go only to other nodes of \(C\) (it is a component of the interior subgraph, and has no boundary neighbours). So \(\hat{\mathbf 1}_C\) — 1 on \(C\), 0 elsewhere — has \(\Delta = 0\) on every edge, and \(\mathbf 1_C^{\mathsf T}L_{II}\mathbf 1_C = 0\) with \(\mathbf 1_C \ne 0\): not positive definite; and since \(L_{II}\) is positive semi-definite, \(L_{II}\mathbf 1_C = 0\), so it is singular.

Example. Boundary nodes 1 (at 1 V) and 0 (at 0 V) joined by a resistor \(G\); two interior nodes \(x\), \(y\) joined to each other by \(G\) and to nothing else. \(L_{II} = \begin{pmatrix}G & -G\\ -G & G\end{pmatrix}\), with kernel spanned by \((1, 1)\). Physically: a pair of resistors floating, unconnected to anything driven. Any common potential \(t\) solves the equations — no current flows in either case — so "the" potential is undefined. Real floating conductors acquire a potential from charge and capacitance, which a resistive model does not contain (unit 05).

Check. Ground one of \(x, y\) (make it boundary) and \(L_{II}\) becomes \((G)\), positive ✓. The deck's car network: each of T, S, L reaches E or 0 directly or through T ✓.

P6 Proof: effective resistance is a metric medium

exercises: the maximum principle, the pseudo-inverse, the equality case of an inequality

  1. Prove \(R(a, c)\le R(a, b) + R(b, c)\) on a connected network, where \(R = R_{\text{eff}}\).
  2. Prove equality holds (for distinct \(a, b, c\)) if and only if every path from \(a\) to \(c\) passes through \(b\).
  3. Check both on a path of three resistors \(R_1\), \(R_2\) (from \(a\) to \(b\) to \(c\)), and then with an extra resistor \(R_3\) from \(a\) to \(c\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything reduces to the sign of one number, \(\psi_a - \psi_b\), for the potential \(\psi\) of a unit current from \(c\) to \(b\).

Rung 2

Expand \((x - y)^{\mathsf T}L^+(x - y)\) with \(x = e_a - e_b\), \(y = e_c - e_b\). For equality use the strong maximum principle: an interior node at the minimum forces its neighbours to the minimum.

Rung 3

First line: \(R(a, c) = R(a, b) + R(c, b) - 2x^{\mathsf T}L^+y\), and \(x^{\mathsf T}L^+y = \psi_a - \psi_b\) with \(\psi = L^+y\).

Worked solution

Idea. The cross term is a voltage produced by a current leaving at \(b\); \(b\) is therefore the lowest point, and the voltage cannot be negative. It is zero exactly when \(a\) is cut off from that current.

1. \(e_a - e_c = x - y\); \(L^+\) is symmetric, so \(R(a, c) = x^{\mathsf T}L^+x + y^{\mathsf T}L^+y - 2x^{\mathsf T}L^+y = R(a, b) + R(c, b) - 2(\psi_a - \psi_b)\), where \(L\psi = y\): \(+1\) at \(c\), \(-1\) at \(b\). Boundary \(\{b, c\}\), all else interior and connected to it; by the maximum principle \(\psi\) lies in \([\psi_b, \psi_c]\) (and \(\psi_c - \psi_b = R(c, b) \gt 0\)). So \(\psi_a \ge \psi_b\) and the triangle inequality holds.

2. Equality iff \(\psi_a = \psi_b\). If every \(a\)–\(c\) path passes through \(b\): remove \(b\); the component \(K\) containing \(a\) does not contain \(c\). On \(K\cup\{b\}\), \(\psi\) is harmonic at every node of \(K\) (no injection there) with the single boundary node \(b\); by the maximum principle it is constant, \(= \psi_b\). Only if: suppose \(\psi_a = \psi_b\), the minimum. Let \(Z\) be the set of nodes reachable from \(a\) without passing through \(b\). At any node \(k\in Z\) other than \(c\) with \(\psi_k = \psi_b\), harmonicity (\(\psi_k\) = weighted average of neighbours, all \(\ge\psi_b\)) forces every neighbour to \(\psi_b\). Starting from \(a\) this spreads to all of \(Z\). If \(c\) were in \(Z\) we would get \(\psi_c = \psi_b\), contradicting \(\psi_c - \psi_b \gt 0\). So \(c\notin Z\): every path from \(a\) to \(c\) passes through \(b\).

3. Path: \(R(a, c) = R_1 + R_2 = R(a, b) + R(b, c)\), equality, and \(b\) is a cut vertex ✓. With \(R_3\) from \(a\) to \(c\): take all three equal to 1 Ω; a triangle: every pair is at \(1\parallel2 = 2/3\ \Omega\), and \(2/3 \lt 4/3\): strict, and now the edge \(ac\) avoids \(b\) ✓.

Check. Dimensions: all three terms are resistances ✓. The cube of P1: \(R(a, b) = 5/6 \le 7/12 + 3/4 = 4/3\) for \(a\) to an adjacent corner then to the opposite corner along a face diagonal (times \(R\)) ✓.

P7 Proof: maximum power, and its 50% routine

exercises: the maximum-power theorem, efficiency, the power–efficiency trade-off — the checkpoint's

  1. A source \(V_{\text{th}}\) behind \(R_{\text{th}} \gt 0\) feeds a load \(R\). Prove that the load power is largest at \(R = R_{\text{th}}\), find the maximum, and prove the efficiency there is exactly \(\tfrac12\).
  2. With \(x = R/R_{\text{th}}\), write \(P/P_{\max}\) and \(\eta\) as functions of \(x\); show \(\eta\) increases monotonically and that \(P/P_{\max}\) takes each value in \((0, 1)\) twice. Which of the two loads does a designer who pays for losses choose?
  3. Unit 01's battery: \(\mathcal E = 12\) V, \(r_i = 0.1\ \Omega\), driving \(6\ \Omega\). What load takes the most power, how much, at what current, and how does that compare with the \(6\ \Omega\) load?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answers are a load resistance, a power, a dimensionless efficiency. One line of algebra bounds \(P(R)\) for every \(R\).

Rung 2

Compare \((R_{\text{th}} + R)^2\) with \(4R_{\text{th}}R\). The efficiency is the load's power over the EMF's, \(I^2R/V_{\text{th}}I\).

Rung 3

First line: \(P(R) = \dfrac{V_{\text{th}}^2R}{(R_{\text{th}} + R)^2}\) and \((R_{\text{th}} + R)^2 - 4R_{\text{th}}R = (R_{\text{th}} - R)^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. The load's power is a product of a current that falls with \(R\) and a voltage that rises with it; the balance point is the source's own resistance, where the source's resistance takes as much as the load.

1. \(I = V_{\text{th}}/(R_{\text{th}} + R)\), \(P = I^2R = V_{\text{th}}^2R/(R_{\text{th}} + R)^2\). Since \((R_{\text{th}} + R)^2 \ge 4R_{\text{th}}R\), with equality iff \(R = R_{\text{th}}\), \(P \le V_{\text{th}}^2/4R_{\text{th}} = P_{\max}\), attained only at the match. Efficiency \(\eta = I^2R/(V_{\text{th}}I) = IR/V_{\text{th}} = R/(R_{\text{th}} + R)\), which is \(\tfrac12\) at \(R = R_{\text{th}}\).

2. \(P/P_{\max} = 4x/(1 + x)^2\), \(\eta = x/(1 + x) = 1 - 1/(1 + x)\), increasing from 0 to 1. Under \(x\mapsto1/x\), \(4x/(1 + x)^2\) is unchanged, so each value in \((0, 1)\) is taken once at \(x \lt 1\) and once at \(1/x \gt 1\). Of the pair, \(x \gt 1\) has the higher efficiency: a designer who pays for losses takes the large load. At \(x = 3\): \(P = \tfrac34P_{\max}\), \(\eta = \tfrac34\); at \(x = \tfrac13\): the same power at \(\eta = \tfrac14\).

3. Most power at \(R = r_i = 0.1\ \Omega\): \(I = 12/0.2 = 60\) A, \(P_{\max} = 144/0.4 = 360\) W, at 50% — another 360 W heats the battery. The \(6\ \Omega\) load (unit 01's P7): \(I = 12/6.1 = 1.967\) A, 23.22 W at \(\eta = 6/6.1 = 98.4\%\). It gets 6.4% of the maximum power, and wastes 0.39 W instead of 360 W.

Check. Units \(\mathrm V^2/\Omega = \mathrm W\) ✓. Limits: \(R\to0\), \(P\to0\), \(\eta\to0\); \(R\to\infty\), \(P\to0\), \(\eta\to1\) ✓. Energy: \(V_{\text{th}}I = I^2R_{\text{th}} + I^2R\) by KVL ✓; at the match \(12\times60 = 720 = 360 + 360\) W ✓.

P8 Proof: Foster's theorem hard

exercises: pseudo-inverses, orthogonal projections, trace equals rank — the checkpoint's; series–parallel by hand

  1. For a connected network with \(n\) nodes, prove \(\sum_eR_{\text{eff}}(e)/R_e = n - 1\), where \(R_{\text{eff}}(e)\) is the effective resistance between the ends of edge \(e\). State every property of \(L^+\) you use, and prove it from the spectral form.
  2. What is the sum for a network with \(c\) components?
  3. Check the theorem on unit 03's bridge with the source removed — five resistors on four nodes — computing each \(R_{\text{eff}}(e)\) by hand.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The sum is the trace of an \(m\times m\) matrix whose diagonal entries are the terms. Show that matrix is an orthogonal projection and find its rank.

Rung 2

Use \(P = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}L^+AG^{1/2}\). You need \(L^+LL^+ = L^+\) and \(L^+L = \) the projection onto \((\ker L)^\perp\). For part 3, removing any one edge from the bridge leaves a series–parallel network.

Rung 3

First line: \(P_{ee} = G_e\,a_e^{\mathsf T}L^+a_e = G_eR_{\text{eff}}(e)\), since \(a_e = e_{\text{tail}} - e_{\text{head}}\) is a unit injection across \(e\).

Worked solution

Idea. Each term is a diagonal entry of the projection onto the (rescaled) cut space; the trace of a projection is the dimension it projects onto, and the cut space has dimension \(n - 1\).

The pseudo-inverse. \(L = \sum_k\lambda_ku_ku_k^{\mathsf T}\), orthonormal \(u_k\), \(\lambda_k\ge0\); \(L^+ = \sum_{\lambda_k\gt0}\lambda_k^{-1}u_ku_k^{\mathsf T}\). Then (i) \(L^+\) is symmetric; (ii) \(L^+L = \sum_{\lambda_k\gt0}u_ku_k^{\mathsf T} = \Pi\), the orthogonal projection onto \(\operatorname{span}\{u_k: \lambda_k\gt0\} = (\ker L)^\perp\); (iii) \(L^+LL^+ = \sum\lambda_k^{-1}\lambda_k\lambda_k^{-1}u_ku_k^{\mathsf T} = L^+\). And \(R_{\text{eff}}(a, b) = (e_a - e_b)^{\mathsf T}L^+(e_a - e_b)\) (the deck's Effective resistance).

1. Let \(P = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}L^+AG^{1/2}\). Diagonal: \(P_{ee} = G_ea_e^{\mathsf T}L^+a_e = R_{\text{eff}}(e)/R_e\), so \(\operatorname{tr}P\) is Foster's sum. Symmetric by (i). Idempotent: \(P^2 = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}L^+(AGA^{\mathsf T})L^+AG^{1/2} = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}(L^+LL^+)AG^{1/2} = P\) by (iii). So \(P\) is an orthogonal projection, with eigenvalues 0 and 1, and \(\operatorname{tr}P = \operatorname{rank}P\). Its image: clearly \(\subseteq W = \operatorname{im}(G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T})\); and for \(w = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}\varphi\), \(Pw = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}(L^+L\varphi) = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}(\varphi - k)\) with \(k\in\ker L\) by (ii); \(\ker L = \ker A^{\mathsf T}\) (deck), so \(Pw = w\). Hence \(\operatorname{im}P = W\), and \(\operatorname{rank}P = \dim W = \operatorname{rank}A^{\mathsf T} = n - 1\) (unit 03: \(\operatorname{rank}A = n - c\), \(c = 1\)). \(\square\)

2. Every step holds with \(\operatorname{rank}A = n - c\): the sum is \(n - c\). (Apply the connected case to each component and add.)

3. For each edge, \(R_{\text{eff}}(e) = R_e\parallel(\text{rest})\), and the rest is series–parallel:

  • \(e = R_g\) (B–D): the rest is B–A–D (400) in parallel with B–C–D (600), 240 Ω; \(50\parallel240 = 1200/29\ \Omega\); ratio \(24/29\).
  • \(e = R_1\) (A–B): rest A–D (300), then D to B: \(50\parallel(400 + 200) = 600/13\); total \(4500/13\). \(100\parallel4500/13 = 2250/29\); ratio \(45/58\).
  • \(e = R_2\) (B–C): rest B to D: \(50\parallel(100 + 300) = 400/9\), then D–C 400; total \(4000/9\). \(200\parallel4000/9 = 4000/29\); ratio \(20/29\).
  • \(e = R_3\) (A–D): rest A–B 100, then B to D: \(50\parallel600 = 600/13\); total \(1900/13\). \(300\parallel1900/13 = 2850/29\); ratio \(19/58\).
  • \(e = R_4\) (D–C): rest D to B: \(50\parallel400 = 400/9\), then B–C 200; total \(2200/9\). \(400\parallel2200/9 = 4400/29\); ratio \(11/29\).

Sum: \(\tfrac{48 + 45 + 40 + 19 + 22}{58} = \tfrac{174}{58} = 3 = 4 - 1\) ✓.

Check. Every ratio is in \((0, 1]\) ✓. The detector edge has the largest ratio (\(24/29\)): it is the lowest resistance, so most of a current injected across it takes it. Why the source is left out: Foster is about a network of resistors; an ideal source is not one (set to zero it would be a short, merging A and C, and then \(n = 3\) and the sum is 2 — a different network).

P9 Rayleigh bounds on a 4 × 4 grid medium

exercises: shorting for lower bounds, Thomson (any flow) for upper bounds, bracketing a resistance you do not compute

Sixteen nodes \((x, y)\), \(0\le x, y\le3\), joined by 24 unit resistors (\(R = 1\ \Omega\)) between horizontal and vertical neighbours. Bound \(R_{\text{eff}}\) between the corners \((0, 0)\) and \((3, 3)\).

  1. Lower bound: short together all nodes with the same \(x + y\).
  2. Upper bound: the flow of \(\tfrac12\) A along each of the two boundary paths.
  3. A better upper bound: spread the unit flow equally over all monotone (right-or-up) paths from corner to corner.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Shorting lowers \(R_{\text{eff}}\); any unit flow's power is at least \(R_{\text{eff}}\). Each bound is a short sum of fractions.

Rung 2

After shorting, layer \(k\) is joined to layer \(k + 1\) by \(c_k\) parallel edges; count them. There are \(\binom63 = 20\) monotone paths; an edge's flow is the fraction of paths using it.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(c_0, \dots, c_5 = 2, 4, 6, 6, 4, 2\), so the shorted network is \(\sum_k1/c_k\) in series.

Worked solution

Idea. Shorting gives a network that is easier and no more resistive; any flow you can write down gives a power no smaller than the true one. Between them the true value is pinned.

1. Layers \(x + y = 0, \dots, 6\) hold \(1, 2, 3, 4, 3, 2, 1\) nodes. Every edge joins layer \(k\) to \(k + 1\); the counts are \(2, 4, 6, 6, 4, 2\) (each node \((x, y)\) has an edge to \((x + 1, y)\) if \(x \lt 3\) and to \((x, y + 1)\) if \(y \lt 3\)). Shorted: \[R_{\text{eff}} \ge \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac16 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac{11}6 \approx 1.833\ \Omega .\]

2. Each boundary path has 6 edges carrying \(\tfrac12\) A: power \(2\times6\times\tfrac14 = 3\) W, so \(R_{\text{eff}}\le3\ \Omega\). (It is the same as cutting every interior edge: two 6 Ω paths in parallel.)

3. Each of the 20 paths carries \(\tfrac1{20}\) A. An edge from \((x, y)\) to its right neighbour is used by \(\binom{x + y}{x}\binom{5 - x - y}{3 - x - 1}\) paths (choose a route to \((x, y)\), then from \((x + 1, y)\) on). The edge flows come out as \(\tfrac12\) on the two edges at each corner; \(\tfrac15, \tfrac3{10}\) on the next ones; and so on. Summing \(j_e^2\) over all 24 edges gives \[R_{\text{eff}} \le \tfrac{99}{50} = 1.98\ \Omega .\]

The exact value, from solving the 16-node system, is \(13/7\ \Omega \approx 1.857\ \Omega\): \(1.833\le1.857\le1.98\) ✓. The lower bound is within 1.3%.

Check. Symmetry: the uniform-path flow is symmetric under swapping \(x\) and \(y\) and under the half-turn, as the true current is ✓. The edge flows conserve current at every node (each path enters and leaves each interior node it visits) ✓. Units: \(\Omega\cdot\mathrm A^2\) per \(\mathrm A^2\) ✓.

The full tally for part 3

By the symmetries, list one representative of each class with its count of edges: \(\tfrac12\) (4 edges: two at each corner), \(\tfrac3{10}\) (8), \(\tfrac15\) (4), \(\tfrac3{20}\) (4), \(\tfrac1{20}\) (4). Check: \(4 + 8 + 4 + 4 + 4 = 24\) ✓. Power: \(4\cdot\tfrac14 + 8\cdot\tfrac9{100} + 4\cdot\tfrac1{25} + 4\cdot\tfrac9{400} + 4\cdot\tfrac1{400} = 1 + 0.72 + 0.16 + 0.09 + 0.01 = 1.98\) ✓.

P10 Dirichlet's principle medium

exercises: the dual variational principle, two-sided bounds on an effective resistance

  1. Fix the potentials on a set \(B\) of boundary nodes. Prove that among all \(\varphi\) with those boundary values, \(E(\varphi) = \varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = \sum_eG_e(\Delta\varphi)^2\) is least for the actual potentials (harmonic at every interior node), and only for them.
  2. With \(B = \{a, b\}\), \(\varphi_a = 1\), \(\varphi_b = 0\), show the minimum is \(1/R_{\text{eff}}(a, b)\). Combine with Thomson: for any trial \(\varphi\) with these values and any unit flow \(j\), \[\frac1{E(\varphi)}\le R_{\text{eff}}(a, b)\le\sum_eR_ej_e^2 .\]
  3. For the cube's opposite corners, use the trial potential falling by \(\tfrac13\) per layer and the flow of \(\tfrac13\) A along three edge-disjoint paths. What bracket results?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The structure mirrors Thomson's proof: write the competitor as the actual solution plus a perturbation, and show the cross term vanishes.

Rung 2

The perturbation \(w\) vanishes on \(B\), and \(L\varphi^*\) vanishes off \(B\). The power at 1 V across \(R_{\text{eff}}\) is \(1/R_{\text{eff}}\).

Rung 3

First line: \(E(\varphi^* + w) = E(\varphi^*) + 2w^{\mathsf T}L\varphi^* + w^{\mathsf T}Lw\), and \(w^{\mathsf T}L\varphi^* = \sum_kw_k(L\varphi^*)_k = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. Thomson minimises over currents that obey the current law; Dirichlet minimises over potentials that obey the voltage law (automatically, being potentials). Each gives bounds on one side.

1. Let \(\varphi^*\) be the Dirichlet solution and \(\varphi = \varphi^* + w\), \(w_B = 0\). Then \(w^{\mathsf T}L\varphi^* = \sum_kw_k(L\varphi^*)_k\): for \(k\in B\), \(w_k = 0\); for \(k\in I\), \((L\varphi^*)_k = 0\). So \(E(\varphi) = E(\varphi^*) + w^{\mathsf T}Lw \ge E(\varphi^*)\), with equality iff \(w^{\mathsf T}Lw = 0\), i.e. \(w\) constant on components — and \(w = 0\) on \(B\) forces \(w = 0\) when every component touches \(B\). The continuous version is MR 11's Dirichlet principle for \(\int|\nabla u|^2\).

2. With 1 V across \(a, b\) the current is \(1/R_{\text{eff}}\) and the power is \(1^2/R_{\text{eff}}\), which is \(E(\varphi^*)\). So \(E(\varphi)\ge1/R_{\text{eff}}\), i.e. \(R_{\text{eff}}\ge1/E(\varphi)\); Thomson gives the upper bound.

3. Trial potential (with \(R = 1\)): \(a\) at 1, layer 1 at \(\tfrac23\), layer 2 at \(\tfrac13\), \(b\) at 0. Each of the 12 edges has \(\Delta = \tfrac13\): \(E = 12/9 = \tfrac43\), so \(R_{\text{eff}}\ge\tfrac34\). Trial flow: three edge-disjoint paths \(a\to x_k\to y\to b\) (the middle edges chosen as alternate edges of the hexagon), \(\tfrac13\) A each, 9 edges: power \(9\cdot\tfrac19 = 1\), so \(R_{\text{eff}}\le1\). Bracket: \(0.75R\le R_{\text{eff}}\le R\), and the true \(\tfrac56R\) is inside ✓.

Check. With the true potentials (\(1, \tfrac35, \tfrac25, 0\) by layer): \(E = 3\cdot\tfrac4{25} + 6\cdot\tfrac1{25} + 3\cdot\tfrac4{25} = \tfrac65\), and \(1/E = \tfrac56\): the lower bound is attained ✓. With the true flow, \(\tfrac56\): the upper bound is attained ✓.

P12 The power station routine

exercises: efficiency against power, and what each costs

A source of resistance \(R_{\text{th}}\) (generator and lines, by Thévenin) serves a load \(R\) with \(R_{\text{th}}/R = 0.01\) — an assumption for the comparison.

  1. Find the efficiency, and the delivered power in units of \(V_{\text{th}}^2/R_{\text{th}}\); compare both with the matched point.
  2. If fuel burnt is proportional to the electrical energy generated, how much fuel per delivered kWh does each design burn, in units of the fuel for 1 kWh generated? What fraction of the matched source's possible output does the efficient one deliver?
  3. Why does the efficient design not "waste" the unused 96% of possible output?

Rung 1

Everything is a function of \(x = R/R_{\text{th}} = 100\): two dimensionless numbers and their ratios.

Rung 2

\(\eta = x/(1 + x)\), \(P/(V_{\text{th}}^2/R_{\text{th}}) = x/(1 + x)^2\). Energy generated per unit delivered is \(1/\eta\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\eta = 100/101 = 0.990\).
