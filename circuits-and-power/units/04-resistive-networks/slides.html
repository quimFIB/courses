Resistive networks: the graph Laplacian, Thomson's principle, Thévenin, and maximum power

Put Ohm's law on every edge of unit 03's graph and the current law becomes $L\varphi = s$ with a symmetric, positive semi-definite $L$ — discrete electrostatics, with a uniqueness theorem, a maximum principle and a minimum-energy principle, from which Thévenin's theorem and the 50% price of maximum power follow.

Unit 03 used no element law at all: Kirchhoff's two laws as a pair of orthogonal subspaces, Tellegen's theorem as their orthogonality. This unit adds the simplest element law, Ohm's law, on every edge, and finds that a network of resistors is a discrete copy of MR 23's electrostatics. The engineer's tools come out as theorems: series and parallel, Thévenin's equivalent, star–delta, and the maximum-power theorem with its exact 50% efficiency.

the running example: the car

The puzzle. Lamps on, engine off: 114.2 W. Turn the key, and they visibly dim. By how much — and is the starter getting as much power as the battery could give it?

The system, from nothing. A car's 12 V battery is a lead–acid battery: six electrochemical cells in series, each a pair of lead-based plates in sulphuric acid. Its EMF, the open-circuit voltage of a fully charged battery, is taken here as $\mathcal E = 12.6$ V, 2.1 V per cell — a representative value, assumed. Like unit 01's battery it has an internal resistance $r_i$ (the plates, the electrolyte, the connections between cells). The negative terminal is bolted to the car's steel body, which serves as the return conductor for every circuit: "ground" here is literally the chassis.

The starter motor is an electric motor that turns the engine over until it fires. At the instant it engages it is not yet turning, and a motor that is not turning opposes current only by the resistance of its windings, so it is modelled as a resistance $R_s = 45\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$ (assumed). Once it turns it also generates a voltage opposing the current — its back-EMF — which is unit 12's subject; the model here holds only at the instant of engagement. The cables and connections, $R_c = 5\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$, are assumed; for scale, 1 m of $25\ \mathrm{mm^2}$ copper is $1.68\times10^{-8}/(25\times10^{-6}) = 0.672\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$ (unit 00's $\rho$), so unless the cables are several metres long, most of an $R_c$ this size would have to be in the clamps and joints.

The internal resistance from a rating. SAE J537 (revised 2023, §3.9.1) defines the cold-cranking test: a fully charged battery, stabilised at −18 °C, is discharged at its rated current for 30 s, and passes if its terminal voltage at 30 s is at least 7.2 V — 1.2 V per cell. (Other standards define their own cold-cranking tests; this deck uses only J537's.) In a linear model $v = \mathcal E - r_iI$, a battery rated 540 A therefore has $r_i \le (12.6 - 7.2)/540 = 10\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$ at −18 °C. The deck uses $r_i = 10\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$: a crude, cold-weather figure for a battery whose chemistry is not linear. The rating of 540 A is itself an assumption for the example, not any product's specification.

The headlamps. UN ECE Regulation No. 37, which governs approval of vehicle filament lamps, rates the H7 halogen headlamp at 12 V and 55 W (Annex 1, sheet H7/3, in the consolidated Revision 4 of 2005; its tests are made at 13.2 V). Two such lamps in parallel, treated as a fixed resistance at their rated point, are $R_L = 12^2/110 = 1.309\ \Omega$. Their wiring, fuse and switch, $R_w = 30\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$, are assumed. A filament is not ohmic; the deck says what that costs when it matters (Why the headlights dim).

section 1

The Laplacian

Ohm's law on every edge turns the current law into one symmetric linear system. Its matrix is positive semi-definite, its solutions exist and are unique, and they obey a maximum principle — MR 23's electrostatics on a graph.

04 · the Laplacian

Ohm's law on every edge

Unit 03's graph: $n$ nodes, $m$ edges, incidence matrix $A$ ($n\times m$). Edge $e$ runs from its tail to its head; under the passive convention $v_e = \varphi_{\text{tail}} - \varphi_{\text{head}}$ and $i_e$ flows tail to head.

three laws, three matrices

Voltage law: $v = A^{\mathsf T}\varphi$.   Current law, with currents $s$ injected at the nodes: $Ai = s$.   Ohm's law, edge by edge, with conductance $G_e = 1/R_e > 0$:

$$i = Gv,\qquad G = \operatorname{diag}(G_1,\dots,G_m).$$

Substitute the first into the third, and the third into the second:

$$\boxed{\,L\varphi = s,\qquad L = AGA^{\mathsf T}\,}$$

Units: $G$ in siemens, $\mathrm S = \Omega^{-1} = \mathrm{A\,V^{-1}}$; $L\varphi$ is $\mathrm{S\cdot V} = \mathrm A$, the units of $s$ ✓.

One edge. The node on the left also receives a current $s_k$ from outside the network — a source's terminal, say. The current law at node $k$: the currents leaving through its edges sum to $s_k$.

The substitution, written out. $i = Gv = GA^{\mathsf T}\varphi$, so $s = Ai = AGA^{\mathsf T}\varphi$. Three linear laws compose into one. The unknowns are the $n$ potentials; once they are known, $v = A^{\mathsf T}\varphi$ and $i = Gv$ give every edge.

Signs. $(Ai)_k = \sum_eA_{ke}i_e$ is the current leaving node $k$ through its edges (an edge leaving $k$ has $A_{ke} = +1$ and carries $i_e$ away; an edge entering has $-1$). In a steady state the charge at the node does not change (unit 02's (H2)), so that outflow equals what is fed in from outside, $s_k$. This is unit 03's convention: $s = 0$ for an isolated network, and a current source's terminals appear as nonzero entries of $s$.

What "conductance" is. The reciprocal of resistance: how many amperes per volt the element passes. Ohm's law $i = Gv$ is the form that makes a parallel combination a sum. Unit 05 replaces each $G_e$ by a complex admittance; nothing on this slide uses that $G_e$ is real except the boxed formula's interpretation as dissipation on the next slides.

04 · the Laplacian

The weighted graph Laplacian

the weighted graph Laplacian

$L = AGA^{\mathsf T}$, $n\times n$. Its entries, from $L_{kj} = \sum_eA_{ke}G_eA_{je}$:

  • $L_{kk}$ = the sum of the conductances of the edges at node $k$;
  • $L_{kj} = -G_e$ for an edge $e$ joining $k$ and $j$, $0$ if none;
  • every row sums to zero.

It is unit 03's nodal matrix. For the bridge's five resistors, in mS, rows A, B, C, D:

$$L = \begin{pmatrix} 13.33 & -10 & 0 & -3.33\\ -10 & 35 & -5 & -20\\ 0 & -5 & 7.5 & -2.5\\ -3.33 & -20 & -2.5 & 25.83 \end{pmatrix}\mathrm{mS}$$

Unit 13 reuses this matrix with complex weights, as a power grid's bus admittance matrix.

Unit 03's bridge, each resistor marked + at its tail as unit 03 orients it (A→B, B→C, A→D, D→C, B→D). The resistors give $L$; the source is not a resistor, and enters by fixing $\varphi_A$ and $\varphi_C$ (next slides).

The entries, derived. $L_{kj} = \sum_eA_{ke}G_eA_{je}$. For $k = j$ each edge at $k$ contributes $(\pm1)^2G_e = G_e$. For $k\ne j$, an edge contributes only if it joins $k$ and $j$, and then one of $A_{ke}, A_{je}$ is $+1$ and the other $-1$: $-G_e$. Row sums: $L\mathbf 1 = AG(A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1) = 0$ since every column of $A$ has one $+1$ and one $-1$. The orientation of the edges has dropped out entirely: $L$ depends only on which nodes are joined and how well.

The bridge's numbers. $G_1 = 1/100\ \Omega = 10$ mS, $G_2 = 5$, $G_3 = 3.333$, $G_4 = 2.5$, $G_g = 20$ mS. Row A: $10 + 3.333 = 13.333$ on the diagonal, $-10$ to B, $-3.333$ to D. Row B: $10 + 5 + 20 = 35$. Row C: $5 + 2.5 = 7.5$. Row D: $3.333 + 2.5 + 20 = 25.833$. Each row sums to zero.

Unit 03's promise. Unit 03 set up nodal analysis for the bridge and solved it, and said the matrix it produced had special properties studied here. This is that matrix, for every network of resistors at once. Why "Laplacian": $(L\varphi)_k = \sum_jG_{kj}(\varphi_k - \varphi_j)$, a weighted sum of differences from the neighbours — the discrete analogue of $-\nabla^2\varphi$, which on a grid of spacing $h$ is $\sum(\varphi_k - \varphi_j)/h^2$ over the $2d$ neighbours. The sign is chosen so that $L$ is positive semi-definite.

Forward. Unit 05 gives capacitors and inductors complex admittances $i\omega C$ and $1/(i\omega L)$; with those as weights, $AYA^{\mathsf T}$ is the network's admittance matrix, and unit 13 calls it the bus admittance matrix of a power grid. Symmetry survives; positive semi-definiteness becomes a statement about real parts.

04 · the Laplacian

Symmetric, positive semi-definite, and its kernel

theorem

$L = AGA^{\mathsf T}$ is symmetric, and for every $\varphi\in\mathbb R^n$

$$\varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = \sum_eG_e\big(\varphi_{\text{tail}(e)} - \varphi_{\text{head}(e)}\big)^2 \ \ge 0 .$$

Its kernel is exactly the vectors constant on each connected component: $\dim\ker L = c$, $\operatorname{rank}L = n - c$.

proof

$L^{\mathsf T} = AG^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T} = L$. $\varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = (A^{\mathsf T}\varphi)^{\mathsf T}G(A^{\mathsf T}\varphi) = \sum_eG_ev_e^2$. Since every $G_e > 0$, it vanishes iff $v_e = 0$ on every edge, iff $\varphi$ is equal across every edge — constant on each component. For symmetric positive semi-definite $L$, $L\varphi = 0 \iff \varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = 0$. $\square$

physics and algebra, the same line

$G_ev_e^2 = v_ei_e$ is the power edge $e$ dissipates. So $\varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi$ is the total power dissipated in the network when its nodes sit at $\varphi$; positive semi-definiteness is the statement that resistors do not generate.

Full proof

The step "$L\varphi = 0 \iff \varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = 0$". One way is trivial. For the other, by the spectral theorem write $\varphi = \sum c_ku_k$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis, $Lu_k = \lambda_ku_k$ with every $\lambda_k\ge0$. Then $\varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = \sum\lambda_kc_k^2 = 0$ forces $c_k = 0$ whenever $\lambda_k > 0$, so $L\varphi = \sum\lambda_kc_ku_k = 0$.

Constant on components. If $\varphi$ is equal across every edge, then along any path its value does not change, so it is constant on each connected component; conversely such a vector makes every $v_e$ zero. The space of such vectors has a basis of the $c$ indicator vectors of the components, so $\dim\ker L = c$ and, by rank–nullity, $\operatorname{rank}L = n - c$. This agrees with unit 03: $\ker L = \ker A^{\mathsf T}$ (shown in the proof), and unit 03 proved $\operatorname{rank}A = \operatorname{rank}A^{\mathsf T} = n - c$.

The continuous version (MR 23). $\varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = \sum G_e(\Delta\varphi)^2$ is the discrete $\int\sigma|\nabla\varphi|^2\,\mathrm dV$ — the power dissipated in a conductor at potential $\varphi$ (unit 00: $\vec J\cdot \vec E = \sigma|\vec E|^2$), or with $\varepsilon_0$ in place of $\sigma$, twice the electrostatic field energy. Green's first identity in MR 23 is the continuous form of $\varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = (A^{\mathsf T}\varphi)^{\mathsf T}G(A^{\mathsf T}\varphi)$: summation by parts.

04 · the Laplacian

When the potentials exist, and how unique they are

existence and uniqueness

$L\varphi = s$ has a solution iff the injected currents sum to zero on each connected component. The solution is unique up to adding a constant on each component; fixing one node's potential per component — grounding it — makes it unique.

proof

For a symmetric matrix, $\operatorname{im}L = (\ker L^{\mathsf T})^\perp = (\ker L)^\perp$. By the last slide $\ker L$ is spanned by the components' indicator vectors $\mathbf 1_C$, so $s\in\operatorname{im}L \iff \mathbf 1_C^{\mathsf T}s = 0$ for each component $C$. Two solutions differ by an element of $\ker L$. $\square$

The condition is unit 02's (H2) once more: charge cannot pile up in a component, so what is fed in must be taken out.

One connected network. The injections balance, so potentials exist; grounding one node removes the free constant.

The linear algebra used. For any matrix, $\operatorname{im}M = (\ker M^{\mathsf T})^\perp$ — the fundamental theorem of linear algebra, assumed (unit 03 used the same fact for $A$). With $M = L = L^{\mathsf T}$ it reads $\operatorname{im}L = (\ker L)^\perp$.

Grounding. Choose one node $g$ per component and require $\varphi_g = 0$. Among the solutions $\varphi + \sum_C k_C\mathbf 1_C$ exactly one satisfies this. Equivalently: delete $g$'s row and column from $L$ (and $g$'s entry from $s$); the reduced matrix is invertible — it is the case of the Dirichlet problem on the next slide with one boundary node per component. The physical choice does not matter: only differences of potential are measurable.

This is MR 23's uniqueness theorem, case 2 ("conductor data"), on a graph: there the field was unique and the potential unique up to a constant, and the proof was by energy — $\int|\nabla w|^2 = 0$ forced $\nabla w = 0$. Here $w^{\mathsf T}Lw = 0$ forces $w$ constant on components. Same proof.

Unit 03's loose end. Unit 03 noted that a circuit problem is well posed in its count of equations, and left solvability for resistors to this unit; and that superposition for resistive networks needs the solution map to be linear, which needs uniqueness. Both are now settled for networks of positive resistors driven by current injections; the next slide adds voltage sources.

04 · the Laplacian

The discrete Dirichlet problem

An ideal voltage source does not inject a known current; it fixes a potential. So split the nodes: boundary nodes $B$, whose potentials are given (source terminals, ground), and interior nodes $I$, where nothing is injected.

the discrete Dirichlet problem

Given $\varphi_B$, find $\varphi_I$ with $(L\varphi)_I = 0$, i.e. $L_{II}\varphi_I = -L_{IB}\varphi_B$. If every connected component of the interior is joined by an edge to the boundary, then $L_{II}$ is positive definite, and the solution exists and is unique.

proof

Extend $x\in\mathbb R^I$ by zeros on $B$ to $\hat x$. Then $x^{\mathsf T}L_{II}x = \hat x^{\mathsf T}L\hat x\ge0$, and $= 0$ only if $\hat x$ is constant on each component of the graph — but every interior component touches a boundary node where $\hat x = 0$, so $x = 0$. Positive definite ⟹ invertible. $\square$

The car's network is this kind of problem: the battery's EMF fixes one node at 12.6 V above the chassis, and the other three nodes are interior.

Full proof

Order the nodes with $I$ first: $L = \begin{pmatrix}L_{II} & L_{IB}\\ L_{BI} & L_{BB}\end{pmatrix}$, symmetric, so $L_{BI} = L_{IB}^{\mathsf T}$. For $x\in\mathbb R^I$ let $\hat x = (x, 0)$. Then $\hat x^{\mathsf T}L\hat x = x^{\mathsf T}L_{II}x$, which is $\ge0$. If it is $0$, then $\hat x$ is constant on every component of the whole graph (the kernel theorem). Each interior node $k$ lies in a component that contains a boundary node, since its interior component is joined by an edge to $B$; on that component $\hat x$ equals its value at the boundary node, $0$. So $x_k = 0$ for every $k$. A symmetric matrix with $x^{\mathsf T}Mx > 0$ for $x\ne0$ has no zero eigenvalue, so it is invertible, and $\varphi_I = -L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}\varphi_B$ is the unique solution.

When it fails. If an interior component touches no boundary node — a floating island of resistors — then the constant vector on that island is in $\ker L_{II}$: its potential is undetermined, physically meaningful only relative to itself. Problem P5 makes this precise.

The analogy, exactly. MR 23's uniqueness theorem, case 1: Poisson's equation in $D$ with $\varphi$ given on $\partial D$ has at most one solution. Here: "Laplace's equation" $(L\varphi)_I = 0$ at the interior nodes, $\varphi$ given on the boundary nodes, exactly one solution. The hypothesis that every interior component touches the boundary is the graph's version of $D$ being bounded by $\partial D$.

Sources in general. A connected network of positive resistors, ideal voltage sources and ideal current sources has exactly one solution (potentials up to one common constant) when the voltage sources form no loop and the current sources form no cut. Proof. Delete each current source and record its current as equal and opposite injections at its two ends. The voltage sources, forming no loop, form a forest: merge the nodes of each of its trees into one supernode, inside which every potential is a known offset from a single unknown. Sum the current law over the nodes of a supernode: the unknown source currents cancel, each entering one node of it and leaving another. With Ohm's law on every resistor this is a Laplacian system $L'\psi = s'$ on the supernodes, with positive conductances and a known right side whose entries sum to zero (the current sources contribute $+I$ and $-I$; the offsets enter through a Laplacian, whose columns sum to zero). Since the current sources form no cut, resistors and voltage sources alone connect every node, so the supernode graph is connected, and the existence and uniqueness theorem gives $\psi$ up to one constant. Last, each voltage source's current follows from the current law, working inward from the leaves of its tree, uniquely, since a tree has no loop for a current to circulate round. $\square$

Why the conditions matter. Without them a solution can exist and still not be unique: two equal ideal voltage sources in parallel share their current in any proportion, and the node between two equal ideal current sources in series can sit at any potential. The course assumes the conditions throughout. With one voltage source to ground, as in the car and the bridge, the reduction above is all that is needed.

04 · the Laplacian

The maximum principle

At an interior node, $(L\varphi)_k = \sum_jG_{kj}(\varphi_k - \varphi_j) = 0$ says

$$\varphi_k = \frac{\sum_jG_{kj}\varphi_j}{\sum_jG_{kj}} :$$

a conductance-weighted average of its neighbours': $\varphi$ is harmonic at $k$ — MR 05's mean value property on a graph.

the discrete maximum principle

If every interior component touches the boundary, $\min_B\varphi \le \varphi_k \le \max_B\varphi$ at every node.

proof

Let $M = \max\varphi$, attained at interior $k$. An average of numbers $\le M$ equals $M$ only if each does: every neighbour of $k$ is at $M$. Walk on through the interior component to a boundary node. Minimum: use $-\varphi$. $\square$

An interior node is a weighted average of its neighbours, so it cannot exceed them all — as no point of empty space is a strict minimum of a harmonic potential (MR 05, Earnshaw).

Full proof

Let $M = \max_k\varphi_k$ over all $n$ nodes, attained at node $k$. If $k\in B$ we are done. If $k$ is interior, $\sum_jG_{kj}(M - \varphi_j) = 0$ with every term $\ge 0$ (since $G_{kj} > 0$ and $\varphi_j \le M$), so every neighbour $j$ has $\varphi_j = M$. Each neighbour is either a boundary node — done — or interior, and then the same argument applies to it. Since $k$'s interior component is connected and joined by an edge to $B$, this walk reaches a boundary node at value $M$. Hence $\max_{\text{all}}\varphi = \max_B\varphi$. Applying the result to $-\varphi$, also a solution with boundary data $-\varphi_B$, gives the minimum.

The strong form — an interior maximum forces the whole interior component to the maximum — is the graph version of MR 05's statement that a harmonic function with an interior maximum on a connected set is constant. The proof there used the mean value property on small spheres; here the "sphere" round $k$ is its set of neighbours, and the mean value property is the definition of the equation.

The actual currents are the cheapest way to move the given currents through the network. That one fact gives effective resistance a variational formula, and every bound in this unit.

Thomson's principle

A flow with injections $s$ is any $j\in\mathbb R^m$ with $Aj = s$ — currents that obey the current law, whether or not they obey Ohm's law.

Thomson's principle

Among all flows $j$ with $Aj = s$, the dissipated power $P(j) = \sum_eR_ej_e^2$ is least for the actual currents $i$, and only for them.

proof · from Tellegen

Write $j = i + c$; then $Ac = s - s = 0$, so $c$ is in unit 03's cycle space. Expand: $$P(j) = P(i) + 2\sum_eR_ei_ec_e + \sum_eR_ec_e^2 .$$ The middle sum is $\sum_ev_ec_e = v^{\mathsf T}c = 0$ by Tellegen, since $v = A^{\mathsf T}\varphi$ and $Ac = 0$. So $P(j) = P(i) + \sum R_ec_e^2 \ge P(i)$, with equality iff $c = 0$. $\square$

Units: $\Omega\cdot\mathrm A^2 = \mathrm W$ ✓.

$P(t) = t^2\cdot1 + (1 - t)^2\cdot2$ for the two ways of sharing 1 A. Ohm's law gives $t = 2/3$ (equal voltages, $2/3$ V); that is exactly the minimum.

The figure's numbers. $P'(t) = 2t - 4(1 - t) = 0$ at $t = 2/3$; $P(2/3) = 4/9 + 2/9 = 2/3$ W. The voltage across either resistor is $2/3$ V, so $R_{\text{eff}} = (2/3)/1 = 2/3\ \Omega = 1\parallel2$ ✓. The even split: $1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4$ W.

The cross term, carefully. $\sum_eR_ei_ec_e = \sum_ev_ec_e$ because $R_ei_e = v_e$ on every edge (Ohm's law, used for $i$ only). Then $v\in\operatorname{im}A^{\mathsf T}$ and $c\in\ker A$, so $v^{\mathsf T}c = \varphi^{\mathsf T}Ac = 0$: unit 03's Tellegen, applied to the voltages of one "network" (the actual one) and the currents of another (the difference flow $c$) on the same graph — exactly the cross-network form unit 03 proved.

Full proof of the continuous versions

In a conductor. In a conductor $D$, among all current densities $\vec j$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec j = 0$ in $D$ and the same normal component $\vec j\cdot\hat n$ as the actual current at every point of $\partial D$ (zero on the insulated sides), $\int_D|\vec j|^2/\sigma\,\mathrm dV$ is least for the actual $\vec J = -\sigma\nabla\varphi$, and only for it. Proof, the same as on the slide: write $\vec j = \vec J + \vec c$, so $\nabla\cdot\vec c = 0$ in $D$ and $\vec c\cdot\hat n = 0$ on $\partial D$. Then $\int|\vec j|^2/\sigma = \int|\vec J|^2/\sigma + 2\int\vec J\cdot\vec c/\sigma + \int|\vec c|^2/\sigma$, and the cross term is $-2\int\nabla\varphi\cdot\vec c = -2\oint\varphi\,\vec c\cdot\mathrm d\vec A + 2\int\varphi\,\nabla\cdot\vec c = 0$ by the divergence theorem (MR 05) applied to $\varphi\vec c$. The last term is $\ge 0$, and $= 0$ only if $\vec c = 0$.

In electrostatics — the statement MR 23 uses but does not prove. Among all fields $\vec E'$ in a bounded region $D$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec E' = \rho/\varepsilon_0$ in $D$ and the same normal component as the electrostatic field $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$ on $\partial D$, the energy $\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}2\int_D|\vec E'|^2$ is least for $\vec E$, and only for it. Proof: $\vec c = \vec E' - \vec E$ has $\nabla\cdot\vec c = 0$ and $\vec c\cdot\hat n = 0$ on $\partial D$, so the cross term $\varepsilon_0\int\vec E\cdot\vec c = -\varepsilon_0\int\nabla\varphi\cdot\vec c$ vanishes by the same two lines. With $\sigma$ replaced by $\varepsilon_0$ and $\vec J$ by $\vec E$, it is the same theorem.

Its dual is Dirichlet's principle, which MR 11 names: among functions $u$ with given values on $\partial D$, the harmonic one, $\varphi$, minimises $\int_D|\nabla u|^2$. Proof: with $u = \varphi + w$, $w = 0$ on $\partial D$, the cross term $2\int\nabla\varphi\cdot\nabla w = 2\oint w\,\partial_n\varphi - 2\int w\nabla^2\varphi = 0$ by Green's first identity (MR 23). This shows that the harmonic function, given that it exists, is the minimiser. The converse route — getting existence by minimising — is what MR 11's history of the principle shows cannot be taken for granted in the continuum; on a finite graph there is no such difficulty. The discrete dual is problem P10.

History. Doyle and Snell (§1.3.5, "Currents minimize energy dissipation") state the principle under this name and attribute it to Thomson, citing Thomson and Tait's Treatise on Natural Philosophy (1879 edition). Their proof is the one on the slide.

04 · Thomson

Effective resistance

effective resistance

In a connected network, inject 1 A at $a$ and extract it at $b$: $s = e_a - e_b$. Then

$$R_{\text{eff}}(a, b) = \varphi_a - \varphi_b = s^{\mathsf T}L^+s = \sum_eR_ei_e^2 ,$$

the voltage it needs, and the power it dissipates.

$L^+$ is the pseudo-inverse: if $L = \sum_k\lambda_ku_ku_k^{\mathsf T}$, then $L^+ = \sum_{\lambda_k > 0}\lambda_k^{-1}u_ku_k^{\mathsf T}$; as $s\perp\ker L$, $L^+s$ solves $L\varphi = s$.

by Thomson $$R_{\text{eff}}(a, b) = \min_{j\,:\,Aj = e_a - e_b}\ \sum_eR_ej_e^2 .$$

Any unit flow from $a$ to $b$ gives an upper bound.

Units: $\mathrm V/\mathrm A = \Omega$, and $\mathrm W/\mathrm A^2 = \Omega$ ✓.

Seen from two of its nodes, any network of resistors acts as one resistor. Section 3 puts sources inside.

Full proof

The pseudo-inverse. $L$ is symmetric with eigenvalues $\lambda_k\ge0$ and orthonormal eigenvectors $u_k$. Define $L^+$ as on the slide. Then $LL^+ = L^+L = \sum_{\lambda_k>0}u_ku_k^{\mathsf T} = \Pi$, the orthogonal projection onto $\operatorname{im}L = (\ker L)^\perp$, and $L^+LL^+ = L^+$. For a connected network $\ker L = \operatorname{span}\mathbf 1$, and $s = e_a - e_b$ has $\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}s = 0$, so $\Pi s = s$ and $L(L^+s) = s$: $\varphi = L^+s$ is a solution (the one with $\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}\varphi = 0$); all others add a constant, which cancels in $\varphi_a - \varphi_b$.

The three expressions agree. $\varphi_a - \varphi_b = s^{\mathsf T}\varphi = s^{\mathsf T}L^+s$. And the dissipated power is $\sum G_ev_e^2 = \varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = s^{\mathsf T}L^+LL^+s = s^{\mathsf T}L^+s$. Physically: by Tellegen, the power absorbed by the resistors equals the power delivered at the injection points, $\varphi^{\mathsf T}s = \varphi_a\cdot1 + \varphi_b\cdot(-1)$.

Positivity. For $a\ne b$, $R_{\text{eff}} = \varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi > 0$, because $\varphi$ is not constant ($\varphi^{\mathsf T}L\varphi = 0$ would force $L\varphi = 0 \ne s$).

Unit 00's resistance was $R = \rho L/A$ from a boundary-value problem: the voltage needed per ampere through a conductor. Effective resistance is the same definition for a graph. Doyle and Snell (§1.3.4) define it the same way and compute the cube's adjacent-corner value, $7/12$, which section 4 meets.

04 · Thomson

Rayleigh's monotonicity law

Rayleigh's monotonicity law

If no edge's resistance decreases, no effective resistance decreases: $R_e' \ge R_e$ for every $e$ implies $R'_{\text{eff}}(a, b) \ge R_{\text{eff}}(a, b)$ for every pair.

proof

Let $i'$ be the unit current from $a$ to $b$ in the new network. It is also a unit flow in the old one (the graph is the same), so by Thomson there $$R_{\text{eff}} \le \sum_eR_ei_e'^2 \le \sum_eR'_ei_e'^2 = R'_{\text{eff}}. \ \square$$

Two limits make it a tool. Cutting an edge ($R_e\to\infty$, remove it) can only raise $R_{\text{eff}}$. Shorting ($R_e\to0$, merge its two nodes) can only lower it.

The law allows equality. In this balanced diamond the middle edge's ends are equipotential, so cutting it or shorting its ends costs nothing — the trick section 2's last slide uses on the cube.

Full proof of cutting and shorting

Both follow from Thomson directly, with no limit taken. Cutting (the network stays connected): any unit flow from $a$ to $b$ in the cut network is, with $0$ on the removed edge, a unit flow in the original at the same power; so the original's minimum, $R_{\text{eff}}$, is no larger than the cut network's. Shorting nodes $u$ and $w$: take the original's actual current and keep its values on every edge except those joining $u$ to $w$, which become loops and are dropped. The current law at the merged node is the sum of the laws at $u$ and $w$, so this is a unit flow in the shorted network, at no greater power; so the shorted network's $R_{\text{eff}}$ is no larger. Doyle and Snell (§2.2.2, "The Shorting Law and the Cutting Law; Rayleigh's idea") state both and set them as an exercise equivalent to the monotonicity law; their preface notes that Rayleigh introduced the techniques in connection with an investigation of musical instruments.

Equality. Shorting two nodes at the same potential changes nothing: the old solution still satisfies every equation of the new network (no current would flow through the short). Cutting an edge carrying no current changes nothing, for the same reason. In the figure's diamond, both side paths are 2 Ω, the top and bottom nodes are both at half the voltage, and $R_{\text{eff}} = 2\parallel2 = 1\ \Omega$ in all three versions.

Why it is useful. Most networks cannot be solved by hand. Rayleigh's law turns them into bounds: cut edges until the network is series–parallel (upper bound), or short nodes until it is (lower bound). Problem P9 does this on a $4\times4$ grid and brackets the exact $13/7\ \Omega$ between $11/6$ and $99/50$.

Why it is not obvious. Raising one resistance can raise the current in some other edge — the redistributed current goes somewhere. The law says the total effect on $R_{\text{eff}}$ is never a decrease; the proof goes through the variational principle, not through tracking currents.

Series and parallel, as theorems

the two exact reductions

Series: two edges meeting at a node of degree 2 with no injection act as one edge of resistance $R_1 + R_2$.

Parallel: two edges joining the same two nodes act as one edge of conductance $G_1 + G_2$, i.e. $R_1\parallel R_2 = R_1R_2/(R_1 + R_2)$.

proof

Series: KCL at the middle node forces one current $i$ through both, and $v_1 + v_2 = (R_1 + R_2)i$. Parallel: one voltage $v$ across both, and $i_1 + i_2 = (G_1 + G_2)v$. In each case the rest of the network sees the same law between the outer nodes. $\square$

Unit 00's kettle cable "properly needs two resistances in series": unit 02 used the first rule for it. The car is series–parallel too (notes).

Resistances add in series; conductances add in parallel. Both are exact, so Rayleigh's inequality holds with equality.

"Act as one edge" made precise. Two networks are equivalent at a set of nodes if, for every choice of the potentials at those nodes, they draw the same currents into those nodes. For series, the middle node is eliminated and the outer two kept; for parallel, nothing is eliminated and two edges merge. Section 3 calls the general operation Kron reduction; series is the case of one interior node of degree 2, as star–delta is the case of degree 3.

The car, by series and parallel. Seen from the EMF: $r_i$ in series with $\big((R_c + R_s)\parallel(R_w + R_L)\big)$ $= 0.010 + (0.050\parallel1.33909) = 0.010 + 0.048200 = 0.058200\ \Omega$, so $I_b = 12.6/0.058200 = 216.49$ A, as the nodal solution found. The terminal voltage $12.6 - 2.1649 = 10.435$ V divides between the two branches in proportion to their resistances.

Not every network is series–parallel. The bridge is not: no node has degree 2, and no two edges are parallel. That is why unit 03 needed nodal analysis for it, and why star–delta (section 3) exists.

Worked: the cube, by symmetry

worked example 3 — twelve equal resistors $R$ on a cube's edges; corner $a$ to the opposite corner $b$

1. Symmetry. Rotating the cube by $120°$ about the diagonal $ab$ permutes the three neighbours of $a$ (and the three of $b$) cyclically and fixes $a$, $b$. The solution is unique, so it is unchanged: the three nodes at distance 1 from $a$ share one potential, and so do the three at distance 2.

2. Short each layer. Joining equipotential nodes changes nothing (Rayleigh, with equality). The cube becomes a chain of three bundles: 3 edges in parallel, then 6, then 3.

3. Series.

$$R_{\text{eff}}(a, b) = \frac R3 + \frac R6 + \frac R3 = \frac{5R}6 .$$

Check by Thomson. The actual unit current: $\tfrac13$ A on each of the 3 edges at $a$, $\tfrac16$ on each of the 6 middle edges, $\tfrac13$ on the 3 at $b$. Power $3\cdot\tfrac19R + 6\cdot\tfrac1{36}R + 3\cdot\tfrac19R = \tfrac56R$ ✓.

The symmetry argument, made rigorous. Let $\sigma$ be the rotation, acting on the nodes as a permutation that fixes $a$ and $b$ and maps edges to edges. If $\varphi$ solves $L\varphi = e_a - e_b$, so does $\varphi\circ\sigma^{-1}$, because $L$ commutes with the permutation and the injections are fixed by it. Ground $b$: the solution is unique, so $\varphi\circ \sigma^{-1} = \varphi$. The three neighbours of $a$ form one orbit of $\sigma$, so they share a potential; likewise the other three.

The bundle counts. $a$ has 3 edges, to the three distance-1 nodes. Each distance-1 node has 2 edges to distance-2 nodes: 6 in all. Each distance-2 node has 1 edge to $b$: 3. Total $3 + 6 + 3 = 12$ ✓.

The potentials. With $b$ grounded: the distance-2 layer is at $R/3$, the distance-1 layer at $R/3 + R/6 = R/2$, and $a$ at $5R/6$ (volts per ampere). Every interior potential lies between $0$ and $5R/6$ ✓ (maximum principle).

With numbers. Twelve 1 Ω resistors: $0.833\ \Omega$. A computer solving $L\varphi = s$ for the $8\times8$ Laplacian gives $5/6$ exactly. Section 3 gets the same answer with no symmetry at all, by Kron reduction.

04 · Thévenin

Thévenin's theorem

A one-port: a network with two terminals brought out, $p$ and $q$. Mark the port by the passive convention: $v = \varphi_p - \varphi_q$, and $i$ enters at $p$.

Thévenin's theorem

Let a one-port contain linear resistors and ideal sources, and be uniquely solvable when driven by any current $i$ at its port. Then

$$\boxed{\,v = V_{\text{th}} + R_{\text{th}}\,i\,}$$

where $V_{\text{th}}$ is the open-circuit voltage ($i = 0$) and $R_{\text{th}} = R_{\text{eff}}(p, q) \ge 0$ is the effective resistance between $p$ and $q$ with every source set to zero. Nothing outside can tell the network from an EMF $V_{\text{th}}$ in series with $R_{\text{th}}$.

Units: $\Omega\cdot\mathrm A = \mathrm V$ ✓.

The port marked by the passive convention, as for any element. A network delivering power has $i < 0$. Unit 02's real battery, $v = \mathcal E + r_i\,i$, is the simplest case.

Unit 02's promise. Unit 02 drew a battery's characteristic $v = \mathcal E + r_ii$ and said this unit analyses it. The theorem says much more: every linear network of resistors and sources, seen at two terminals, has a characteristic of exactly this form. The battery is not special; it is what any linear source looks like.

The hypothesis. "Uniquely solvable when driven by a current at the port" is what makes $v$ a function of $i$. It holds whenever, with the driving current source added at the port, the voltage sources still form no loop and the current sources no cut (proved in the notes of The discrete Dirichlet problem). An ideal voltage source straight across the port is allowed: whatever $i$ is driven, it fixes $v$, and the theorem holds with $R_{\text{th}} = 0$. What is excluded is a current source alone across the port: with the driving source it forms a cut, the network has a solution for one value of $i$ only, and $v$ is not a function of $i$. Norton's form (next slide), with conductance $1/R_{\text{th}} = 0$, describes it.

History, cited. D. H. Johnson, "Origins of the equivalent circuit concept: the voltage-source equivalent", Proc. IEEE 91(4), 636–640 (2003), traces the result to H. Helmholtz's 1853 paper on the distribution of currents in conductors, with applications to experiments on animal electricity (Annalen der Physik und Chemie 89, 211–233), and to L. Thévenin's "Extension de la loi d'Ohm aux circuits électromoteurs complexes", Annales Télégraphiques (3rd series) 10, 222–224 (1883), published apparently without knowledge of Helmholtz's. The name that stuck in English is Thévenin's.

04 · Thévenin

Proof: an affine map

proof

1. Drive the port with an ideal current source $i$. The network's equations — Kirchhoff's laws, $v_e = R_ei_e$, $v = \mathcal E_k$ on each voltage source, $i = I_k$ on each current source — are linear in the unknowns, with right-hand sides linear in $(i, \mathcal E_1, \dots, I_1, \dots)$.

2. By hypothesis the solution is unique: the system's matrix is invertible, so the solution is a linear function of $(i, \mathcal E_1, \dots, I_1, \dots)$ jointly. In particular $$v = R_{\text{th}}\,i + \textstyle\sum_k\alpha_k\mathcal E_k + \sum_k\beta_kI_k .$$

3. Put $i = 0$: the sums are $V_{\text{th}}$. Put every source to zero: $v = R_{\text{th}}i$ for a network of resistors alone, so $R_{\text{th}} = R_{\text{eff}}(p, q)\ge0$. $\square$

Linear in the sources jointly is superposition; unit 03 needed uniqueness for it and deferred it here.

The port's characteristic. It crosses $i = 0$ at the open-circuit voltage and $v = 0$ at minus the short-circuit current; any load picks one point on it.

Full proof

Collect the unknowns — every edge's $v_e$ and $i_e$, and the port's $v$ — into a vector $x$. The equations are: KCL at each node, with the port current $i$ entering at $p$ and leaving at $q$ as an injection; KVL in the form $v_e = \varphi_{\text{tail}} - \varphi_{\text{head}}$ (or round a basis of cycles); and one element law per edge. Each is linear in $x$, and its constant term is one of $i$, $\mathcal E_k$, $I_k$, or zero. So the system is $Mx = Ny$ with $y = (i, \mathcal E_1, \dots, I_1, \dots)$ and fixed matrices $M$, $N$. Unique solvability for every $y$ means $M$ is invertible (square and injective), and $x = M^{-1}Ny$ is linear in $y$. The port voltage is one coordinate of $x$.

Why "affine" and not "linear". As a function of $i$ alone, with the sources held at their values, $v$ is linear plus a constant: affine. The constant is everything the sources do when nothing is drawn.

Positivity. $R_{\text{th}} = R_{\text{eff}}(p, q)$ of the dead network, which is $\ge0$ (section 2), and $> 0$ unless $p$ and $q$ are joined by a path of zero resistance. A network of positive resistors cannot present a negative resistance; unit 05 extends this to $\operatorname{Re}Z \ge 0$.

What is not claimed. That the network inside dissipates the same power as the equivalent. It does not: the equivalent reproduces the port's behaviour, not the internal bookkeeping. The car's battery and starter, seen from the lamps, are "10.5 V behind 38.3 mΩ", which dissipates nothing when the lamps are off; the real network dissipates 2646 W then.

Setting a source to zero, and Norton's form

what "zero" means for each source

A voltage source with $\mathcal E = 0$ obeys $v = 0$ for every current: it is a short, an ideal wire. A current source with $I = 0$ obeys $i = 0$ for every voltage: it is an open, a removed edge.

So to find $R_{\text{th}}$: replace voltage sources by wires, delete current sources, and compute $R_{\text{eff}}(p, q)$.

Norton's form

If $R_{\text{th}} > 0$, the same line solved for $i$: $$i = \frac{v}{R_{\text{th}}} - I_{\text{N}},\qquad I_{\text{N}} = \frac{V_{\text{th}}}{R_{\text{th}}},$$ a current source $I_{\text{N}}$ in parallel with $R_{\text{th}}$. $I_{\text{N}}$ is the short-circuit current: with $p$ and $q$ joined, $v = 0$ and $I_{\text{N}}$ flows out of $p$ through the wire.

Zeroing is what the element law says at zero, not a convention. The Norton equivalent (bottom) and the Thévenin equivalent have the same characteristic line.

Why zeroing is forced. Step 3 of the proof sets $\mathcal E_k = 0$ and $I_k = 0$ in the equations. The equation of a voltage source then reads $v_e = 0$, which is the equation of an ideal wire; the equation of a current source reads $i_e = 0$, which is the equation of no edge at all. Shorting a current source instead would add a wrong equation, and is the commonest error with this theorem.

Norton, derived. From $v = V_{\text{th}} + R_{\text{th}}i$, $i = (v - V_{\text{th}})/R_{\text{th}} = v/R_{\text{th}} - I_{\text{N}}$. Read it as KCL at $p$: the current entering at $p$ splits into $v/R_{\text{th}}$ through the resistor and $-I_{\text{N}}$ into the source. When $R_{\text{th}} = 0$ there is no Norton form (an ideal voltage source has no finite short-circuit current); when the network is a current source alone, there is no Thévenin form.

The car's numbers, seen from T (the battery's terminals, with nothing attached): $V_{\text{th}} = 12.6$ V, $R_{\text{th}} = 10\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$, so $I_{\text{N}} = 1260$ A — the current a dead short across the terminals would draw in this linear model, dissipating $1260^2\times0.01 = 15.9$ kW inside the battery. A real battery is not linear that far beyond its rating, so only the order of magnitude is meant.

04 · Kron

Kron reduction: eliminating interior nodes

Keep a set $B$ of nodes, at which current may be injected; eliminate the interior $I$, where none is. Block-partition $L$:

$$\begin{pmatrix}L_{BB} & L_{BI}\\ L_{IB} & L_{II}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_B\\ \varphi_I\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}s_B\\ 0\end{pmatrix}.$$
Kron reduction

If every interior component touches $B$, then $s_B = L_{\text{red}}\varphi_B$ with the Schur complement

$$L_{\text{red}} = L_{BB} - L_{BI}L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}.$$
proof · two lines

The second row: $\varphi_I = -L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}\varphi_B$ ($L_{II}$ is invertible, section 1). Into the first: $s_B = L_{BB}\varphi_B + L_{BI}\varphi_I = L_{\text{red}}\varphi_B$. $\square$

It is Thévenin at many ports at once: the network as seen from $B$, whatever is attached there.

Equivalent at nodes 1, 2, 3: for any potentials there, both draw the same currents into those nodes. The next slide proves the right-hand side is a network of positive conductances.

The block identity, written out. The second block row is $L_{IB}\varphi_B + L_{II}\varphi_I = 0$ (no injection at interior nodes). $L_{II}$ is positive definite under the hypothesis (The discrete Dirichlet problem), hence invertible, so $\varphi_I = -L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}\varphi_B$: the Dirichlet problem's solution with boundary data $\varphi_B$. The first block row gives the boundary injections in terms of $\varphi_B$ alone. That is all a Schur complement is: Gaussian elimination of a block of unknowns.

Done by hand already. In worked example 1, eliminating S and L from the T row was Kron reduction of $\{S, L\}$, keeping $\{E, T, 0\}$.

History, cited. The reduction is named after G. Kron, for his Tensor Analysis of Networks (Wiley, 1939): F. Dörfler and F. Bullo, "Kron reduction of graphs with applications to electrical networks", IEEE Trans. Circuits Syst. I 60(1), 150–163 (2013), §1, give this attribution. Dörfler and Bullo also give a full modern account of the next slide's theorem, including the spectrum and effective resistances of the reduced network; nothing here depends on it.

Forward. Unit 13 reduces a power grid's buses this way: the buses with generators or loads are kept, the passive junctions eliminated, and the reduced admittance matrix is what the power-flow equations use.

04 · Kron

The reduced matrix is again a Laplacian

theorem

$L_{\text{red}}$ is symmetric, its rows sum to zero, and its off-diagonal entries are $\le 0$. So it is the Laplacian of a network on $B$ alone, with conductances $G'_{jk} = -(L_{\text{red}})_{jk}\ge0$.

proof · symmetry and row sums

$L_{BI}^{\mathsf T} = L_{IB}$ and $L_{II}^{-1}$ is symmetric, so $L_{\text{red}}^{\mathsf T} = L_{\text{red}}$. Rows: $L\mathbf 1 = 0$ gives $L_{IB}\mathbf 1_B + L_{II}\mathbf 1_I = 0$, so $\mathbf 1_I = -L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}\mathbf 1_B$ — constant boundary data give constant interior potentials — and $L_{\text{red}}\mathbf 1_B = L_{BB}\mathbf 1_B + L_{BI}\mathbf 1_I = 0$. $\square$

proof · signs, by the maximum principle

$(L_{\text{red}})_{jk}$, $j\ne k$, is the current injected at $j$ when $\varphi_B = e_k$: node $k$ at 1 V, the rest of $B$ at 0. The interior then lies in $[0, 1]$, so $j$, at 0, sits at the minimum: $\sum_iG_{ji}(0 - \varphi_i)\le0$. $\square$

One column of $L_{\text{red}}$, physically: hold one boundary node at 1 V and the others at 0, and read off the currents. Current can only flow into the grounded boundary nodes. Computed with every edge 1 S: the column is $(8/15, -1/3, -1/5)$, summing to zero.

Full proof of the signs

Fix $k\in B$ and set $\varphi_B = e_k$. The interior potentials $\varphi_I = -L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}e_k$ solve the Dirichlet problem with boundary values $1$ at $k$ and $0$ elsewhere on $B$. By the maximum principle (The maximum principle) every interior value lies in $[0, 1]$. The $j$-th entry of $L_{\text{red}}e_k = s_B$ is the current injected at boundary node $j$, which equals $(L\varphi)_j = \sum_iG_{ji}(\varphi_j - \varphi_i)$ summed over $j$'s neighbours (boundary or interior). With $j\ne k$, $\varphi_j = 0$, and every neighbour has $\varphi_i\ge0$, so the sum is $\le0$. That sum is $(L_{\text{red}})_{jk}$.

When it is strictly negative. $(L_{\text{red}})_{jk} < 0$ iff some neighbour of $j$ has positive potential, which happens iff $j$ and $k$ are joined by an edge, or by a path through the interior (the strong maximum principle puts every interior node of a component touching $k$ strictly between 0 and 1). So the reduced network joins $j$ and $k$ exactly when the original connects them without passing through another boundary node.

Effective resistances are preserved between nodes of $B$: they are defined by injecting at boundary nodes only, and the reduced network draws the same currents for the same boundary potentials. That is what makes Kron reduction a computational tool: the cube on the next slide but one keeps only $a$ and $b$.

Star–delta: one interior node of degree three

Eliminate a node 0 joined to $a$, $b$, $c$ by $g = (G_a, G_b, G_c)^{\mathsf T}$ and to nothing else. Then $L_{II} = G_a + G_b + G_c =: \Sigma$, $L_{BI} = -g$, and

$$L_{\text{red}} = \operatorname{diag}(g) - gg^{\mathsf T}/\Sigma .$$
the star–delta transformation $$G_{ab} = \frac{G_aG_b}{G_a + G_b + G_c},\quad\text{and cyclically.}$$

A star of three edges is equivalent, at its three tips, to a triangle (a "delta").

Units: $\mathrm S\cdot\mathrm S/\mathrm S = \mathrm S$ ✓. Check: diagonal $G_a - G_a^2/\Sigma = G_a(G_b + G_c)/\Sigma = G_{ab} + G_{ac}$ ✓.

The star is Kron-reduced to the delta. The reverse direction — delta to star — is problem P3.

The computation. With $B = \{a, b, c\}$ and $I = \{0\}$: $L_{BB} = \operatorname{diag}(G_a, G_b, G_c)$ (each tip's only edge goes to 0), $L_{II} = \Sigma$, $L_{BI} = -g$ with $g = (G_a, G_b, G_c)^{\mathsf T}$, $L_{IB} = -g^{\mathsf T}$. So $L_{\text{red}} = \operatorname{diag}(g) - gg^{\mathsf T}/\Sigma$. Its $(a, b)$ entry is $-G_aG_b/\Sigma$, so $G_{ab} = G_aG_b/\Sigma$.

In resistances, with $R_a = 1/G_a$ and so on: $R_{ab} = 1/G_{ab} = (R_aR_b + R_bR_c + R_cR_a)/R_c$. Both forms are common; the conductance form is the one that falls out of the Schur complement.

Why engineers want it. It turns a network that is not series–parallel into one that may be. The bridge's star at node B ($R_1$, $R_2$, $R_g$ to A, C, D) becomes a delta on A, C, D, after which everything is series and parallel. Unit 11 uses the transformation with complex weights for star- and delta-connected three-phase loads.

Degree two is the series rule: eliminating a node joined to $a$ and $b$ only gives $G_{ab} = G_aG_b/(G_a + G_b)$, i.e. $R_{ab} = R_a + R_b$.

Worked: the cube, by Kron reduction

worked example 3, continued — the same $5R/6$, with no symmetry argument

1. Partition. $B = \{a, b\}$; interior: $x_1, x_2, x_3$ next to $a$, $y_1, y_2, y_3$ next to $b$. Each $x$ has two $y$ neighbours and vice versa (a hexagon).

2. One column. Put $\varphi_a = 1$, $\varphi_b = 0$, $R = 1$. The equations $(L\varphi)_I = 0$: $3x_k - (\text{its two }y) = 1$ and $3y_k - (\text{its two }x) = 0$.

3. Sum them, with $X = \sum x_k$, $Y = \sum y_k$:

$$3X - 2Y = 3,\ \ 3Y - 2X = 0,$$

so $X = \tfrac95$, $Y = \tfrac65$.

4. Read off $L_{\text{red}}$. Injected at $a$: $\sum(1 - x_k) = 3 - X = \tfrac65$. At $b$: $\sum(0 - y_k) = -\tfrac65$. So

$$L_{\text{red}} = \frac6{5R}\begin{pmatrix}1 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}:$$

one edge of conductance $6/5R$, so $R_{\text{eff}} = 5R/6$.

checks

Row sums zero, off-diagonal $\le0$ ✓. The values $x_k = \tfrac35$, $y_k = \tfrac25$ solve every equation and lie in $[0, 1]$ ✓. Same as by symmetry ✓.

Why no symmetry was needed. Step 4 needs only the sums $X$ and $Y$, and step 3 gets them from the sum of the equations, using only that each interior node has one boundary neighbour and two interior ones of the other kind. The individual $x_k$ were never needed. (They are all $3/5$, and the $y_k$ all $2/5$: substitute and every equation holds, and the Dirichlet problem has one solution.)

The bookkeeping in step 3. Summing $3x_k - (y\text{'s adjacent to }x_k) = 1$ over $k$: each $y_j$ is adjacent to exactly two $x$'s, so the $y$'s contribute $-2Y$, and the right side sums to $3$. Likewise $3Y - 2X = 0$. From the second, $Y = 2X/3$; then $3X - 4X/3 = 5X/3 = 3$, $X = 9/5$.

A computer algebra system computing $L_{BB} - L_{BI}L_{II}^{-1}L_{IB}$ directly gives the same $\tfrac65\begin{pmatrix}1 & -1\\ -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.

section 4

A metric, and Foster's theorem

Effective resistance measures distance on the graph, better than counting edges. And summed over the edges, weighted by conductance, it always gives $n - 1$ — the trace of a projection.

04 · metric

Effective resistance is a metric

theorem · the resistance distance

On a connected network, $R_{\text{eff}}$ is a metric on the nodes: $R(a, a) = 0$, $R(a, b) > 0$ for $a\ne b$, $R(a, b) = R(b, a)$, and

$$R(a, c) \le R(a, b) + R(b, c).$$
proof of the triangle inequality

Let $x = e_a - e_b$, $y = e_c - e_b$, so $e_a - e_c = x - y$ and

$$R(a, c) = R(a, b) + R(c, b) - 2\,x^{\mathsf T}L^+y .$$

$\psi = L^+y$ is the potential of 1 A in at $c$, out at $b$, and $x^{\mathsf T}\psi = \psi_a - \psi_b$. Every node but $b$, $c$ is interior, so by the maximum principle $\psi$ lies between $\psi_b$ and $\psi_c$, and $\psi_c - \psi_b = R(c, b) > 0$: $b$ is the minimum. So $\psi_a - \psi_b\ge0$. $\square$

The cross term is the voltage that a current from $c$ to $b$ produces between $a$ and $b$. It cannot be negative, because $b$ is where that current leaves.

Full proof

The expansion. $R(a, c) = (e_a - e_c)^{\mathsf T}L^+(e_a - e_c) = (x - y)^{\mathsf T}L^+(x - y) = x^{\mathsf T}L^+x + y^{\mathsf T}L^+y - 2x^{\mathsf T}L^+y$, using the symmetry of $L^+$. The first two terms are $R(a, b)$ and $R(c, b)$, both differences of unit vectors whose entries sum to zero.

The maximum principle, applied. $\psi = L^+y$ satisfies $L\psi = y$: injections $+1$ at $c$, $-1$ at $b$, zero elsewhere. Treat $\{b, c\}$ as the boundary; every other node is interior (no injection) and the graph is connected, so every interior component touches the boundary. The maximum principle puts every $\psi_k$ in $[\min(\psi_b, \psi_c), \max(\psi_b, \psi_c)]$, and $\psi_c - \psi_b = y^{\mathsf T}L^+y = R(c, b) > 0$. So $\psi_b$ is the minimum, and $x^{\mathsf T}\psi = \psi_a - \psi_b \ge 0$. (If $a$ is $b$ or $c$ the inequality is trivial.)

When equality holds. Exactly when $\psi_a = \psi_b$, which by the strong maximum principle happens iff every path from $a$ to $c$ passes through $b$ — $b$ is a cut vertex separating them, and the network is two pieces joined at $b$, whose resistances then simply add. Problem P6 proves this.

Cited. D. J. Klein and M. Randić, "Resistance distance", J. Math. Chem. 12, 81–95 (1993), introduced effective resistance as a distance on molecular graphs, under this name. The proof here does not depend on theirs. It is a better distance than counting edges for many purposes, because it counts how many routes there are: two nodes joined by many parallel paths are "close".

04 · Foster

Foster's theorem

Foster's theorem

For a connected network with $n$ nodes, let $R_{\text{eff}}(e)$ be the effective resistance between the two ends of edge $e$. Then

$$\sum_{e}\frac{R_{\text{eff}}(e)}{R_e} = n - 1 .$$

Each term lies in $(0, 1]$; the sum depends on the number of nodes only, not on the resistances or the shape.

what each term means

Inject 1 A at one end of $e$ and extract it at the other. The voltage across $e$ is $R_{\text{eff}}(e)$, so the current through $e$ itself is $R_{\text{eff}}(e)/R_e$; the rest goes round. Foster: those fractions always total $n - 1$.

Dimensions: each term is $\Omega/\Omega$, dimensionless ✓.

The physical reading of one term. A cut edge — one whose removal disconnects the graph — carries all of the current, and contributes exactly 1.

Why each term is in $(0, 1]$. By Rayleigh, $R_{\text{eff}}(e)$ is at most what it would be with every other edge cut, which is $R_e$; and it is positive. Equality $R_{\text{eff}}(e) = R_e$ holds iff no other path joins the ends of $e$.

A tree. If the network is a tree, every edge is a cut edge, each term is 1, and there are $n - 1$ edges: the theorem is a count. In general cycles share the load, and the sum stays at $n - 1$ however the edges are weighted.

Cited, for history and a second proof. R. M. Foster, "The average impedance of an electrical network", in Reissner Anniversary Volume: Contributions to Applied Mechanics (J. W. Edwards, Ann Arbor, 1949), pp. 333–340 (Lyons and Peres date the paper 1948; the volume appeared in 1949). R. Lyons and Y. Peres, Probability on Trees and Networks (Cambridge, 2016), state it as Exercise 2.65 ("Foster's Theorem") and note in their hints that the proof by a trace, the one on the next slide, is first due to Flanders (1974). Bollobás's ch. IX treats effective resistance and random walks together; Doyle and Snell's random-walk reading of all of this is on the reading list, and is not needed here.

Proof: the trace of an orthogonal projection

Let $a_e$ be column $e$ of $A$: $+1$ at the tail, $-1$ at the head. Then $R_{\text{eff}}(e) = a_e^{\mathsf T}L^+a_e$, and

$$\sum_eG_e\,a_e^{\mathsf T}L^+a_e = \operatorname{tr}P,$$

where $P = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}L^+AG^{1/2}$ is $m\times m$.

proof

1. $P$ is an orthogonal projection. Symmetric, since $L^+$ is; and $P^2 = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}L^+(AGA^{\mathsf T})L^+AG^{1/2} = P$, because $AGA^{\mathsf T} = L$ and $L^+LL^+ = L^+$.

2. Its image is $W = \operatorname{im}(G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T})$: $\operatorname{im}P\subseteq W$, and on $W$, $P(G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}\varphi) = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}(L^+L\varphi) = G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}\varphi$, because $L^+L\varphi$ differs from $\varphi$ by an element of $\ker L = \ker A^{\mathsf T}$.

3. Trace = rank for an orthogonal projection (eigenvalues 0 and 1). $\dim W = \operatorname{rank}A^{\mathsf T} = n - 1$. $\square$

$P$ projects the edge space orthogonally onto the cut space, rescaled by $G^{1/2}$. Its diagonal entries are Foster's terms.

Full proof

The diagonal of $P$. $P_{ee} = G_e^{1/2}\,a_e^{\mathsf T}L^+a_e\,G_e^{1/2} = G_eR_{\text{eff}}(e) = R_{\text{eff}}(e)/R_e$, since $a_e = e_{\text{tail}} - e_{\text{head}}$ is a unit injection across $e$ (section 2's formula). So $\operatorname{tr}P$ is Foster's sum.

$L^+LL^+ = L^+$. From the spectral form, $L^+LL^+ = \sum_{\lambda_k>0}\lambda_k^{-1}\lambda_k\lambda_k^{-1}u_ku_k^{\mathsf T} = L^+$.

Step 2 in detail. $L^+L = \Pi$, the orthogonal projection onto $(\ker L)^\perp$, so $L^+L\varphi = \varphi - k$ with $k\in\ker L$. The kernel theorem gave $\ker L = \ker A^{\mathsf T}$, so $A^{\mathsf T}k = 0$ and $A^{\mathsf T}L^+L\varphi = A^{\mathsf T}\varphi$. Hence $P$ fixes every vector of $W$; with $\operatorname{im}P\subseteq W$ (visible from the factor $G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}$ on the left), $\operatorname{im}P = W$.

Step 3. A symmetric $P$ with $P^2 = P$ has eigenvalues satisfying $\lambda^2 = \lambda$, so $0$ or $1$; by the spectral theorem the number of 1s is $\operatorname{rank}P$, and the trace is the sum of the eigenvalues. $\dim W = \operatorname{rank}(G^{1/2}A^{\mathsf T}) = \operatorname{rank}A^{\mathsf T}$ ($G^{1/2}$ is invertible) $= n - c = n - 1$ (unit 03).

The geometry. Unit 03 split $\mathbb R^m$ into the cut space $\operatorname{im}A^{\mathsf T}$ and the cycle space $\ker A$, orthogonal. Rescale each coordinate by $G_e^{1/2}$ and the same split happens in the conductance-weighted picture: $P$ projects onto the rescaled cut space, $I - P$ onto the rescaled cycle space, and $\operatorname{tr}(I - P) = m - n + 1$ is the cyclomatic number. Foster's theorem is the statement that a projection's trace is its rank.

section 5

Maximum power, and its price

A source delivers the most power to a load equal to its own resistance — and then wastes exactly as much as it delivers. Where that trade is right, and where it is absurd.

04 · maximum power

The maximum-power theorem

By Thévenin, any linear resistive source is $V_{\text{th}}$ behind $R_{\text{th}} > 0$. A load $R$ draws $I = V_{\text{th}}/(R_{\text{th}} + R)$, and

$$P(R) = I^2R = \frac{V_{\text{th}}^2\,R}{(R_{\text{th}} + R)^2}.$$
the maximum-power theorem

$P(R)$ is largest at the matched load $R = R_{\text{th}}$: $P_{\max} = V_{\text{th}}^2/4R_{\text{th}}$. There the efficiency $\eta = I^2R/V_{\text{th}}I = R/(R_{\text{th}} + R)$, the share of the EMF's output that reaches the load, is exactly $\tfrac12$.

proof

$(R_{\text{th}} + R)^2 - 4R_{\text{th}}R = (R_{\text{th}} - R)^2\ge0$, so $P(R)\le V_{\text{th}}^2/4R_{\text{th}}$, with equality iff $R = R_{\text{th}}$. Then $\eta = R_{\text{th}}/2R_{\text{th}}$. $\square$

Units: $\mathrm V^2/\Omega = \mathrm W$ ✓; $\eta$ dimensionless ✓.

At the matched point the source's own resistance carries the same current as the load and dissipates the same power. Half of everything the EMF produces is lost before it leaves.

By calculus, too. $P'(R) = V_{\text{th}}^2\big[(R_{\text{th}} + R) - 2R\big]/(R_{\text{th}} + R)^3 = V_{\text{th}}^2(R_{\text{th}} - R)/(R_{\text{th}} + R)^3$: positive below $R_{\text{th}}$, negative above. The algebraic proof on the slide needs no derivative and gives the bound for every $R$ at once.

Limits. $R\to0$: a short, $I = V_{\text{th}}/R_{\text{th}} = I_{\text{N}}$, no power to the load (no voltage across it), all power in $R_{\text{th}}$, $\eta = 0$. $R\to\infty$: open, no current, no power, $\eta\to1$. Maximum power lies between, and so does every efficiency.

Energy check. The EMF delivers $V_{\text{th}}I$; $R_{\text{th}}$ takes $I^2R_{\text{th}}$; the load $I^2R$; and $V_{\text{th}}I = I^2(R_{\text{th}} + R)$ by KVL ✓. Note that "efficiency" here is defined against the equivalent's EMF: when the source is a real network, its internal losses are not those of the equivalent (Proof: an affine map, notes). For the car the equivalent and the real network coincide — battery, cables, starter, one loop — so the efficiency is real.

Unit 01's question, "what load takes the most power from such a source", answered: its own internal resistance. For unit 01's battery the load would be $r_i$.

04 · maximum power

Worked: power and efficiency against the load

worked example 5 — the curve, and the starter's two operating points

1. Normalise. With $x = R/R_{\text{th}}$:

$$\frac P{P_{\max}} = \frac{4x}{(1 + x)^2},\qquad \eta = \frac x{1 + x}.$$

$P/P_{\max}$ is symmetric under $x\mapsto1/x$; $\eta$ rises monotonically to 1. Beyond the matched point, efficiency is bought with power.

2. The car. The starter's source: $\mathcal E = 12.6$ V behind $r_i + R_c = 15\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$, so $P_{\max} = 12.6^2/0.060 = 2646$ W.

The real starter, $R_s = 45\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$: $x = 3$, $P = \tfrac{12}{16}P_{\max} = 1984.5$ W at $\eta = \tfrac34$.

The matched starter, $R_s = 15\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$: $x = 1$, $P = 2646$ W at $\eta = \tfrac12$.

The match buys 33% more power at the starter for twice the current — 420 A against 210 A — and four times the loss in battery and cables.

Computed from the two formulas. At $x = 3$ the two curves happen to cross: $4\cdot3/16 = 3/4$.

The numbers. $P_{\max} = V_{\text{th}}^2/4R_{\text{th}} = 158.76/0.060 = 2646$ W. Real starter: $I = 12.6/0.060 = 210$ A, $P = 210^2\times0.045 = 1984.5$ W $= 0.75\times2646$ ✓; loss $210^2\times0.015 = 661.5$ W; $\eta = 1984.5/2646 = 0.75$ ✓. Matched: $I = 12.6/0.030 = 420$ A, $P = 420^2\times0.015 = 2646$ W; loss in $r_i$ $1764$ W and in the cables $882$ W, $2646$ W in all; the EMF delivers $12.6\times420 = 5292$ W. Losses: $2646/661.5 = 4$ times as large.

The symmetry $x\mapsto1/x$. $4(1/x)/(1 + 1/x)^2 = 4x/(x + 1)^2$. A load three times too large and a load three times too small get the same power — but the large one wastes a quarter of the energy and the small one three quarters.

The crossing. $4x/(1 + x)^2 = x/(1 + x)$ iff $4 = 1 + x$, i.e. $x = 3$: a coincidence of the starter's assumed values, of no significance.

04 · maximum power

The matched starter, and why nobody builds it

A starter of 15 mΩ would get 2646 W instead of 1984.5 W — and the battery would have to supply 5292 W, half of it heating its own plates and the cables, through which 420 A would flow instead of 210 A.

what "maximum power" assumed

That the load is only a resistance, and that the source is linear. A turning motor is not a resistance: it develops a back-EMF that reduces its current as it speeds up (unit 12). And the battery's linear model was fitted at one rating; nothing here says how it behaves far from it.

The design question is not "most power into the starter" but "enough torque to turn the engine, without flattening or overheating the battery". That is a statement about efficiency and current, and it points away from the match.

Bar lengths to scale. Matching doubles the battery's output and quadruples its losses, for a third more power at the starter.

The numbers. Real: $I = 210$ A; $r_i$: $210^2\times0.010 = 441$ W; cables: $220.5$ W; starter: $1984.5$ W; total $2646$ W. Matched: $I = 420$ A; $r_i$: $1764$ W; cables: $882$ W; starter: $2646$ W; total $5292$ W. Ratio of starter powers $2646/1984.5 = 1.333$.

Why the model breaks down most for the match. The internal resistance was inferred from a rating at 540 A. Asking the battery for 420 A is within that, but its terminals would then sit at $12.6 - 4.2 = 8.4$ V, not far above the 7.2 V the J537 test accepts — the linear model is being used near the edge of the regime it was fitted to. That is a reason to distrust the 2646 W, not a reason to prefer the match.

Heat. 1764 W inside the battery is 1.76 kJ into the plates and electrolyte for every second the crank lasts. Nothing here claims what that does to a real battery; the point is only the comparison, 1764 W against 441 W, for the same battery.

04 · maximum power

Why a power station refuses the matched point

A power station turns fuel into electrical energy in a generator (unit 12) and sends it through transformers and lines (unit 07) to loads far away. Seen from the loads, the whole chain is a source with some resistance in series — the generator's windings, the lines. It is built so that this resistance is far below the loads'.

Take $R_{\text{th}}/R = 0.01$ (an assumption for the comparison). Then

  • $\eta = 1/1.01 = 99.0\%$, against 50% matched;
  • energy generated per kWh delivered: $1/\eta = 1.01$ kWh, against 2 kWh matched.

At a fixed fuel cost per kWh generated, the match would burn twice the fuel per kWh sold, and half of it would heat the generator and the lines. The power the source could deliver is not the scarce thing; the fuel is.

At 99% efficiency the loss is the sliver on top of the left bar. The matched source loses as much as it sells.

The numbers. $x = R/R_{\text{th}} = 100$: $\eta = 100/101 = 0.990099$; $P/(V_{\text{th}}^2/R_{\text{th}}) = x/(1 + x)^2 = 100/10201 = 0.0098$, against $0.25$ matched. So the efficient source delivers only $0.0098/0.25 = 3.9\%$ of the power it could deliver into a matched load. Nobody wants that power: the station is sized for its loads, not for the match.

What is assumed. The ratio 0.01 is chosen for the comparison, not a figure for any real grid; a real grid's losses depend on its loading and its lines, and are not claimed here. The qualitative statement — power systems are designed so that source and line resistances are small beside the loads they serve, and run far from the matched point — follows from the economics above. Unit 07 adds the reason lines run at high voltage: the loss in a line of fixed resistance at fixed delivered power falls as $1/V^2$.

04 · maximum power

Where matching is right: a radio receiver

A radio receiver takes its signal from an antenna: a conductor in which a passing radio wave drives a tiny current. At its terminals the antenna acts as a source with an internal resistance (branch 16 shows why, and what the resistance is). The transmitter has already paid for the energy; whatever the antenna does not hand over is gone anyway.

the opposite economics

Here the source's energy is free and the received power is scarce: it decides whether the signal stands out above the receiver's own noise. So the receiver's input is made to match the antenna, and the 50% is accepted.

Unit 06 turns this into conjugate matching for alternating current, where the source has reactance too.

The same theorem, the opposite choice. What the "lost" half in $R_a$ is physically is branch 16's question.

Why the economics flip. For the power station, the cost is fuel per delivered kWh, so efficiency rules. For the receiver, the cost of the received energy is zero to the receiver, and the figure of merit is how much signal power arrives compared with the noise generated inside the receiver; so power rules, and matching wins. Nothing numerical is claimed about any receiver; the point is the direction of the design choice.

A correction. An audio amplifier driving a loudspeaker is sometimes offered as an example of matching. It is not one: such an amplifier is designed with an output resistance far below its loudspeaker's, so that the speaker's voltage is set by the amplifier and not by the speaker's varying impedance. It sits on the efficient side of the curve, with the power station.

The radio wave is MR 24's radiation; how a conductor exposed to it acts as a source with a resistance, and how much power it can collect, is branch 16. Unit 08 explains why the receiver's input and the antenna are joined by a cable of matching characteristic impedance.
