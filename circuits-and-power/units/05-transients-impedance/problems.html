Part C · unit 05 · problems

Circuits that remember: transients, phasors, and impedance as a positive-real function

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. On paper and without notes, you prove that charging a capacitor from a fixed voltage through a resistor wastes exactly half the energy drawn, whatever the resistance — and check it: 0.2 μF at 12 V draws 28.8 μJ and loses 14.4 μJ; and you prove, from Tellegen's theorem with a complex conjugate, that the impedance of every RLC network is positive-real — and check it on the deck's worked example 5 network: \(Z = 2 + 0.5i\ \Omega\) at \(s = (1 + i)\ \mathrm s^{-1}\).

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Thirteen problems, about four and a half hours. P2 and P10 are the checkpoint's. Conventions as in the deck: the passive sign convention; element laws \(v = Ri\), \(i = C\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt\), \(v = L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt\); RMS phasors, \(v(t) = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2\,Ve^{i\omega t})\); impedances \(R\), \(i\omega L\), \(1/(i\omega C)\), or \(R\), \(sL\), \(1/(sC)\) at complex frequency \(s\); \(Z = R + iX\); the one-sided Laplace transform \(F(s) = \int_0^\infty f e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt\) with \(\mathcal L[\dot f] = sF - f(0)\); a series RLC has \(\gamma = R/L\), \(\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}\), \(Q = \omega_0/\gamma\).

The ignition coil (all values assumed): a 12 V battery, the coil's primary \(L = 6\) mH in series with \(R = 3\ \Omega\), a contact, and \(C = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm F\) across the contact. Switched on, \(\tau = 2\) ms and the current rises to 4 A, storing 48 mJ. A four-cylinder four-stroke engine fires twice per crankshaft turn; the contact is closed for 60% of each interval.

Memory and the first-order circuit

P1 Time constants routine

exercises: the first-order solution, the 63% and 99.3% rules computed, dimensions of \(RC\) and \(L/R\)

  1. Show that \(RC\) and \(L/R\) are times, from the units \(\Omega = \mathrm{V\,A^{-1}}\), \(\mathrm F = \mathrm{C\,V^{-1}}\), \(\mathrm H = \mathrm{V\,s\,A^{-1}}\).
  2. A quantity moves as \(x(t) = x(\infty) + (x(0) - x(\infty))e^{-t/\tau}\). What fraction of its change has it made after \(\tau\), and after \(5\tau\)? How long does it take to make 90% and 99% of the change, in units of \(\tau\)?
  3. A 4.7 μF capacitor, uncharged, is connected at \(t = 0\) through 10 kΩ to a 9 V source. Find \(\tau\), the time after which it is within 0.7% of 9 V, and its voltage at \(t = 100\) ms.
  4. A 0.5 H inductor in series with 100 Ω is switched onto a DC source. Find \(\tau\) and the time for the current to reach 90% of its final value.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every answer is a number of seconds, or a pure number times \(\tau\). Part 2 is the whole problem; parts 3 and 4 are part 2 with numbers.

Rung 2

The fraction of the change made by time \(t\) is \(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\). Setting it equal to \(f\) and solving gives \(t = -\tau\ln(1 - f)\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(\Omega\cdot\mathrm F = \mathrm{V\,A^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{C\,V^{-1}} = \mathrm{C\,A^{-1}} = \mathrm s\).

Worked solution

Idea. One store and constant sources give one exponential; everything is read off \(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\).

1. \(\Omega\cdot\mathrm F = \mathrm{V\,A^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{C\,V^{-1}} = \mathrm{C\,A^{-1}} = \mathrm s\), since an ampere is a coulomb per second. \(\mathrm H/\Omega = \mathrm{V\,s\,A^{-1}}/\mathrm{V\,A^{-1}} = \mathrm s\).

2. After \(\tau\): \(1 - e^{-1} = 0.632\). After \(5\tau\): \(1 - e^{-5} = 0.993\). From \(t = -\tau\ln(1 - f)\): 90% at \(\tau\ln10 = 2.303\tau\), 99% at \(\tau\ln100 = 4.605\tau\).

3. \(\tau = 10^4\times4.7\times10^{-6} = 47\) ms. Within 0.7% of 9 V means 99.3% of the change: \(5\tau = 235\) ms. At 100 ms, \(v = 9(1 - e^{-100/47}) = 9\times0.8809 = 7.93\) V.

4. \(\tau = 0.5/100 = 5\) ms; 90% at \(2.303\times5 = 11.5\) ms.

Check. Limits: \(t = 0\) gives fraction 0, \(t\to\infty\) gives 1 ✓. The tangent at \(t = 0\) has slope \(1/\tau\) in fraction per second, so it would reach 1 at \(t = \tau\), above the curve's 0.632 ✓ (the exponential is concave). Units in part 3: \(t/\tau\) is ms/ms, a pure number ✓.

P2 Proof: half the energy, whatever R medium

exercises: the checkpoint's first proof, twice; energy bookkeeping; why gradual charging loses less

An uncharged capacitor \(C\) is connected at \(t = 0\) through a resistance \(R\) to a fixed voltage \(V\).

  1. Prove that, by the time it is charged, the source has supplied \(CV^2\) and the resistor has dissipated \(\tfrac12CV^2\), first by integrating \(i^2R\) with the exponential current, then without solving any differential equation.
  2. Say what the second proof uses about the resistor. Does it still hold with an inductor in series with \(R\)? (Assume the current eventually dies away.)
  3. Now charge the capacitor in \(N\) equal steps: the source is set to \(V/N\), then, once the capacitor has charged, to \(2V/N\), and so on up to \(V\). Prove that the total energy lost is \(\tfrac12CV^2/N\).
  4. For the contact's capacitor, \(C = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm F\) at 12 V, compute the loss for \(N = 1\) and \(N = 10\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each answer is an energy proportional to \(CV^2\), with no \(R\) in it. The second proof is a change of variable from \(t\) to \(v_C\).

Rung 2

KVL gives \(v_R = V - v_C\); the capacitor gives \(i\,\mathrm dt = C\,\mathrm dv_C\). For part 3, each step is the theorem again, with a source of \(kV/N\) and a capacitor starting at \((k - 1)V/N\): only the size of the jump matters.

Rung 3

First line of the second proof: \(\int_0^\infty v_Ri\,\mathrm dt = \int_0^\infty(V - v_C)\,C\,\dot v_C\,\mathrm dt\).

Worked solution

Idea. The source pushes all the charge \(CV\) through the full voltage \(V\); the capacitor stores it at the average voltage \(V/2\). The difference is lost, whatever the path between.

1, by the exponential. \(v_C = V(1 - e^{-t/RC})\), so \(i = C\dot v_C = (V/R)e^{-t/RC}\). The source supplies \(\int_0^\infty Vi\,\mathrm dt = V\cdot CV = CV^2\). The resistor dissipates \(\int_0^\infty R(V/R)^2e^{-2t/RC}\,\mathrm dt = (V^2/R)(RC/2) = \tfrac12CV^2\). The capacitor ends with \(\tfrac12CV^2\), and \(CV^2 = \tfrac12CV^2 + \tfrac12CV^2\) ✓.

1, without the exponential. \(\int_0^\infty v_Ri\,\mathrm dt = \int_0^\infty(V - v_C)C\dot v_C\,\mathrm dt = \big[C(Vv_C - \tfrac12v_C^2)\big]_0^\infty = C(V^2 - \tfrac12V^2) = \tfrac12CV^2\), using only that \(v_C\) goes from 0 to \(V\).

2. Nothing about the resistor beyond its being the only other element in the loop: not Ohm's law, not the value of \(R\). With an inductor in series, KVL becomes \(v_R = V - v_C - L\dot\imath\), and \(\int L\dot\imath\,i\,\mathrm dt = [\tfrac12Li^2]_0^\infty = 0\) because the current starts and ends at zero. So the resistive loss is still \(\tfrac12CV^2\), though \(v_C\) may overshoot on the way — the deck's R → 0 slide.

3. Step \(k\): the source is at \(kV/N\), the capacitor goes from \((k - 1)V/N\) to \(kV/N\). The second proof with these end values gives a loss \(\int(kV/N - v_C)C\,\mathrm dv_C = \tfrac12C(V/N)^2\) — the square of the jump. \(N\) steps lose \(N\cdot\tfrac12CV^2/N^2 = \tfrac12CV^2/N\). The capacitor still ends with \(\tfrac12CV^2\); the source now supplies \(\tfrac12CV^2(1 + 1/N)\).

4. \(N = 1\): \(\tfrac12\times0.2\times10^{-6}\times144 = 14.4\ \mu\mathrm J\). \(N = 10\): \(1.44\ \mu\mathrm J\).

Check. Units: \(\mathrm F\cdot\mathrm V^2 = \mathrm J\) ✓. \(N = 1\) recovers the theorem ✓. \(N\to\infty\): no loss — a source that rises with the capacitor never has a voltage across the resistor. This is the idea behind "adiabatic" charging: make the step small and the loss goes with it.

P3 The order of a network medium

exercises: counting state variables, capacitor loops and inductor cuts, what is not a constraint, one state equation

Find the order of each network, naming every capacitor loop and inductor cut.

  1. A voltage source \(V_s\), a resistor \(R\), and two capacitors \(C_1\), \(C_2\) in parallel, all in one loop.
  2. A voltage source, \(R\), and two capacitors \(C_1\), \(C_2\) in series, all in one loop.
  3. Three capacitors forming a triangle on nodes a, b, c, with a resistor from each of a, b, c to a common node d, and a voltage source from d to a through a fourth resistor.
  4. A current source \(I_s\) in series with an inductor \(L_1\) feeding a node n; from n, a resistor \(R\) and an inductor \(L_2\) both go to the return.
  5. A voltage source \(V_s\) through \(R\) feeding two inductors \(L_1\), \(L_2\) in parallel. Then write its state equation \(\dot x = Ax + Bu\) with \(x = (i_1, i_2)\), and find the eigenvalues of \(A\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each answer is a whole number: stores minus independent constraints. A constraint is a loop of capacitors (and voltage sources) only, or a cut of inductors (and current sources) only — not the other way round.

Rung 2

Two capacitors in series share a node that touches only them: that is a cut of capacitors, which constrains nothing. Two inductors in parallel form a loop of inductors, which constrains only derivatives.

Rung 3

First line of part 5: the node voltage is \(v_a = V_s - R(i_1 + i_2)\), and \(L_1\dot\imath_1 = L_2\dot\imath_2 = v_a\).

Worked solution

Idea. Count stores; subtract one for each independent KVL relation among capacitor voltages and sources alone, and one for each KCL relation among inductor currents and sources alone.

1. Two stores; \(C_1\) and \(C_2\) form a capacitor loop (\(v_1 = v_2\)). Order 1: one capacitor of \(C_1 + C_2\).

2. Two stores; no loop of capacitors alone (the loop contains \(R\)); the node between them is a cut of capacitors, which is not a constraint. Order 2. The charge on that middle node, \(C_2v_2 - C_1v_1\), is conserved, so one natural frequency is \(s = 0\).

3. Three stores; the triangle is one capacitor loop (\(v_{ab} + v_{bc} + v_{ca} = 0\)). Order 2.

4. Two stores; the node joining \(I_s\) and \(L_1\) is a cut made of a current source and an inductor: \(i_1 = I_s\). Order 1: the state is \(i_2\), and \(L_2\dot\imath_2 = R(I_s - i_2)\), with \(\tau = L_2/R\).

5. Two stores; the parallel pair is an inductor loop, not a constraint. Order 2. With \(v_a = V_s - R(i_1 + i_2)\):

\[\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\begin{pmatrix} i_1\\ i_2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -R/L_1 & -R/L_1\\ -R/L_2 & -R/L_2\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} i_1\\ i_2\end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 1/L_1\\ 1/L_2\end{pmatrix}V_s .\]

\(A\) has rank 1, so one eigenvalue is \(0\); the trace gives the other, \(-R(1/L_1 + 1/L_2) = -R/L_\parallel\) with \(L_\parallel = L_1L_2/(L_1 + L_2)\). Its eigenvector: \(A(1, -1)^{\mathsf T} = 0\), the motion \(i_1 = -i_2\) — a current circulating round the inductor loop, which no resistor sees and which persists for ever.

Check. Units: \(R/L\) in \(\mathrm s^{-1}\) ✓, \(V_s/L\) in \(\mathrm A\,\mathrm s^{-1}\) ✓. The nonzero eigenvalue is \(-1/\tau\) for the single inductor \(L_\parallel\) behind \(R\), the answer one would get by first combining the pair ✓.

P4 The coil at speed routine

exercises: the time constant against a deadline, energy as the square of current, a number that says "impossible"

  1. For the coil at 1000, 3000, 4500 and 6000 rpm, find the spark interval, the dwell, the current at opening and the stored energy.
  2. What dwell would store 90% of the coil's 48 mJ? Compare it with the interval between sparks at 6000 rpm.
  3. Up to what engine speed can the 60% dwell store 90% of the energy?

Rung 1

Each answer is a time, a current, an energy or a speed. The energy fraction is the square of the current fraction.

Rung 2

Interval \(= 60/(2N)\) s at \(N\) rpm. For 90% of the energy the current must reach \(\sqrt{0.9}\) of 4 A.

First line of part 2: \(1 - e^{-t/\tau} = \sqrt{0.9} = 0.9487\), so \(t = -\tau\ln(1 - 0.9487)\).

Idea. The dwell shrinks with speed while \(\tau\) stays 2 ms; the stored energy goes as \((1 - e^{-t_d/\tau})^2\).

Ringing, Laplace and stability

P5 The snubber medium

exercises: the characteristic resistance as a design rule, the ringing frequency, critical damping, and why it is the wrong goal

When the coil's contact opens at 4 A, the capacitor across it takes the current. Ignore the battery's 12 V throughout.

  1. Choose \(C\) so that, in the lossless estimate, the contact sees at most 400 V. What happens to the ringing frequency, and to the time at which the peak arrives?
  2. With this \(C\) and the coil's \(R = 3\ \Omega\), what is the actual peak?
  3. Return to \(C = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm F\). A resistor \(R_s\) is put in series with the capacitor, in its branch, to kill the ringing. What \(R_s\) makes the switch-off critically damped?
  4. With that \(R_s\), what voltage does the contact see at the instant it opens? Why does this defeat the purpose?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 are a capacitance, a frequency, a time and a voltage; part 3 a resistance; part 4 a voltage. The lossless peak is \(I\sqrt{L/C}\).

Rung 2

Part 3: the loop is \(L\), \(R\), \(R_s\), \(C\) in series, critically damped when the total resistance is \(2\sqrt{L/C}\). Part 4: the contact is across the series pair \(R_s\), \(C\), and at \(t = 0^+\) the capacitor still has no voltage but the current is still 4 A.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12C(400\ \mathrm V)^2\), so \(C = L(I/400\ \mathrm V)^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. The capacitor limits the voltage by giving the current somewhere to go; anything in series with it that resists the current brings the voltage back.

1. \(C = 0.006\times(4/400)^2 = 6\times10^{-7}\) F \(= 0.6\ \mu\mathrm F\). The frequency \(1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) = 1/(2\pi\times6\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm s) = 2.65\) kHz, down from 4.59 kHz by \(\sqrt3\); the peak arrives a quarter-period after opening, \(\tfrac\pi2\sqrt{LC} = 94.2\ \mu\mathrm s\), later than 54 μs — more time for the points to separate.

2. \(\gamma/2 = R/2L = 250\ \mathrm s^{-1}\), \(\omega_{\mathrm d} = 16\,665\ \mathrm s^{-1}\); \(v_C = \frac{I}{\omega_{\mathrm d}C}e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\omega_{\mathrm d}t\) peaks where \(\tan\omega_{\mathrm d}t = 2\omega_{\mathrm d}/\gamma\), at \(93.4\ \mu\mathrm s\), at \(390.8\) V.

3. \(2\sqrt{L/C} = 2\times173.2 = 346.4\ \Omega\) in total, so \(R_s = 346.4 - 3 = 343.4\ \Omega\).

4. At \(t = 0^+\): \(v_C = 0\) (continuity) and \(i = 4\) A (continuity of the coil's current), so the contact sees \(R_si = 343.4\times4 = 1374\) V at the instant it opens — nearly twice the 693 V peak without \(R_s\), and arriving at once instead of after 54 μs. The capacitor's whole value was that its voltage cannot jump; a series resistor gives the voltage a part that can.

Check. Energy in part 1: \(\tfrac12\times0.6\times10^{-6}\times400^2 = 48\) mJ ✓. Part 2 below part 1's 400 V ✓ (loss), and only by 2.3%, as \(Q = \sqrt{L/C}/R = 100/3 = 33\) suggests. Units: \(\Omega\cdot\mathrm A = \mathrm V\) ✓.

P6 Q and bandwidth against MR 06 routine

exercises: resonance, \(Q\), the half-power frequencies exactly, the dictionary between the circuit and MR 06's oscillator

A coil of \(L = 200\ \mu\mathrm H\) and resistance \(R = 10\ \Omega\) is in series with a capacitor, tuned to \(f_0 = 1\) MHz.

  1. Find \(C\), \(Q\) and the half-power bandwidth in hertz.
  2. Find the two half-power frequencies exactly, and check that their product is \(f_0^2\).
  3. Identify MR 06's \(\gamma\) and check that the half-power width is exactly \(\gamma\). How many cycles does the free ringing take to lose a factor \(e\) of its energy?
  4. Driven at resonance by 10 mV (RMS), what is the voltage across the capacitor?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A capacitance, a pure number, frequencies, a count of cycles and a voltage. The circuit's equation divided by \(L\) is MR 06's.

Rung 2

With \(x = \omega/\omega_0\), half power is \(Q(x - 1/x) = \pm1\). Energy decays as \(e^{-\gamma t}\).

Rung 3

First line: \(C = 1/((2\pi f_0)^2L)\).

Worked solution

Idea. \(L\ddot q + R\dot q + q/C = v\) is MR 06's oscillator with \(\gamma = R/L\); every result there transfers.

1. \(C = 1/((6.2832\times10^6)^2\times2\times10^{-4}) = 126.7\) pF. \(Q = \omega_0L/R = 1256.6/10 = 125.7\). Bandwidth \(\Delta\omega = R/L = 5\times10^4\ \mathrm s^{-1}\), \(\Delta f = 7.96\) kHz.

2. \(x_\pm = \pm\frac1{2Q} + \sqrt{1 + \frac1{4Q^2}}\): \(f_- = 996.03\) kHz, \(f_+ = 1003.99\) kHz. Product: \(x_+x_- = (1 + \frac1{4Q^2}) - \frac1{4Q^2} = 1\) exactly, so \(f_+f_- = f_0^2\); numerically \(996.029\times1003.987 = 1.000\times10^6\ \mathrm{kHz^2}\) ✓. Difference 7.96 kHz ✓.

3. \(\gamma = R/L = 5\times10^4\ \mathrm s^{-1}\), which is the half-power width \(\Delta\omega\) — MR 06's "a width that is exactly \(\gamma\)". The energy \(e\)-folds in \(1/\gamma = 20\ \mu\mathrm s\), which at 1 MHz is 20.0 cycles, \(= Q/2\pi\).

4. \(I = 10\ \mathrm{mV}/10\ \Omega = 1\) mA; \(|V_C| = I/(\omega_0C) = \omega_0LI = 1.257\) V, which is \(Q\) times the drive.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{s^2/H} = \mathrm F\) ✓, \(\Omega/\mathrm H = \mathrm s^{-1}\) ✓. Two routes to the bandwidth, \(R/L\) and \(\omega_0/Q\), agree ✓. Limit \(R\to0\): \(Q\to\infty\), zero width, infinite \(V_C\) — the undamped resonance MR 06 found has no steady state.

P7 Laplace with a charged capacitor medium

exercises: initial conditions as s-domain sources, inversion by residues, the energy balance as \(t\to\infty\)

A capacitor \(C = 1\ \mu\mathrm F\) charged to \(V_0 = 100\) V is switched at \(t = 0\) across a series \(R = 20\ \Omega\), \(L = 1\) mH, which carries no current before. Let \(i\) be the current leaving the capacitor's + plate.

  1. Draw the s-domain circuit and find \(I(s)\).
  2. Invert it by residues. Find the time and size of the first current peak.
  3. Prove that the energy dissipated in \(R\) over \(0\le t < \infty\) is exactly \(\tfrac12CV_0^2\), and give it in millijoules.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

\(I(s)\) is a rational function with a quadratic denominator; \(i(t)\) a decaying sinusoid. Is it underdamped? Compare \(R\) with \(2\sqrt{L/C}\).

Rung 2

The charged capacitor is an uncharged one in series with a source \(V_0/s\). The inductor, empty, is just \(sL\). \(\int_0^\infty e^{-2\alpha t}\sin^2\omega_{\mathrm d}t\,\mathrm dt = \frac{\omega_{\mathrm d}^2}{4\alpha(\alpha^2 + \omega_{\mathrm d}^2)}\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\frac{V_0}s = \big(R + sL + \frac1{sC}\big)I\).

Worked solution

Idea. The initial charge is a step source; the loop is a series RLC from rest.

1. KVL round the loop: \(V_0/s = (R + sL + 1/(sC))I\), so \(I = \dfrac{V_0C}{LCs^2 + RCs + 1} = \dfrac{V_0/L}{s^2 + (R/L)s + 1/(LC)}\).

2. \(R/2L = \alpha = 10^4\ \mathrm s^{-1}\), \(1/\sqrt{LC} = 31\,623\ \mathrm s^{-1}\), \(\omega_{\mathrm d} = \sqrt{10^9 - 10^8} = 3\times10^4\ \mathrm s^{-1}\) (\(R = 20 < 2\sqrt{L/C} = 63.2\ \Omega\): underdamped). Poles \(p_\pm = -\alpha\pm i\omega_{\mathrm d}\), residues of \(Ie^{st}\): \(\pm\frac{V_0}{L}\frac{e^{p_\pm t}}{2i\omega_{\mathrm d}}\). Sum: \(i(t) = \dfrac{V_0}{\omega_{\mathrm d}L}e^{-\alpha t}\sin\omega_{\mathrm d}t = 3.333\,e^{-10^4t}\sin(3\times10^4t)\) A. Peak where \(\tan\omega_{\mathrm d}t = \omega_{\mathrm d}/\alpha = 3\): \(t = 41.6\ \mu\mathrm s\), \(i = 2.085\) A.

3. \(\int_0^\infty Ri^2\,\mathrm dt = R\frac{V_0^2}{\omega_{\mathrm d}^2L^2}\cdot\frac{\omega_{\mathrm d}^2}{4\alpha(\alpha^2 + \omega_{\mathrm d}^2)}\). With \(\alpha = R/2L\) and \(\alpha^2 + \omega_{\mathrm d}^2 = 1/(LC)\) this is \(\frac{RV_0^2}{L^2}\cdot\frac{2L\cdot LC}{4R} = \tfrac12CV_0^2 = 5\) mJ.

Check. Initial value: \(sI\to0\) as \(s\to\infty\), so \(i(0^+) = 0\), as the empty inductor demands ✓; and \(\mathrm di/\mathrm dt(0^+) = V_0/L = 10^5\) A/s, from \(s^2I\to V_0/L\) ✓. As \(t\to\infty\), \(i\to0\) and \(v_C\to0\): all the stored energy has gone to \(R\) ✓. Units: \(\mathrm V/(\mathrm s^{-1}\mathrm H) = \mathrm A\) ✓.

P8 A negative resistance medium

exercises: stability as poles on the left, an energy argument for the same threshold, what positive-real excludes

Define a negative resistance as a two-terminal element with law \(v = -ri\), \(r > 0\), under the passive convention. Put one in series with the tuned circuit of P6 (\(L = 200\ \mu\mathrm H\), \(C = 126.7\) pF, \(R = 10\ \Omega\)).

  1. Where are the natural frequencies, as functions of \(r\)? For which \(r\) is the circuit stable?
  2. Prove the same threshold from the stored energy \(W = \tfrac12Li^2 + \tfrac12Cv_C^2\).
  3. At the threshold, what does the circuit do? At \(r = 12\ \Omega\), how fast does a small oscillation grow?
  4. Why can no network of positive R, L and C behave like this element, and what must a real one contain?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The loop is a series RLC with resistance \(R - r\). The answers are a threshold resistance, a frequency and a growth rate.

Rung 2

\(\dot W = -(R - r)i^2\) along any free motion. The element absorbs \(vi = -ri^2\le0\).

Rung 3

First line: \(Ls^2 + (R - r)s + 1/C = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. A negative resistance subtracts damping; when it outweighs \(R\), the poles cross the imaginary axis.

1. \(s = -\frac{R - r}{2L}\pm\sqrt{\frac{(R - r)^2}{4L^2} - \frac1{LC}}\). Both have negative real part iff \(R - r > 0\): stable for \(r < 10\ \Omega\).

2. \(\dot W = Li\dot\imath + Cv_C\dot v_C = i(L\dot\imath + v_C) = i\cdot(-(R - r)i) = -(R - r)i^2\) by KVL with no source. For \(r < R\), \(W\) decreases whenever \(i\ne0\), and cannot stay constant on a nonzero free motion (if \(i\equiv0\) then \(v_C\equiv0\)); for \(r > R\) it increases. Now compare with the modes. A free motion \(\operatorname{Re}(ce^{pt})\) with \(\operatorname{Re}p\ge0\) returns, one period later, with every state variable multiplied by \(e^{\operatorname{Re}p\,T}\ge1\), so \(W\) cannot have fallen over that period; for \(r < R\) it must have, so every natural frequency has \(\operatorname{Re}p < 0\). (A real \(p\ge0\), with no period, is excluded the same way: \(W\) would be non-decreasing.) For \(r > R\) the same argument with the inequality reversed excludes \(\operatorname{Re}p\le0\). Threshold \(r = R\).

3. At \(r = R\): poles at \(\pm i\omega_0\), a sustained oscillation at 1 MHz whose energy is constant — an oscillator. At \(r = 12\ \Omega\): \(\operatorname{Re}s = (r - R)/2L = 2/(4\times10^{-4}) = 5000\ \mathrm s^{-1}\), amplitude doubling every \(\ln2/5000 = 139\ \mu\mathrm s\).

4. Section 5: any network of positive R, L, C has a positive-real impedance, but \(Z = -r\) has \(\operatorname{Re}Z < 0\). The element delivers power \(ri^2 > 0\), so it must contain an energy source — in practice an amplifier with a power supply.

Check. Units: \(\Omega/\mathrm H = \mathrm s^{-1}\) ✓. Limit \(r\to0\): back to the passive circuit's \(-R/2L = -25\,000\ \mathrm s^{-1}\) ✓. The energy and pole arguments give the same threshold ✓.

P9 Is it positive-real? medium

exercises: the definition, a witness for "no", a proof or a network for "yes", poles on the axis

Decide whether each is positive-real. For "no", give a witness \(s\) with \(\operatorname{Re}s > 0\) and \(\operatorname{Re}Z(s) < 0\); for "yes", give a proof or a network of positive R, L, C.

\[\text{(a)}\ \frac{s + 2}{s + 1},\qquad\text{(b)}\ \frac{s - 1}{s + 1},\qquad\text{(c)}\ \frac s{s^2 + 1},\qquad\text{(d)}\ \frac{s^2 + s + 1}{s^2 + 1}.\]

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each answer is yes or no with a reason. Long division or partial fractions turns each into a sum of things you recognise as elements.

Rung 2

\(\operatorname{Re}\frac1{s + a} > 0\) for \(\operatorname{Re}s > -a\). A zero in the right half-plane is a pole of \(1/Z\) there.

Rung 3

First line of (d): \(\frac{s^2 + s + 1}{s^2 + 1} = 1 + \frac s{s^2 + 1}\).

Worked solution

Idea. A "no" needs one point; a "yes" is quickest as a network, since every RLC network is positive-real.

(a) Yes. \(1 + \frac1{s + 1}\), and \(\operatorname{Re}\frac1{s + 1} = \frac{\operatorname{Re}s + 1}{|s + 1|^2} > 0\): \(\operatorname{Re}Z > 1\). Network: 1 Ω in series with (1 Ω ∥ 1 F).

(b) No. At \(s = \tfrac12\): \(-\tfrac12/\tfrac32 = -\tfrac13\). Its zero at \(s = 1\) makes \(1/Z\) have a right-half-plane pole.

(c) Yes. \(1/Z = s + 1/s\): a 1 F capacitor in parallel with a 1 H inductor. Poles \(\pm i\), residues \(\tfrac12\), real and positive.

(d) Yes. \(1 + \frac s{s^2 + 1}\): 1 Ω in series with the tank of (c). Its poles are on the axis at \(\pm i\), with residue \(\frac{i^2 + i + 1}{2i} = \frac i{2i} = \tfrac12\) at \(i\): simple and positive, as required. On the axis \(\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega) = 1\) except at \(\omega = \pm1\).

Check. (b) on the axis: \(Z(0) = -1\), already negative, consistent with the witness ✓. (a) at \(s\to\infty\): \(Z\to1\) ✓ (the capacitor shorts the parallel resistor); at \(s = 0\): 2 ✓ (both resistors).

P10 Proof: every RLC impedance is positive-real hard

exercises: Tellegen's theorem with a complex conjugate — the checkpoint's second proof — and its check on the axis

  1. Prove that the driving-point impedance of any network of positive resistors, inductors and capacitors is positive-real. Say exactly why the conjugated currents \(\bar I_e\) still obey Kirchhoff's current law, and where \(\operatorname{Re}s > 0\) is used.
  2. Show that for \(\operatorname{Re}s > 0\) the network's equations have a unique solution, so that \(Z(s)\) is defined there.
  3. Extend the proof to a network containing a pair of coupled inductors with inductance matrix \(\mathcal L\), \(M^2\le L_1L_2\).
  4. Check on the deck's worked example 5 network (\(R_1 = 1\ \Omega\), \(L = 1\) H, \(R_2 = 2\ \Omega\), \(C = 0.5\) F) at \(s = i\ \mathrm s^{-1}\): compute \(Z\) and verify \(\operatorname{Re}Z = \sum R|I_e|^2/|I|^2\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is an identity \(Z|I|^2 = \sum_e z_e|I_e|^2\) followed by a sign check term by term. Part 4 is a complex number and a sum of two real terms.

Rung 2

Unit 03's proof of Tellegen, \(v^{\mathsf T}i = \varphi^{\mathsf T}Ai\), never used that the vectors were real. \(A\) is real, so \(A\bar{\mathbf I} = \overline{A\mathbf I}\). For part 2, apply the identity with the source set to zero.

Rung 3

First line: include the port as an edge carrying \(-I\); then \(\sum_{\text{all }e}V_e\bar I_e = \Phi^{\mathsf T}A\bar{\mathbf I} = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. Tellegen pairs the voltages with any currents that obey KCL; the conjugate currents do, and the pairing turns \(z_eI_e\bar I_e\) into \(z_e|I_e|^2\), whose real part has a sign.

1. Drive the port with a current source \(I\) at complex frequency \(s\) and solve the s-domain network with empty stores; every edge obeys \(V_e = z_eI_e\) with \(z_e\in\{R, sL, 1/(sC)\}\). The port is an edge from one terminal to the other with current \(-I\) and voltage \(V = ZI\). KVL: \(V = A^{\mathsf T}\Phi\). KCL: \(A\mathbf I = 0\). Because every entry of \(A\) is \(0\) or \(\pm1\), \(A\bar{\mathbf I} = \overline{A\mathbf I} = 0\): the conjugated currents obey KCL — this is the step a complex incidence matrix would break. Unit 03's proof is algebra over any field: \(\sum_eV_e\bar I_e = (A^{\mathsf T}\Phi)^{\mathsf T}\bar{\mathbf I} = \Phi^{\mathsf T}A\bar{\mathbf I} = 0\). The port term is \(ZI\cdot(-\bar I) = -Z|I|^2\), so

\[Z|I|^2 = \sum_RR|I_e|^2 + s\sum_LL|I_e|^2 + \frac1s\sum_C\frac{|I_e|^2}C .\]

For \(\operatorname{Re}s > 0\): \(\operatorname{Re}(sL) = L\operatorname{Re}s > 0\) and \(\operatorname{Re}\frac1{sC} = \frac{\operatorname{Re}s}{|s|^2C} > 0\) — here the half-plane is used. So \(\operatorname{Re}Z\ge0\). \(Z\) is rational with real coefficients (Cramer's rule on the nodal equations). \(\square\)

2. Ground a node: unknowns are \(n - 1\) potentials and \(m\) currents; equations are \(n - 1\) KCL and \(m\) element laws — square. With \(I = 0\), the identity gives \(0 = \sum R|I_e|^2 + \operatorname{Re}s\sum L|I_e|^2 + \frac{\operatorname{Re}s}{|s|^2}\sum|I_e|^2/C\) in real part, so every \(I_e = 0\), every \(V_e = 0\), and then every \(\Phi = 0\) (on a connected network, with the ground fixed). The homogeneous system has only the trivial solution, so the square system is invertible.

3. The coupled pair contributes \(\bar{\mathbf I}_L^{\mathsf T}(s\mathcal L)\mathbf I_L = s\,\bar{\mathbf I}_L^{\mathsf T}\mathcal L\mathbf I_L\), and \(\bar{\mathbf x}^{\mathsf T}\mathcal L\mathbf x = (\operatorname{Re}\mathbf x)^{\mathsf T}\mathcal L(\operatorname{Re}\mathbf x) + (\operatorname{Im}\mathbf x)^{\mathsf T}\mathcal L(\operatorname{Im}\mathbf x)\ge0\) for a real symmetric positive semi-definite \(\mathcal L\) (unit 02). So the term is \(s\) times a non-negative number, real part \(\ge0\).

4. \(sL = i\), \(1/(sC) = -2i\), right branch \(2 - 2i\). Parallel: \(\frac{i(2 - 2i)}{2 - i} = \frac{2 + 2i}{2 - i} = 0.4 + 1.2i\); \(Z = 1.4 + 1.2i\ \Omega\). With \(I = 1\) A: \(I_2 = \frac{0.4 + 1.2i}{2 - 2i} = -0.2 + 0.4i\), \(|I_2|^2 = 0.2\); \(\sum R|I_e|^2 = 1\times1 + 2\times0.2 = 1.4 = \operatorname{Re}Z\) ✓. \(I_L = \frac{0.4 + 1.2i}{i} = 1.2 - 0.4i\), \(|I_L|^2 = 1.6\), and the imaginary part \(1\times1.6 - 0.2/0.5 = 1.2\) ✓.

Check. Units: \(\Omega\cdot\mathrm A^2\) in every term ✓. On the axis the identity says \(\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega)|I|^2\) is the mean power in the resistors, which cannot be negative ✓. Without the conjugate the terms would be \(z_eI_e^2\), whose real parts have no sign: that is why the proof needs it.

P11 Proof: Foster's reactance theorem hard

exercises: Tellegen without the conjugate, differentiating the element laws, the alternation of poles and zeros

Let a network of inductors and capacitors only be driven at its port by a real current \(I\) at real frequency \(\omega\), where its reactance \(X(\omega)\) is finite.

  1. Show that every branch current \(I_e\) is real.
  2. Prove \(\dfrac{\mathrm dX}{\mathrm d\omega} = \dfrac{\sum_LLI_e^2 + \sum_CI_e^2/(\omega^2C)}{I^2} > 0\).
  3. Deduce that the poles and zeros of \(X\) on \(\omega > 0\) are simple and alternate.
  4. Check part 2 directly on the lossless coil, \(L\) in parallel with \(C\): \(X = \omega L/(1 - \omega^2LC)\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 2 is an identity from two applications of Tellegen's theorem, at the same frequency, with no conjugates: one with \(V'\) (the \(\omega\)-derivative), one with \(\mathbf I'\).

Rung 2

Fix \(I\) independent of \(\omega\); then \(\mathbf I'\) obeys KCL with zero port current. Differentiate \(V_e = i\omega LI_e\) and \(V_e = I_e/(i\omega C)\); each derivative contains a term \(z_eI'_e\), and those terms sum to \(\sum V_eI'_e = 0\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\sum_eV'_eI_e = 0\), whose port term is \((iXI)'\cdot(-I) = -iX'I^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. The derivative of the reactance is the stored energy, and energy is positive.

1. Nodal analysis: every admittance is \(-i/(\omega L)\) or \(i\omega C\), so the nodal matrix is \(i\mathcal B\) with \(\mathcal B\) real. With a real injection \(J\), and \(\mathcal B\) invertible (the network's equations solvable at this \(\omega\)), \(\Phi = -i\mathcal B^{-1}J\) is purely imaginary, and \(I_e = y_eV_e\) is imaginary times imaginary: real.

2. Tellegen with \(V'\) and \(\mathbf I\) (\(V'\in\operatorname{im}A^{\mathsf T}\), \(\mathbf I\in\ker A\)): \(iX'I^2 = \sum_{\text{elements}}V'_eI_e\). Tellegen with \(V\) and \(\mathbf I'\) (\(\mathbf I'\in\ker A\), port entry 0): \(\sum_{\text{elements}}V_eI'_e = 0\). Differentiate: inductor \(V'_e = iLI_e + i\omega LI'_e\); capacitor \(V'_e = iI_e/(\omega^2C) + I'_e/(i\omega C)\). So \(\sum V'_eI_e = i\big(\sum_LLI_e^2 + \sum_CI_e^2/(\omega^2C)\big) + \sum V_eI'_e\), and the last sum vanishes. Dividing by \(iI^2\) gives the formula. Each term is \(\ge0\) since the \(I_e\) are real, and not all vanish since the port current enters some element: \(X' > 0\).

3. At a simple pole on the axis, positive residue \(k\) (section 5) gives \(X\approx-k/(\omega - \omega_i)\): \(+\infty\) below, \(-\infty\) above. Between consecutive poles \(X\) is continuous and increasing from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\): exactly one zero, simple because \(X' > 0\). Two zeros with no pole between would need \(X\) to decrease somewhere. So they alternate.

4. \(X' = \frac{L(1 - \omega^2LC) + 2\omega^2L^2C}{(1 - \omega^2LC)^2} = \frac{L(1 + \omega^2LC)}{(1 - \omega^2LC)^2}\). From the formula with \(I = 1\): \(I_L = 1/(1 - \omega^2LC)\) and \(I_C = -\omega^2LC/(1 - \omega^2LC)\), both real; \(LI_L^2 + I_C^2/(\omega^2C) = \frac{L + \omega^2L^2C}{(1 - \omega^2LC)^2}\) ✓.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm H\cdot\mathrm A^2/\mathrm A^2 = \Omega\,\mathrm s\), as \(\mathrm dX/\mathrm d\omega\) is ✓. In energy terms the numerator is \(2(\bar W_L + \bar W_C)\) with RMS currents. The coil's \(X\) has a zero at 0, a pole at \(\omega_0\), a zero at \(\infty\): alternating ✓.

\(Z(s) = \dfrac{s^2 + 1}{s(s^2 + 4)}\) (values in ohms, henries, farads and \(\mathrm s^{-1}\), normalised).

  1. Check that its critical frequencies alternate on the \(\omega\) axis.
  2. Realise it as a series chain: a capacitor and parallel LC tanks (the first Foster form).
  3. Realise it as a parallel combination from the partial fractions of \(Y = 1/Z\) (the second Foster form).
  4. Check both by recomputing \(Z\) at \(s = 1\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each realisation has three elements. A term \(k_0/s\) is a capacitor \(1/k_0\) in an impedance and a term \(k_\infty s\) is a capacitor \(k_\infty\) in an admittance.

Rung 2

Rung 3

First line: \(Z - \frac1{4s} = \frac{3s}{4(s^2 + 4)}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Peel off the pole at 0 and pair the conjugate poles; each term is an element or a tank.

2. \(k_0 = 1/4\); \(Z - \frac1{4s} = \frac{4s^2 + 4 - s^2 - 4}{4s(s^2 + 4)} = \frac{(3/4)s}{s^2 + 4}\). So a 4 F capacitor in series with a tank with \(1/C_1 = 3/4\), \(1/(L_1C_1) = 4\): \(C_1 = 4/3\) F, \(L_1 = 3/16\) H.

4. \(Z(1) = 2/5 = 0.4\). First form: \(\frac14 + \frac{3/4}{5} = 0.25 + 0.15 = 0.4\) ✓. Second: \(Y(1) = 1 + \frac32 = 2.5\), \(Z = 0.4\) ✓.

Check. All element values positive, as Foster's form guarantees ✓. The tank resonates at \(1/\sqrt{L_1C_1} = 2\): \(Z\) has its pole there ✓; the series branch resonates at \(1/\sqrt{LC} = 1\), shorting the port: \(Z\) has its zero there ✓.
