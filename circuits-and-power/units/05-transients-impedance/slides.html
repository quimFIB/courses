Circuits that remember: transients, phasors, and impedance as a positive-real function

Switched, a network with memory relaxes or rings; driven at one frequency, it is unit 04's network with complex weights, and its impedance is positive-real.

Units 03 and 04 solved networks with no memory: every voltage and current at an instant was fixed by the sources at that instant. Unit 02 gave the capacitor and the inductor their element laws, $i = C\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt$ and $v = L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$, and stopped. This unit solves networks that contain them. First in the time domain: a linear system of ODEs whose state is the energy stores, relaxing with time constants (first order) or ringing (second order). Then with the Laplace transform, which turns the ODEs into algebra with the initial conditions as sources. Last, what passivity means for an impedance: it is a positive-real function — the unit's central theorem, proved from unit 03's Tellegen theorem — and for a lossless network, Foster's reactance theorem.

05 · overview

How twelve volts makes a spark

the running example: the ignition coil

A petrol engine fires each cylinder with a spark across a plug's gap, which takes far more than the car's 12 V. The ignition coil bridges the two: a contact lets current build up in the coil's primary winding, then opens. The values are assumed for the example: $L = 6$ mH, $R = 3\ \Omega$, $C = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm F$, 12 V.

The questions. How long must the contact stay closed, and what happens at high revs? When it opens, what voltage appears across it, and what is the capacitor for?

The plan. 1 memory, and the first-order circuit · 2 the series RLC · 3 the Laplace transform · 4 phasors and impedance · 5 positive-real · 6 lossless networks.

A contact with a dotted underline is a glossary term (click, or G); a solid one like the coil is the running example (hover); N shows the notes.

The primary circuit. The coil is drawn as its resistance and inductance in series; its secondary winding, which drives the spark plug, is left open here; unit 07 puts it back. Marked by the passive convention; $i$ is the coil current.

The system, from nothing. A petrol engine burns its fuel–air mixture in each cylinder at a chosen moment in the cycle, lit by an electric spark that jumps a small gap between two electrodes of a spark plug. Making a spark jump a gap in a compressed gas takes a voltage far above the 12 V a car's electrical system supplies — how far is not claimed here, since no source for it was checked; unit 07 finds what voltage the coil can raise. The conventional coil ignition bridges this with an ignition coil: two windings on a common iron core, a primary of few turns connected to the battery, and a secondary of many turns connected to the plug. Current is allowed to build up in the primary; then it is interrupted, and the collapse of the core's magnetic flux induces a high voltage in the secondary. The interruption is made by a contact — the contact breaker, a pair of points opened by a cam in older systems; a transistor in later ones — with a capacitor, the "condenser", in the circuit with it. Denton (3rd ed., §8.1.6, p. 172) describes the contact breaker as switching "the primary ignition circuit on and off to charge and discharge the coil", and the condenser as suppressing "most of the arcing as the contact breakers open". This deck draws the capacitor directly across the contact, the place where it can take the coil's current as the contact opens; section 2 computes what that does. (The Bosch Automotive Handbook's section on conventional coil ignition, named in the title slide's notes, was not checked for this deck, and nothing here rests on it.)

What this unit does with it. It models only the primary circuit, with the secondary left open: a battery, the primary's inductance $L$ and resistance $R$ (the winding plus any series resistor), the contact, and the capacitor. All four values are assumptions for the example: they are of a size that makes the arithmetic clean and the physics typical, and they are not the data of any coil. The battery is taken as an ideal 12 V source; unit 04's car battery had an internal resistance of $10\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$, which is $0.3\%$ of $R = 3\ \Omega$ and is neglected. Unit 07 puts the secondary back and finds the voltage the secondary can reach, from the turns ratio and the secondary's own capacitance; the spark itself is modelled in neither unit. This unit finds what happens in the primary, which is where the energy is stored and where the switching is done.

Why "remember". Close the contact, and the current does not jump to $12/3 = 4$ A: it rises, with a time constant. Open it, and the current does not stop: an inductor's current is continuous while its voltage is finite (unit 02). In both cases what the circuit does next depends on what it was doing — on its state.

section 1

Memory, and the first-order circuit

A network with capacitors and inductors is a linear system of ODEs. Its state is the energy stores; switched, a single store relaxes with one time constant — and charging a capacitor from a fixed voltage loses exactly half the energy, whatever the resistance.

05 · memory

Kirchhoff's laws with derivatives

Unit 03's laws do not care what the edges are: $Ai = s$ and $v = A^{\mathsf T}\varphi$ hold at every instant. Unit 02's element laws now include derivatives:

$$v_e = R_ei_e,\qquad i_e = C_e\frac{\mathrm dv_e}{\mathrm dt},\qquad v_e = L_e\frac{\mathrm di_e}{\mathrm dt}.$$

Together: a linear system of ODEs with constant coefficients, plus algebraic equations.

the state of a network

The capacitor voltages and the inductor currents. They are continuous whenever the other variable is finite (unit 02); the stored energy $\sum\tfrac12C_ev_e^2 + \sum\tfrac12L_ei_e^2$ is a function of them alone; and with the sources from $t_0$ on, their values at $t_0$ fix every voltage and current after $t_0$.

Units: $C\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt$ is $\mathrm F\cdot\mathrm V\,\mathrm s^{-1} = \mathrm{C\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm A$ ✓; $L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$ is $\mathrm{H\cdot A\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{Wb\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm V$ ✓.

Three energy stores, three state variables: the two capacitor voltages and the inductor current. Everything else — the resistor currents, the node potentials — follows from them and the source at the same instant.

Why the state is the stores. In the figure, knowing $v_1$, $i_L$, $v_2$ and $V_s$ at an instant fixes everything at that instant: the current in $R_1$ is $(V_s - v_1)/R_1$, the current in $R_2$ is $v_2/R_2$, and the capacitor currents follow by KCL — $C_1\dot v_1 = (V_s - v_1)/R_1 - i_L$ and $C_2\dot v_2 = i_L - v_2/R_2$ — while the inductor's voltage follows by KVL, $L\,\dot\imath_L = v_1 - v_2$. These three equations are of the form $\dot x = Ax + Bu$ with $x = (v_1, i_L, v_2)$ and $u = V_s$: a linear first-order system with constant coefficients, so by MR 01's existence and uniqueness theorem its solution from any initial $x(t_0)$ exists and is unique for all $t$ (the right-hand side is globally Lipschitz). That is the third clause of the definition, for this network.

Cited, for the general case. That networks of R, L, C and independent sources have state equations of this form, with the capacitor voltages and inductor currents (less the dependent ones counted on the next slide) as state, is the subject of C. A. Desoer and E. S. Kuh, Basic Circuit Theory (McGraw-Hill, 1969), ch. 12, "State Equations". The usual construction chooses a spanning tree containing every capacitor and no inductor, when one exists, and writes KCL on its fundamental cuts and KVL on its fundamental cycles — unit 03's bases. This deck uses only the cases it writes out.

Continuity, which the switching uses. Unit 02: $v_C(t) = v_C(t_0) + \tfrac1C\int_{t_0}^ti\,\mathrm dt'$ is continuous whenever $i$ is integrable, and likewise $i_L$ whenever $v$ is. So at the instant a switch moves, the state carries over: $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$, $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$. The other quantities may jump. This is what "the circuit remembers" means, exactly.

The order: counting energy stores

the order of a network

The number of independent state variables. It equals the number of capacitors and inductors, less one for each independent capacitor loop (a cycle made only of capacitors and voltage sources) and each independent inductor cut (a cut made only of inductors and current sources). "At most" is proved below; equality is not proved (notes).

proof of "at most"

Round a capacitor loop, KVL gives $\sum\pm v_{C} = \sum\pm v_{\text{source}}(t)$: one capacitor voltage is fixed by the others and the sources. Across an inductor cut, KCL gives $\sum\pm i_L = \sum\pm i_{\text{source}}(t)$. Each independent constraint removes one free initial value. $\square$

The coil, contact closed: the capacitor is in a loop with the closed contact, a 0 V source — order 1, the current $i$. Contact open: order 2, $(i, v_C)$. The switch changes the order.

The three examples. Left: two capacitors in parallel are a cycle of capacitors, so KVL forces $v_1 = v_2$ at every instant; the pair is one store of capacitance $C_1 + C_2$, order 1. Middle: three inductors meet at one node, and the cut around that node meets only them, so KCL forces $i_1 + i_2 + i_3 = 0$; three inductors, order 2. Right: the series RLC has one capacitor and one inductor and no such loop or cut: order 2, which section 2 solves.

What is not a constraint. A loop of inductors, or a cut of capacitors, is not one. Two inductors in parallel obey $L_1\dot\imath_1 = L_2\dot\imath_2$ — a relation between derivatives, which leaves both initial currents free (a current can circulate round the inductor loop for ever, since nothing in it dissipates). Problem P3 counts both kinds.

"Independent". Capacitor loops are elements of unit 03's cycle space restricted to capacitor and voltage-source edges; the count is the dimension of the space they span, not the number of loops one can draw. Likewise for cuts.

Not proved here: equality. That no other constraint appears, so that the count is exact, is stated and not proved. The construction that settles it is the general one for state equations — Desoer and Kuh, Basic Circuit Theory, ch. 12, is this deck's source for that construction, not for the count in this form. Its idea, sketched only: choose a spanning tree holding every voltage source, as many capacitors as possible, as few inductors as possible and no current source. Tree-branch voltages can be assigned freely and fix every potential, and link currents can be assigned freely and fix every current (unit 03's bases), so the tree capacitors' voltages and the link inductors' currents are independent; their number is the count above. What the sketch leaves out is the proof that they then fix everything else, which is the construction itself. A closed ideal switch is a 0 V source, an open one a 0 A source; a switch therefore changes the graph, and the order can change with it, as the coil shows.

05 · first order

One store, one time constant

One capacitor or inductor, with resistors and constant sources: by Thévenin (unit 04) the rest is $V_{\text{th}}$ behind $R_{\text{th}}$, so

$$R_{\text{th}}C\,\dot v_C + v_C = V_{\text{th}}\quad\text{or}$$ $$\frac{L}{R_{\text{th}}}\,\dot\imath_L + i_L = \frac{V_{\text{th}}}{R_{\text{th}}} .$$
the time constant

Every voltage and current moves from its initial to its final value as

$$\boxed{\,x(t) = x(\infty) + \big(x(0^+) - x(\infty)\big)e^{-t/\tau}\,}$$

with $\tau = RC$ or $L/R$: MR 00's solution of $\tau\dot x + x = x(\infty)$, unique by MR 01.

Units: $\Omega\cdot\mathrm F = \mathrm{V\,A^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{C\,V^{-1}} = \mathrm s$ ✓; $\mathrm H/\Omega = \mathrm{V\,s\,A^{-1}}/\mathrm{V\,A^{-1}} = \mathrm s$ ✓.

After one $\tau$ a rising quantity has covered $1 - e^{-1} = 63.2\%$ of the way; after $5\tau$, $1 - e^{-5} = 99.3\%$. The falling one is the remainder.

The solution, in one line. $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big[(x - x_\infty)e^{t/\tau}\big] = e^{t/\tau}(\dot x + (x - x_\infty)/\tau) = 0$, so $(x - x_\infty)e^{t/\tau}$ is constant: the integrating factor of MR 00. Every other voltage and current in such a circuit is an affine function of the one state variable and the constant sources, so it moves with the same exponential — which is why the box says "every".

The rules of thumb, computed. $1 - e^{-1} = 0.6321$, $1 - e^{-3} = 0.9502$, $1 - e^{-4} = 0.9817$, $1 - e^{-5} = 0.9933$. "Five time constants and it is done" means 99.3%, to within 0.7% of the final value — a convention, not a theorem: the exponential never arrives.

The tangent. $\dot x(0^+) = (x_\infty - x(0^+))/\tau$: if the initial rate were kept up, the quantity would arrive in exactly one $\tau$. This is how a time constant is read off a measured curve.

Thévenin is legitimate here. Unit 04 proved it for networks of resistors and sources; the capacitor or inductor is the load at the port, and the theorem says nothing about what the load is. With one store, the rest of the network is resistive, so the reduction holds at every instant.

Limits. $R\to0$ in an RC circuit: $\tau\to0$ and the capacitor charges instantly — the singular limit two slides on. $R\to\infty$ in an RL circuit: $\tau = L/R\to0$, the inductor's current is forced to change instantly — the singular limit of opening a contact, which the next slides meet.

05 · first order · example

Worked: the coil switched on

worked example 1 — the coil switched on: time constant, current, energy

1. The circuit. Contact closed at $t = 0$, the capacitor shorted: one loop, order 1. KVL: $12 = 3i + 0.006\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$.

2. Initial and final. $i(0^+) = i(0^-) = 0$ (continuity). Finally $\mathrm di/\mathrm dt = 0$: $i(\infty) = 12/3 = 4$ A.

3. Time constant. $\tau = L/R = 0.006/3 = 2$ ms, so $i(t) = 4\,(1 - e^{-t/2\,\mathrm{ms}})$ A.

4. Energy. Full: $\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12\times0.006\times4^2 = 48$ mJ. After time $t$ it is $(1 - e^{-t/\tau})^2$ of that.

5. Numbers. After $1, 1.5, 2, 3\,\tau$: current $0.632, 0.777, 0.865, 0.950$ of 4 A; energy $0.400, 0.604, 0.748, 0.903$ of 48 mJ.

checks

Units: $\mathrm{H\cdot A^2} = \mathrm{V\,s\,A^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm A^2 = \mathrm J$ ✓. Energy: at the end the battery's 48 W all heats $R$, $3\times4^2 = 48$ W ✓; on the way, $12i = 3i^2 + \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\tfrac12Li^2$ term by term.

Step 1, the order. With the contact closed the capacitor has 0 V across it permanently: its voltage is not free. The circuit is battery, $R$, $L$, contact — one loop, one store, $i$ the state.

Step 3, as an instance of the box. $x = i$, $x(0^+) = 0$, $x(\infty) = 4$ A, $\tau = L/R$; the boxed formula gives $i = 4 + (0 - 4)e^{-t/\tau}$.

Step 5, the arithmetic. $1 - e^{-1} = 0.6321$, $1 - e^{-1.5} = 0.7769$, $1 - e^{-2} = 0.8647$, $1 - e^{-3} = 0.9502$; squared, $0.3996$, $0.6035$, $0.7476$, $0.9029$; times 48 mJ, 19.2, 29.0, 35.9 and 43.3 mJ. The energy fraction is the square of the current fraction, so it lags: 95% of the current is only 90% of the energy.

The energy balance, integrated. Multiply KVL by $i$: $12i = 3i^2 + Li\,\dot\imath$. The battery's power splits into heat in $R$ and the rate of storing $\tfrac12Li^2$. Integrated from $0$ to $\infty$ the battery's energy and the heat both diverge (48 W for ever); their difference converges to 48 mJ, the stored energy. Unit 03's Tellegen theorem says this split holds in any network: power delivered = power dissipated + rate of change of stored energy.

Why the dashed curve is drawn times four. So that energy fraction and current share an axis: the dashed curve is $4(i/4)^2 = i^2/4$, which reaches 4 when the coil is full.

The four-stroke cycle, from nothing. The assumption. The 60% dwell is an assumption for the example, as are $L$ and $R$. The physics it shows does not depend on them: with a fixed fraction of a shrinking interval, the dwell eventually falls below a few time constants, and the stored energy, which goes as the square of the current, falls faster than the current. 05 · first order · the coil

Opening the contact with nothing across it

An inductor's current is continuous while its voltage is finite (unit 02). Suppose the contact simply opened and the 4 A had to stop in 1 μs, a time chosen only for scale:

$$|v| = L\left|\frac{\mathrm di}{\mathrm dt}\right| = 0.006\times\frac{4\ \mathrm A}{1\ \mu\mathrm s} = 24\ \mathrm{kV}.$$

That voltage appears across the opening contact, whose gap is still tiny. The current does not stop: it crosses the gap as an arc, and the energy that should have gone to the spark burns the contact.

the ideal switch, and its limit

"Open the switch" in a circuit with an inductor is not a legitimate instruction in the ideal model: it asks $i_L$ to jump, which needs an infinite voltage. Something must give the current somewhere to go. Here, the capacitor.

Stopping 4 A in 1 μs needs 24 kV; in 1 ns, 24 MV. The model's demand grows without bound as the opening gets faster.

The number. $L\,\Delta i/\Delta t = 0.006\ \mathrm H\times4\ \mathrm A/10^{-6}\ \mathrm s = 2.4\times10^4$ V. Units: $\mathrm{H\cdot A\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm V$ ✓. The 1 μs is not a measured opening time; it is chosen to show the size of the demand, and any short time gives a large voltage.

What the arc is, qualitatively. Just after the points separate, the gap between them is microscopic, and a voltage of even a few hundred volts across it is a very large field; the air breaks down and conducts, and the current continues through the gap. This unit does not model the arc; it only notes that the inductor's current must flow somewhere, and that if no element provides a path, the arc does. The capacitor across the contact in the coil-ignition circuit is there to provide that path (Denton, the ignition chapter). Its effect is computed in section 2.

Energy. The coil holds 48 mJ at 4 A. With no capacitor, whatever does not reach the spark is dissipated in the arc at the contact: a few tens of millijoules, two hundred times a second at 6000 rpm, delivered to a pair of small metal points. That is why the points would erode.

Unit 02's sentence, spent. Unit 02 said: "an inductor's current is continuous whenever its voltage is finite. Unit 05's ignition coil spends this." This is the spending: the continuity is what makes the coil useful — the current must go on, and the secondary turns that insistence into a spark — and what makes the contact's life hard.

05 · first order · energy

Half the energy, whatever R

theorem

A capacitor $C$, uncharged, is connected at $t = 0$ through a resistance $R$ to a fixed voltage $V$. By the time it is charged, the source has supplied $CV^2$, the capacitor holds $\tfrac12CV^2$, and the resistor has dissipated $\tfrac12CV^2$ — whatever $R$ is.

proof, by the exponential

$\tau = RC$, $v_C = V(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, $i = C\dot v_C = (V/R)e^{-t/\tau}$. The source supplies $\int_0^\infty Vi\,\mathrm dt = V\cdot CV$; the resistor dissipates

$$\int_0^\infty\frac{V^2}{R}e^{-2t/RC}\,\mathrm dt = \tfrac12CV^2 .\ \square$$

Units: $\mathrm F\cdot\mathrm V^2 = \mathrm{C\,V} = \mathrm J$ ✓. $R$ cancels: it sets how fast, not how much.

The circuit of the theorem. The same loop appears in the coil: after the contact opens and the ringing dies, the battery has charged the capacitor to 12 V through the coil.

Full proof

The charging current: by the time-constant slide, $v_C$ goes from $0$ to $V$ with $\tau = RC$, and $i = C\,\mathrm dv_C/\mathrm dt = C\cdot(V/\tau)e^{-t/\tau} = (V/R)e^{-t/RC}$. The source is at fixed voltage $V$, so the energy it supplies is $V\int i\,\mathrm dt = V q_{\text{final}} = V\cdot CV$. The resistor: $\int_0^\infty i^2R\,\mathrm dt = (V^2/R)\int_0^\infty e^{-2t/RC}\mathrm dt = (V^2/R)(RC/2)$. The capacitor: $\tfrac12CV^2$ (unit 02). Check: $CV^2 = \tfrac12CV^2 + \tfrac12CV^2$ ✓.

Why it is surprising. A small resistor carries a large current briefly; a large one a small current for a long time. The product comes out the same. The next slide shows that this is not a coincidence of exponentials: the result holds for any resistor law at all.

Where it matters. Every time a capacitor is charged from a fixed supply through a switch, half the energy drawn is lost. Digital electronics does exactly this, charging small capacitances from a fixed supply at every switching event, so the theorem prices every such event at half the energy drawn; how large a share of a real chip's power that is depends on the chip, and no figure is claimed here. Problem P2 shows that charging in $N$ equal voltage steps loses only $\tfrac12CV^2/N$: the loss is the price of a sudden step, not of charging as such.

The coil. When the contact opens and the ringing of section 2 has died away, the capacitor sits at the battery's 12 V, charged through the coil, with $C = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm F$, $V = 12$ V and the coil's $R$ and $L$ in the loop. The inductor starts with 4 A, not zero, so its own 48 mJ also ends in $R$. What the battery adds is still this theorem's: the second proof on the next slide gives $\int(V - v_C)\,i\,\mathrm dt = \tfrac12CV^2$ whatever else is in the loop, and here that integral is the heat in $R$ minus the 48 mJ the inductor gave up. So the battery draws $CV^2$, half of it stays in $C$, and the heat in $R$ is 48 mJ plus the other half. Problem P2 treats an inductor that starts and ends empty; section 3 does the coil's ledger.

Worked: half the energy, with no exponential

worked example 2 — the second proof, and the contact's capacitor in numbers

1. Use only the element laws. The resistor's voltage is $v_R = V - v_C$ (KVL), and $i\,\mathrm dt = C\,\mathrm dv_C$ (the capacitor).

2. Dissipated energy, changing variable from $t$ to $v_C$, which runs from $0$ to $V$:

$$\int v_Ri\,\mathrm dt = \int_0^V(V - v_C)\,C\,\mathrm dv_C = \tfrac12CV^2 .$$

3. Nothing about $R$ was used — not its value, not even Ohm's law. Any resistor law, linear or not, loses $\tfrac12CV^2$, provided $v_C$ ends at $V$.

4. The contact's capacitor, $C = 0.2\ \mu\mathrm F$ at 12 V: drawn $CV^2 = 28.8\ \mu\mathrm J$; stored $14.4\ \mu\mathrm J$; lost $14.4\ \mu\mathrm J$.

5. With $R = 3\ \Omega$: peak current 4 A, $\tau = 0.6\ \mu\mathrm s$. With $R = 3\ \mathrm{k\Omega}$: 4 mA, $\tau = 0.6$ ms. Loss: $14.4\ \mu\mathrm J$ both times.

check

Areas: rectangle $CV\cdot V$; triangle under $v_C = q/C$, $\tfrac12CV\cdot V$; the other triangle, the rest ✓.

Step 2, the change of variable. $\int_0^\infty(V - v_C(t))\,i(t)\,\mathrm dt = \int_0^\infty(V - v_C(t))\,C\,\dot v_C(t)\,\mathrm dt$ $= \big[C(Vv_C - \tfrac12v_C^2)\big]_{t=0}^{t=\infty}$, since the integrand is the exact derivative of $C(Vv_C - \tfrac12v_C^2)$. The bracket depends only on the end values, $v_C = 0$ and $v_C = V$. So the path in between — fast or slow, monotone or not — is irrelevant.

The figure. The horizontal axis is the charge $q$ delivered, from $0$ to $CV$. The source pushes each $\mathrm dq$ through voltage $V$: the rectangle, $CV^2$. The capacitor stores $\int v_C\,\mathrm dq$ with $v_C = q/C$: the lower triangle. The difference, $\int(V - v_C)\,\mathrm dq$, is the upper triangle: the energy dropped across the resistor. Step 5, the numbers. $V/R = 12/3 = 4$ A and $RC = 3\times0.2\times10^{-6} = 0.6\ \mu\mathrm s$; $12/3000 = 4$ mA and $3000\times0.2\times10^{-6} = 0.6$ ms. $CV^2 = 0.2\times10^{-6}\times144 = 28.8\ \mu\mathrm J$. These are microjoules: the capacitor's own energy at 12 V is three thousand times smaller than the coil's 48 mJ. It is not for storing energy at 12 V; section 2 shows what it holds at 690 V.

When the theorem fails. If the source's voltage is not fixed — if it rises with $v_C$, as in problem P2's steps — the rectangle is replaced by a staircase hugging the triangle, and the loss shrinks. A source with an inductor in the loop can also do better than half; the ideal limit is lossless charging by resonance, which section 2's ringing shows.

05 · first order · energy

R → 0: where does the half go?

As $R\to0$ the loss stays $\tfrac12CV^2$, while the current becomes a pulse of height $V/R\to\infty$ and width $RC\to0$, area $CV$ fixed — an impulse $CV\,\delta(t)$, in MR 20's language.

At $R = 0$ exactly, the model has no solution: $v_C$ would jump from $0$ to $V$, which a finite current cannot do. The limit of the solutions exists, and the energy it loses is in no element at all.

what a lumped model says when it breaks

The loss goes into what the model left out. A real loop has inductance (unit 02's stray $L$), which turns a tiny-$R$ charge into a ringing that the resistance then damps; and a loop carrying a fast pulse radiates (unit 02's (H3)). The $\tfrac12CV^2$ is lost either way.

Halving $R$ doubles the peak and halves the width; the charge delivered and the energy lost do not change.

The pulses. $i = (V/R)e^{-t/RC}$ has $\int_0^\infty i\,\mathrm dt = CV$ for every $R$, and $\int i^2R\,\mathrm dt = \tfrac12CV^2$ for every $R$. As $R\to0$ the family converges, as a distribution, to $CV\delta(t)$ (MR 20's pulses that become $\delta$), but its square does not converge to anything: $i^2R$ is a pulse of area $\tfrac12CV^2$ and height $V^2/R\to\infty$. The energy loss is a fixed amount concentrated at a single instant.

Adding the stray inductance. With a small loop inductance $\ell$ in series, the circuit is the series RLC of section 2: for $R < 2\sqrt{\ell/C}$ it rings, with $v_C$ overshooting towards $2V$ and the current oscillating at $1/\sqrt{\ell C}$. The total resistive loss after the ringing dies is still $\tfrac12CV^2$ — the second proof did not care about the path — but it is now spread over many cycles. If $R$ is so small that the ringing lasts long, the loop radiates some of the energy instead: unit 02's estimate puts radiation's share, against $R$, at order $(\ell/\lambda)^2\mu_0c/R$, small for a loop small against the ringing's wavelength unless $R$ is small too — and $R\to0$ is exactly the limit taken here. Which mechanism takes the energy is a question the RC model cannot answer; that it is taken is a theorem.

The general lesson. A lumped model whose limit is singular is warning that the neglected physics — here inductance and radiation, the things (H1) and (H3) set aside — has become the leading physics. The answer to "how much is lost" survives; the answer to "where" does not.

05 · second order

Free ringing, and the characteristic resistance

Three regimes (MR 06, cited): underdamped for $R < 2\sqrt{L/C}$ ($Q > \tfrac12$), ringing at $\omega_{\mathrm d} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/4}$ inside an envelope $e^{-\gamma t/2}$; critical at $R = 2\sqrt{L/C}$; overdamped above.

the characteristic resistance

In a lossless free oscillation the energy passes wholly between the two stores, so $\tfrac12L\hat\imath^2 = \tfrac12C\hat v^2$ and

$$\frac{\hat v}{\hat\imath} = \sqrt{\frac LC}.$$

Peak voltage per ampere of peak current. $Q = \sqrt{L/C}/R$: the characteristic resistance against the actual one.

For the coil and its capacitor: $\sqrt{L/C} = \sqrt{0.006/(0.2\times10^{-6})} = 173.2\ \Omega$, against $R = 3\ \Omega$. Deeply underdamped.

Free response from rest at $q = q_0$, in units of $1/\omega_0$. The critical curve is the fastest return with no overshoot (MR 06, "Critical damping, and why it is genuinely special").

The regimes, in circuit terms. The roots of $\lambda^2 + (R/L)\lambda + 1/(LC)$ are real and distinct when $(R/L)^2 > 4/(LC)$, i.e. $R > 2\sqrt{L/C}$; equal at equality; complex below. MR 06 solved each case. The curves in the figure are $q/q_0$ with $\dot q(0) = 0$: for $Q = 3$, $e^{-\gamma t/2}(\cos\omega_{\mathrm d}t + \frac{\gamma}{2\omega_{\mathrm d}}\sin\omega_{\mathrm d}t)$; for critical, $e^{-\omega_0t}(1 + \omega_0t)$; for $Q = \tfrac14$, the two-exponential combination.

The characteristic resistance, more carefully. With $R = 0$, $q = \hat q\cos(\omega_0t + \theta)$, so $v_C = \hat q/C\cdot\cos$ and $i = -\omega_0\hat q\sin$. The ratio of amplitudes is $(\hat q/C)/(\omega_0\hat q) = 1/(\omega_0C) = \sqrt{L/C}$. The energy argument on the slide gives the same thing without solving anything. With small $R$ it is still the ratio, to $O(1/Q)$.

Why $Q = \sqrt{L/C}/R$ reads well. The energy stored is $\tfrac12L\hat\imath^2$; the energy lost per radian of oscillation is about $\tfrac12R\hat\imath^2/\omega_0$; their ratio is $\omega_0L/R = Q$ — MR 06's "radians per $e$-fold of energy", in circuit language. A resistance small against $\sqrt{L/C}$ hardly disturbs the exchange.

The name. $\sqrt{L/C}$ is called the characteristic impedance of the resonator in some texts; unit 08 meets a quantity of the same form, $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ per unit length, as the characteristic impedance of a transmission line. This deck says "characteristic resistance" because here it is a real number of ohms with no frequency in it.

section 3

The Laplace transform

MR 20 built the transform and its inversion. Here it does circuit work: initial conditions become sources, every response is a rational function times the input, and a circuit is stable exactly when its poles are on the left.

05 · Laplace

What MR 20 already proved

cited: MR 20, "The Laplace transform and the Bromwich contour"

For $f$ on $[0,\infty)$ with $|f|\le Me^{\sigma t}$,

$$F(s) = \mathcal L[f](s) = \int_0^\infty f(t)\,e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt$$

converges and is holomorphic for $\operatorname{Re}s > \sigma$, and is inverted by the Bromwich integral along any line $\operatorname{Re}s = c > \sigma$, where $f$ is continuous and $F$ is integrable along the line:

$$f(t) = \frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c - i\infty}^{c + i\infty}F(s)\,e^{st}\,\mathrm ds .$$

And the rule that makes it solve initial value problems: $\mathcal L[\dot f] = sF - f(0)$.

Units: if $f$ is in volts, $F$ is in $\mathrm{V\,s}$ and $s$ in $\mathrm s^{-1}$, so $sF$ and $f(0)$ are both in volts, as $\mathcal L[\dot f]$ is ✓.

The transform lives to the right of its singularities; the inversion runs up a line in that region.

Cited, with where. MR 20 defines the one-sided transform, proves that it converges and is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s > \sigma$, derives the Bromwich integral from Fourier inversion applied to $f(t)e^{-ct}H(t)$ with $s = c - i\omega$, and proves $\mathcal L[\dot f] = sF - f(0)$ by integrating by parts. This deck does not repeat any of it. It also used the rule on MR 06's oscillator, getting $(s^2 + \gamma s + \omega_0^2)X = U + (s + \gamma)x(0) + \dot x(0)$: the initial conditions arrive as a polynomial on the right. This section does the same with circuits, element by element.

The units, properly. $F(s) = \int f e^{-st}\mathrm dt$ has the units of $f$ times seconds; $s$ has units $\mathrm s^{-1}$, so $sF$ has the units of $f$, and so does $f(0)$ — and $\mathcal L[\dot f]$ is ($f$ per second) times seconds, the units of $f$ ✓: $sF - f(0)$ is homogeneous. In circuit work, $I(s)$ is in A·s and $V(s)$ in V·s, and their ratio $Z(s)$ is in ohms.

What is new here. Three facts MR 20 did not need: the transform of an integral, the convolution theorem for the one-sided transform, and the inversion of a rational $F$ by partial fractions. The next two slides prove them.

05 · Laplace

Integration and convolution

the integral rule $$\mathcal L\Big[\int_0^tf(t')\,\mathrm dt'\Big] = \frac{F(s)}s .$$
proof

$g = \int_0^tf$ has $\dot g = f$, $g(0) = 0$, and $|g|\le Me^{\sigma t}/\sigma$ for $\sigma > 0$. MR 20's rule: $F = sG - 0$. $\square$

So a capacitor charged by current $i$, $v_C = v_C(0) + \tfrac1C\int_0^ti$, has $V_C = v_C(0)/s + I/(sC)$.

the convolution theorem, one-sided

With $(f*g)(t) = \int_0^tf(t')\,g(t - t')\,\mathrm dt'$,

$$\mathcal L[f*g] = F\,G .$$
proof

$\int_0^\infty\!\!\int_0^tf(t')g(t-t')e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt'\,\mathrm dt$; swap the order (Fubini, absolutely convergent for $\operatorname{Re}s$ large) and put $u = t - t'$: $\int_0^\infty f(t')e^{-st'}\mathrm dt'\int_0^\infty g(u)e^{-su}\mathrm du$. $\square$

MR 06's Duhamel integral $x = G*f$ becomes $X = \mathcal L[G]\,F$: the response is a product.

Full proof of the integral rule

$g(t) = \int_0^tf$. If $|f|\le Me^{\sigma t}$ with $\sigma > 0$, then $|g(t)|\le M(e^{\sigma t} - 1)/\sigma\le(M/\sigma)e^{\sigma t}$, so $G$ exists for $\operatorname{Re}s > \sigma$. $g$ is differentiable with $\dot g = f$ wherever $f$ is continuous (the fundamental theorem of calculus; for piecewise continuous $f$, MR 20's integration by parts still holds). Then $F = \mathcal L[\dot g] = sG - g(0) = sG$. If $\sigma\le0$ one may replace it by any positive number.

Full proof of the convolution theorem

For $\operatorname{Re}s = c > \sigma$ (both $f$ and $g$ bounded by $Me^{\sigma t}$), $\int_0^\infty\int_0^t|f(t')||g(t-t')|e^{-ct}\,\mathrm dt'\,\mathrm dt \le\int_0^\infty M^2te^{(\sigma - c)t}\mathrm dt < \infty$, so Fubini applies (MR 20 cites it for the two-sided version). The region $0\le t'\le t < \infty$ is the same as $0\le t' < \infty$, $t'\le t < \infty$; integrating in $t$ first with $u = t - t'$ and $e^{-st} = e^{-st'}e^{-su}$ separates the integral into $F(s)G(s)$. This is MR 20's Fourier convolution theorem with the functions extended by zero to $t < 0$ — the one-sided convolution is the two-sided one of two causal functions.

Why it matters for circuits. MR 06 wrote any response of the oscillator as $x = G*f$, $G$ the response to an impulse. A linear circuit with zero initial state is the same: response = impulse response convolved with input. Under the transform this is multiplication, which is why a whole circuit can be summarised by one function of $s$ — the network function, two slides on.

05 · Laplace

Inverting a rational function

inversion by residues

If $F = N/D$ is rational with $\deg N < \deg D$, then for $t > 0$

$$f(t) = \sum_{\text{poles }p}\operatorname{Res}_{s = p}\big[F(s)e^{st}\big],$$

and for a simple pole the residue is $\dfrac{N(p)}{D'(p)}e^{pt}$; a pole of order $k$ gives $t^{k-1}e^{pt}$ times a polynomial's worth of constants.

proof

Close the Bromwich line with a large semicircle to the left. For $t > 0$, $|e^{st}|$ is small there and $F = O(1/|s|)$, so the arc's integral → 0 by Jordan's lemma (MR 20), turned a quarter-turn. The residue theorem (MR 20) collects the poles, all inside. $\square$

MR 20's "The running example, turned a quarter-turn": the same contour, closed to the left for $t > 0$.

Full proof

A rational $F$ with $\deg N < \deg D$ has finitely many poles and satisfies $|F(s)|\le K/|s|$ for large $|s|$, so $f$ grows at most exponentially and the Bromwich line can be placed at $c$ to the right of every pole. Let $\Gamma_\rho$ be the line segment from $c - i\rho$ to $c + i\rho$ followed by the left arc of radius $\rho$ about $c$. For $\rho$ large, $\Gamma_\rho$ encloses every pole, anticlockwise, and the residue theorem (MR 20) gives $\oint F e^{st}\mathrm ds/2\pi i = \sum\operatorname{Res}$. On the arc, $s = c + \rho e^{i\theta}$, $\pi/2\le\theta\le3\pi/2$, and $|e^{st}| = e^{ct}e^{\rho t\cos\theta}$. Substituting $s - c = iz$ turns this arc into MR 20's upper semicircle with $e^{iaz}$, $a = t > 0$; Jordan's lemma gives a bound $\pi e^{ct}\max|F|/t\to0$. So the Bromwich integral equals the sum of residues.

The residue at a simple pole. $D(p) = 0$, $D'(p)\ne0$: $\lim_{s\to p}(s - p)N(s)e^{st}/D(s) = N(p)e^{pt}/D'(p)$. At a pole of order $k$, $\frac1{(k-1)!}\frac{\mathrm d^{k-1}}{\mathrm ds^{k-1}}[(s - p)^kFe^{st}]$ at $s = p$, which produces $t^je^{pt}$ for $j\le k - 1$. This is partial fractions done by residues: $F = \sum c_{p,j}/(s - p)^j$ term by term inverts to $c_{p,j}t^{j-1}e^{pt}/(j-1)!$.

When $\deg N = \deg D$. Divide first: $F = d + N_1/D$ with $\deg N_1 < \deg D$, and the constant $d$ is the transform of $d\,\delta(t)$ — an impulse at $t = 0$ (MR 20's distributions). An impedance like $sL$ grows; the corresponding response is a derivative. The theorem is stated for the strictly proper case, which is all the switching problems need.

When $\deg D = \deg N + 1$. Then $|F|\sim K/|s|$ along the line, which is not integrable, so MR 20's theorem does not apply as stated and the Bromwich integral above is the symmetric limit $\rho\to\infty$ — which is what the arc argument computes. Two facts make the result safe anyway. Directly, by parts, $\mathcal L[t^je^{pt}/j!] = 1/(s - p)^{j+1}$, so the residue sum has transform $F$. And a continuous $f$ is fixed by its transform: $g = \int_0^tf$ has transform $F/s$ (the integral rule), which is $O(1/|s|^2)$ and integrable, so MR 20's inversion fixes $g$, and $f = \dot g$.

Real coefficients. In circuits $N$ and $D$ have real coefficients (the next slides), so complex poles come in conjugate pairs with conjugate residues, and each pair contributes $2\operatorname{Re}[ce^{pt}]$, a real decaying or growing sinusoid.

Initial conditions become sources

Transform each element law, with the passive convention:

$$V = RI,\qquad V = sLI - L\,i(0),$$ $$I = sCV - C\,v(0).$$

Read each as a circuit: an impedance $Z(s)$ — $R$, $sL$, $1/(sC)$ — and, for a store that is not empty at $t = 0$, a source. KCL and KVL hold for the transforms, since they are linear with constant coefficients.

the s-domain circuit

The same graph, with $I_e(s)$ and $V_e(s)$ on the edges, impedances in place of elements, and the initial state as sources. Unit 04's methods solve it.

Units: $sL$ and $1/(sC)$ are $\mathrm{s^{-1}H} = \Omega$ and $1/(\mathrm{s^{-1}F}) = \Omega$ ✓; $L\,i(0)$ is $\mathrm{V\,s}$, as $V(s)$ is ✓.

The capacitor's law, divided by $sC$, gives the series form drawn here: $V = I/(sC) + v(0)/s$.

Each law, transformed. $v = L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$ gives $V = L(sI - i(0))$. $i = C\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt$ gives $I = C(sV - v(0))$, equivalently $V = I/(sC) + v(0)/s$. The resistor has no memory and no source.

The orientation of the inductor's source. $V = sLI - Li(0)$: going from the + terminal to the − terminal, the potential drops by $sLI$ across the impedance and then rises by $Li(0)$ across the source, so the source's + terminal is on the − side of the element, where the current leaves. Check: short the inductor ($V = 0$) and $I = i(0)/s$, a constant current $i(0)$ for all $t$ — an ideal inductor shorted on itself keeps its current, as it must ✓. The capacitor's $v(0)/s$ is the transform of a constant $v(0)$: the capacitor is its own initial voltage in series with an uncharged capacitor ✓.

Network functions, and what stable means

the network function

With every store empty at $t = 0$, the transform of any response is $H(s)$ times the transform of the input. By Cramer's rule on unit 04's nodal system with weights $1/R$, $sC$, $1/(sL)$, $H$ is a ratio of polynomials in $s$ with real coefficients. Its inverse transform is the impulse response $h$.

theorem: stable means poles on the left

For a proper rational $H$, every bounded input gives a bounded output iff every pole of $H$ has $\operatorname{Re}s < 0$.

proof, one direction

$h = d\,\delta + \sum c\,t^ke^{pt}$; if every $\operatorname{Re}p < 0$, $\int_0^\infty|h| < \infty$ and $|y(t)|\le(|d| + \int|h|)\sup|u|$. $\square$ (Converse: notes.)

Each pole contributes $t^ke^{pt}$ to the impulse response. Left of the axis it dies; on it, it persists; right of it, it grows.

Why $H$ is rational with real coefficients. Ground one node; the s-domain nodal equations are $Y(s)\Phi = J$ with $Y = A\,\operatorname{diag}(y_e)A^{\mathsf T}$ (grounded row and column deleted) and $y_e\in\{1/R, sC, 1/(sL)\}$. Multiply every $1/(sL)$ by $s/s$: each entry of $sY$ is a polynomial in $s$ with real coefficients. By Cramer's rule each $\Phi_k$ is a ratio of determinants of such matrices, so a rational function with real coefficients times the input; voltages and currents are linear combinations of the $\Phi_k$ with the same kind of weights. Its poles are among the zeros of $\det Y(s)$ and $s = 0$ — the network's natural frequencies, the exponents of its free motion. In the coil they are $p_\pm$, the eigenvalues of the state matrix $A$.

Full proof of the converse

Suppose a pole $p$ of $H$ has $\operatorname{Re}p\ge0$. Take the bounded input $u(t) = e^{i(\operatorname{Im}p)t}$ for $t\ge0$ (its real or imaginary part, if a real input is wanted: one of them works, since $H$ has real coefficients). $U = 1/(s - i\operatorname{Im}p)$ has no zeros, so $Y = HU$ still has the pole $p$, and if $\operatorname{Re}p = 0$ the pole of $U$ coincides with it and raises its order. The residue expansion of $y$ then contains $e^{pt}$ with $\operatorname{Re}p > 0$, or $t^ke^{pt}$ with $k\ge1$ and $|e^{pt}| = 1$: unbounded, and not cancelled by the other terms, which have different exponents. So the output is unbounded. $\square$

"Proper". $\deg N\le\deg D$, so $h$ is a function plus at most an impulse. An impedance $sL$, as a network function from current to voltage, is not proper: its output is a derivative, and a bounded input with a jump gives an impulse. The theorem is about proper $H$.

A caution: cancellations. A pole of the network can cancel against a zero in one particular $H$ and be invisible in that response. Stability of the circuit — every free motion decays — is the statement that every natural frequency is on the left; for a network of positive R, L, C this is section 5's theorem (or the energy argument of problem P8).

The sinusoidal steady state

$e^{st}$ is an eigenfunction of $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$, with eigenvalue $s$; on the imaginary axis, $e^{i\omega t}$ with eigenvalue $i\omega$. A linear circuit therefore maps $e^{i\omega t}$ to $H(i\omega)e^{i\omega t}$.

the sinusoidal steady state

If every natural frequency of a circuit has $\operatorname{Re}s < 0$, and from $t = 0$ an input $u(t) = \operatorname{Re}(Ue^{i\omega t})$ is applied, then from any initial state every response tends to

$$y(t)\to\operatorname{Re}\big(H(i\omega)\,U\,e^{i\omega t}\big).$$
proof

$\mathcal L[e^{i\omega t}] = 1/(s - i\omega)$, so $Y = H(s)U/(s - i\omega) + (\text{initial-state terms})$, the latter with poles at natural frequencies only. The residue at $i\omega$ is $H(i\omega)Ue^{i\omega t}$; every other term has $\operatorname{Re}p < 0$ and decays. Take real parts: MR 06's complex-drive trick. $\square$

A series RL circuit, at rest, driven by a cosine from $t = 0$ with $\omega\tau = \pi$. The transient is the residue at $-1/\tau$; after a few $\tau$ only the steady state is left.

Full proof

Work with the complex input $\tilde u = Ue^{i\omega t}$; by MR 06's complex-drive trick (L linear with real coefficients), the response to $\operatorname{Re}\tilde u$ is $\operatorname{Re}$ of the response to $\tilde u$. In the s-domain, $\tilde Y(s) = H(s)\,U/(s - i\omega) + Y_0(s)$, where $Y_0$ comes from the initial-state sources and has poles only at natural frequencies (the zeros of the network determinant). $i\omega$ is not a natural frequency, since those have $\operatorname{Re}s < 0$; so the pole at $i\omega$ is simple with residue $H(i\omega)Ue^{i\omega t}$. Every other pole $p$ is a natural frequency or a pole of $H$ (also a natural frequency), with $\operatorname{Re}p < 0$, and contributes $t^ke^{pt}\to0$. If $H$ is not strictly proper, its constant part contributes $dUe^{i\omega t}$, which is already of the steady form. $\square$

The figure's numbers. $R = 1$, $L = \tau = 1$ in scaled units, $\omega = \pi$. Steady state $\operatorname{Re}[e^{i\omega t}/(R + i\omega L)]$, amplitude $1/\sqrt{1 + \pi^2} = 0.303$ and lag $\arctan\pi = 72.3^\circ$. The current starts at 0 (continuity of $i_L$), so the transient is $-i_{\mathrm{ss}}(0)e^{-t/\tau}$, with $i_{\mathrm{ss}}(0) = 1/(1 + \pi^2) = 0.092$.

Why this licenses everything after. From here on, a circuit "at frequency $\omega$" means its steady state. Every quantity is a sinusoid at $\omega$, fixed by one complex number. The theorem needs stability; an undamped LC circuit driven at its own resonance has no steady state (MR 06, "No damping, driven at $\omega_0$: the ansatz fails"), and the formula's $H(i\omega)$ is infinite there.

RMS, and the phasor

the RMS value

Of a periodic $v$ with period $T$: $\ V_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{\tfrac1T\int_0^Tv^2\,\mathrm dt}$. For $v = \hat V\cos(\omega t + \theta)$ the mean of $\cos^2$ is $\tfrac12$, so $V_{\mathrm{rms}} = \hat V/\sqrt2$.

the phasor

The complex number $V$ with

$$\boxed{\,v(t) = \operatorname{Re}\big(\sqrt2\,V e^{i\omega t}\big)\,}$$

$|V|$ is the RMS value, $\arg V$ the phase. Phasors are upper case, instantaneous values lower case.

Why RMS: a resistor's mean power is $\langle v^2\rangle/R = |V|^2/R$, with no ½. The 230 V of the mains is an RMS value; its peak is $230\sqrt2 = 325.3$ V. Unit 06 develops AC power.

Engineers write $j$ for $\sqrt{-1}$, keeping $i$ for current; this course keeps $i$, as Mechanics to Relativity does, and the context says which.

One cycle of the mains, 50 Hz. A resistor on it heats exactly as on a steady 230 V.

The mean of $\cos^2$. $\cos^2x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos2x)$, and $\cos2x$ averages to zero over a period. So $\tfrac1T\int\hat V^2\cos^2 = \hat V^2/2$. For the phasor: $v = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2|V|e^{i(\omega t + \arg V)}) = \sqrt2|V|\cos(\omega t + \arg V)$, peak $\hat V = \sqrt2|V|$, RMS $|V|$ ✓.

The ½ that moved. MR 06's power slide had $\langle\operatorname{Re}z\operatorname{Re}w\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(A\bar B)$ for complex amplitudes $A$, $B$ that are peaks. With the $\sqrt2$ put into the definition, the same average is $\operatorname{Re}(V\bar I)$ for RMS phasors — the ½ is absorbed once, in the definition, and never written again. That is the whole reason for the convention, and why power engineers use it. This course fixed it in AUTHORING; peak amplitudes are written with a hat and appear only where a unit says so.

The general definition. The RMS value is defined for any periodic signal, not only sinusoids; the identity $V_{\mathrm{rms}} = \hat V/\sqrt2$ is special to sinusoids (a square wave of height $\hat V$ has RMS $\hat V$). Unit 00 promised "the RMS phasors of the course arrive in unit 05": here they are.

Mains. 230 V at 50 Hz, as the course has used since unit 00; the 400/230 V three-phase system is unit 11's. A period is $1/50 = 20$ ms.

Phasors obey Kirchhoff's laws

lemma

If $\operatorname{Re}(We^{i\omega t}) = 0$ for all $t$ and $\omega\ne0$, then $W = 0$.

proof

At $t = 0$: $\operatorname{Re}W = 0$. At $\omega t = \pi/2$: $\operatorname{Re}(iW) = -\operatorname{Im}W = 0$. $\square$

So KCL $\sum_eA_{ke}i_e(t) = 0$ for all $t$ gives $\operatorname{Re}\big(\sqrt2\,(\sum_eA_{ke}I_e)e^{i\omega t}\big) = 0$ and hence

$$AI = 0,\qquad V = A^{\mathsf T}\Phi,$$

the same matrix, with complex vectors. And $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$ acts as $i\omega$: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2Ie^{i\omega t}) = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2\,i\omega I\,e^{i\omega t})$.

Every quantity in the steady state is an arrow turning at $\omega$. Kirchhoff's laws hold for the arrows because they hold for every shadow, at every instant.

What is used. Only that $A$ is real. $\operatorname{Re}$ is real-linear, so $\sum_eA_{ke}\operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2I_ee^{i\omega t}) = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2(\sum_eA_{ke}I_e)e^{i\omega t})$, which needs $A_{ke}$ real. Then the lemma. The same argument with complex $s$ in place of $i\omega$ is what the s-domain slide used.

The map is injective. The lemma says the phasor of a sinusoid at a given $\omega$ is unique: two sinusoids are equal for all $t$ iff their phasors are equal. So any linear identity with real coefficients among sinusoids at one frequency holds among their phasors, and conversely. Products are another matter: $v(t)i(t)$ is not $\operatorname{Re}(2VIe^{2i\omega t})$, and power is not the product of phasors — MR 06's warning, and unit 06's subject.

The figure. The current arrow trails the voltage arrow by the angle $\varphi = \arg V - \arg I$, the course's phase convention; for an inductive load $\varphi > 0$, the current lags. The physical $v(t)$ and $i(t)$ are the dots on the real axis, sliding back and forth as the arrows turn.

Impedance and admittance

impedance and admittance

For an element or a two-terminal network in the steady state at $\omega$: $Z = V/I$, and $Y = 1/Z$. Write $Z = R + iX$ and $Y = G + iB$: resistance and reactance, conductance and susceptance.

$$Z_R = R,\qquad Z_L = i\omega L,\qquad Z_C = \frac1{i\omega C}.$$

It is the network function from current to voltage, evaluated at $s = i\omega$: $Z(i\omega)$. An inductor's reactance $\omega L$ grows with frequency, a capacitor's $-1/(\omega C)$ shrinks in size: at DC an inductor is a short and a capacitor an open circuit, at high frequency the reverse.

Units: $\omega L$ is $\mathrm{s^{-1}\cdot V\,s\,A^{-1}} = \Omega$ ✓; $1/(\omega C)$ is $1/(\mathrm{s^{-1}\cdot A\,s\,V^{-1}}) = \Omega$ ✓.

With $I$ drawn along the real axis. $V = i\omega L\,I$ is $I$ turned by $+90^\circ$; $V = I/(i\omega C)$ is $I$ turned by $-90^\circ$.

Each element, derived. $v = L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$ with $i = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2Ie^{i\omega t})$ gives $v = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2\,i\omega LI\,e^{i\omega t})$, so $V = i\omega LI$ by the lemma. $i = C\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt$ gives $I = i\omega CV$, so $V = I/(i\omega C) = -iI/(\omega C)$. The resistor has no derivative and $V = RI$.

Lead and lag. $\varphi = \arg V - \arg I$ is $+90^\circ$ for an inductor (the current lags the voltage by a quarter cycle) and $-90^\circ$ for a capacitor (the current leads). In a capacitor the current is largest when the voltage is changing fastest, which is when it passes zero; in an inductor, the reverse.

Admittances. $Y_R = 1/R = G$, $Y_L = 1/(i\omega L) = -i/(\omega L)$, $Y_C = i\omega C$: the complex weights unit 04 promised, "unit 05 gives capacitors and inductors complex admittances $i\omega C$ and $1/(i\omega L)$". Parallel admittances add, as conductances did.

Signs to remember. The course's conventions (AUTHORING): $Z = R + iX$, an inductor $i\omega L$, a capacitor $1/(i\omega C)$. A capacitor's reactance is negative. Some texts quote the size $1/(\omega C)$ and call it "capacitive reactance"; the sign is then carried in words.

Unit 04, with complex weights

Kirchhoff's laws hold for phasors, and every element is $I_e = Y_eV_e$. So unit 04's derivation goes through word for word:

$$\boxed{\,\mathcal Y\Phi = J,\qquad \mathcal Y = A\,\operatorname{diag}(Y_e)\,A^{\mathsf T}\,}$$
what carries over

what does not

Thomson's principle, the maximum principle and the effective-resistance metric: they rest on $G_e > 0$ and an order, and complex numbers have no order. Section 5 finds what survives: a statement about real parts.

Right: unit 02's kettle. Its impedance is real, so voltage and current phasors are in phase, and unit 02's DC treatment of the mains was exact for RMS values.

The derivation, again. $I = YV$ edge by edge with $Y = \operatorname{diag}(Y_e)$; $V = A^{\mathsf T}\Phi$; $AI = J$ for injected currents $J$. Substitute: $AYA^{\mathsf T}\Phi = J$. Unit 04 called $AGA^{\mathsf T}$ the weighted Laplacian; with complex weights it is the network's admittance matrix, symmetric (since $\operatorname{diag}(Y_e)$ is) but not Hermitian, and unit 13 calls it the bus admittance matrix of a power grid.

Thévenin, verbatim. Unit 04's proof: the port voltage is an affine function of the port current, because the network's equations are linear and uniquely solvable. Nothing used that the coefficients were real; with complex coefficients the same argument gives $V = V_{\text{th}} + Z_{\text{th}}I$ (passive convention at the port), with $V_{\text{th}}$ the open-circuit phasor and $Z_{\text{th}}$ the impedance of the network with its sources set to zero. Unique solvability now has to be assumed or checked; for networks of positive R, L, C at real $\omega$ it can fail only at a lossless resonance: by section 5's identity at $s = i\omega$, a source-free solution has $\sum R|I_e|^2 = 0$, so it carries no current in any resistor and lives on inductors and capacitors alone.

What fails, and why. Thomson's principle said the actual currents minimise $\sum R_ei_e^2$; the "minimum" of a complex quadratic form means nothing. The maximum principle said a node's potential is a weighted average of its neighbours' with positive weights; with complex weights the average can lie outside — an LC network can have an internal voltage much larger than its source, as the tuned circuit shows (the voltage across its capacitor is $Q$ times the drive).

The series RLC as arrows

Series: one current $I$, drawn as the reference. Each element's voltage is $Z_eI$:

$$V = \Big(R + i\big(\omega L - \tfrac1{\omega C}\big)\Big)I .$$
resonance

The reactance $\omega L - 1/(\omega C)$ vanishes at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$: $Z = R$, real and of least magnitude, and the inductor's and capacitor's voltages cancel exactly. Below $\omega_0$ the circuit is capacitive ($X < 0$, $V$ lags $I$); above, inductive.

At resonance $|V_L| = |V_C| = \omega_0L|I| = Q\,|V|$: each is $Q$ times the drive. The phasor diagram shows it — two long arrows that cancel.

Drawn with $R = \omega_0L = 1/(\omega_0C)$, so $Q = 1$, to keep the arrows on the page; at high $Q$ the vertical arrows dwarf $V$.

Reading the diagrams. $V_R = RI$ lies along $I$; $V_L = i\omega LI$ points up; $V_C = -iI/(\omega C)$ points down; $V$ is their vector sum, by KVL for phasors. Below resonance $1/(\omega C) > \omega L$, the downward arrow wins, and $V$ points below the axis: the current leads the voltage. Above, the upward one wins. At resonance the two cancel and $V = V_R$.

The $Q$ multiplication. At $\omega_0$, $|I| = |V|/R$ and $|V_L| = \omega_0L|V|/R = Q|V|$. With $Q = 57.7$, as in the coil's ringing circuit, a 1 V drive at resonance would put 57.7 V across the capacitor. This is the maximum principle failing: a node voltage far outside the range of the source's. It is also why a high-$Q$ resonant circuit is selective — the next two slides.

Energy at resonance. The inductor's and capacitor's stored energies oscillate out of phase and their sum is constant (as in the free lossless oscillation): the source supplies only what $R$ dissipates. Unit 06 makes this the statement that the reactive power of the two cancels.

Resonance, Q and bandwidth

Drive the series RLC with a fixed voltage phasor $V$. Then $|I| = |V|/|Z|$ with $|Z|^2 = R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2$, and

$$\frac{|I|^2}{|I|^2_{\max}} = \frac1{1 + Q^2\big(\frac\omega{\omega_0} - \frac{\omega_0}\omega\big)^2}.$$
the half-power bandwidth

$|I|^2$ — and so the power in $R$ — falls to half its peak at two frequencies $\omega_\pm$ with

$$\boxed{\,\omega_+ - \omega_- = \frac RL = \frac{\omega_0}Q\,}$$

exactly: MR 06's half-power width $\gamma$ ("Velocity, power, and a width that is exactly $\gamma$"), with $\gamma = R/L$.

Units: $R/L$ in $\mathrm s^{-1}$, an angular frequency ✓. Limit $R\to0$: zero width, infinite $Q$ ✓.

Relative current against $\omega/\omega_0$, drawn for $Q = 5$. The width is measured at $1/\sqrt2$ in current, $\tfrac12$ in power.

The formula. $|Z|^2 = R^2 + \big(\omega L - \frac1{\omega C}\big)^2 = R^2\Big[1 + \frac{\omega_0^2L^2}{R^2}\Big(\frac\omega{\omega_0} - \frac{\omega_0}\omega\Big)^2\Big]$, using $\omega L - 1/(\omega C) = \omega_0L(\omega/\omega_0 - \omega_0/\omega)$ since $1/C = \omega_0^2L$. And $\omega_0L/R = Q$. At $\omega_0$, $|Z| = R$, the minimum.

Full proof of the width

Half power when $Q^2(x - 1/x)^2 = 1$ with $x = \omega/\omega_0$, i.e. $x - 1/x = \pm1/Q$, i.e. $x^2\mp x/Q - 1 = 0$. The positive roots are $x_\pm = \pm\frac1{2Q} + \sqrt{1 + \frac1{4Q^2}}$, so $x_+ - x_- = 1/Q$ and $\omega_+ - \omega_- = \omega_0/Q = R/L$. No small-$Q^{-1}$ approximation was made. Note $\omega_+\omega_- = \omega_0^2$: the two half-power frequencies are placed geometrically, not arithmetically, about $\omega_0$.

Against MR 06. There the mean power absorbed was $\tfrac12\gamma F_0^2/(g(\omega)^2 + \gamma^2)$ with $g = \omega_0^2/\omega - \omega$, and half-power at $g = \pm\gamma$, width exactly $\gamma$. section 5

Positive-real

What it means for a box of resistors, inductors and capacitors to be unable to deliver energy, written as a property of one function of $s$ — and proved from Tellegen's theorem with a complex conjugate.

05 · positive-real

Positive-real: the definition

a positive-real function

A rational function $Z(s)$ with real coefficients such that

$$\operatorname{Re}Z(s)\ge0\quad\text{whenever}\quad\operatorname{Re}s > 0 .$$

Why this is the right condition. At $s = i\omega$, $\operatorname{Re}Z$ is the resistance: $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega)|I|^2$ is the mean power the network absorbs (a resistor's mean power is $R|I|^2$, the RMS slide; the next slide sums it over a network), so $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega)\ge0$ says it never delivers net energy at any frequency. At $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$ the drive $e^{st}$ is growing, and the condition says the network absorbs even then.

The name is O. Brune's (1931), who proved the condition sufficient as well as necessary; W. Cauer had used it earlier as a necessary one. The impedance seen at a pair of terminals of a network is its driving-point impedance.

A positive-real function never sends a point of the open right half $s$-plane into the open left half $Z$-plane.

Real coefficients. Equivalently, $Z(\bar s) = \overline{Z(s)}$, and $Z$ is real on the real axis. Every network function is of this kind (section 3). It makes $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega)$ even in $\omega$ and $\operatorname{Im}Z(i\omega)$ odd.

Why the boundary is included by continuity. If $Z$ has no pole at $i\omega$, then $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega) = \lim_{\sigma\downarrow0}\operatorname{Re}Z(\sigma + i\omega)\ge0$. So "Re Z ≥ 0 on the right" implies "resistance ≥ 0 at every real frequency". The converse fails: a function can have $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega)\ge0$ everywhere on the axis and a pole on the right ($-1/(s - 1)$ has $\operatorname{Re} = 1/(1 + \omega^2) > 0$ on the axis). The definition asks for the whole half-plane because that is what excludes such a pole.

Unit 04's promise. Unit 04 proved that a network of positive resistors presents $R_{\text{th}}\ge0$ at any port, and said "unit 05 extends this to $\operatorname{Re}Z\ge0$". The extension is the next slide.

History, carefully. The term "positive real" was coined by Otto Brune in his 1931 MIT doctoral thesis, published as "Synthesis of a finite two-terminal network whose driving-point impedance is a prescribed function of frequency", J. Math. Phys. 10, 191–236 (1931), in which he proved the converse stated two slides on. That the term is Brune's, and that W. Cauer had earlier (1926) found the condition necessary without proving it sufficient, is from secondary sources — the Wikipedia articles "Otto Brune" and "Positive-real function" — and is stated no more precisely than they support. Desoer and Kuh introduce the concept in ch. 19, "Energy and Passivity".

05 · positive-real

Every RLC impedance is positive-real

theorem

The driving-point impedance of any network of positive resistors, inductors and capacitors is positive-real.

proof

Drive the port with current $I$ at complex $s$, $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$; the port is an edge carrying $-I$. Then $V = A^{\mathsf T}\Phi$ and $A\mathbf I = 0$; $A$ is real, so $A\bar{\mathbf I} = 0$ too. Unit 03's Tellegen, over $\mathbb C$: $\sum_eV_e\bar I_e = 0$, i.e.

$$\begin{aligned} Z|I|^2 = {}&\sum_RR|I_e|^2 + s\sum_LL|I_e|^2\\ &+ \frac1s\sum_C\frac{|I_e|^2}C .\end{aligned}$$

$\operatorname{Re}s > 0$ and $\operatorname{Re}(1/s) = \operatorname{Re}s/|s|^2 > 0$: every term has non-negative real part. $\square$

On the axis, $s = i\omega$: $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega)|I|^2 = \sum_RR|I_e|^2$, the power dissipated. The inductor and capacitor terms are purely imaginary — they store, and unit 06 calls their sum reactive. Units: every term is $\Omega\cdot\mathrm A^2$, as $Z|I|^2$ is ✓.

Full proof

The setting. "At complex frequency $s$" means: every quantity is $\operatorname{Re}(Xe^{st})$, or equivalently we solve the s-domain network with empty stores and a current source $I$ at the port. Each edge obeys $V_e = z_eI_e$ with $z_e\in\{R, sL, 1/(sC)\}$. The graph includes the port as an edge from one port terminal to the other.

Existence. For $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$ the network equations have exactly one solution. Ground a node; the unknowns are the other node potentials and the edge currents, and the equations are KCL at the other nodes and one element law per edge: a square system. If the source is zero and a solution exists, the identity below with $I = 0$ gives $0 = \sum R|I_e|^2 + \operatorname{Re}s\sum L|I_e|^2 + \frac{\operatorname{Re}s}{|s|^2}\sum|I_e|^2/C$ in real part, so every $I_e = 0$, hence every $V_e = z_eI_e = 0$: the homogeneous system has only the trivial solution, and the square system is uniquely solvable.

Tellegen with a conjugate. Unit 03's proof, $v^{\mathsf T}i = \varphi^{\mathsf T}Ai = 0$, is algebra valid for complex vectors. Take $v = V\in\operatorname{im}A^{\mathsf T}$ and $i = \bar{\mathbf I}$, which is in $\ker A$ because $A\bar{\mathbf I} = \overline{A\mathbf I} = 0$ — this is where the incidence matrix being real is used. So $\sum_{\text{all }e}V_e\bar I_e = 0$. The port edge contributes $V\cdot\overline{(-I)} = -ZI\bar I = -Z|I|^2$; the others $z_e|I_e|^2$. Rearranged: $Z|I|^2 = \sum z_e|I_e|^2$.

The real parts. $\operatorname{Re}(sL) = L\operatorname{Re}s\ge0$ and $\operatorname{Re}(1/(sC)) = \operatorname{Re}\bar s/(|s|^2C) = \operatorname{Re}s/(|s|^2C)\ge0$. Hence $\operatorname{Re}Z\ge0$. $Z$ is rational with real coefficients by section 3. $\square$

The source. Desoer and Kuh use Tellegen's theorem with sinusoidal steady-state phasors to relate $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega)$ to the power dissipated (they say so at the opening of their ch. 19, pointing back to ch. 9), and treat positive-real functions in ch. 19, §5. This is the promise unit 03 made: "taking $v$ from the network and $i$ from its complex conjugate".

Mutual inductance. With coupled coils the inductor terms become $s\,\bar{\mathbf I}_L^{\mathsf T}\mathcal L\mathbf I_L$, and unit 02's $\mathcal L\succeq0$ keeps its real part non-negative; the theorem holds for transformers too.

05 · positive-real

What positive-real forbids

consequences
  • No poles with $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$.
  • Poles on the imaginary axis are simple, with real positive residues; so is a pole at $\infty$ ($Z\approx ks$, $k > 0$).
  • $1/Z$ is positive-real too.
proof

Near a pole $p$ of order $n$, $Z\approx k/(s - p)^n$. On a small circle round $p$, $\arg Z$ sweeps $n$ full turns, so a right-half-plane pole would send nearby right-half-plane points to $\operatorname{Re}Z < 0$. For $p = i\omega_0$ only the right half-disc is allowed: $\arg Z = \arg k - n\theta$ with $|\theta| < \pi/2$ must stay in $[-\tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi2]$, which forces $n = 1$ and $\arg k = 0$. Last: $\operatorname{Re}(1/Z) = \operatorname{Re}Z/|Z|^2$. $\square$

The right half-disc at a pole on the axis. A simple pole with $k > 0$ maps it into $\operatorname{Re}Z > 0$; a double pole wraps it round the whole plane.

Full proof

Local form. If $p$ is a pole of order $n$, $Z(s) = (s - p)^{-n}g(s)$ with $g$ holomorphic near $p$ and $g(p) = k\ne0$. For $s = p + \varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $Z = \varepsilon^{-n}e^{-in\theta}(k + O(\varepsilon))$. As $\varepsilon\to0$ the argument of $Z$ tends to $\arg k - n\theta$, and $|Z|\to\infty$.

Right-half-plane pole. All $\theta\in(-\pi, \pi]$ are allowed for small $\varepsilon$ (the disc is inside $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$), and $\arg k - n\theta$ takes every value, among them $\pi$: there $\operatorname{Re}Z\approx-|k|\varepsilon^{-n} < 0$. Contradiction.

Pole on the axis. Only $|\theta| < \pi/2$ keeps $s$ in the right half-plane. The values $\arg k - n\theta$ fill an open interval of length $n\pi$, which must lie within $[-\pi/2, \pi/2]$ (mod $2\pi$) up to the $O(\varepsilon)$ error: so $n\pi\le\pi$, $n = 1$, and the interval $(\arg k - \pi/2, \arg k + \pi/2)$ must be $(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$, so $\arg k = 0$: $k > 0$. A real-coefficient $Z$ with a pole at $i\omega_0$ also has one at $-i\omega_0$ with the conjugate residue — real and positive too.

At infinity. Apply the same to $Z(1/w)$ near $w = 0$; the map $s\mapsto1/s$ preserves the right half-plane. So $Z\approx ks^n$ for large $s$ needs $n\le1$ and $k > 0$: an impedance can grow at most like an inductor.

$1/Z$. If $Z$ is positive-real and not identically zero, $1/Z$ is rational with real coefficients and $\operatorname{Re}(1/Z) = \operatorname{Re}\bar Z/|Z|^2 = \operatorname{Re}Z/|Z|^2\ge0$ wherever $Z\ne0$. It remains to exclude a zero of $Z$ in the open right half-plane, where $1/Z$ would have a pole: near a zero $p$ of order $n$, $Z\approx k(s - p)^n$ with $k\ne0$, and on a small circle round $p$ — inside the half-plane — $\arg Z$ takes every value, among them $\pi$, so $\operatorname{Re}Z < 0$ there, which $Z$ positive-real forbids. So admittances of RLC networks are positive-real as well.

The converse, cited with care. Brune's thesis (MIT, 1931; J. Math. Phys. 10, 191–236) proved that a positive-real rational function can be realised; his construction uses ideal transformers (unit 07 meets them). That the transformers can be dispensed with — every positive-real rational function is the impedance of a network of positive R, L and C alone — is R. Bott and R. J. The tests. (1) $\operatorname{Re}\frac1{s + 1} = \frac{\operatorname{Re}s + 1}{|s + 1|^2} > 0$ for $\operatorname{Re}s > -1$, so $\operatorname{Re}Z > 1$ on the right half-plane. 05 · positive-real · the coil

The contact's impedance, checked

The coil seen from the contact is a network of positive R, L, C, so the theorem says $Z$ is positive-real. Directly, on the axis: multiply above and below by the conjugate of $D = 1 - \omega^2LC + i\omega RC$; the real part of $(R + i\omega L)\bar D$ is $R(1 - \omega^2LC) + \omega^2LRC = R$, so

$$\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega) = \frac{R}{|1 - \omega^2LC + i\omega RC|^2}.$$

Positive for every $\omega$; it is $R = 3\ \Omega$ at DC and $10\,000\ \Omega$ at $\omega_0$. The poles $-250\pm28\,866i$ are on the left, and at $\infty$, $Z\approx1/(sC)$: a zero, allowed.

what it means for the contact

Whatever the frequency, the coil-and-capacitor absorbs on average: the contact cannot be made to see a negative resistance by any choice of $L$, $R$, $C$. Instability needs an energy source inside the box — problem P8.

$Z(i\omega)$ for $\omega$ from 0 to $\infty$: a near-circle of diameter 10 kΩ, all of it in the right half-plane.

The algebra. Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator. The numerator's real part is $R(1 - \omega^2LC) + \omega L\cdot\omega RC = R$: the $\omega^2LRC$ terms cancel. So the resistance seen from the contact is $R$ divided by a positive number, and it is largest where the denominator is smallest, near resonance: there $|1 - \omega^2LC + i\omega RC|^2\approx(\omega_0RC)^2 = 1/Q^2$, and $\operatorname{Re}Z\approx Q^2R$.

Why a near-circle. Near resonance $Y = sC + 1/(R + sL)$ is approximately $G_p + iB(\omega)$ with a constant $G_p = 1/(Q^2R)$ and a susceptance $B$ that sweeps through zero; the image of a vertical line $G_p + iB$ under inversion is a circle through 0 of diameter $1/G_p = Q^2R$. Far from resonance the locus leaves the circle, starting at 3 Ω and returning to 0.

The theorem and the computation agree. The theorem gave $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega)|I|^2 = R|I_{\text{coil}}|^2$: here the only resistor is in the coil's branch, carrying $I_{\text{coil}} = I/(1 + sC(R + sL))$, so $\operatorname{Re}Z = R/|1 + i\omega C(R + i\omega L)|^2$ — the same expression.

Lossless networks

05 · lossless

Foster's reactance theorem

Foster's reactance theorem

For a lossless network — inductors and capacitors only — $Z(i\omega) = iX(\omega)$ with $X$ real and odd, and wherever $X$ is finite,

$$\boxed{\,\frac{\mathrm dX}{\mathrm d\omega} = \frac{\sum_LL\,I_e^2 + \sum_C I_e^2/(\omega^2C)}{I^2} \;> 0\,}$$

with the port current $I$ real, which makes every $I_e$ real. Reactance rises with frequency.

proof, the idea

Tellegen twice, with no conjugate: $V'$ (the $\omega$-derivative) against $\mathbf I$, and $V$ against $\mathbf I'$. Differentiating $V_e = i\omega LI_e$ and $V_e = I_e/(i\omega C)$ leaves exactly the two sums. Full proof in the notes.

Units: $\mathrm H\cdot\mathrm A^2/\mathrm A^2 = \Omega\,\mathrm s$, and $\mathrm dX/\mathrm d\omega$ is $\Omega/\mathrm s^{-1}$ ✓.

energy forces the slope

The mean energies stored are $\tfrac12L|I_e|^2$ in each inductor and $\tfrac12C|V_e|^2 = \tfrac12|I_e|^2/(\omega^2C)$ in each capacitor, so

$$\frac{\mathrm dX}{\mathrm d\omega} = \frac{2\,(\bar W_L + \bar W_C)}{|I|^2}.$$

A network that stores energy and dissipates none must have a reactance that increases with $\omega$.

Named after R. M. Foster (1924) — a different theorem from unit 04's, which bears the same name. The lossless coil circuit, $R\to0$, is one: $X = \omega L/(1 - \omega^2LC)$.

Full proof

Odd and imaginary. At $s = i\omega$ the theorem of section 5 gives $Z|I|^2 = i\omega\sum L|I_e|^2 + \frac1{i\omega}\sum|I_e|^2/C$: purely imaginary. Since $Z$ has real coefficients, $X(-\omega) = \operatorname{Im}Z(-i\omega) = -\operatorname{Im}Z(i\omega) = -X(\omega)$.

Real currents. Ground a node and write nodal analysis: $\mathcal Y\Phi = J$ with $\mathcal Y = A\operatorname{diag}(y_e)A^{\mathsf T}$, and every $y_e$ is $-i/(\omega L)$ or $i\omega C$, so $\mathcal Y = i\mathcal B$ with $\mathcal B$ real. Where $X$ is finite and the equations are solvable, $\Phi = -i\mathcal B^{-1}J$ is purely imaginary for a real injection, and $I_e = y_eV_e$ is (imaginary)×(imaginary) = real.

Two Tellegen identities. Fix the port current $I$ real and independent of $\omega$. The port is an edge with current $-I$ and voltage $iX I$. The currents $\mathbf I(\omega)$ satisfy $A\mathbf I = 0$ for every $\omega$, so their derivatives do too: $A\mathbf I' = 0$, with the port entry of $\mathbf I'$ equal to $0$. Likewise $V' = A^{\mathsf T}\Phi'$. Tellegen (no conjugate, complex vectors) gives

(a) $\sum_eV'_eI_e = 0$, whose port term is $(iXI)'\cdot(-I) = -iX'I^2$, so $iX'I^2 = \sum_{\text{elements}}V'_eI_e$;

(b) $\sum_eV_eI'_e = 0$, whose port term is $iXI\cdot0 = 0$, so $\sum_{\text{elements}}V_eI'_e = 0$.

The element laws, differentiated. Inductor: $V_e = i\omega LI_e$, $V'_e = iLI_e + i\omega LI'_e$. Capacitor: $V_e = I_e/(i\omega C)$, $V'_e = -I_e/(i\omega^2C) + I'_e/(i\omega C) = iI_e/(\omega^2C) + I'_e/(i\omega C)$. In each, the second term is $z_eI'_e = V_eI'_e/I_e$, so $\sum V'_eI_e = \sum_LiLI_e^2 + \sum_CiI_e^2/(\omega^2C) + \sum_eV_eI'_e$, and the last sum is zero by (b). With (a): $X'I^2 = \sum_LLI_e^2 + \sum_CI_e^2/(\omega^2C)$. Every term is non-negative because the $I_e$ are real; and not all $I_e$ vanish, since the port current $I\ne0$ enters some element. $\square$

05 · lossless

Poles and zeros alternate

corollary

On $\omega > 0$ the poles and zeros of $X$ are simple and alternate. At $\omega = 0$ there is a pole or a zero, and at $\omega = \infty$ likewise.

proof

Between two consecutive poles $X$ is continuous and increasing, from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$: exactly one zero, simple since $X' > 0$ there. Two zeros with no pole between would need $X$ to fall. Poles are simple by section 5 (on the axis). $X$ odd: $X(0) = 0$ or a pole. At $\infty$, $Z\approx ks^{\pm1}$ (section 5). $\square$

Example (problem P12): $Z(s) = \dfrac{s^2 + 1}{s(s^2 + 4)}$, so $X(\omega) = \dfrac{\omega^2 - 1}{\omega(4 - \omega^2)}$: pole at 0, zero at 1, pole at 2, zero at $\infty$.

Every branch rises. Between the poles at 0 and 2 the curve must cross zero exactly once; beyond 2 it rises towards zero from below.

Why the curve goes from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$ between poles. Near a simple pole $\omega_i$ on the axis, $Z\approx\frac{k}{s - i\omega_i}$ with $k > 0$ (section 5), so $X(\omega) = \operatorname{Im}\frac{k}{i(\omega - \omega_i)} = -\frac{k}{\omega - \omega_i}$: $+\infty$ just below the pole and $-\infty$ just above. So on the interval after one pole $X$ starts at $-\infty$, and on the interval before the next it ends at $+\infty$; being increasing, it crosses zero exactly once.

At zero and infinity. $X$ odd and rational: near $0$ either $X\approx a\omega$ (a zero) or $X\approx-b/\omega$ (a pole, $b > 0$); near $\infty$, $X\approx k\omega$ (a pole at infinity) or $X\approx-k'/\omega$ (a zero). Higher powers are excluded because the poles are simple and $X' > 0$ forbids a multiple zero.

The example. $Z(i\omega) = \frac{1 - \omega^2}{i\omega(4 - \omega^2)} = -i\frac{1 - \omega^2}{\omega(4 - \omega^2)}$, so $X = \frac{\omega^2 - 1}{\omega(4 - \omega^2)}$. At $\omega = 0.5$: $X = -0.75/1.875 = -0.4$; at $1.5$: $1.25/2.625 = 0.476$; at $3$: $8/(-15) = -0.533$. Rising on each branch, as drawn.

Counting. The alternation means a lossless impedance is fixed, up to one positive constant, by the list of its critical frequencies. Foster's paper gives both of the realisations the next slide and problem P12 use — parallel tanks in series, and series LC branches in parallel (as its published abstract says).

05 · lossless

The Foster form, as a chain of tanks

the Foster form

A lossless driving-point impedance has the partial-fraction form

$$Z(s) = k_\infty s + \frac{k_0}s + \sum_i\frac{2k_is}{s^2 + \omega_i^2},$$

with every $k\ge0$.

proof

Poles only on the axis (none on the right by section 5, none on the left since $Z(-s) = -Z(s)$), simple, with residues $k\ge0$. Pair $\pm i\omega_i$: $\frac{k_i}{s - i\omega_i} + \frac{k_i}{s + i\omega_i} = \frac{2k_is}{s^2 + \omega_i^2}$. What is left after subtracting every pole term has no poles, so it is a constant, and it is odd: zero. $\square$

Each term is an element: $k_\infty s$ an inductor $k_\infty$; $k_0/s$ a capacitor $1/k_0$; $\frac{2k_is}{s^2 + \omega_i^2}$ a parallel tank with $C_i = 1/(2k_i)$, $L_i = 2k_i/\omega_i^2$. In series, they realise $Z$.

the example, realised

$\dfrac{s^2 + 1}{s(s^2 + 4)} = \dfrac{1/4}s + \dfrac{(3/4)\,s}{s^2 + 4}$: a capacitor of 4 F in series with a tank of $C_1 = \tfrac43$ F, $L_1 = \tfrac3{16}$ H. Check at $s = 1$: $\tfrac14 + \tfrac3{20} = \tfrac25 = \tfrac{2}{1\cdot5}$ ✓.

$Z(-s) = -Z(s)$. On the axis $Z(i\omega)$ is purely imaginary, so $Z(i\omega) = -\overline{Z(i\omega)} = -Z(-i\omega)$ (real coefficients). The rational function $Z(s) + Z(-s)$ vanishes on the whole imaginary axis, hence identically. So a pole at $p$ is matched by one at $-p$, and since none is allowed on the right, none is on the left either.

The tank. An inductor $L_i$ in parallel with a capacitor $C_i$: $Y = sC_i + 1/(sL_i)$, $Z = \frac{s/C_i}{s^2 + 1/(L_iC_i)}$. Matching to $2k_is/(s^2 + \omega_i^2)$: $1/C_i = 2k_i$ and $1/(L_iC_i) = \omega_i^2$. Its impedance is infinite at $\omega_i$ — the tank resonates, and a parallel resonance blocks current.

The example, step by step. $k_0 = \lim_{s\to0}sZ = 1/4$. Subtract: $\frac{s^2 + 1}{s(s^2 + 4)} - \frac1{4s} = \frac{4s^2 + 4 - s^2 - 4}{4s(s^2 + 4)} = \frac{3s}{4(s^2 + 4)}$. So $2k_1 = 3/4$, $\omega_1 = 2$, $k_\infty = 0$. Elements: $C_0 = 4$ F; $C_1 = 1/(3/4) = 4/3$ F; $L_1 = (3/4)/4 = 3/16$ H; check $L_1C_1 = 1/4 = 1/\omega_1^2$ ✓. The values are normalised (a real filter scales them); the structure is the point.

The other Foster form. Expanding the admittance $1/Z$ the same way — it is also positive-real and lossless — gives inductors, capacitors and series LC branches in parallel. Problem P12 does both and checks by recomputing $Z$. Other realisations (Cauer's ladders) exist; Guillemin is the reference.

05 · closing

The coil, closed

the ignition coil, every number

Switched on: $\tau = 2$ ms, 4 A, 48 mJ — but 29.0 mJ at 6000 rpm, where the dwell is 1.5τ. Switched off: the current rings into the capacitor, 693 V lossless (684 V with $R$, 695 V with the battery), at 4.59 kHz with $Q = 57.7$ — instead of the 24 kV a bare contact would face.

Seen from the contact: $Z(s) = (R + sL)/(LCs^2 + RCs + 1)$, its poles the ringing and its zero the switch-on; positive-real, as every RLC impedance is.

Energy, accounted. With the secondary open, the coil's 48 mJ heats $R$ as the ringing dies; the battery recharges $C$ to 12 V, drawing 28.8 μJ and losing 14.4 μJ — worked example 2, exactly.

The energy in the coil at the instant the contact opens, at three engine speeds, and the same 48 mJ in the capacitor at the lossless peak.

Summary of the unit, in the coil's terms. Section 1 counted its state (order 1 closed, order 2 open) and charged it with one time constant; section 2 rang it into the capacitor; section 3 did both again with the initial current as a source; section 4 found its impedance from the contact; section 5 proved that impedance positive-real, as every passive one is; section 6 showed what is left when $R\to0$.

The energy ledger. Opening at full current: the coil holds $\tfrac12\times0.006\times16 = 48$ mJ, the capacitor nothing. At the end, with the secondary open: $i = 0$, $v_C = 12$ V, the capacitor holds $14.4\ \mu\mathrm J$; the battery has moved $q = CV = 2.4\ \mu\mathrm C$ through 12 V, $28.8\ \mu\mathrm J$. The resistor has taken $48\ \mathrm{mJ} + 28.8\ \mu\mathrm J - 14.4\ \mu\mathrm J = 48.0144$ mJ. When the contact next closes, it shorts the capacitor and its $14.4\ \mu\mathrm J$ is dissipated in the contact — negligible against the coil's energy.

The deck's values are illustrative, and the physics — a time constant that limits the dwell at speed, a capacitor that turns an arc into a ringing — is the same.

Done when

checkpoint

On paper and without notes, you prove that charging a capacitor from a fixed voltage through a resistor wastes exactly half the energy drawn, whatever the resistance — and check it: 0.2 μF at 12 V draws 28.8 μJ and loses 14.4 μJ; and you prove, from Tellegen's theorem with a complex conjugate, that the impedance of every RLC network is positive-real — and check it on worked example 5's network: $Z = 2 + 0.5i\ \Omega$ at $s = (1 + i)\ \mathrm s^{-1}$.

Failing it is unambiguous

A half-energy proof whose answer depends on $R$, or that needs $R$ to be ohmic for the second proof; a positive-real proof that never conjugates the currents, or that cannot say why $\bar{\mathbf I}$ still obeys KCL; $\operatorname{Re}(1/(sC))$ taken as negative; a numerical $Z$ with negative real part.
