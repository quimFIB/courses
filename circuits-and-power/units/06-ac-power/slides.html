06

AC power: real, reactive, complex — and conjugate matching

At 50 Hz the power into a load pulses at 100 Hz: one part does work, the other sloshes back and forth and does none — yet its current heats every wire on the way. Complex power $S = VI^*$ keeps both books at once, and Tellegen's theorem says it balances across every network.

needs 05, MR 06~2 h slides + 4 h problemsproblems: ./cap problems 06

Unit 05 built the sinusoidal steady state: RMS phasors $V$, $I$ with $v(t) = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2Ve^{i\omega t})$, impedances $Z = R + iX$, Thévenin's theorem for phasors, and the theorem that every network of positive R, L and C has $\operatorname{Re}Z\ge0$. This unit asks what the power is. The answer has two parts, and the second is the new idea: the instantaneous power $p(t) = v(t)i(t)$ splits into a part with a non-zero mean, which does work, and a part that averages to nothing but still needs current. Complex power $S = VI^*$ packages both; it is conserved across any network, element by element; and two engineering consequences follow — power-factor correction, and conjugate matching.

Instantaneous power at 50 Hz

Multiply $v(t)$ by $i(t)$ and the product pulses at 100 Hz. It splits, exactly, into a part that never changes sign and a part that averages to zero.

06 · instantaneous power

The mean of a product of two sinusoids

Unit 05's RMS phasors: $v(t) = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2\,Ve^{i\omega t})$, $i(t) = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2\,Ie^{i\omega t})$. Take $V$ real and write $\varphi = \arg V - \arg I$, so

$$v = \sqrt2|V|\cos\omega t,\qquad i = \sqrt2|I|\cos(\omega t - \varphi).$$

$\varphi > 0$: the current lags the voltage by $\varphi/\omega$ in time. The instantaneous power into the load is $p(t) = v(t)\,i(t)$, by unit 01's theorem.

the mean, by MR 06's averaging rule

For $z = Ae^{i\omega t}$, $w = Be^{i\omega t}$: $\langle\operatorname{Re}z\,\operatorname{Re}w\rangle = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(A\bar B)$ (cited, MR 06). With $A = \sqrt2V$, $B = \sqrt2I$:

$$\langle p\rangle = \operatorname{Re}(VI^*) = |V||I|\cos\varphi .$$

No ½: it was absorbed into the RMS normalisation, which is why unit 05 chose it. Units: $\mathrm{V\cdot A} = \mathrm W$ ✓.

The workshop's voltage and current over two cycles, each scaled to its own peak (325.3 V and 175.7 A). The current's peaks come $\varphi/\omega = 2.53$ ms after the voltage's.

MR 06's rule, recalled. On the slide Velocity, power, and a width that is exactly $\gamma$, MR 06 proves: with $A = |A|e^{ia}$, $B = |B|e^{ib}$, $\operatorname{Re}z\operatorname{Re}w = |A||B|\cos(\omega t + a)\cos(\omega t + b) = \tfrac12|A||B|[\cos(a - b) + \cos(2\omega t + a + b)]$, and the second term averages to zero over a period, leaving $\tfrac12|A||B|\cos(a - b) = \tfrac12\operatorname{Re}(A\bar B)$. There it gave the mean power a drive delivers to an oscillator, with peak amplitudes and a ½. Here $A = \sqrt2V$ and $B = \sqrt2I$ are √2 times the RMS phasors, so the ½ cancels the 2: $\langle p\rangle = \operatorname{Re}(V\bar I)$. This course writes the conjugate as $I^*$; MR 06 wrote $\bar B$. Same operation. Unit 05's slide RMS, and the phasor put the $\sqrt2$ into the definition for exactly this moment, and ended "Unit 06 develops AC power": this is where the ½ it removed would have reappeared.

MR 06's angle is not this one. MR 06's $\varphi$ was the lag of the oscillator's displacement behind the force, so its mean power came out with $\sin\varphi$ (the velocity is a quarter cycle ahead of the displacement). Here $\varphi$ is the lag of the current — the analogue of velocity — behind the voltage, the analogue of force, and the cosine appears. Unit 05's dictionary (charge ↔ displacement, current ↔ velocity) makes the two agree.

Why $p = vi$ at all. Unit 01 proved that, for quasi-static fields outside a two-terminal component, the Poynting flux into any closed surface round it is $v(t)i(t)$ at every instant. Unit 02's lumped hypotheses are what make the fields quasi-static here: at 50 Hz the wavelength is 6000 km.

The figure's numbers. $\hat V = \sqrt2\times230 = 325.3$ V; $\hat I = \sqrt2\times124.2 = 175.7$ A; $\varphi = \arccos0.7 = 0.7954$ rad $= 45.57^\circ$; $\varphi/\omega = 0.7954/(2\pi\cdot50) = 2.53$ ms.

06 · instantaneous power

$p(t)$ splits in two

the two parts of $p(t)$

With $v$, $i$ as above, and $V$, $I$ now short for $|V|$, $|I|$:

$$\begin{aligned}p(t) &= \underbrace{VI\cos\varphi\,(1 + \cos2\omega t)}_{\text{never negative}}\\ &\quad + \underbrace{VI\sin\varphi\,\sin2\omega t}_{\text{mean }0}.\end{aligned}$$
proof

$2\cos\omega t\cos(\omega t - \varphi) = \cos\varphi + \cos(2\omega t - \varphi)$, and $\cos(2\omega t - \varphi) = \cos2\omega t\cos\varphi + \sin2\omega t\sin\varphi$. Multiply by $VI$. $\square$

The first part is the mean, the real power $P = VI\cos\varphi$, delivered in pulses at 100 Hz. The second carries no net energy; its amplitude is the reactive power $Q = VI\sin\varphi$. Together $p$ swings between $P - VI$ and $P + VI$.

The workshop's $p(t)$, computed, over one 50 Hz cycle: two pulses. The dashed part never goes negative; the dotted part averages to zero. Their sum dips below zero twice a cycle.

The identity, in full. $v(t)i(t) = 2VI\cos\omega t\cos(\omega t - \varphi)$, and the product-to-sum formula $2\cos a\cos b = \cos(a - b) + \cos(a + b)$ with $a = \omega t$, $b = \omega t - \varphi$ gives $VI[\cos\varphi + \cos(2\omega t - \varphi)]$. That is the compact form, and the one problem P11 uses. Expanding $\cos(2\omega t - \varphi)$ separates the parts that are in phase with $\cos2\omega t$ and with $\sin2\omega t$: $VI\cos\varphi + VI\cos\varphi\cos2\omega t + VI\sin\varphi\sin2\omega t$.

The range. From the compact form, $p$ runs between $VI(\cos\varphi - 1)$ and $VI(\cos\varphi + 1)$, i.e. $P - VI$ and $P + VI$. Section 2 names $VI$ the apparent power $|S|$.

Why "reactive" is the amplitude of the second part. For a pure inductor, $\varphi = \pi/2$, the first part vanishes and $p = VI\sin2\omega t$: all of the power is this second kind, and it is exactly the rate of change of the inductor's stored energy (next slide). The second part is the share of $p$ that goes into the load's fields and comes back.

The workshop's numbers. $VI = 230\times124.22 = 28.57$ kW; $P = 0.7\times28.57 = 20.00$ kW; $Q = 28.57\sin45.57^\circ = 20.40$ kW in amplitude. So $p$ runs from $20 - 28.57 = -8.57$ kW to $48.57$ kW. The axis scale is 3 px per kW; the curves were computed, not sketched.

06 · instantaneous power

A resistor and an inductor

Resistor, $\varphi = 0$: $p = VI(1 + \cos2\omega t)\ge0$. Energy flows in at every instant, in pulses at 100 Hz, with mean $VI$. Unit 00's instruction to "treat the 10 A as steady" was exact for the mean: the kettle's 2.3 kW is the average of a flow pulsing between 0 and 4.6 kW (unit 01's P13).

Inductor, $\varphi = \pi/2$: $p = VI\sin2\omega t$, mean zero. For a quarter cycle energy flows in and is stored as $\tfrac12Li^2$; for the next it flows back out. Check: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\tfrac12Li^2 = Li\,\frac{\mathrm di}{\mathrm dt} = vi$ ✓ (unit 02).

Capacitor, $\varphi = -\pi/2$: the same with the sign reversed; unit 01's P13 found the swing, 166 W for $10\ \mu$F.

one convention

$P$ and $Q$ are defined for sinusoidal $v$ and $i$. Unit 14 meets currents that are not sinusoids, where "reactive power" needs a more careful definition.

One cycle each, $v$ and $i$ normalised. For the resistor $v$ and $i$ change sign together, so $p$ never does.

Unit 00's forward reference, landed. Unit 00 told the reader to treat the kettle's 10 A as steady "until unit 06 develops AC power". For a resistor $P = VI$ with RMS values, exactly unit 00's steady-current formula, so every mean power and every mean heating rate unit 00 computed was right. What was hidden is the 100 Hz pulsation: the kettle's element is heated at a rate that rises from 0 to 4.6 kW and back a hundred times a second. Its thermal mass smooths that completely.

Unit 01's P13, recalled. A $10\ \mu$F capacitor on 230 V, 50 Hz: $p = -\tfrac12C\omega\hat V^2\sin2\omega t$, amplitude $\tfrac12(10^{-5})(2\pi\cdot50)(325.3)^2 = 166$ W. In this unit's language that amplitude is $|Q| = \omega CV^2 = 166$ var with RMS $V = 230$ V — the same number, since $\hat V^2/2 = V^2$. Unit 01 promised that "unit 06 calls the swinging part reactive power".

The inductor's energy check, as a mean. $\tfrac12Li^2$ is periodic, so its derivative averages to zero; hence $\langle p\rangle = 0$ for any pure inductor, whatever $L$. The same for a capacitor with $\tfrac12Cv^2$. Energy balance forces it.

Why the warning. The decomposition on the previous slide used that $v$ and $i$ are sinusoids at one frequency. For distorted waveforms — the currents drawn by rectifiers, unit 14 — the mean of $vi$ is still the real power, but the remainder no longer has one amplitude, and several inequivalent "reactive powers" have been proposed. This course's $Q$ is the sinusoidal one only.

Real, reactive, apparent, complex

Three products of $V$ and $I$, three names for one unit, and one complex number that holds two of them — with a physical meaning for $Q$: stored energy, sloshing.

06 · definitions

Complex power

four powers

For a two-terminal element with RMS phasors $V$, $I$ under the passive convention, $\varphi = \arg V - \arg I$:

  • complex power $S = VI^* = P + iQ$;
  • real power $P = \operatorname{Re}S = |V||I|\cos\varphi$, in W;
  • reactive power $Q = \operatorname{Im}S = |V||I|\sin\varphi$, in var;
  • apparent power $|S| = |V||I|$, in VA.

$VI^* = |V||I|e^{i(\arg V - \arg I)} = |V||I|e^{i\varphi}$, so $\arg S = \varphi$. $P$ is the mean of $p(t)$ (section 1).

three names, one dimension

W, var and VA are all $\mathrm{V\cdot A} = \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}$. The names say which product is meant: a "20 kW" and a "20 kvar" load are different things. The var is the "volt-ampere reactive".

The phasors at $t = 0$, $V$ taken real. The component of $I$ along $V$ carries $P$; the component at right angles carries $Q$.

Why $VI^*$ and not $VI$. $VI$ would have argument $\arg V + \arg I$, which depends on where $t = 0$ is put: shift time by $t_0$ and both phasors rotate by $e^{i\omega t_0}$, so $VI$ rotates by $e^{2i\omega t_0}$. $VI^*$ is unchanged by that rotation — $e^{i\omega t_0}e^{-i\omega t_0} = 1$ — so it is a property of the load, not of the clock. And its real part is the mean power, by MR 06's rule.

The component picture. Write $I = I_p + I_q$ with $I_p$ along $V$ and $I_q$ at right angles. Then $VI_p^*$ is real and $VI_q^*$ is imaginary: $P = |V||I_p|$, $|Q| = |V||I_q|$. So $P$ is "voltage times the in-phase current" and $Q$ is "voltage times the quadrature current". Section 4 shows both components heat the cable.

Conventions, stated as conventions. AUTHORING.md fixes, for the whole course: $S = VI^*$ (some texts use $V^*I$, which flips the sign of $Q$); $Q > 0$ for an inductive load, whose current lags; and $\varphi = \arg V - \arg I$. The names "real" (or "active"), "reactive" and "apparent" power and the units W, var and VA are those used throughout power engineering; this deck quotes no standard for them, because the one that defines them could not be checked from here.

Units. $\mathrm V\cdot\mathrm A = (\mathrm{J\,C^{-1}})(\mathrm{C\,s^{-1}}) = \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm W$ ✓, for all three.

One letter, two quantities. Unit 05 used $Q$ for a resonator's quality factor, $\omega_0L/R$, a pure number. From here on $Q$ is also reactive power, in var; the units say which is meant. They meet once: in a series RLC at resonance the inductor's reactive power $\omega_0L|I|^2$ is exactly the quality factor times the real power $R|I|^2$.

06 · definitions

For an impedance: the resistance takes $P$, the reactance $Q$

For an impedance $Z = R + iX$ with $V = ZI$:

$$\boxed{\,S = VI^* = Z|I|^2 = \frac{|V|^2}{Z^*}\,}$$

So $P = R|I|^2$ and $Q = X|I|^2$: the resistance absorbs the real power, the reactance the reactive power. And $\varphi = \arg Z$.

  • Resistor: $S = R|I|^2$, real.
  • Inductor, $Z = i\omega L$: $Q = \omega L|I|^2 > 0$. An inductive load absorbs positive $Q$.
  • Capacitor, $Z = 1/i\omega C$: $S = |V|^2/(1/i\omega C)^* = -i\omega C|V|^2$, so $Q = -\omega C|V|^2\lt0$.

Unit 05's $\operatorname{Re}Z\ge0$ for every passive network is now $P\ge0$: a passive load never returns net energy.

Units: $\Omega\cdot\mathrm A^2 = \mathrm{V\,A}$ ✓; $\mathrm V^2/\Omega = \mathrm{V\,A}$ ✓.

Where $S$ lies. Unit 05's positive-real theorem confines every passive load to the right half-plane; the sign of $Q$ says inductive or capacitive.

Full proof of the box. $S = VI^* = (ZI)I^* = Z|I|^2$. And $I = V/Z$, so $I^* = V^*/Z^*$ and $S = VV^*/Z^* = |V|^2/Z^*$. Real and imaginary parts of $Z|I|^2$ give $P = R|I|^2$, $Q = X|I|^2$; and since $|I|^2 > 0$, $\arg S = \arg Z$, so $\varphi = \arg Z$.

Limits, as a check. $X\to0$: $Q\to0$, $S = R|I|^2$, unit 02's Joule heating ✓. A short ($Z = 0$) absorbs nothing at finite current; an open circuit ($|Z|\to\infty$) absorbs nothing at finite voltage, by $|V|^2/Z^*\to0$ ✓.

Unit 05's theorem, read as power. Unit 05 proved that the impedance of any network of positive R, L, C has $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega)\ge0$, from Tellegen's theorem: $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega) = \sum_R R|I_e|^2/|I|^2$. Multiplying by $|I|^2$ it says $P = \sum_RR|I_e|^2$: the real power into the network is the heat in its resistors. Unit 05 left that mean power to be named here; it is $P$.

Which loads are capacitive. A capacitor bank (section 5); a long unloaded cable, whose capacitance between its conductors dominates (unit 08 and unit 14). Loads that contain coils — motors, the transformers of unit 07 — are inductive; the claim is about where reactance comes from, not a statistic about which loads are common.

06 · definitions

The power triangle, and the power factor

$S = P + iQ$ drawn in the plane is the power triangle: sides $P$ and $Q$, hypotenuse $|S|$, angle $\varphi$ at the origin.

power factor

$$\mathrm{pf} = \cos\varphi = \frac{P}{|S|} = \frac{P}{|V||I|},$$ described as lagging when $Q > 0$ (current lags voltage: inductive) and leading when $Q\lt0$ (capacitive).

The power factor says how much of the current you pay for in copper does work: at fixed $P$ and $|V|$,

$$|I| = \frac{P}{|V|\cos\varphi}.$$

The cosine alone does not say the sign of $\varphi$; the word "lagging" or "leading" does.

One triangle for each sign of $Q$. The power factor is the ratio of the bottom side to the hypotenuse.

Pythagoras. $|S|^2 = P^2 + Q^2$, since $S = P + iQ$. So, given any two of $P$, $Q$, $|S|$, $\cos\varphi$ (with its sense), the others follow. Also $Q = P\tan\varphi$, used for correction in section 5.

Range. For a passive load $P\ge0$, so $|\varphi|\le\pi/2$ and $0\le\cos\varphi\le1$. Unity power factor means $Q = 0$: the load looks resistive at this frequency, which a resistor does, and so does a tuned circuit at resonance (next slides).

Units of the boxed formula. $\mathrm W/(\mathrm V\cdot1) = \mathrm A$ ✓. A limit: $\cos\varphi\to0$ at fixed $P$ sends $|I|\to\infty$ — a nearly pure reactance can only absorb a given real power by drawing an enormous current, all but a sliver of it in quadrature.

06 · definitions

What $Q$ is: stored energy, sloshing

reactive power from stored energy

For a one-port network of positive R, L and C in the sinusoidal steady state,

$$\boxed{\,Q = 2\omega\big(\langle W_m\rangle - \langle W_e\rangle\big)\,}$$

where $\langle W_m\rangle = \sum_L\tfrac12L|I_e|^2$ and $\langle W_e\rangle = \sum_C\tfrac12C|V_e|^2$ are the mean energies stored in its inductors and capacitors.

proof, from unit 05's sum

Unit 05's Tellegen identity at $s = i\omega$: $VI^* = \sum_RR|I_e|^2 + \sum_Li\omega L|I_e|^2 + \sum_C|I_e|^2/(i\omega C)$. Take imaginary parts and use $|I_e| = \omega C|V_e|$ on each capacitor. $\square$

Units: $\mathrm s^{-1}\cdot\mathrm J = \mathrm W$ ✓ — $Q$ is a rate of energy.

A series LC at resonance, $\omega^2LC = 1$: energy passes between the coil and the capacitor and none goes back to the source. Where the means differ, the source must carry the difference.

Full proof

Drive the one-port with a sinusoidal source; let $V_e$, $I_e$ be the branch phasors. Unit 05 showed that the phasors obey Kirchhoff's laws (they are linear with real coefficients), and that the conjugated currents $I_e^*$ still obey the current law because the incidence matrix $A$ is real: $AI^* = (AI)^* = 0$. Tellegen's theorem (unit 03), whose one-line proof $V^{\mathsf T}J = \Phi^{\mathsf T}AJ = 0$ never used that the vectors are real, then gives $\sum_eV_eI_e^* = 0$ over every branch, the driving source included. The source's branch contributes $-VI^*$ under the passive convention, so

$VI^* = \sum_RR|I_e|^2 + \sum_Li\omega L|I_e|^2 + \sum_C\dfrac{|I_e|^2}{i\omega C}.$

The imaginary part: $Q = \omega\sum_LL|I_e|^2 - \sum_C|I_e|^2/(\omega C)$. On a capacitor $I_e = i\omega CV_e$, so $|I_e|^2/(\omega C) = \omega C|V_e|^2$. Hence $Q = 2\omega\big(\sum_L\tfrac12L|I_e|^2 - \sum_C\tfrac12C|V_e|^2\big)$. Finally, $\langle\tfrac12Li^2\rangle = \tfrac12L\langle i^2\rangle = \tfrac12L|I_e|^2$, because the mean of $i^2$ is the square of the RMS value (unit 05's definition) — this is where RMS phasors make the formula clean. The same for $\tfrac12Cv^2$. $\square$

Special cases. An inductor alone: $Q = \omega L|I|^2 = 2\omega\langle W_m\rangle$. A capacitor alone: $Q = -\omega C|V|^2 = -2\omega\langle W_e\rangle$. A tuned series LC at resonance: the same current through both, $\omega L|I|^2 = |I|^2/(\omega C)$, so $\langle W_m\rangle = \langle W_e\rangle$ and $Q = 0$. Unit 05's resonance — $Z$ real at $\omega_0$ — is exactly this statement.

Why "twice $\omega$". Stored energy oscillates at $2\omega$, since it goes as $\cos^2$. For an inductor alone, $W_m(t) = \langle W_m\rangle(1 + \cos2\omega t')$ with $t'$ measured from a current peak: a swing of amplitude $\langle W_m\rangle$ at angular frequency $2\omega$, whose rate of change has amplitude $2\omega\langle W_m\rangle = Q$. So $Q$ is the amplitude of the rate at which stored energy is pushed into the load and taken back.

Coupled coils. With mutual inductance (unit 02's inductance matrix $\mathbf L$, unit 07's transformers) the inductor terms become $i\omega\,I^{\mathsf H}\mathbf LI$, still $2i\omega\langle W_m\rangle$ since $\mathbf L$ is real and symmetric; the theorem stands. Problem P2 proves the uncoupled case.

For the workshop. $\langle W_m\rangle - \langle W_e\rangle = Q/2\omega = 20404/628.3 = 32.5$ J: about 32 J more is stored, on average, in the workshop's magnetic fields than in its electric ones, and the supply pushes it in and takes it back a hundred times a second.

Complex power is conserved

Unit 03's Tellegen theorem, with voltages from a network and the conjugates of its currents: real and reactive power each balance, element by element. Apparent power does not.

06 · conservation

The conjugate currents obey the current law

A network of any linear elements and sinusoidal sources at one frequency, on a graph with incidence matrix $A$. Its branch phasors obey Kirchhoff's laws (unit 05): $V = A^{\mathsf T}\Phi$ and $AI = 0$.

$A$ is real, so conjugating the current law gives $AI^* = (AI)^* = 0$: the conjugates are also a current distribution on the same graph.

the conservation of complex power

$$\sum_{\text{all elements}}S_e = \sum_eV_eI_e^* = 0 .$$ Hence $\sum_eP_e = 0$ and $\sum_eQ_e = 0$ separately.

proof

$V^{\mathsf T}I^* = (A^{\mathsf T}\Phi)^{\mathsf T}I^* = \Phi^{\mathsf T}(AI^*) = 0$: unit 03's proof of Tellegen's theorem, unchanged. $\square$

The current law at a node says its current phasors close into a polygon. Conjugation reflects the polygon in the real axis, and a reflected closed polygon is closed.

Unit 03's promise, landed. Unit 03 said that "unit 06 uses the same pairing to prove that complex power is conserved": $v$ from the network, $i$ from its complex conjugate. Unit 05 used exactly this pairing at complex frequency $s$ to prove $\operatorname{Re}Z\ge0$; here it is used at $s = i\omega$ for every element at once.

Why the bilinear form, not the Hermitian one. Tellegen's orthogonality is $\sum_ev_ej_e = 0$ for $v\in\operatorname{im}A^{\mathsf T}$ and $j\in\ker A$. Over $\mathbb C$ the same one-line proof holds for the bilinear sum (no conjugation), because it only uses associativity of matrix products. So we may pair $V$ with $J = I^*$, which lies in $\ker A$ because $A$ is real. Pairing $V$ with $I$ itself is also legitimate — $\sum V_eI_e = 0$ too — but that sum is not a power (it depends on the time origin, as the definitions slide showed).

What is summed. Every branch, under the passive convention: sources contribute $S_e$ with negative real part when they deliver real power, and with negative imaginary part when they deliver reactive power (supplying an inductive load). "What the sources deliver, the rest absorb" — now twice, once for $P$ and once for $Q$.

Instantaneous power, too. Unit 03's theorem applied to the instantaneous $v_e(t)$, $i_e(t)$ already gave $\sum p_e(t) = 0$ at every instant. Its mean is $\sum P_e = 0$. The statement $\sum Q_e = 0$ is new: it is not the mean of anything, and needs the phasor version.

Correcting the power factor

A capacitor across the load supplies its reactive power locally. The cable then carries only the in-phase current, and its loss falls by $\cos^2\varphi$.

06 · correction

A capacitor across the load

Power-factor correction: connect a capacitor $C$ across the load, at its terminals. Its complex power (the definitions):

$$S_C = -i\omega C|V|^2,\qquad Q_C = -\omega C|V|^2 .$$

The load's $P$ and $Q$ are unchanged — it sees the same 230 V. By conservation the cable now delivers $P + i(Q + Q_C)$. To cancel $Q$ entirely:

$$\boxed{\,C = \frac{Q}{\omega|V|^2}\,}$$

Units: $\mathrm{var}/(\mathrm s^{-1}\,\mathrm V^2) = \mathrm{A\,s\,V^{-1}} = \mathrm{C\,V^{-1}} = \mathrm F$ ✓.

In stored-energy terms: the capacitor's mean electric energy is made equal to the load's excess of magnetic energy, $\tfrac12C|V|^2 = \langle W_m\rangle - \langle W_e\rangle$. The energy sloshes between the motors and the capacitor, both at the workshop end, instead of along 50 m of cable.

The current law at the intake: $I_s = I_L + I_C$. The capacitor changes what the cable carries, not what the workshop takes.

$S_C$ in one line. $Z_C = 1/(i\omega C)$, so $S_C = |V|^2/Z_C^* = |V|^2/(1/(-i\omega C)) = -i\omega C|V|^2$. A capacitor "supplies" reactive power in the sense of the convention: it absorbs negative $Q$. Equivalently, $Q_C = -2\omega\langle W_e\rangle$ by the stored-energy theorem.

Why the load is unchanged. The capacitor is in parallel with the workshop, and the workshop's voltage is held at 230 V in this model. So the workshop's current and complex power are what they were. (If instead the supply's voltage is held fixed, the workshop's voltage changes slightly after correction; the notes of The current falls by $\cos\varphi$, the loss by $\cos^2\varphi$ say by how much.)

Limits. $C\to0$: nothing changes. $\omega\to0$: $C = Q/\omega|V|^2\to\infty$ — but at DC there is no $Q$ to cancel; the formula is for one frequency and the capacitor is right only at that frequency. A load whose $Q$ varies needs a $C$ that varies: the switched banks of Capacitor banks in the switch room.

full correction

With $C = Q/\omega|V|^2$, the supply current falls from $|S|/|V|$ to $P/|V|$, by the factor $\cos\varphi$; the loss in a resistive cable, $R|I_s|^2$, falls by $\cos^2\varphi$.

proof

The cable delivers $S_s = P + i(Q - \omega C|V|^2) = P$, so $|I_s| = P/|V| = (|S|/|V|)\cos\varphi$. Square, and multiply by $R$. $\square$

partial correction

To raise the power factor from $\cos\varphi_1$ to $\cos\varphi_2$ (both lagging) at fixed $P$:

$$-Q_C = P(\tan\varphi_1 - \tan\varphi_2).$$

Since $Q = P\tan\varphi$ before and after. Units: W ✓.

The workshop corrected, 1.5 px per ampere. The capacitor's current, a quarter cycle ahead of $V$, cancels the workshop's quadrature current exactly.

The proof as currents. $I_C = i\omega CV$ leads $V$ by $90^\circ$, with magnitude $\omega C|V| = Q/|V| = |I_q|$. So $I_C = -I_q$ exactly, and $I_s = I_L + I_C = I_p$: the supply current is the in-phase part alone. The loss $R|I_s|^2 = R|I_p|^2$, which is the 363 W "from $I_p$" of the bill slide; the 378 W "from $I_q$" is gone.

The cable's own reactance, neglected here, does not change that loss: a reactance dissipates nothing. Capacitor banks in the switch room

An installation whose load varies — motors starting and stopping through the day — cannot be corrected by one fixed capacitor without over-correcting at light load. So the correction is done by a capacitor bank:

  • several capacitors connected across the supply at the installation's intake, in the switch room beside the main switchgear;
  • a controller that measures the reactive power drawn;
  • contactors that switch capacitor steps in and out as that $Q$ changes, keeping the power factor near its target.

Placed at the intake, the bank relieves the supply cable and everything upstream of it — the utility's transformer and lines — but not the wiring inside the workshop, which still carries each motor's quadrature current (problem P8).

06 · matching

A source with reactance

By unit 05's Thévenin theorem for phasors, a linear source at one frequency is $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ behind $Z_s = R_s + iX_s$, with $R_s > 0$. A load $Z_L = R_L + iX_L$, $R_L\ge0$, draws

$$I = \frac{V_{\mathrm{th}}}{Z_s + Z_L},$$

and absorbs $P_L = R_L|I|^2$ (the definitions):

$$P_L = \frac{|V_{\mathrm{th}}|^2R_L}{(R_s + R_L)^2 + (X_s + X_L)^2}.$$

Two knobs now. The reactances appear only in the second term of the denominator — and the load can choose $X_L$ of either sign: an inductor, or a capacitor.

Units: $\mathrm V^2\,\Omega/\Omega^2 = \mathrm W$ ✓. Check: $X_s = X_L = 0$ gives unit 04's $V_{\mathrm{th}}^2R/(R_{\mathrm{th}} + R)^2$ ✓.

An inductive source and a capacitive load. The load's two elements are marked + and − together; open circles are the port.

The derivation. One loop: $V_{\mathrm{th}} = (Z_s + Z_L)I$ by the voltage law with phasors. The load's complex power is $Z_L|I|^2$ (the definitions), whose real part is $R_L|I|^2$; and $|Z_s + Z_L|^2 = (R_s + R_L)^2 + (X_s + X_L)^2$.

Where such sources occur. A source whose internal impedance has a reactance: unit 04's antenna feeding a radio receiver (branch 16 shows that a short antenna is mostly reactive); a winding with leakage inductance seen through a transformer (unit 07); a coil in a wireless charger (unit 15). Not the workshop's supply: its cable is 48 mΩ against the workshop's 1.85 Ω, a power station's choice in unit 04's sense, far from any match.

Why $R_L\ge0$. A passive load (unit 05). $X_L$ can be any real number at one frequency, since an inductor gives any positive reactance and a capacitor any negative one.

06 · matching

The conjugate-matching theorem

conjugate matching

Over all passive loads $Z_L$, $P_L$ is largest at the conjugate match $Z_L = Z_s^* = R_s - iX_s$:

$$P_{\max} = \frac{|V_{\mathrm{th}}|^2}{4R_s},$$

and the efficiency there, $R_L|I|^2/(R_s + R_L)|I|^2$, is exactly $\tfrac12$.

proof

For fixed $R_L$ the denominator is least when $X_L = -X_s$. Then $P_L = |V_{\mathrm{th}}|^2R_L/(R_s + R_L)^2$, which unit 04's theorem maximises at $R_L = R_s$. So any $Z_L$ is beaten by $R_L - iX_s$, which is beaten by $Z_s^*$. $\square$

Unit 04's theorem "becomes conjugate matching": cancel the reactance, then match the resistance.

The inductive source and its conjugate match, 24 px per ohm. The loop's reactances cancel; what is left is unit 04's resistive problem.

Why the efficiency is defined this way. $R_s|I|^2$ is the power dissipated inside the Thévenin equivalent and $R_L|I|^2$ in the load; the reactances dissipate nothing. So "efficiency" is the load's share of the real power the equivalent's EMF delivers, as in unit 04, with the same caveat: when the source is a real network, its internal losses are not those of its equivalent.

Energy check. The EMF delivers $\operatorname{Re}(V_{\mathrm{th}}I^*) = \operatorname{Re}((Z_s + Z_L)|I|^2) = (R_s + R_L)|I|^2$ ✓, and $\operatorname{Im}$ of the same is $(X_s + X_L)|I|^2$, the loop's net reactive power — zero at the match. Conservation of complex power, for one loop.

At the match the source sees a resistance. The total loop impedance $Z_s + Z_s^* = 2R_s$ is real: the current is in phase with $V_{\mathrm{th}}$, the loop draws no net reactive power, and the reactive energy of the source's inductance sloshes into the load's capacitance and back — the tuned circuit of the stored-energy slide, built across a port.

Only at one frequency. $X_L = -X_s$ is a statement at one $\omega$. Unit 05's Foster theorem says a lossless reactance rises with frequency, so a capacitor cancelling an inductance does so at one frequency only; matching over a band is a harder problem (unit 09 begins it, on a line).

Unit 15. A wireless charger meets a problem in which the load of maximum efficiency is not the conjugate match: maximum power and maximum efficiency are different design points, again, as unit 04 found for the starter motor.

06 · matching

When only the resistance can change

Often the load cannot carry a reactance — a heater, a resistive sensor. With $X_L = 0$:

$$P_L(R_L) = \frac{|V_{\mathrm{th}}|^2R_L}{(R_s + R_L)^2 + X_s^2}.$$
the best resistive load

$P_L$ is largest at $R_L = |Z_s| = \sqrt{R_s^2 + X_s^2}$, where $P_L = \dfrac{|V_{\mathrm{th}}|^2}{2(R_s + |Z_s|)}$ — less than $P_{\max}$ unless $X_s = 0$.

proof

$P_L = |V_{\mathrm{th}}|^2/f(R_L)$ with $f(R) = R + 2R_s + |Z_s|^2/R$. By AM–GM, $R + |Z_s|^2/R\ge2|Z_s|$, with equality iff $R = |Z_s|$. So $f\ge2(R_s + |Z_s|)$. $\square$

Matching the magnitude is the best a resistor can do; the reactance it cannot cancel costs power.

Computed for the inductive source. The two curves differ only by the uncancelled $X_s^2 = 9\ \Omega^2$ in the denominator.

The proof by calculus, too. $\mathrm dP_L/\mathrm dR_L\propto(R_s + R_L)^2 + X_s^2 - 2R_L(R_s + R_L) = R_s^2 + X_s^2 - R_L^2$, positive below $|Z_s|$ and negative above. The AM–GM proof needs no derivative and bounds every $R_L$ at once, like unit 04's.

The value at the optimum. $f(|Z_s|) = |Z_s| + 2R_s + |Z_s| = 2(R_s + |Z_s|)$, so $P_L = |V_{\mathrm{th}}|^2/2(R_s + |Z_s|)$. Since $|Z_s|\ge R_s$ with equality iff $X_s = 0$, this is at most $|V_{\mathrm{th}}|^2/4R_s$ ✓, and equals it only for a resistive source — unit 04's case.

Efficiency at the resistive optimum. $R_L/(R_s + R_L) = |Z_s|/(R_s + |Z_s|) > \tfrac12$. Less power, but a larger share of it delivered: the uncancelled reactance limits the current. For the inductive source, $5/9 = 55.6\%$.
