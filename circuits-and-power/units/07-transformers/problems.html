Transformers, and why power lines run at 400 kV

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You derive the ideal transformer as a limit of coupled inductors, prove that a transformer's efficiency peaks at the load where copper loss equals core loss, and show that sending 100 MW down a 10 Ω line loses 0.6% at 400 kV and more than the whole 100 MW at 20 kV.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about three and a half hours. P2, P8 and P9 are the checkpoint's. Conventions as in the deck: RMS phasors with \(e^{+i\omega t}\); both ports of a transformer marked by the passive convention, with both reference currents entering the dotted terminals; turns ratio \(n = N_1/N_2\); an ideal transformer obeys \(V_1 = nV_2\), \(I_1 = -I_2/n\); complex power \(S = VI^*\); \(\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\) H/m; 50 Hz throughout, \(\omega = 314.16\) rad/s.

The street transformer (single-phase model): 400 kVA, 20 kV / 400 V, \(n = 50\); no-load loss \(P_0 = 387\) W and load loss \(P_k = 3250\) W (the Tier 2 limits of Regulation (EU) 548/2014, Table I.1); short-circuit impedance 4% (IEC 60076-5, Table 1); no-load current 0.5% of rated, assumed. Unit 06's workshop: 20 kW at power factor 0.7 lagging from 230 V, single-phase, assumed. Unit 05's ignition coil: primary \(L = 6\) mH, a capacitor \(C = 0.2\ \mu\)F across the contact, 4 A when the contact opens, all assumed.

Coupled inductors and the ideal limit

P1 \(k \le 1\) and the dots routine

exercises: the coupling coefficient, unit 02's energy bound, reading the dot convention from a winding

  1. Two coupled coils have \(L_1 = 40\) mH, \(L_2 = 10\) mH and \(M = 15\) mH. Find \(k\).
  2. A data sheet claims \(M = 25\) mH for the same two coils. Show the claim is impossible by exhibiting two currents for which unit 02's energy \(W = \tfrac12L_1i_1^2 + Mi_1i_2 + \tfrac12L_2i_2^2\) would be negative.
  3. Two windings sit on one vertical limb of a core. Winding A runs from terminal \(a_1\) at the top to \(a_2\) at the bottom, going clockwise round the limb as seen from above. Winding B runs from \(b_1\) at the top to \(b_2\) at the bottom, going anticlockwise as seen from above. Which terminal of B gets the dot if A's dot is at \(a_1\)?
  4. With the dots placed as in part 3, the reference current of B is drawn into \(b_1\). What sign does \(M\) take in the coupled equations?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a number between 0 and 1. Part 2 wants a pair \((i_1, i_2)\). Parts 3 and 4 are about which way a current circulates round the limb, hence which way its flux points.

Rung 2

For part 2, the quadratic \(W/i_1^2\) in \(x = i_2/i_1\) is smallest at \(x = -M/L_2\). For part 3, reversing the direction in which you travel along a helix reverses the sense in which you circulate round its axis.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: take \(i_1 = 1\) A and \(i_2 = -M/L_2 = -2.5\) A.

Worked solution

Idea. \(|k| \le 1\) is a statement that a field energy cannot be negative; the dot is a statement about which way a current drives flux.

1. \(k = 15/\sqrt{40\times10} = 15/20 = 0.75\).

2. With \(M = 25\) mH, \(k = 25/20 = 1.25 \gt 1\). Take \(i_1 = 1\) A, \(i_2 = -2.5\) A: \[W = \tfrac12(0.040)(1) + (0.025)(1)(-2.5) + \tfrac12(0.010)(6.25) = 0.0200 - 0.0625 + 0.03125 = -0.01125\ \mathrm J .\] A magnetic field's energy is \(\int B^2/2\mu_0\,\mathrm dV \ge 0\), so no pair of coils has these three inductances.

3. Current entering \(a_1\) travels down A and circulates clockwise seen from above, so by the right-hand rule its flux in the limb points down. Current entering \(b_1\) travels down B, anticlockwise seen from above: flux up. Current entering \(b_2\) travels up B, which reverses its sense of circulation: clockwise seen from above, flux down, the same as A's. So the dots are at \(a_1\) and \(b_2\).

4. With B's reference current into the undotted \(b_1\), a positive \(i_2\) drives flux against \(i_1\)'s: the mutual flux linkages subtract, and the equations carry \(-|M|\) where they had \(+|M|\) (for coils like those of part 1, \(M = -15\) mH). The physics is unchanged; only the reference direction moved.

Check. Part 2's numbers: the minimum of \(W/i_1^2\) is \(\tfrac12L_1 - M^2/(2L_2) = 0.020 - 0.03125 = -0.01125\) J/A\(^2\), matching. With the data sheet's own \(M\) replaced by 15 mH it would be \(0.020 - 0.01125 = +0.00875\) ✓. Units: \(\mathrm{H\,A^2} = \mathrm J\) ✓.

P2 Proof: the ideal transformer as a limit medium

exercises: the coupled-inductor equations in phasors, the magnetising current, the order of limits — the checkpoint's first part

Two coupled inductors \(L_1, L_2, M\) at angular frequency \(\omega\) feed a load \(Z_L\) on the secondary (\(V_2 = -Z_LI_2\)).

  1. Show that \(Z_{\text{in}} = V_1/I_1 = i\omega L_1 + \omega^2M^2/(Z_L + i\omega L_2)\).
  2. At \(k = 1\), with \(n = \sqrt{L_1/L_2}\), show that \(V_1 = nV_2\) exactly, that \(I_1 + I_2/n = V_1/(i\omega L_1)\), and that \(Z_{\text{in}}\) is \(i\omega L_1\) in parallel with \(n^2Z_L\).
  3. Let \(L_1 \to \infty\) with \(k = 1\) and \(L_1/L_2 = n^2\) fixed. Show that \(V_1 = nV_2\), \(I_1 = -I_2/n\) and \(Z_{\text{in}} = n^2Z_L\) in the limit, and that the energy stored tends to zero.
  4. Now hold \(k \lt 1\) fixed and let \(L_1 \to \infty\). Show that \(Z_{\text{in}}\) does not tend to \(n^2Z_L\), and find what it does.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every part is algebra on the two equations \(V_1 = i\omega L_1I_1 + i\omega MI_2\), \(V_2 = i\omega MI_1 + i\omega L_2I_2\). The answers are identities, a limit, and (in part 4) a reactance that grows.

Rung 2

At \(k = 1\) both right-hand sides contain the same bracket \(\sqrt{L_1}I_1 + \sqrt{L_2}I_2\). For part 4, write \(a = i\omega L_2\) and \(L_1 = n^2L_2\), and simplify \(Z_{\text{in}}\) to a single fraction in \(a\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: from the second equation, \(-Z_LI_2 = i\omega MI_1 + i\omega L_2I_2\), so \(I_2 = -i\omega MI_1/(Z_L + i\omega L_2)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Perfect coupling makes the voltage ratio exact for any currents; the currents then differ from the ideal relation only by the magnetising current \(V_1/(i\omega L_1)\), which an infinite inductance kills. Imperfect coupling leaves a leakage reactance that the same limit inflates rather than kills.

1. \(I_2 = -i\omega MI_1/(Z_L + i\omega L_2)\) (rung 3). Then \(V_1 = i\omega L_1I_1 + i\omega M I_2 = I_1\big[i\omega L_1 + (i\omega M)(-i\omega M)/(Z_L + i\omega L_2)\big]\), and \((i\omega M)(-i\omega M) = \omega^2M^2\).

2. With \(M = \sqrt{L_1L_2}\): \(V_1 = i\omega\sqrt{L_1}\,(\sqrt{L_1}I_1 + \sqrt{L_2}I_2)\) and \(V_2 = i\omega\sqrt{L_2}\,(\sqrt{L_1}I_1 + \sqrt{L_2}I_2)\). So \(V_1 = \sqrt{L_1/L_2}\,V_2 = nV_2\) whatever the currents. Dividing the first by \(i\omega L_1\): \(V_1/(i\omega L_1) = I_1 + \sqrt{L_2/L_1}\,I_2 = I_1 + I_2/n\). For the impedance, \(\omega^2M^2 = \omega^2L_1L_2 = -(i\omega L_1)(i\omega L_2)\), so over a common denominator \[Z_{\text{in}} = \frac{i\omega L_1(Z_L + i\omega L_2) - (i\omega L_1)(i\omega L_2)}{Z_L + i\omega L_2} = \frac{i\omega L_1Z_L}{Z_L + i\omega L_2} = \frac{i\omega L_1\cdot n^2Z_L}{n^2Z_L + i\omega L_1},\] multiplying top and bottom by \(n^2\) and using \(n^2L_2 = L_1\). That is the parallel combination.

3. \(V_1 = nV_2\) holds for every \(L_1\). For fixed \(V_1\), \(|I_1 + I_2/n| = |V_1|/(\omega L_1) \to 0\), so \(I_1 \to -I_2/n\). And \(Z_{\text{in}} = n^2Z_L/(1 + n^2Z_L/(i\omega L_1)) \to n^2Z_L\). Energy: at \(k = 1\), unit 02's \(W = \tfrac12(\sqrt{L_1}i_1 + \sqrt{L_2}i_2)^2 = \tfrac12L_1i_m^2\) with \(i_m = i_1 + i_2/n\); for \(v_1 = \hat V\cos\omega t\), \(i_m = \hat V\sin\omega t/(\omega L_1)\), so \(W = \hat V^2\sin^2\omega t/(2\omega^2L_1) \to 0\).

4. Write \(a = i\omega L_2\), so \(i\omega L_1 = n^2a\) and \(\omega^2M^2 = -k^2n^2a^2\). Then \[Z_{\text{in}} = n^2a - \frac{k^2n^2a^2}{Z_L + a} = n^2a\,\frac{Z_L + (1 - k^2)a}{Z_L + a}.\] As \(L_1 \to \infty\), \(a \to \infty\) and the fraction tends to \(1 - k^2\): \(Z_{\text{in}} \approx i\omega(1 - k^2)L_1 \to \infty\). The leakage reactance grows with the inductance. Only if \((1 - k^2)L_1 \to \varepsilon\) does the limit exist, and then it is \(n^2Z_L + i\omega\varepsilon\): an ideal transformer with a series leakage reactance in front. The ideal transformer needs \(\varepsilon = 0\): \(k \to 1\) first.

Check. Limits of part 2's formula: \(Z_L = 0\) gives \(Z_{\text{in}} = 0\) (a short seen as a short) ✓; \(Z_L \to \infty\) gives \(i\omega L_1\), the primary alone ✓. Units: \(\omega^2M^2/Z\) is \(\mathrm{s^{-2}H^2/\Omega} = \Omega\) since \(\mathrm H = \Omega\,\mathrm s\) ✓. Power: in the limit \(V_1I_1^* = nV_2(-I_2/n)^* = -V_2I_2^*\) ✓. A number: the street transformer has \(\omega L_1 = 204\ \mathrm{k\Omega}\) on the 20 kV side, and at full load \(|n^2Z_L| = 20\,000/20 = 1\ \mathrm{k\Omega}\), so the shunt changes \(Z_{\text{in}}\) by about \(1/204 = 0.5\%\).

P3 An output transformer for a loudspeaker routine

exercises: reflected impedance, choosing a turns ratio, power through an ideal transformer, unit 04's correction about matching

A valve amplifier's output stage is designed to drive a load of 5 kΩ (an assumption for this problem: this is the load its designer specified, not the output stage's own internal resistance). It is to drive an 8 Ω loudspeaker, taken as a resistance.

  1. Find the turns ratio \(n = N_1/N_2\) of an ideal output transformer that makes the speaker look like 5 kΩ to the stage.
  2. The stage delivers 10 W. Find the primary and secondary voltages and currents.
  3. Is this "matching" in unit 04's sense? Say what is being matched to what.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a pure number; part 2 four RMS values; part 3 a sentence about which resistance equals which.

Rung 2

A secondary load \(Z_L\) is seen at the primary as \(n^2Z_L\). Power passes unchanged, so \(P = V_1^2/5000 = V_2^2/8\).

Rung 3

First line: \(n^2\times8 = 5000\).

Worked solution

Idea. An ideal transformer turns one resistance into another by \(n^2\), passing the power unchanged.

1. \(n^2 = 5000/8 = 625\), \(n = 25\): the primary has 25 times the turns of the secondary.

2. \(V_1 = \sqrt{PR_1} = \sqrt{10\times5000} = 223.6\) V, \(I_1 = 223.6/5000 = 44.7\) mA. \(V_2 = V_1/n = 8.94\) V, \(|I_2| = n|I_1| = 1.118\) A. Check: \(8.94^2/8 = 10.0\) W ✓.

3. No. Unit 04's maximum-power theorem matches a load to the source's internal resistance, and gives 50% efficiency. Here the speaker is converted to the load resistance the output stage was designed to work into — a design specification of the stage, assumed in the statement, not its internal resistance. Unit 04's notes corrected exactly this: an audio amplifier is not conjugate-matched to its speaker. The transformer is doing what it does on the street — making a load look like the load the source is built for.

Check. Units: \(n\) dimensionless, \(n^2\times\Omega = \Omega\) ✓. Power in \(= V_1I_1 = 223.6\times0.0447 = 10.0\) W \(=\) power out ✓. Limit \(n = 1\): the stage would see 8 Ω, 625 times less than intended.

P4 The T circuit, and the freedom in it medium

exercises: deriving the T equivalent circuit, the free ratio \(a\), negative elements in a model, referring a source impedance

  1. Show that for every \(a \gt 0\) the T network — series \(L_1 - aM\), shunt \(aM\), series \(a^2L_2 - aM\), then an ideal transformer \(a : 1\) — has the same two-port equations as the coupled pair.
  2. For the coils of P1 (\(L_1 = 40\), \(L_2 = 10\), \(M = 15\) mH), give the three T elements for \(a = \sqrt{L_1/L_2}\), \(a = L_1/M\) and \(a = M/L_2\), and show that \(a = 4\) makes one element negative.
  3. Explain why no measurement at the terminals can determine \(a\).
  4. A source with internal impedance \(Z_s\) drives the primary of an ideal transformer of ratio \(n\). Show that, seen from the secondary terminals, it is a Thévenin source of EMF \(V_s/n\) behind \(Z_s/n^2\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is two applications of Kirchhoff's voltage law; part 2 is arithmetic; part 3 is a count of parameters; part 4 uses the ideal transformer's two equations once.

Rung 2

Behind an ideal \(a : 1\) transformer, its primary has \(V_2' = aV_2\) and carries \(I_2' = I_2/a\) into the T. The shunt carries \(I_1 + I_2'\). The coupled pair has three parameters; the T has four.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(V_1 = i\omega(L_1 - aM)I_1 + i\omega aM(I_1 + I_2/a)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Three numbers \((L_1, L_2, M)\) can be dressed as four \((a\) and three inductances\()\) in a one-parameter family of ways.

1. Left loop: \(V_1 = i\omega(L_1 - aM)I_1 + i\omega aM(I_1 + I_2/a) = i\omega L_1I_1 + i\omega MI_2\), the first coupled equation. Right loop: \(aV_2 = i\omega(a^2L_2 - aM)(I_2/a) + i\omega aM(I_1 + I_2/a) = i\omega aL_2I_2 + i\omega aMI_1 = a(i\omega MI_1 + i\omega L_2I_2)\); divide by \(a\): the second.

2. In mH. \(a = 2\): series \(40 - 30 = 10\), shunt \(30\), series \(40 - 30 = 10\) — equal leakages, \((1 - k)L_1\) each, shunt \(kL_1\). \(a = 8/3\): series \(40 - 40 = 0\), shunt \(40\), series \((64/9)(10) - 40 = 31.1\) \(= L_1(1/k^2 - 1)\). \(a = 1.5\): series \(40 - 22.5 = 17.5\) \(= L_1(1 - k^2)\), shunt \(22.5 = k^2L_1\), series \(22.5 - 22.5 = 0\). \(a = 4\): series \(40 - 60 = -20\), shunt 60, series \(160 - 60 = 100\). A negative inductance is not a component anyone can buy, but as an element of a model it is legitimate: the network as a whole still has the coupled pair's (positive semi-definite) inductance matrix.

3. The terminal behaviour is fixed by \(L_1, L_2, M\) — three numbers — and part 1 shows every \(a\) reproduces it. So measurements at the terminals see only those three; \(a\) is invisible. Choosing \(a = N_1/N_2\) uses a fact about the inside (the turn counts), which is why it is the natural choice, and why with it the elements are the permeances of real flux paths.

4. At the primary, \(V_1 = V_s - Z_sI_1\) (current \(I_1\) leaving the source into the primary's dot). With \(V_1 = nV_2\) and \(I_1 = -I_2/n\): \(nV_2 = V_s + Z_sI_2/n\), so \(V_2 = V_s/n + (Z_s/n^2)I_2\). With the secondary marked by the passive convention, that is a port obeying \(v = V_{\text{th}} + Z_{\text{th}}i\) — unit 04's Thévenin form — with \(V_{\text{th}} = V_s/n\), \(Z_{\text{th}} = Z_s/n^2\).

Check. Units: \(a\) dimensionless, every element in H ✓. The sum of the series elements referred to the primary is \(L_1 + a^2L_2 - 2aM\), which for \(a = n = \sqrt{L_1/L_2}\) is \(2(1 - k)L_1 = 20\) mH ✓ (part 2's \(10 + 10\)). Part 4 limit: \(n = 1\) gives back the source itself ✓.

The real transformer

P5 Eddy currents scale as \(d^2\) medium

exercises: Faraday's law pointwise in a conductor, a time and space average, the thin-sheet assumption and the skin depth

  1. A steel sheet of thickness \(d\) and conductivity \(\sigma\) carries a uniform flux density \(B = \hat B\cos\omega t\) along its length. Show that the time-averaged power dissipated per unit volume by eddy currents is \(p = \sigma\omega^2\hat B^2d^2/24\).
  2. With \(\rho = 0.5\ \mu\Omega\,\)m, density \(7650\ \mathrm{kg/m^3}\) (both assumed), \(\hat B = 1.5\) T and 50 Hz, find \(p\) in W/kg for \(d = 0.3\) mm and 0.5 mm.
  3. Branch 10 proves that an alternating field penetrates a conductor of permeability \(\mu\) to a skin depth \(\delta = \sqrt{2/(\omega\mu\sigma)}\). With an assumed \(\mu_r = 1000\), find \(\delta\) for this steel at 50 Hz, and say why the formula of part 1 would overstate the loss in a 5 mm plate.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 wants a field \(E(x)\) across the thickness, then \(\sigma E^2\) averaged twice. Part 2 is two numbers; part 3 one length and a sentence.

Rung 2

By symmetry \(\vec E = E_y(x)\hat y\), odd in \(x\). The \(z\) component of \(\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t\) is \(\partial_xE_y\). The averages: \(\langle\sin^2\rangle = \tfrac12\), and \(\frac1d\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}x^2\,\mathrm dx = d^2/12\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\partial_xE_y = -\dot B\), so \(E_y = -x\dot B\).

Worked solution

Idea. In a thin sheet the induced field grows linearly from the mid-plane, so the dissipation density grows as \(x^2\), and its average over the sheet as \(d^2\).

1. Coordinates: \(x\) across the sheet (\(|x| \le d/2\)), \(z\) along the flux. Far from the edges nothing depends on \(y\) or \(z\); reflecting \(x \to -x\) maps the problem to itself, so \(E_y\) is odd in \(x\) and \(E_x = 0\). Faraday (MR 24): \(\partial_xE_y = -\dot B\), so \(E_y = -x\dot B\). Ohm: power density \(\sigma E_y^2 = \sigma x^2\dot B^2\), with \(\dot B = -\omega\hat B\sin\omega t\). Time average: \(\tfrac12\sigma x^2\omega^2\hat B^2\). Space average over the thickness: \(\tfrac12\sigma\omega^2\hat B^2\cdot d^2/12 = \sigma\omega^2\hat B^2d^2/24\). \(\square\)

2. \(\sigma = 2\times10^6\) S/m; \(\sigma\omega^2\hat B^2/24 = 2\times10^6\times314.16^2\times2.25/24 = 1.851\times10^{10}\ \mathrm{W/m^5}\). At 0.3 mm: \(\times9\times10^{-8}\) gives \(1665\ \mathrm{W/m^3}\), \(0.218\) W/kg. At 0.5 mm: \(4626\ \mathrm{W/m^3}\), \(0.605\) W/kg. Ratio \((0.5/0.3)^2 = 2.78\) ✓.

3. \(\delta = \sqrt{2/(314.16\times1000\times4\pi\times10^{-7}\times2\times10^6)} = \sqrt{2/789\,568} = 1.59\) mm. Part 1 assumed \(B\) uniform through the sheet, i.e. the eddy currents too weak to alter the field — true when \(d \ll \delta\), as for 0.3 and 0.5 mm. In a 5 mm plate, \(d \approx 3\delta\): the eddy currents push the flux towards the surfaces and the field inside is far from uniform. The formula would give \(0.218\times(5/0.3)^2 = 60.5\) W/kg, computed for a field that is not there; branch 10 does the problem properly.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}\,s^{-2}\,T^2\,m^2}\); with \(\mathrm T = \mathrm{V\,s\,m^{-2}}\) and \(\mathrm S = \mathrm{A/V}\) this is \(\mathrm{A\,V\,m^{-3}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\) ✓. Limits: \(\sigma \to 0\) (an insulator) or \(\omega \to 0\) (DC) give no loss ✓. Total loss of a stack at fixed total volume falls as \(d^2\): halving the sheets quarters it ✓.

P6 Hysteresis as a loop area medium

exercises: the energy delivered to a winding, \(\vec H\) as cited, Green's theorem for a loop's area, frequency scaling of the two core losses

  1. A toroidal core of volume \(V_c = Al\) carries an \(N\)-turn winding of negligible resistance. Using \(Hl = Ni\) (cited from Griffiths ch. 6) and Faraday's law, show that the energy delivered to the winding over one cycle is \(V_c\oint H\,\mathrm dB\).
  2. Show that \(\oint H\,\mathrm dB\) is the area enclosed by the B–H loop when the loop is traversed anticlockwise, and zero for a linear material.
  3. A core has volume 0.05 m³ and, at the operating peak flux density, a loop area of \(40\ \mathrm{J/m^3}\) (both assumed). Find the hysteresis loss at 50 Hz. Add the classical eddy loss for 0.3 mm sheets from P5, and find both losses at 60 Hz with the same peak flux density.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is \(\int vi\,\mathrm dt\) with \(v\) and \(i\) rewritten in terms of \(B\) and \(H\). Part 2 is Green's theorem in the \((H, B)\) plane. Part 3 is two losses that scale differently with \(f\).

Rung 2

The flux linkage is \(NBA\). In Green's theorem \(\oint(P\,\mathrm dH + Q\,\mathrm dB) = \iint(\partial_HQ - \partial_BP)\,\mathrm dH\,\mathrm dB\), take \(P = 0\), \(Q = H\). A fixed loop costs a fixed energy per cycle; P5's eddy loss goes as \(\omega^2\).

Rung 3

First line: \(vi = (NA\,\dot B)(Hl/N) = Al\,H\dot B\).

Worked solution

Idea. The source does work \(H\,\mathrm dB\) per unit volume on the core; round a closed cycle the net work is the area of the loop.

1. Faraday: \(v = \mathrm d(NBA)/\mathrm dt = NA\dot B\). Cited (Griffiths ch. 6, §6.3): \(\oint\vec H\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = I_{\text{free}}\), so round the core \(Hl = Ni\), \(i = Hl/N\). Hence \(vi = AlH\dot B\), and over a cycle \(\int vi\,\mathrm dt = Al\oint H\,\mathrm dB\).

2. With \(H\) across and \(B\) up, Green's theorem (MR 04) with \(P = 0\), \(Q = H\) gives \(\oint H\,\mathrm dB = \iint1\,\mathrm dH\,\mathrm dB\), the enclosed area, for an anticlockwise loop. A ferromagnet's loop is anticlockwise (rising \(H\) on the lower branch), so the energy absorbed is positive. For \(B = \mu H\), \(H\,\mathrm dB = \mathrm d(B^2/2\mu)\), an exact differential, whose integral round a closed path is zero.

3. At 50 Hz: \(40\times0.05\times50 = 100\) W. Eddy (P5): \(1665\ \mathrm{W/m^3}\times0.05 = 83.3\) W. Total 183.3 W. At 60 Hz, same \(\hat B\): the loop is the same, so hysteresis is \(100\times60/50 = 120\) W; eddy scales as \(f^2\): \(83.3\times1.44 = 119.9\) W. Total 239.9 W, up 31%.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}\cdot T} = \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}\cdot V\,s\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\) ✓; \(\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}\cdot m^3\cdot s^{-1}} = \mathrm W\) ✓. Energy: in a lossless linear core the winding takes energy on the way up and returns it on the way down, and the net is zero ✓. The two assumed figures are illustrative, not a steel's data.

P7 Two tests, one circuit medium

exercises: the open- and short-circuit tests, referring elements, the approximate voltage-drop formula and its sign for a leading load

The street transformer is tested. Open circuit, on the 400 V side: 400 V, 5.00 A, 387 W. Short circuit, on the 20 kV side: 800 V, 20.0 A, 3250 W.

  1. Find \(R_c\) and \(X_m\) on the 400 V side, and the series \(R\) and \(X\) on the 20 kV side. Refer each to the other side.
  2. Justify, with numbers, that each test sees only one branch.
  3. Find the voltage drop \(\Delta V \approx |I|(R\cos\varphi + X\sin\varphi)\) at full load for unity power factor, 0.8 lagging and 0.8 leading.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four resistances and reactances, two cross-checks, three voltages — one of them negative.

Rung 2

Open circuit: \(P = |V|^2/R_c\), \(Q = \sqrt{|VI|^2 - P^2} = |V|^2/X_m\). Short circuit: \(R = P/|I|^2\), \(|Z| = |V|/|I|\). Referring multiplies or divides by \(n^2 = 2500\). A leading current has \(\sin\varphi \lt 0\).

Rung 3

First line: \(|S_0| = 400\times5 = 2000\) VA, so \(Q_0 = \sqrt{2000^2 - 387^2} = 1962\) var.

Worked solution

Idea. Each test makes one branch carry almost nothing, so the meters read the other branch alone.

1. Open circuit: \(R_c = 400^2/387 = 413.4\ \Omega\); \(X_m = 400^2/1962.2 = 81.54\ \Omega\), \(L_m = 0.2596\) H. On the 20 kV side: \(1.034\ \mathrm{M\Omega}\) and \(203.9\ \mathrm{k\Omega}\) (649 H). Short circuit: \(R = 3250/400 = 8.125\ \Omega\); \(|Z| = 800/20 = 40\ \Omega\); \(X = \sqrt{1600 - 66.0} = 39.17\ \Omega\). On the 400 V side: \(3.25\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\) and \(15.67\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\).

2. Open circuit: 5 A through the series branch (\(16.0\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\) at 400 V) drops 0.080 V, \(2\times10^{-4}\) of 400 V, and dissipates at most \(25\times0.00325 = 0.081\) W against 387 W. Short circuit: at 4% of rated voltage the shunt branch draws 4% of its 0.1 A, 0.004 A against 20 A, and its loss is \(0.04^2\times387 = 0.62\) W against 3250 W.

3. At 1000 A on the 400 V side: unity, \(1000\times0.00325 = 3.25\) V (0.81%). 0.8 lagging: \(1000(0.00325\times0.8 + 0.01567\times0.6) = 2.60 + 9.40 = 12.0\) V (3.0%). 0.8 leading: \(2.60 - 9.40 = -6.80\) V — the voltage rises under load, because the capacitive current's drop across the leakage reactance is in phase-opposition to \(V\).

Check. The exact \(|V + I(R + iX)| - 400\) for the three cases is 3.55, 12.14 and \(-6.53\) V; the approximation drops a term of order \((IX\cos\varphi - IR\sin\varphi)^2/2V\), a few tenths of a volt ✓. Referred two ways: \(0.2596\times2500 = 649\) H ✓. \(R/|Z| = 0.20\): mostly leakage ✓. Units ✓.

P8 Proof: efficiency peaks at equal losses medium

exercises: the efficiency model, AM–GM or calculus, independence from the power factor — the checkpoint's second part

  1. A transformer of rating \(|S|\), core loss \(P_0\) and full-load copper loss \(P_k\) runs at load fraction \(x\) and power factor \(\cos\varphi\), at rated voltage. Write \(\eta(x)\) and prove that it is greatest where \(x^2P_k = P_0\), for every \(\cos\varphi\). Give \(\eta_{\max}\).
  2. For the street transformer find \(x^*\) and \(\eta_{\max}\) at unity and at 0.8.
  3. Repeat part 2 with the regulation's earlier Tier 1 limits, \(P_0 = 430\) W, \(P_k = 4600\) W.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is a load fraction and a formula; parts 2 and 3 are percentages near 99.4%.

Rung 2

\(1/\eta = 1 + (P_0/x + xP_k)/(|S|\cos\varphi)\). Minimise the bracket; its two terms have a product that does not depend on \(x\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\eta(x) = x|S|\cos\varphi/(x|S|\cos\varphi + P_0 + x^2P_k)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Loss per delivered watt is \(P_0/x + xP_k\) up to a constant; a sum of two terms with a fixed product is least when they are equal.

1. At rated voltage the flux, hence the core loss, is \(P_0\); the current is \(x\) times rated, so the copper loss is \(x^2P_k\); the output is \(x|S|\cos\varphi\). So \(\eta = x|S|\cos\varphi/(x|S|\cos\varphi + P_0 + x^2P_k)\) and \(1/\eta = 1 + f(x)/(|S|\cos\varphi)\) with \(f(x) = P_0/x + xP_k\). AM–GM: \(f(x) \ge 2\sqrt{P_0P_k}\), equality iff \(P_0/x = xP_k\), i.e. \(x^2P_k = P_0\). (Or: \(f' = -P_0/x^2 + P_k = 0\) at \(x^* = \sqrt{P_0/P_k}\), \(f'' = 2P_0/x^3 \gt 0\).) The power factor only divides \(f\) by a constant, so the maximising \(x\) is the same for all \(\cos\varphi\), and \[\eta_{\max} = \Big(1 + \frac{2\sqrt{P_0P_k}}{|S|\cos\varphi}\Big)^{-1}. \quad\square\]

2. \(x^* = \sqrt{387/3250} = 0.345\). \(2\sqrt{387\times3250} = 2243\) W. Unity: \(1/(1 + 2243/400\,000) = 99.44\%\). At 0.8: \(1/(1 + 2243/320\,000) = 99.30\%\).

3. \(x^* = \sqrt{430/4600} = 0.306\); \(2\sqrt{430\times4600} = 2813\) W; unity \(1/(1 + 2813/400\,000) = 99.30\%\); 0.8: \(99.13\%\). Tier 2 raised the peak by 0.14 points and moved it to a heavier load.

Check. At \(x^*\) the street transformer's copper loss is \(0.1191\times3250 = 387\) W = core ✓. Units: \(\sqrt{\mathrm{W/W}}\) and \(\mathrm W/\mathrm{VA}\) dimensionless ✓. Limits: \(P_0 \to 0\) sends \(x^* \to 0\) and \(\eta_{\max} \to 1\) — with no fixed loss, the lighter the load the better ✓.

P9 100 MW down a 10 Ω line routine

exercises: line loss \(P^2R/V^2\), the voltage for a target loss, unit 04's maximum-power theorem as a transfer limit — the checkpoint's third part

A load takes 100 MW at unity power factor through a line of total loop resistance 10 Ω (single-phase or DC-equivalent model, assumed).

  1. Find the current and the line loss, in MW and as a percentage of 100 MW, when the load's voltage is 400 kV, 132 kV and 20 kV.
  2. What voltage gives exactly 1% loss?
  3. Explain the 20 kV result. If instead the sending end is held at 20 kV, what is the most power any load can receive?
  4. At 400 kV, what is the loss if the load's power factor is 0.8?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three currents and losses, one voltage, one power, one percentage.

Rung 2

\(I = P/V\), loss \(I^2R\). For part 3, the line is a source \(V_s\) behind \(R\), and unit 04's theorem caps the load's power at \(V_s^2/4R\). For part 4, \(I = P/(V\cos\varphi)\).

Rung 3

First line: at 400 kV, \(I = 10^8/(4\times10^5) = 250\) A.

Worked solution

Idea. The power is \(VI\); the loss depends only on \(I\). Raise \(V\), lower \(I\), and the loss falls as \(1/V^2\).

1. 400 kV: 250 A, \(250^2\times10 = 0.625\) MW, 0.625% ≈ 0.6%. 132 kV: 757.6 A, 5.74 MW, 5.74%. 20 kV: 5000 A, \(5000^2\times10 = 250\) MW, 250% — more than the whole 100 MW.

2. \(PR/V^2 = 0.01\): \(V = \sqrt{10^8\times10/0.01} = \sqrt{10^{11}} = 316\) kV.

3. A "loss" of 250 MW to deliver 100 MW would need 350 MW sent, through a line dropping \(IR = 50\) kV — the sending end would have to be at 70 kV. The calculation is consistent, but it describes a 70 kV line, not a 20 kV one: a line whose own voltage is 20 kV cannot reach that operating point. With the sending end held at \(V_s = 20\) kV, the load receives \(p = V_sI - I^2R\), greatest at \(I = V_s/2R = 1000\) A: \(p_{\max} = V_s^2/4R = 4\times10^8/40 = 10\) MW (unit 04's maximum-power theorem). 100 MW is ten times beyond reach.

4. \(I = 10^8/(4\times10^5\times0.8) = 312.5\) A; loss \(312.5^2\times10 = 0.977\) MW, 0.977% — the unity figure divided by \(0.8^2\).

Check. Scaling: \((400/132)^2 = 9.18 = 5.74/0.625\) ✓, \((400/20)^2 = 400 = 250/0.625\) ✓. Units: \(\mathrm{A^2\,\Omega} = \mathrm W\) ✓. For a resistive line the fractional loss equals the fractional drop: \(IR/V = 2.5\ \mathrm{kV}/400\ \mathrm{kV} = 0.625\%\) ✓.

exercises: power conservation through an ideal transformer, unit 06's power-factor correction seen upstream, copper loss at light load

Unit 06's workshop is the only load on the street transformer (an assumption). Treat the transformer as ideal for parts 1 and 2. In the single-phase model the workshop's 230 V is taken as provided by a winding of the model's full rating, so its load fraction is \(|S|/400\ \mathrm{kVA}\); unit 11 says what changes when, in the real three-phase transformer, one phase carries it all.

  1. Find the current in the 20 kV line feeding the transformer, before and after unit 06's capacitor corrects the workshop to unity power factor.
  2. By what factor does the correction change the loss in a given resistance on the 20 kV side?
  3. Now use the real transformer: find its load fraction and copper loss before and after correction, and its efficiency in each case at the workshop's 20 kW.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two currents of order an ampere; one factor you have met before; two load fractions well below the efficiency peak.

Rung 2

An ideal transformer passes \(|S|\) unchanged, so \(|I_1| = |S|/20\ \mathrm{kV}\) whatever the ratio. The load fraction is \(|S|/400\ \mathrm{kVA}\); copper loss \(x^2P_k\).

P11 The ignition coil, finished medium

exercises: reflected capacitance, the \(k = 1\) model with its magnetising inductance, an energy check, a design inverse

Unit 05's coil: when the contact opens, 4 A flows in its primary inductance \(L = 6\) mH, and a capacitor \(C = 0.2\ \mu\)F across the contact takes it up, ringing to \(I\sqrt{L/C} = 693\) V in the lossless estimate. Now add the secondary: \(N_2 = 100N_1\) and a capacitance \(C_2 = 50\) pF across it (both assumed). Model the coil as an ideal transformer with \(L\) in shunt on its primary (the \(k = 1\) picture), and neglect losses.

  1. What does the naive estimate "693 V times the turns ratio" give?
  2. Find the capacitance seen at the primary, the peak primary and secondary voltages, and the ringing frequency.
  3. Check the energy at the peak against \(\tfrac12LI^2\), and say how it divides between the two capacitors.
  4. What \(C_2\) would halve the naive secondary voltage?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each answer is a voltage, a capacitance, a frequency or an energy; nothing here claims what voltage fires a spark plug.

Rung 2

With \(n = N_1/N_2 = 1/100\), an impedance \(1/(i\omega C_2)\) on the secondary is seen at the primary as \(n^2/(i\omega C_2) = 1/(i\omega\,C_2/n^2)\): a capacitor of \(C_2/n^2\). It sits in parallel with \(C\). Then unit 05's \(I\sqrt{L/C}\) with the total.

Rung 3

First line: \(C_2/n^2 = 50\ \mathrm{pF}\times10^4 = 0.5\ \mu\mathrm F\).

Worked solution

Idea. A small capacitance on a high-turns secondary looks large from the primary, and soaks up the same stored energy at a lower voltage.

1. \(693\times100 = 69.3\) kV.

2. Reflected \(0.5\ \mu\)F, total \(0.7\ \mu\)F. Peak primary \(4\sqrt{6\times10^{-3}/0.7\times10^{-6}} = 4\times92.58 = 370.3\) V; secondary \(370.3\times100 = 37.0\) kV. Frequency \(1/(2\pi\sqrt{6\times10^{-3}\times0.7\times10^{-6}}) = 2.46\) kHz, against 4.59 kHz without the secondary.

3. \(\tfrac12(0.7\times10^{-6})(370.3)^2 = 48.0\) mJ \(= \tfrac12(6\times10^{-3})(4)^2\) ✓. On \(C_2\): \(\tfrac12(50\times10^{-12})(37\,033)^2 = 34.3\) mJ, i.e. \(0.5/0.7 = 71\%\); on \(C\): 13.7 mJ.

4. Halving needs \(\sqrt{L/C_{\text{tot}}}\) halved, \(C_{\text{tot}} = 4\times0.2 = 0.8\ \mu\)F, so the reflected part is \(0.6\ \mu\)F and \(C_2 = 0.6\ \mu\mathrm F/10^4 = 60\) pF.

Check. Limit \(C_2 \to 0\): 693 V and 69.3 kV, the naive figures ✓. Units: \(\mathrm A\sqrt{\mathrm{H/F}} = \mathrm A\cdot\Omega = \mathrm V\) ✓. The model neglects leakage, winding resistance and whatever the spark does; it says what the capacitances do to an ideal coil, no more.

P12 Corona, and the bundle medium

exercises: the two-wire surface field from MR 23's logarithmic potential, Peek's onset (cited), the geometric-mean radius of a bundle

  1. Two thin parallel conductors of radius \(r\), centres \(D \gg r\) apart, carry \(\pm\lambda\) per metre. Show that \(V = (\lambda/\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(D/r)\) and that the surface field is \(E_s = V/(2r\ln(D/r))\).
  2. At 400 kV RMS (single-phase model, assumed) with \(D = 10\) m, find the peak surface field for \(r = 0.5\) cm and 1.5 cm, and compare with Peek's onset \(g_v = 29.8(1 + 0.301/\sqrt r)\) kV/cm peak (\(r\) in cm; cited, Peek 1920, ch. III).
  3. Each conductor is replaced by \(N\) subconductors of radius \(r\) at the corners of a regular polygon on a circle of radius \(R\), all at one potential. Show that, seen from far away, the bundle acts like one conductor of radius \(r_{\text{eq}} = (NrR^{N-1})^{1/N}\), and find the average surface field on each subconductor.
  4. Evaluate for four 1.5 cm subconductors on a 45 cm square, and for two 1.5 cm subconductors 45 cm apart.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is the two-dimensional potential of two line charges; parts 2 and 4 are fields in MV/m between 1 and 8; part 3 is a potential summed over the subconductors.

Rung 2

A line charge's potential is \(-(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln\rho\). On subconductor 1 the bundle's own charges contribute \(-\frac{\lambda/N}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_k\ln d_{1k}\), with \(d_{11} = r\). For \(N\) equally spaced points, \(\prod_{k\ne1}d_{1k} = NR^{N-1}\). Gauss's law gives the average surface field of one subconductor exactly.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: on conductor 1's surface, \(\varphi_1 = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln r + \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln D\).

Worked solution

Idea. The surface field is the charge per unit length over \(2\pi\varepsilon_0r\); the voltage fixes the charge through a logarithm. Splitting the conductor shares the charge and so the field, while the logarithm barely notices the split.

1. \(\varphi_1 = (\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(D/r)\), \(\varphi_2 = -\varphi_1\) by symmetry, so \(V = (\lambda/\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(D/r)\) (to order \(r/D\)). The field at conductor 1's surface is \(\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)\) from its own charge plus \(\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0D)\) from the other, smaller by \(r/D\). Eliminating \(\lambda\): \(E_s = V/(2r\ln(D/r))\).

2. \(\hat V = 565.7\) kV. \(r = 0.5\) cm: \(565\,685/(0.01\times\ln2000) = 7.44\) MV/m against Peek's \(4.25\) MV/m — far into corona. \(r = 1.5\) cm: \(565\,685/(0.03\times\ln666.7) = 2.90\) MV/m against 3.71 (smooth) — below onset for a smooth wire. But a line conductor is stranded, and for stranded cables Peek's ch. III multiplies \(g_v\) by \(m_v = 0.72\) (local corona along the cable) to 0.82 (decided corona): 2.67 to 3.04 MV/m. So 2.90 MV/m is already past the start of corona on a 1.5 cm stranded conductor.

3. With \(\lambda/N\) on each subconductor (symmetry), the potential on subconductor 1 is \(-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac1N\sum_k\ln d_{1k} + \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln D\): the single-conductor formula with \(\ln r\) replaced by \(\ln r_{\text{eq}} = \frac1N\sum_k\ln d_{1k}\). The distances from one vertex of a regular \(N\)-gon to the others are \(R|1 - \zeta^k|\), \(\zeta = e^{2\pi i/N}\), and \(\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}(1 - \zeta^k) = N\) (it is \((z^N - 1)/(z - 1)\) at \(z = 1\)). So \(r_{\text{eq}}^N = r\cdot NR^{N-1}\). Gauss's law round one subconductor: its normal field integrates to \((\lambda/N)/\varepsilon_0\), so its average is \((\lambda/N)/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)\), i.e. \(E_s = \hat V/(2Nr\ln(D/r_{\text{eq}}))\).

4. Square: \(R = 0.45/\sqrt2 = 0.318\) m, \(r_{\text{eq}} = (4\times0.015\times0.318^3)^{1/4} = 0.210\) m, \(E_s = 565\,685/(8\times0.015\times\ln(10/0.210)) = 1.22\) MV/m. Pair: \(r_{\text{eq}} = \sqrt{0.015\times0.45} = 0.0822\) m, \(E_s = 565\,685/(4\times0.015\times\ln(10/0.0822)) = 1.96\) MV/m.

Check. \(N = 1\) gives \(r_{\text{eq}} = r\) ✓. Units: \(\mathrm V/\mathrm m\) ✓. Monotone: one wire 2.90, two 1.96, four 1.22 MV/m ✓. The neighbours' field makes the outer side of each subconductor worse than average by the factor \(1 + (N - 1)r/R\): 1.39 MV/m for the square, still far below even the stranded cable's 2.67 MV/m.
