07

Transformers, and why power lines run at 400 kV

Two coils on one iron core, taken to a limit, become a device that scales voltage up and current down with no loss of power. That is what lets a grid choose its voltage at every stage — and the loss in a line falls as the square of that voltage.

07 · two coils

Unit 02's pair, with its dots

the coupled pair (unit 02)

Two windings on one core, each marked by the passive convention:

$$v_1 = L_1\frac{\mathrm di_1}{\mathrm dt} + M\frac{\mathrm di_2}{\mathrm dt},$$ $$v_2 = M\frac{\mathrm di_1}{\mathrm dt} + L_2\frac{\mathrm di_2}{\mathrm dt}.$$

Units: $\mathrm{H\cdot A/s} = \mathrm{V\,s\,A^{-1}\cdot A\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm V$ ✓.

the dot convention

A dot marks one terminal of each winding, chosen so that current entering both dotted terminals drives flux the same way round the core. With both current arrows into the dots, $M > 0$.

The winding fed from the source is the primary, the one feeding the load the secondary.

Both windings are wound so that current into the dotted terminal drives the flux $\Phi$ clockwise round the core. Seen from the terminals, that is all the dot records.

What the dot replaces. Unit 02 defined $M$ by Neumann's formula, whose sign depends on the orientations chosen for the two loops: reverse one and $M$ changes sign. On a diagram the orientation of a winding is not visible — a coil is a row of loops however it is wound — so a mark is needed. The dot fixes it: with both reference currents entering dotted terminals, their fluxes add in the core and $M > 0$. If one current arrow enters an undotted terminal, the same physical winding has $M < 0$ in the equations. Problem P1 practises placing dots from a description of how the windings are wound.

Where the equations come from. Unit 02's notes (The elements, collected): Faraday's law round each winding, with flux linkages $\Lambda_1 = L_1i_1 + Mi_2$ and $\Lambda_2 = Mi_1 + L_2i_2$, gives $v_k = \mathrm d\Lambda_k/\mathrm dt$ for each winding marked by the passive convention. The pair is one element with two ports, drawn inside one boundary so that unit 02's hypothesis (H1) — changing flux stays inside elements — holds outside it. Unit 02 called the transformer "a deliberate coupling put inside one element"; this is that element.

Primary and secondary name roles, not windings: a transformer is the same object fed from either side. The street transformer's primary is its 20 kV winding; in the open-circuit test of section 4 it is fed from the 400 V side, which is then the primary.

07 · two coils

The coupling coefficient

the coupling coefficient $$k = \frac{M}{\sqrt{L_1L_2}}, \qquad |k| \le 1 .$$

The bound is unit 02's $M^2 \le L_1L_2$ (cited: The inductance matrix is positive semi-definite), which came from the magnetic energy $\int B^2/2\mu_0 \ge 0$.

Units: $\mathrm H/\sqrt{\mathrm H\cdot\mathrm H}$, dimensionless ✓.

What it measures. $k = 1$ needs a pair of currents with $\vec B = \vec 0$ everywhere (unit 02's notes): every line of one coil's flux threading every turn of the other. $k = 0$: no shared flux.

Where this course uses each end. Near $k = 1$: this unit — iron makes it so. Far below 1: unit 15, a phone on a charging pad.

Schematic. In air, the left coil's field lines mostly close without threading the right coil (only the dashed one does): $k$ well below 1. On a closed iron core they keep to the iron and thread both: $k$ close to 1.

Why $|k|$ and not $k$. The sign of $M$ is the dot's business (previous slide). The bound is on its size.

Unit 02's argument, in one line. The energy of two coils, $W = \tfrac12L_1i_1^2 + Mi_1i_2 + \tfrac12L_2i_2^2$, is the integral of $B^2/2\mu_0$ and so is non-negative for every pair of currents; a quadratic form in $(i_1, i_2)$ that is never negative has non-negative determinant, $L_1L_2 - M^2 \ge 0$. In terms of $k$: $W = \tfrac12\big(\sqrt{L_1}i_1 + k\sqrt{L_2}i_2\big)^2 + \tfrac12(1 - k^2)L_2i_2^2$, which is the same statement, and shows that at $k = 1$ the energy is a perfect square — the form section 2 uses.

Equality. $W = 0$ with currents flowing needs $\vec B = \vec 0$ everywhere: the two coils' fields cancel exactly, point by point. For that, every field line of one coil must thread every turn of the other. No real pair does this; an iron core comes close, because, as the next slides show, it makes the path through the iron so much easier than the path through air.

The figure is schematic: no field was computed for it. Unit 15 computes $k$ for two coils in air from Neumann's formula, and finds it falling as the cube of their distance.

07 · two coils

The transformer as a two-port

In the sinusoidal steady state (unit 05: RMS phasors, $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt \to i\omega$):

$$\begin{pmatrix}V_1\\ V_2\end{pmatrix} = i\omega\begin{pmatrix}L_1 & M\\ M & L_2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I_1\\ I_2\end{pmatrix}.$$
three things to read off

Reciprocal. The impedance matrix is symmetric, $z_{12} = z_{21} = i\omega M$: unit 03's reciprocity, here from Neumann's symmetric formula.

Lossless. $S = V_1I_1^* + V_2I_2^* = i\omega\,I^{\mathsf H}\mathcal LI$, purely imaginary: $P = 0$, and $Q = \omega\,I^{\mathsf H}\mathcal LI \ge 0$ because $\mathcal L$ is positive semi-definite.

Two components. Joined only magnetically, its graph has $c = 2$ (unit 03): KCL holds on each side separately.

Units: $\mathrm{s^{-1}\,H\,A} = \mathrm V$ ✓; $S$ in VA ✓.

Above, the symbol: two inductors, two bars for the core, a dot on each. Below, the graph of a source-and-load circuit through it: one component per side.

Reciprocity. Unit 03 (Reciprocity, from Tellegen) proved that a network of resistors has a symmetric transfer matrix. Here the matrix is $i\omega$ times the inductance matrix, symmetric because Neumann's double integral is symmetric in the two loops (unit 02's notes said reciprocity would return as a theorem about networks; this is the magnetic case). A volt-per-ampere measured one way through the transformer equals the one measured the other way.

Full proof that it is lossless. Write $I = (I_1, I_2)^{\mathsf T}$ and $\mathcal L$ for the real symmetric inductance matrix. Then $V = i\omega\mathcal LI$ and the total complex power absorbed is $S = \sum_kV_kI_k^* = V^{\mathsf T}\bar I = i\omega\,I^{\mathsf T}\mathcal L\bar I = i\omega\,\bar I^{\mathsf T}\mathcal LI$ (a scalar equals its transpose, and $\mathcal L$ is symmetric). Since $\mathcal L$ is real symmetric, $\bar I^{\mathsf T}\mathcal LI$ is real, and it is $\ge 0$ because $\mathcal L$ is positive semi-definite (apply the real form to the real and imaginary parts of $I$ separately and add). So $S$ is purely imaginary with $Q \ge 0$: the pair absorbs no mean power and only ever draws reactive power, as an inductor does. In unit 06's terms, $Q = 2\omega\langle W_m\rangle$ with $\langle W_m\rangle = \tfrac12\bar I^{\mathsf T}\mathcal LI$, the mean stored magnetic energy for RMS phasors.

$c = 2$. Unit 03's notes promised that two circuits coupled only magnetically have $c = 2$, and that the count matters: the incidence matrix then has rank $n - 2$, there are two independent node potentials to fix (one ground on each side), and KCL gives no relation between the currents on the two sides. The relation between them comes from the element law above, and section 2 shows it becomes $I_1 = -I_2/n$.

07 · two coils

Iron, in one slide

a toroid of $N$ turns, length $l$, section $A$

In air, Ampère's law round the core (MR 23): $Bl = \mu_0Ni$, so the flux linkage is $NBA$ and

$$L = \frac{\mu_0N^2A}{l}.$$

On a closed core of a linear magnetic material, the same current gives $\mu_r$ times the field (cited: Griffiths ch. 6; Fitzgerald ch. 1):

$$L = N^2\mathcal P,\qquad \mathcal P = \frac{\mu_r\mu_0A}{l}.$$

Units: $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\cdot m^2/m} = \mathrm H$ ✓. $\mathcal P$ is flux per ampere-turn.

What iron buys. Transformer steel has relative permeability $\mu_r \gg 1$: $L$ is large, and the flux keeps to the iron, so $k \approx 1$. What it costs. Linearity ends at saturation, and every cycle loses energy (section 3).

Schematic, not a measured curve. Iron's $B$ rises $\mu_r$ times faster than air's while it is linear, then flattens. Fitzgerald §1.3 treats real electrical steels.

Full proof (air)

A toroid of $N$ turns carrying $i$, mean radius $\rho$, thin enough that $B$ is uniform over its section $A$. By symmetry $\vec B = B(\rho)\hat\theta$ inside the winding. Ampère's law round a circle of radius $\rho$ inside the core (MR 23, Ampère's law is Stokes' theorem applied to $\vec B$): $B\cdot2\pi\rho = \mu_0Ni$, the circle being threaded $N$ times by the current. With $l = 2\pi\rho$, $B = \mu_0Ni/l$. The flux through one turn is $BA$, through all $N$ turns $\Lambda = NBA = \mu_0N^2Ai/l$, so $L = \Lambda/i = \mu_0N^2A/l$. Outside the toroid the same argument with a circle threaded $N$ times in each direction (or not at all) gives $B = 0$: all the flux is inside. That is the one geometry in which air already gives $k = 1$ — two windings on one toroid share every line of flux — and that is why the toroid is used.

Iron (cited). Inside matter the field the free current fixes is $\vec H$: $\oint\vec H\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = I_{\text{free, enc}}$ (Griffiths ch. 6, §6.3). A linear magnetic material has $\vec B = \mu\vec H$ with $\mu = \mu_r\mu_0$ (Griffiths §6.4). Round the toroid, $Hl = Ni$, so $B = \mu_r\mu_0Ni/l$ and $L = \mu_r\mu_0N^2A/l$. Iron is not linear (Griffiths §6.4.2, ferromagnetism; the figure is a sketch of why), but below saturation and for the purpose of this unit a constant $\mu_r$ is the model, as in Fitzgerald ch. 1. That is the first of this unit's "as far as needed" approximations; hysteresis, the second, comes in section 3.

How large is $\mu_r$? Large — this deck states no figure, since none could be checked against its source here; Fitzgerald §1.3 treats the properties of real electrical steels. Nothing below depends on a value: the argument needs only $\mu_r \gg 1$. Problem P5 uses $\mu_r = 1000$ as an assumption.

Why the flux keeps to the iron. The flux per ampere-turn of a path is $\mu A/l$, so iron offers a path $\mu_r$ times easier than air of the same shape. A winding on the iron sends nearly all its flux round the iron, and the other winding on the same iron catches nearly all of it. The fraction that escapes into the air is the leakage of section 3.

What is not done here. Magnetic circuits with gaps, reluctance networks, permanent magnets: Fitzgerald ch. 1 and unit 12. Fitzgerald calls $\mathcal P$ the permeance, and its reciprocal the reluctance.

07 · two coils

Two windings on one core: the turns ratio

Suppose every line of flux stays in the core. Both windings' ampere-turns drive the one flux:

$$\Phi = \mathcal P\,(N_1i_1 + N_2i_2),$$

and each turn links it: $\Lambda_1 = N_1\Phi$, $\Lambda_2 = N_2\Phi$.

inductances of a shared core $$L_1 = N_1^2\mathcal P,\quad L_2 = N_2^2\mathcal P,\quad M = N_1N_2\mathcal P .$$

So $M^2 = L_1L_2$: $k = 1$ exactly, and

$$\sqrt{L_1/L_2} = N_1/N_2 \equiv n ,$$

the turns ratio. Units: $\mathrm{Wb/(A\cdot turn)}$ for $\mathcal P$, $\mathrm{Wb/A} = \mathrm H$ ✓; $n$ dimensionless ✓.

The street transformer: $n = 20\,000/400 = 50$. Its rated currents, $400\ \mathrm{kVA}/20\ \mathrm{kV} = 20$ A and $400\ \mathrm{kVA}/400\ \mathrm V = 1000$ A, are in the ratio $1/n$.

One flux, two windings. Each turn contributes $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dt$ volts, so the voltages are in the ratio of the turns.

The derivation. With all flux in the core, the core is one toroid-like path of flux per ampere-turn $\mathcal P$, and the ampere-turns of the two windings add (current into each dotted terminal drives flux the same way, by the dot convention). Each turn of winding 1 links $\Phi$, so $\Lambda_1 = N_1\Phi = N_1^2\mathcal Pi_1 + N_1N_2\mathcal Pi_2$; read off $L_1$ and $M$. Likewise $\Lambda_2$. The symmetry $M_{12} = M_{21}$ is visible here directly.

Why it matters that $L \propto N^2$. One factor of $N$ is in the flux a winding makes (ampere-turns), the other in the flux linkage it collects (turns). Doubling the turns on a winding quadruples its inductance and doubles its voltage per unit $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dt$.

Volts per turn. The figure's statement $v_1 = N_1\,\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dt$, $v_2 = N_2\,\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dt$ is Faraday's law (MR 24) applied to each winding with the winding's resistance neglected, and it gives $v_1/v_2 = N_1/N_2$ at once. The next section derives the same ratio from the circuit equations alone, and the slide after shows what it does not fix: the currents.

The street transformer. Its windings' actual turn counts are not given and are not needed: only the ratio enters, and the ratio is the ratio of the rated voltages. The real three-phase one may be connected so that its turns ratio differs from its voltage ratio by a factor $\sqrt3$ (unit 11, star and delta); in the single-phase model they are the same.

section 2

The ideal transformer, as a limit

At perfect coupling the voltage ratio is already exact; the currents need one more limit, an infinite inductance. What survives is a two-port that scales voltage by $n$, current by $1/n$ and impedance by $n^2$ — and stores nothing.

07 · the ideal limit

At k = 1 the voltage ratio is exact, and the currents are not

lemma

If $M = \sqrt{L_1L_2}$ and $n = \sqrt{L_1/L_2}$, then for any currents

$$v_1 = n\,v_2,$$ $$i_1 + \frac{i_2}{n} = i_m,\quad L_1\frac{\mathrm di_m}{\mathrm dt} = v_1 .$$
proof

At $k = 1$ both equations share one bracket: $v_1 = \sqrt{L_1}\,\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\sqrt{L_1}i_1 + \sqrt{L_2}i_2\big)$ and $v_2 = \sqrt{L_2}\,\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\text{the same}\big)$. Divide; and write the bracket as $\sqrt{L_1}\,i_m$. $\square$

The magnetising current $i_m$ is what the primary draws to make the flux at all. It is fixed by $v_1$ and $L_1$, not by the load. Units: $\mathrm H\cdot\mathrm A/\mathrm s = \mathrm V$ ✓.

A resistive load. The magnetising current lags $v_1$ by a quarter cycle, as an inductor's current does; here it is drawn about 75 times larger, relative to the load's share, than the street transformer's at full load, to be visible.

The proof, written out. With $M = \sqrt{L_1L_2}$: $v_1 = L_1\dot\imath_1 + \sqrt{L_1L_2}\,\dot\imath_2 = \sqrt{L_1}\,\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\sqrt{L_1}i_1 + \sqrt{L_2}i_2)$ and $v_2 = \sqrt{L_1L_2}\,\dot\imath_1 + L_2\dot\imath_2 = \sqrt{L_2}\,\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\sqrt{L_1}i_1 + \sqrt{L_2}i_2)$. So $v_1/v_2 = \sqrt{L_1/L_2} = n$ whenever $v_2 \ne 0$, and $v_1 = nv_2$ always. Divide the bracket by $\sqrt{L_1}$: $i_1 + \sqrt{L_2/L_1}\,i_2 = i_1 + i_2/n$. Call it $i_m$; then $v_1 = L_1\,\mathrm di_m/\mathrm dt$. Nothing about the load was used.

The same thing as physics. At $k = 1$ there is one flux, and every turn sees the same $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dt$ (previous slide), so the voltages are in the ratio of the turns, whatever flows. The flux is set by the ampere-turns, $\Phi = \mathcal P(N_1i_1 + N_2i_2) = \mathcal PN_1(i_1 + i_2/n) = \mathcal PN_1i_m$. The load's current $i_2$ drives flux one way; the primary responds with an extra $-i_2/n$ that drives it back; what is left over, $i_m$, is exactly the flux the applied voltage demands. This is the mechanism by which the primary "knows" what the secondary is doing.

Energy, at $k = 1$. Unit 02's energy becomes a perfect square, $W = \tfrac12(\sqrt{L_1}i_1 + \sqrt{L_2}i_2)^2 = \tfrac12L_1i_m^2$: all the stored energy is the magnetising current's. Section 2's last limit sends it to zero.

The figure is computed for $v_1 = \hat V\sin\omega t$, a load share $-i_2/n$ in phase with it, and $i_m = -\hat I_m\cos\omega t$, with $\hat I_m$ taken as $0.3/0.8 = 37.5\%$ of the load share's peak for visibility. For the street transformer at full load the ratio is $0.098\ \mathrm A/20\ \mathrm A = 0.49\%$ (the worked example), about 75 times smaller: the point of the figure is the quarter-cycle lag, not the size.

07 · the ideal limit

Loading the secondary

Put a load $Z_L$ on the secondary. With the passive marking at both ports, the load absorbs, so $V_2 = -Z_LI_2$. Solve the second equation for $I_2 = -i\omega MI_1/(Z_L + i\omega L_2)$ and put it in the first:

$$Z_{\text{in}} = \frac{V_1}{I_1} = i\omega L_1 + \frac{\omega^2M^2}{Z_L + i\omega L_2}.$$
at $k = 1$ $$Z_{\text{in}} = \frac{i\omega L_1\cdot n^2Z_L}{i\omega L_1 + n^2Z_L},$$

that is, $i\omega L_1$ in parallel with $n^2Z_L$.

The load appears scaled by $n^2$, beside a shunt inductance $L_1$ that carries $I_m$. Units: $\omega^2M^2/Z$ is $\mathrm{s^{-2}H^2/\Omega} = \Omega$ ✓.

Limits. $Z_L \to 0$: $Z_{\text{in}} \to 0$, a short is seen as a short. $Z_L \to \infty$: $Z_{\text{in}} \to i\omega L_1$, the primary alone ✓.

At perfect coupling the transformer looks, from the primary, like its own inductance in parallel with the load scaled by $n^2$. The current splits accordingly.

Full proof

The input impedance. The second equation with $V_2 = -Z_LI_2$: $-Z_LI_2 = i\omega MI_1 + i\omega L_2I_2$, so $I_2 = -i\omega MI_1/(Z_L + i\omega L_2)$. Put it in the first: $V_1 = i\omega L_1I_1 + i\omega M I_2 = I_1\big[i\omega L_1 + \omega^2M^2/(Z_L + i\omega L_2)\big]$, using $(i\omega M)(-i\omega M) = \omega^2M^2$. This needs $Z_L + i\omega L_2 \ne 0$, which holds for any load with $\operatorname{Re}Z_L > 0$, and for any fixed $Z_L$ once $L_2$ is large enough.

At $k = 1$. $M^2 = L_1L_2$ and $L_2 = L_1/n^2$. Over a common denominator, $Z_{\text{in}} = \frac{i\omega L_1(Z_L + i\omega L_2) + \omega^2L_1L_2}{Z_L + i\omega L_2} = \frac{i\omega L_1Z_L}{Z_L + i\omega L_2}$, since $i\omega L_1\cdot i\omega L_2 = -\omega^2L_1L_2$ cancels the second term. Multiply top and bottom by $n^2$: $\frac{i\omega L_1\cdot n^2Z_L}{n^2Z_L + i\omega L_1}$, the parallel combination.

The currents, consistently. The lemma of the previous slide said $I_1 = -I_2/n + I_m$ with $I_m = V_1/(i\omega L_1)$: exactly the split in the figure, the inductor taking $V_1/(i\omega L_1)$ and the scaled load taking $V_1/(n^2Z_L) = -I_2/n$ (check: $V_1 = nV_2 = -nZ_LI_2$, so $V_1/(n^2Z_L) = -I_2/n$ ✓).

Unit 05's positive-real theorem holds here too: $Z_{\text{in}}$ is the parallel combination of an inductor and a scaled passive load, so it is passive. The scaling by the positive number $n^2$ preserves $\operatorname{Re}Z \ge 0$.

07 · the ideal limit

The ideal transformer

theorem: the ideal transformer is a limit

Hold $k = 1$ and $L_1/L_2 = n^2$, and let $L_1 \to \infty$. For every load,

$$V_1 = n\,V_2,\qquad I_1 = -\frac{I_2}{n},$$

and $Z_{\text{in}} \to n^2Z_L$.

proof

$V_1 = nV_2$ is the lemma. $I_1 + I_2/n = I_m = V_1/(i\omega L_1) \to 0$ for fixed $V_1$. And $i\omega L_1 \parallel n^2Z_L \to n^2Z_L$. $\square$

the order of the limits

With $k < 1$ fixed and $L_1 \to \infty$, a series reactance $i\omega(1 - k^2)L_1$ survives and grows. The limit needs the leakage $(1 - k)L_1 \to 0$ as well: $k \to 1$ first.

The symbol for the limit: the same drawing as the coupled pair, with the ratio written over it and no inductances. Its two equations are its whole element law.

Full proof

Fix $\omega$, $n$, the load $Z_L$ and the applied $V_1$. At $k = 1$ the lemma gives $V_1 = nV_2$ exactly, for every $L_1$, and $I_1 + I_2/n = I_m$ with $i\omega L_1I_m = V_1$, so $|I_1 + I_2/n| = |V_1|/(\omega L_1) \to 0$ as $L_1 \to \infty$. The input impedance is $i\omega L_1 \parallel n^2Z_L = n^2Z_L/(1 + n^2Z_L/(i\omega L_1)) \to n^2Z_L$. Each statement holds for every fixed load, which is what "for every load" means: the convergence is not uniform in $Z_L$ — a load with $|n^2Z_L|$ comparable to $\omega L_1$ is not yet in the limit, which is why the street transformer's no-load current matters only when the load is light.

Why $k \to 1$ must come first. For general $k$, with $L_2 = L_1/n^2$ and $a = i\omega L_2$, the input impedance is $Z_{\text{in}} = n^2a\,\frac{Z_L + (1 - k^2)a}{Z_L + a}$. As $L_1 \to \infty$ at fixed $k < 1$, this tends to infinity like $i\omega(1 - k^2)L_1$: the leakage reactance grows with everything else. If instead $L_1 \to \infty$ with $(1 - k^2)L_1 \to \varepsilon$, it tends to $n^2Z_L + i\omega\varepsilon$: an ideal transformer with a series reactance $i\omega\varepsilon$ in front — exactly the leakage element of section 3. The ideal transformer is the double limit with $\varepsilon = 0$. Section 3 keeps $\varepsilon$, and keeps $L_1$ finite, and that is the real transformer's circuit.

Definition versus theorem. Fitzgerald introduces the ideal transformer (§2.3) before the equivalent circuit (§2.4), as a model with stated properties. Here its two equations are the conclusion: an ideal transformer is what two coupled inductors become, and its equations inherit their sign convention — currents into the dots.

DC. The theorem is at a fixed $\omega > 0$. At $\omega = 0$ the magnetising current $V_1/(i\omega L_1)$ is unbounded: a transformer does not transform direct current, and a DC voltage on a winding drives a current limited only by the winding's resistance.

Power passes, nothing is stored, and impedance scales by n²

power

With unit 06's complex power $S = VI^*$ and $n$ real:

$$V_1I_1^* = nV_2\Big(-\frac{I_2}{n}\Big)^* = -V_2I_2^* .$$

What enters port 1 leaves port 2, $P$ and $Q$ alike. Instantaneously, $v_1i_1 + v_2i_2 = 0$; and the stored energy $\tfrac12L_1i_m^2 \le \hat V_1^2/(2\omega^2L_1) \to 0$. The ideal transformer stores nothing.

reflected impedance

A load $Z_L$ on the secondary is seen at the primary as $n^2Z_L$; a load on the primary is seen at the secondary as $Z/n^2$. Moving an impedance across is called referring it.

Units: $n$ dimensionless, so $n^2Z_L$ is in Ω ✓. The phase of $Z_L$ is unchanged: power factor passes through.

From the source's terminals the two circuits cannot be told apart. The power, the power factor and every current on the primary side are the same.

Power. $S_1 = V_1I_1^*$ is the complex power absorbed at port 1 (passive convention), $S_2 = V_2I_2^*$ at port 2. The element law gives $S_1 + S_2 = 0$: the transformer absorbs no complex power, so the source's $P$ and $Q$ reach the load unchanged. That is unit 06's conservation of complex power applied to an element that is itself a conservation law. In time, $v_1i_1 = nv_2\cdot(-i_2/n) = -v_2i_2$ at every instant: not even a momentary store.

Nothing stored, as a limit. At $k = 1$ the stored energy is $\tfrac12L_1i_m^2$ (the lemma's notes). With $v_1 = \hat V_1\cos\omega t$, $i_m = \hat V_1\sin\omega t/(\omega L_1)$, so the energy is $\hat V_1^2\sin^2\omega t/(2\omega^2L_1)$, which vanishes as $L_1 \to \infty$. The flux $\Phi = \mathcal PN_1i_m$ does not vanish — it is fixed by the voltage, $N_1\Phi = \int v_1\,\mathrm dt$ — but the energy it costs, $\Phi^2/2\mathcal P$, does, because the iron makes $\mathcal P$ huge. The ideal core carries flux for free.

Reflected impedance. From the theorem, $V_1/I_1 = nV_2/(-I_2/n) = n^2(-V_2/I_2) = n^2Z_L$. The other direction: a source impedance $Z_s$ in series with the primary is seen from the secondary as $Z_s/n^2$ (problem P4 uses this). A resistor stays a resistor, an inductor an inductor ($n^2\cdot i\omega L = i\omega(n^2L)$), a capacitor a capacitor of $C/n^2$ ($n^2/(i\omega C) = 1/(i\omega C/n^2)$). The next two slides use all three.

The T equivalent circuit

the T equivalent circuit (exact)

For any $a > 0$, the coupled pair $(L_1, L_2, M)$ has the same two-port equations as: series $L_1 - aM$, shunt $aM$, series $a^2L_2 - aM$, then an ideal transformer $a : 1$.

proof

Behind the ideal transformer, $V_2' = aV_2$, $I_2' = I_2/a$. The T gives $V_1 = i\omega(L_1 - aM)I_1 + i\omega aM(I_1 + I_2') = i\omega L_1I_1 + i\omega MI_2$ ✓, and $aV_2 = i\omega(a^2L_2 - aM)I_2' + i\omega aM(I_1 + I_2') = a(i\omega MI_1 + i\omega L_2I_2)$ ✓. $\square$

The natural $a$ is $n = N_1/N_2$. Then the three inductances are $N_1^2$ times the permeances of three flux paths (next slide), all positive. Other choices are allowed and can make one element negative: the split is a choice (problem P4).

Exact for every $a$. With $a = n$ the two series elements are the leakage inductances and the shunt one is the magnetising inductance.

Full proof

Let the ideal transformer of ratio $a$ have primary voltage $V_2'$ and primary current $-I_2'$ flowing into its dot from the T (so $I_2'$ is the current entering the T's right-hand port). By its law, $V_2' = aV_2$ and $-I_2' = -I_2/a$, i.e. $I_2' = I_2/a$. The shunt element carries $I_1 + I_2'$. Kirchhoff's voltage law round the left loop: $V_1 = i\omega(L_1 - aM)I_1 + i\omega aM(I_1 + I_2')$; with $I_2' = I_2/a$ this is $i\omega L_1I_1 + i\omega MI_2$, the first coupled equation. Round the right loop: $V_2' = i\omega(a^2L_2 - aM)I_2' + i\omega aM(I_1 + I_2') = i\omega a^2L_2(I_2/a) + i\omega aMI_1 = a(i\omega L_2I_2 + i\omega MI_1) = aV_2$ ✓, the second coupled equation times $a$. The two two-ports have identical equations at every $\omega$, hence identical behaviour in any circuit; in the time domain the same computation goes through with $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$ in place of $i\omega$.

Why a free parameter. The coupled pair has three numbers, $L_1, L_2, M$; the T has four, three inductances and a ratio. One is redundant, and no terminal measurement can fix it: only the ratio of the turns, a fact about the inside, makes $a = N_1/N_2$ the natural choice. With $a = \sqrt{L_1/L_2}$ the two leakage elements are equal, $(1 - k)L_1$ each referred to the primary, and the shunt is $kL_1$.

Referred. Everything to the left of the ideal transformer is "referred to the primary": the secondary's leakage $L_2 - M/a$ appears multiplied by $a^2$, the reflected-impedance rule. Move the ideal transformer to the far left instead, and everything is referred to the secondary, divided by $a^2$.

Leakage flux and magnetising flux

Split the flux by where it goes. The core flux threads both windings, with permeance $\mathcal P_m$; each winding's leakage flux closes through the air round that winding alone, with permeances $\mathcal P_1$, $\mathcal P_2$:

$$L_1 = N_1^2(\mathcal P_m + \mathcal P_1),\quad L_2 = N_2^2(\mathcal P_m + \mathcal P_2),$$ $$M = N_1N_2\mathcal P_m .$$
the T, read physically ($a = n$)

Primary leakage $L_1 - nM = N_1^2\mathcal P_1$. Secondary leakage, referred: $n^2L_2 - nM = N_1^2\mathcal P_2$. Magnetising: $nM = N_1^2\mathcal P_m$. Each is $N_1^2$ times the permeance of one path.

And $k = \mathcal P_m/\sqrt{(\mathcal P_m + \mathcal P_1)(\mathcal P_m + \mathcal P_2)} < 1$ exactly when some flux leaks. Iron makes $\mathcal P_m \gg \mathcal P_1, \mathcal P_2$.

Schematic. The core flux threads both windings and becomes the magnetising branch; each leakage loop threads one winding and becomes a series element on its own side.

The derivation. Winding 1's ampere-turns $N_1i_1$ drive flux $\mathcal P_mN_1i_1$ round the core and $\mathcal P_1N_1i_1$ round its own leakage path; winding 2's likewise. The core path is threaded by both windings, each leakage path by one. So the flux linkage of winding 1 is $N_1[\mathcal P_m(N_1i_1 + N_2i_2) + \mathcal P_1N_1i_1]$: read off $L_1 = N_1^2(\mathcal P_m + \mathcal P_1)$ and $M = N_1N_2\mathcal P_m$. Then $L_1 - nM = N_1^2(\mathcal P_m + \mathcal P_1) - (N_1/N_2)N_1N_2\mathcal P_m = N_1^2\mathcal P_1$, and the rest likewise.

An idealisation. Real leakage flux threads some turns of a winding and not others, so "a path threaded by one winding" is an average. Fitzgerald §2.4 ("Transformer reactances and equivalent circuits") builds the equivalent circuit from this split of the flux into mutual and leakage parts. Leakage flux runs largely through air, which is linear, so the leakage elements stay good constant inductances even where the iron is not quite linear.

Leakage is small but not negligible. In the street transformer it is all of the 4%: section 4 measures the series reactance as $39.2\ \Omega$ on the 20 kV side, which at 20 A drops 783 V — and that is what limits the current if the 400 V side is short-circuited. A transformer with no leakage at all would let a short circuit draw unlimited current. Some leakage is designed in.

Copper loss and core loss

Two more elements, each a resistance with a physical cause. The windings' own resistances go in series: copper loss $I^2R$, growing as the load squared. The iron's losses go in shunt, across the voltage that makes the flux: core loss $|V|^2/R_c$, nearly independent of load.

The real transformer's circuit, referred to the primary: an ideal transformer plus three imperfections — leakage ($X_1$, $X_2'$), finite magnetising inductance ($X_m$), and losses in copper ($R_1$, $R_2'$) and iron ($R_c$).

Why the core loss sits across the voltage. The core losses of the next two slides depend on the flux in the core: hysteresis on the peak flux density, eddy currents on its square. The flux is set by the voltage — $N_1\,\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dt$ is the voltage across the magnetising branch — so a loss proportional to $\hat B^2$ is proportional to $|V|^2$ at fixed frequency: a resistance across the magnetising branch, $R_c$, is the model that does that. It is exact for the eddy-current loss in the classical estimate (it is $\propto\hat B^2$) and a linearisation for hysteresis, whose loss grows with $\hat B$ less simply. At the nearly constant voltage a distribution transformer runs at, $R_c$ draws nearly the same power at every load.

Copper. The windings are copper or aluminium (unit 00's resistivities); their resistance is fixed, so their loss is $I^2R$, proportional to the square of the load. The name "copper loss" is used whatever the metal.

Names. $X_1 = \omega(L_1 - nM)$ and $X_2' = \omega(n^2L_2 - nM)$ are the leakage reactances, $X_m = \omega nM$ the magnetising reactance. Section 4 measures all of them, two at a time.

Hysteresis: the loss per cycle is the loop's area

Round a toroid of volume $Al$, the winding's current fixes $H$ (cited: Griffiths ch. 6, $\oint\vec H\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = I_{\text{free}}$), and Faraday fixes the voltage:

$$Hl = Ni,\qquad v = NA\,\frac{\mathrm dB}{\mathrm dt}.$$
energy into the core, per cycle $$\oint v\,i\,\mathrm dt = Al\oint H\,\mathrm dB .$$

Per unit volume, the area of the B–H loop. Times $f$ and the volume: the hysteresis loss.

Units: $\mathrm{A\,m^{-1}\cdot T} = \mathrm{A\,m^{-1}\cdot V\,s\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$ ✓.

Check. A linear material, $B = \mu H$: $\oint H\,\mathrm dB = \oint\mathrm d(B^2/2\mu) = 0$ — stored and returned, no loss ✓.

Schematic loop, not a measured one. Traversed anticlockwise, so $\oint H\,\mathrm dB > 0$: the source puts in more on the way up than it gets back on the way down.

Full proof

A thin toroid of mean length $l$ and section $A$ with an $N$-turn winding; neglect the winding's resistance (that is the copper loss, counted separately). (i) Current to $H$ (cited, Griffiths ch. 6, §6.3): in matter, Ampère's law holds for $\vec H$ with the free current alone, $\oint\vec H\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = I_{\text{free, enc}}$; round the core, $H = Ni/l$ by the toroid's symmetry. (ii) Voltage to $B$: Faraday's law (MR 24) for the winding, whose flux linkage is $NBA$, gives $v = NA\,\mathrm dB/\mathrm dt$ (passive convention). (iii) Power in: $vi = NA\,\dot B\cdot Hl/N = (Al)\,H\dot B$. Over one period the state returns to where it started, and the energy delivered is $\int_0^Tvi\,\mathrm dt = Al\oint H\,\mathrm dB$.

Sign of the area. In the plane with $H$ across and $B$ up, Green's theorem (MR 04) gives $\oint H\,\mathrm dB$ = the enclosed area for an anticlockwise circuit. A ferromagnet's loop runs anticlockwise: rising $H$ meets the lower branch (at $H = 0$ the material still remembers its negative magnetisation), falling $H$ the upper one. So the energy absorbed per cycle is positive, and the material heats. That the loop exists at all — $B$ depending on the history of $H$, not only its present value — is ferromagnetism (cited, Griffiths §6.4.2); the shape is the material's, and must be measured; Fitzgerald §1.3 and §1.4 (properties of magnetic materials; AC excitation) treat real steels.

What it says about design. At a fixed peak flux density the loop is the same each cycle, so the loss is proportional to $f$ and to the volume of iron. Steel with a narrow loop — small coercive field $H_c$ — wastes less. An empirical formula for how the loop's area grows with the peak flux density is due to Steinmetz; this deck uses none.

The linear check, in one line. If $B = \mu H$ with constant $\mu$, $H\,\mathrm dB = (B/\mu)\,\mathrm dB = \mathrm d(B^2/2\mu)$, exact, so its integral round a closed path is zero: a linear core stores $B^2/2\mu$ per unit volume and returns it. Problem P6 computes a loss from a given loop area.

Eddy currents, and why the core is laminated

The steel conducts. Faraday's law in the core itself drives currents round the changing flux — eddy currents — and they heat it. In a sheet of thickness $d$, with $B = \hat B\cos\omega t$ along the sheet and uniform:

$$E(x) = -x\,\dot B ,$$

and $\sigma E^2$ averaged over time and over $|x| \le d/2$ gives

classical eddy-current loss $$p = \frac{\sigma\omega^2\hat B^2d^2}{24}\quad \text{per unit volume.}$$

Units: $\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}\cdot s^{-2}\cdot T^2\cdot m^2} = \mathrm{A\,V^{-1}\,m^{-1}\cdot V^2m^{-2}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}$ ✓.

At fixed volume of iron the loss scales as $d^2$. So the core is built of thin insulated sheets: laminations.

Schematic. The eddy current circulates in planes across the flux. Cutting the section into sheets confines each loop to its own sheet, where it has far less EMF to drive it.

Full proof

Set-up. One sheet, thickness $d$ in $x$ ($-d/2 \le x \le d/2$), wide in $y$ and long in $z$; the flux runs along $z$, $\vec B = B(t)\hat z$, uniform through the sheet. Uniform $B$ is the assumption: it holds when the eddy currents are too weak to change the field they are driven by, which is the thin-sheet condition at the end of these notes.

Faraday, pointwise (MR 24): $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial\vec B/\partial t$. Far from the sheet's edges nothing depends on $y$ or $z$, and by the symmetry $x \to -x$ the field is $\vec E = E_y(x)\hat y$ with $E_y$ odd in $x$. The $z$ component of the curl is $\partial_xE_y = -\dot B$, so $E_y = -x\dot B$ (the constant is zero by oddness). The current $J_y = \sigma E_y$ runs up one face and down the other, closing round the ends: the loops in the figure.

Ohm, and the average. Power per unit volume $\sigma E_y^2 = \sigma x^2\dot B^2$. With $B = \hat B\cos\omega t$, $\dot B^2 = \omega^2\hat B^2\sin^2\omega t$, whose time average is $\tfrac12\omega^2\hat B^2$. Over the thickness, $\frac1d\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}x^2\,\mathrm dx = d^2/12$. Product: $p = \sigma\omega^2\hat B^2d^2/24$. With $\omega = 2\pi f$ it is $\pi^2\sigma f^2\hat B^2d^2/6$.

Why laminating wins. For a fixed total volume of iron the loss per unit volume is all that matters, and it is $\propto d^2$: ten sheets of $d/10$ lose a hundredth of what one block of thickness $d$ would, in the same sense. The insulation between sheets is essential: without it the loops would close across the whole stack and the block would be solid again. Note also $\propto f^2$, against hysteresis's $\propto f$.

When the estimate fails. The eddy currents make their own field, opposing the change; if the sheet is thick enough they push the flux towards its surfaces. That is the skin effect, which branch 10 derives: the field penetrates a conductor to a depth $\delta = \sqrt{2/\omega\mu\sigma}$ (stated here, proved in branch 10). The estimate above needs $d \ll \delta$. Problem P5 computes $\delta$ for a steel with an assumed $\mu_r = 1000$.

section 4

Measuring it: the open- and short-circuit tests

Two measurements, each at a condition where one branch of the circuit carries almost nothing, turn a nameplate's loss figures and its 4% into every element of the equivalent circuit.

07 · measuring it

The open-circuit test

the open-circuit test

Rated voltage on one winding, the other left open. Measure $|V|$, $|I|$ and the mean power $P$ (a wattmeter reads the mean of $v(t)i(t)$).

Why it sees only the shunt branch. With the secondary open, only the small no-load current flows, through the series elements and the shunt branch. Its drop across the series elements is tiny, so the shunt branch has the full $V$ across it, and $P$ is the core loss. Then

$$R_c = \frac{|V|^2}{P},\qquad X_m = \frac{|V|^2}{Q},$$

with $Q = \sqrt{|VI|^2 - P^2}$.

Units: $\mathrm{V^2/W} = \Omega$ ✓. The power measured is the no-load loss $P_0$.

Fed from the low-voltage side, where rated voltage is easier to supply. The series branch, greyed, carries the no-load current but drops almost nothing.

The approximation, justified with the street transformer's numbers. On the 400 V side the series impedance (both windings, referred) is $|3.25 + 15.7i|\ \mathrm{m\Omega} = 16.0\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$ (from the short-circuit test, next slide). At the no-load current of 5 A it drops $0.080$ V, a fraction $2\times10^{-4}$ of the 400 V, and dissipates at most $5^2\times3.25\times10^{-3} = 0.081$ W, against 387 W. So to four figures the meters read the shunt branch alone. Strictly the primary's own leakage sits in front of the shunt branch and the secondary's behind it; with the secondary open, only the primary's carries current, and the argument above, using both, is an upper bound.

Why from the low-voltage side. Either side gives the same circuit, referred to that side. Rated voltage at 400 V needs a 400 V supply and instruments; at 20 kV it would need a 20 kV supply. The test is then made at the voltage the branch is meant to see — important because the core loss depends on the flux, set by the voltage. Fitzgerald ch. 2 describes both tests.

The reactive power. A wattmeter reads $P$; the product of the RMS meter readings is $|S| = |V||I|$; unit 06's power triangle gives $Q = \sqrt{|S|^2 - P^2}$. The shunt branch's $R_c$ and $X_m$ are in parallel and share the voltage, so $P = |V|^2/R_c$ and $Q = |V|^2/X_m$ separately. The no-load power factor $P/|S|$ is lagging — the magnetising current that unit 06 meant when it said inductive loads draw lagging current.

07 · measuring it

The short-circuit test

the short-circuit test

One winding shorted; raise the voltage on the other until rated current flows. The voltage needed, as a fraction of rated, is the short-circuit impedance; the power is the load loss $P_k$.

Why it sees only the series branch. The voltage is only a few per cent of rated, so the flux is too: the shunt branch draws a few per cent of its already small current, and its loss falls as $|V|^2$. So $P$ is the copper loss, $|V|/|I|$ the series impedance:

$$R = \frac{P}{|I|^2},\qquad |Z| = \frac{|V|}{|I|},$$

and $X = \sqrt{|Z|^2 - R^2}$.

Units: $\mathrm{W/A^2} = \Omega$ ✓.

Fed from the high-voltage side, where rated current is the smaller one. With the far side shorted, the series branch takes the whole current; the shunt branch, greyed, sees only 4% of its rated voltage.

The approximation, with numbers. At 800 V, 4% of 20 kV, the shunt branch's current is 4% of its no-load value at rated voltage: $0.04\times0.1 = 0.004$ A against the 20 A flowing, and its loss $0.04^2\times387 = 0.62$ W against 3250 W. So the meters read the series branch, to two parts in ten thousand.

Why the same "4%" is two things. Defined as the voltage needed to drive rated current with the far side shorted, $|Z_{\text{eq}}|\,I_{\text{rated}}/V_{\text{rated}}$, it is also the fraction of rated voltage lost across the series impedance at rated current. IEC 60076-5:2006 (§3.2.2.3 and Table 1) gives "recognized minimum values" of short-circuit impedance at rated current, 4.0% for two-winding transformers from 25 to 630 kVA; lower values are, in the standard's words, subject to agreement between manufacturer and purchaser. The street transformer is taken at that 4%.

The load loss. Regulation 548/2014 calls $P_k$ the load loss: the loss at rated current, which this test measures. The no-load loss $P_0$ is what the open-circuit test measures. Both are maximum permitted values in its Table I.1; the example takes a transformer exactly at the limits.

The voltage drop under load, and what the 4% means

With the series branch referred to the secondary and $I$ the current delivered to the load, $V_{\text{open}} = V + I(R + iX)$. Project the small drop on $V$ (current lagging by $\varphi$):

$$\Delta V \approx |I|\,(R\cos\varphi + X\sin\varphi).$$

The street transformer at 1000 A: 3.25 V (0.81%) at unity power factor; 12.0 V (3.0%) at 0.8 lagging. Units: $\mathrm{A\cdot\Omega} = \mathrm V$ ✓.

the 4%, read as a fault current

Short the 400 V terminals with the 20 kV side held: $|I| = 1000\ \mathrm A/0.04 = 25$ kA. The leakage that costs 3% of voltage at full load is also what holds a short circuit to 25 times rated — so it is not designed away.

The drop $I(R + iX)$ is small beside $V$, so $|V_{\text{open}}| - |V|$ is nearly its component along $V$: $IR\cos\varphi + IX\sin\varphi$.

Full proof

Take $V$ real and positive and $I = |I|e^{-i\varphi}$ (lagging by $\varphi$). Then $I(R + iX) = |I|\big[(R\cos\varphi + X\sin\varphi) + i(X\cos\varphi - R\sin\varphi)\big]$. Write $a$ and $b$ for the real and imaginary parts. $|V_{\text{open}}| = \sqrt{(V + a)^2 + b^2} = (V + a)\sqrt{1 + b^2/(V + a)^2} = V + a + b^2/2V + \dots$ So $\Delta V = a + O(b^2/V)$, the formula on the slide, with an error of order $b^2/2V$.

The numbers. On the 400 V side $R = 3.25\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$, $X = 15.67\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$, $|I| = 1000$ A. Unity: $a = 3.25$ V, $b = 15.67$ V, error $15.67^2/800 = 0.31$ V, and the exact $|V_{\text{open}}| - 400 = 3.55$ V. At 0.8 lagging ($\sin\varphi = 0.6$): $a = 2.60 + 9.40 = 12.00$ V; exact $12.14$ V. The approximation misses by 0.3 V at most on 400 V.

The fault current. With the 20 kV side held at 20 kV by a network much stronger than the transformer (an assumption: the network's own impedance neglected) and the 400 V terminals shorted, the current is limited by the series impedance alone: $|I| = 400\ \mathrm V/16.0\ \mathrm{m\Omega} = 25.0$ kA $= 1000/0.04$. That is what the 4% means operationally. A smaller impedance would give a stiffer supply but a larger fault current, which everything downstream — cables, fuses, switchgear — must survive. section 5

Efficiency, and where it peaks

One loss is fixed and one grows as the load squared. Their ratio to the delivered power is smallest at one load — where the two losses are equal.

07 · efficiency

Efficiency against load

At load fraction $x$ of the rated $|S|$, power factor $\cos\varphi$, and rated voltage: the delivered power is $x|S|\cos\varphi$, the core loss $P_0$ (fixed), the copper loss $x^2P_k$ (current $\propto x$). So

$$\eta(x) = \frac{x|S|\cos\varphi}{x|S|\cos\varphi + P_0 + x^2P_k}.$$

Units: W over W ✓. Limits: $x \to 0$, $\eta \to 0$ (all core loss); $x \to \infty$, $\eta \to 0$ (all copper loss) ✓.

The street transformer: $|S| = 400$ kVA, $P_0 = 387$ W, $P_k = 3250$ W. At unity power factor its efficiency stays above 99% from 10.5% to 114% of rated load.

Computed for the street transformer. A broad, flat maximum: the curve is within 0.4 percentage points of its peak from 15% to full load.

The model's assumptions. The voltage is held at its rated value, so the flux and the core loss are fixed at $P_0$, and the current is proportional to the load, so the copper loss is $(x)^2$ times its rated value $P_k$. The small change in voltage with load (previous section, 3% at most) is neglected. Losses other than these two — in the tank, in the oil's cooling — are not modelled separately; the regulation's $P_k$ is defined as the load loss measured at rated current, so it includes whatever the short-circuit test sees.

Points on the curves. Unity power factor: $x = 0.1$, 98.96%; $x = 0.25$, 99.41%; peak at $x = 0.345$, 99.44%; $x = 0.5$, 99.40%; $x = 1$, 99.10%. At 0.8: 98.71%, 99.27%, 99.30%, 99.26%, 98.88%. The power factor changes only the delivered power, not the losses, because the losses depend on $|V|$ and $|I|$, not on the phase between them.

Why so high. At full load the copper loss is $3250/400\,000 = 0.81\%$ of rated $|S|$ — the same fraction as the resistive voltage drop on the voltage-drop slide, as it must be ($I^2R/VI = IR/V$). The core loss is $387/400\,000 = 0.10\%$.

07 · efficiency

Efficiency peaks where copper loss equals core loss

theorem

For any power factor, $\eta(x)$ is greatest at

$$x^* = \sqrt{P_0/P_k},\quad \text{where}\quad x^{*2}P_k = P_0 ,$$ $$\eta_{\max} = \Big(1 + \frac{2\sqrt{P_0P_k}}{|S|\cos\varphi}\Big)^{-1}.$$
proof

$1/\eta = 1 + (P_0/x + xP_k)/(|S|\cos\varphi)$. By AM–GM, $P_0/x + xP_k \ge 2\sqrt{P_0P_k}$, with equality iff $P_0/x = xP_k$. $\square$

Units: $\sqrt{\mathrm{W/W}}$ dimensionless ✓; $\sqrt{\mathrm W\cdot\mathrm W}/\mathrm{VA}$ dimensionless ✓.

Computed. Loss per unit delivered is the slope of the line from the origin to the total-loss curve; it is least where that line touches the curve — at $x^*$, where copper loss has risen to equal the core loss.

Full proof, by calculus

Maximising $\eta$ is minimising $f(x) = P_0/x + xP_k$ on $x > 0$. $f'(x) = -P_0/x^2 + P_k$, zero only at $x^{*2} = P_0/P_k$, and $f''(x) = 2P_0/x^3 > 0$, so it is the global minimum ($f \to \infty$ at both ends). At $x^*$, $f = 2\sqrt{P_0P_k}$, which gives $\eta_{\max}$. Nothing used the power factor: it only scales the delivered power, so the maximising $x$ is the same for every $\cos\varphi$, and the efficiency at it is lower for a lower power factor.

AM–GM, for the record. For $a, b \ge 0$, $a + b \ge 2\sqrt{ab}$ because $(\sqrt a - \sqrt b)^2 \ge 0$, with equality iff $a = b$. Here $a = P_0/x$ and $b = xP_k$, whose product $P_0P_k$ does not depend on $x$: that is what makes the bound attainable and the answer "equal losses".

The tangent picture. Total loss $\ell(x) = P_0 + x^2P_k$ is convex. Loss per delivered watt is $\ell(x)/(x|S|\cos\varphi)$, the slope of the chord from the origin to $(x, \ell(x))$ divided by a constant. The smallest such slope is the tangent from the origin, which touches where $\ell(x)/x = \ell'(x)$: $P_0/x + xP_k = 2xP_k$, the same condition. The figure's line has slope $2\sqrt{P_0P_k} = 2243$ W per unit $x$.

Units of the theorem's statement. $P_0/P_k$ is a ratio of powers, so $x^*$ is a pure number; $2\sqrt{P_0P_k}$ is a power, so its ratio to $|S|\cos\varphi$ is a pure number. Checked on the slide.

The core loss never stops

A street transformer is energised whenever any house on the street might want power: all year, $8760$ h. Over a year,

$$E_{\text{core}} = 8760\ \mathrm h\cdot P_0,$$ $$E_{\text{copper}} = 8760\ \mathrm h\cdot\langle x^2\rangle P_k ,$$

with $\langle x^2\rangle$ the mean of the squared load fraction. For the street transformer, the core loss alone is $8760\times0.387 = 3390$ kWh a year, at any load.

the design point

The two annual losses are equal when $\sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle} = \sqrt{P_0/P_k} = x^*$. A transformer whose load is mostly light gains most from a small $P_0$; one run near full load, from a small $P_k$. The regulation sets both.

Computed for the street transformer. Left of 0.345 the iron costs more than the copper over a year; right of it, the copper does. No particular street's load is claimed.

Why the mean square. The copper loss at each instant is $x(t)^2P_k$, so its energy over a year is $P_k\int x^2\,\mathrm dt = 8760\ \mathrm h\times P_k\langle x^2\rangle$. A load that sits at $x = 0.5$ all year and one that alternates between 0 and $1/\sqrt2$ have the same copper loss; the mean of $x$ is not what matters. The core loss is $P_0$ every hour the transformer is connected, which for a street transformer is every hour.

The numbers. $8760\times387 = 3.390\times10^6$ Wh $= 3.39$ MWh. Copper at $\sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle} = 0.6$: $8760\times3250\times0.36 = 10.25$ MWh. The crossing is at $0.345$, the same $x^*$ as the efficiency peak — the annual version of the same theorem.

What is and is not claimed. No load profile for any real street is stated: none was checked. The point is the structure: a fixed loss paid every hour against a variable one paid in proportion to the square of the load. section 6

Why power lines run at high voltage

Unit 04's $I^2R$, at fixed delivered power: the loss falls as the square of the voltage. The transformer is what makes the voltage a free choice at every stage.

07 · high voltage

At fixed power, line loss falls as 1/V²

line loss

A load takes power $P$ at voltage $V$ and unity power factor through a line of total loop resistance $R$. The current is $P/V$, so the line loses

$$P_{\text{line}} = \frac{P^2R}{V^2},\qquad \frac{P_{\text{line}}}{P} = \frac{PR}{V^2}.$$

Units: $\mathrm{W^2\,\Omega/V^2} = \mathrm{W^2/W} = \mathrm W$ ✓. Ten times the voltage, a hundredth of the loss.

At power factor $\cos\varphi$ the current is $P/(V\cos\varphi)$ and the loss is $1/\cos^2\varphi$ times larger — unit 06's point that reactive current heats every wire on the way.

This is the promise of unit 04: the reason lines run at high voltage.

Computed for 100 MW through 10 Ω. On logarithmic axes the law is a line of slope $-2$; it crosses 1% at 316 kV.

The model. One line, one load, a total loop resistance $R$ (out and back), unity power factor, and the load's voltage $V$ given. It is the DC or single-phase picture: unit 11 gives the line three phases, and unit 08 asks whether a line hundreds of kilometres long can be a lumped resistance at all (at 50 Hz a wavelength is about $c/f = 6000$ km, so a few hundred kilometres is a small fraction of one; unit 08 says what that fraction costs). The 10 Ω is assumed for the comparison, not taken from any real line.

The derivation. $P = VI$ at unity power factor gives $I = P/V$; the line's loss is $I^2R$ (unit 04) $= P^2R/V^2$. With power factor $\cos\varphi$, $P = VI\cos\varphi$ (unit 06), so $I = P/(V\cos\varphi)$ and the loss is $P^2R/(V^2\cos^2\varphi)$. A workshop at 0.7 would cost $1/0.49 = 2.04$ times the loss of the same real power at unity — unit 06's factor 0.49, read backwards.

The law in one sentence. The power is a product $VI$; the loss depends only on $I$. So the same power at a higher voltage needs a smaller current, and the loss, quadratic in current, falls quadratically. The transformer's job, from the line's point of view, is exactly the theorem of section 2: it exchanges current for voltage at constant power.

At 20 kV the line cannot carry it at all

Hold the sending end at $V_s = 20$ kV. Then the line is a Thévenin source, $V_s$ behind $R = 10\ \Omega$, and unit 04's maximum-power theorem caps what any load can receive:

$$P_{\text{load}} \le \frac{V_s^2}{4R} = \frac{(20\ \mathrm{kV})^2}{40\ \Omega} = 10\ \mathrm{MW}.$$

100 MW is ten times beyond reach. At 132 kV the cap is 436 MW; at 400 kV, 4000 MW.

what "250 MW of loss" meant

The formula assumed the load already had 20 kV across it. No sending voltage of 20 kV can arrange that at 100 MW: the voltage at the load collapses first. Unit 13 makes this precise for AC, where the line's reactance sets the limit.

Full proof

Sending voltage $V_s$, line $R$, any load. The current $I$ is whatever the load makes it; the power entering the line is $V_sI$ and the line takes $I^2R$, so the load receives $p(I) = V_sI - I^2R$ (energy balance). Its maximum over $I$ is at $I = V_s/2R$, where $p = V_s^2/4R$: unit 04's maximum-power theorem, with the load matched to the line. For $V_s = 20$ kV and $R = 10\ \Omega$: $I = 1000$ A and $p = 10$ MW, with 10 MW also lost in the line and the load's voltage at 10 kV. At 132 kV, $132\,000^2/40 = 435.6$ MW; at 400 kV, $4\times10^9$ W.

Unit 04 again, the other way round. Unit 04 showed that a power system runs far from its matched point because the match wastes half the energy. Here the matched point is the most it could ever deliver, and it is still a tenth of what is wanted. Both statements are the same parabola.

AC. A real overhead line has reactance as well as resistance, often more, and with reactance the transfer limit involves the phase between the two ends' voltages. Unit 13 derives it, $P_{\max} = V_1V_2/X$, and its stability.

07 · limits

The field at a line conductor's surface

Unit 01's thin-wire picture: two conductors of radius $r$, centres $D \gg r$ apart, carrying $\pm\lambda$ per metre. MR 23's two-dimensional potential, $-(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln\rho$, gives the voltage between them and the field at each surface:

$$V = \frac{\lambda}{\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac Dr,\qquad E_s = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r}.$$
the surface field $$E_s = \frac{\hat V}{2r\ln(D/r)}$$

at peak voltage $\hat V$ between the conductors. Units: V/m ✓.

It grows as $1/r$: a thin wire concentrates the field at its surface. The spacing enters only through a logarithm.

Schematic, not to scale: on a real line $D/r$ is hundreds. The field lines of two opposite line charges are circles through both; they crowd at the surfaces, where the field is largest.

Full proof

A line charge $\lambda$ on the $z$ axis has, by Gauss's law on a cylinder (MR 23), $E = \lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0\rho)$ radially, and so potential $-(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln\rho + $ const — MR 23's radial solution in two dimensions. Put $+\lambda$ at the centre of conductor 1 and $-\lambda$ at the centre of conductor 2 (the thin-wire picture of unit 01's The kettle's two-wire cable, computed). On conductor 1's surface the potential is $-(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)(\ln r - \ln D)$ (its own charge at distance $r$, the other at distance about $D$), and on conductor 2's it is the negative. The difference is $V = (\lambda/\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(D/r)$. The field at conductor 1's surface is its own charge's $\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)$ plus the other's $\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0D)$, which is smaller by $r/D$ and dropped. Eliminate $\lambda$: $E_s = V/(2r\ln(D/r))$. The same holds at every instant, so the peak field goes with the peak voltage $\hat V = \sqrt2|V|$.

What the approximation costs. With $r/D$ of order $10^{-3}$, the neglected terms are parts in a thousand: the charge on each conductor shifts slightly towards the other, as unit 01's notes explained. For the corona estimate that is far below everything else uncertain.

The model, stated. Two conductors with the whole voltage between them: a single-phase line. A real 400 kV line is three-phase, with 400 kV between any two phases and $400/\sqrt3 = 231$ kV from each to earth (unit 11), and its conductors also see the ground. The single-phase figure is an estimate with the right dependence on $r$, $D$ and $\hat V$, not a design calculation.

Corona: when the surface field ionises the air

Above a certain surface field the air next to a conductor breaks down and glows, and power is lost into it: corona. Peek measured the onset for parallel wires in air (cited, Peek 1920, ch. III):

$$g_v = 29.8\Big(1 + \frac{0.301}{\sqrt r}\Big)\ \mathrm{kV/cm\ peak},$$

$r$ in cm, at 76 cm of mercury and 25 °C. For large $r$ it tends to 29.8 kV/cm $\approx$ 3 MV/m.

400 kV ($\hat V = 565.7$ kV), $D = 10$ m: $r = 0.5$ cm gives 7.4 MV/m, far above onset (4.2). $r = 1.5$ cm gives 2.9 MV/m — under the 3.7 MV/m for a smooth wire, but over the 2.7 at which Peek saw corona start along a stranded cable.

Computed: the single-phase estimate at 400 kV, 10 m spacing (solid), against Peek's onset for a smooth wire (dashed) and for a stranded cable, from local to decided corona (band). Thin conductors are far over; a 1.5 cm stranded conductor is already inside the band.

Peek's law, cited. F. W. Peek, Jr., Dielectric Phenomena in High Voltage Engineering, 2nd ed. (McGraw-Hill, 1920), ch. III, "Visual corona": for parallel wires, the visual critical gradient $g_v = 29.8(1 + 0.301/\sqrt r)$ kV per cm, maximum (peak) value, $r$ in cm, at 76 cm barometric pressure and 25 °C, from his experiments; a factor $\delta$ for the relative air density enters at other conditions. For stranded cables of six strands or more the same chapter multiplies $g_v$ by an irregularity factor $m_v$, with $r$ the cable's overall radius: 0.72 for the first, local corona along the cable, 0.82 for decided corona; ch. IX ("Practical corona calculation for transmission lines") repeats both. The band in the figure is $g_v$ times 0.72 to 0.82. Ch. IX also gives a second factor, $m_0$ (1 for a polished wire, 0.98 to 0.93 roughened or weathered, 0.87 to 0.83 for seven-strand cables), but it belongs to a different formula — the disruptive critical voltage, at which his measured corona loss begins, built on the gradient 29.8 kV/cm without the $0.301/\sqrt r$ term — and must not be applied to $g_v$. These are his empirical results; nothing here derives them.

The numbers. $\hat V = \sqrt2\times400 = 565.7$ kV. $r = 0.5$ cm: $\ln(10/0.005) = 7.601$, $E_s = 565\,685/(0.01\times7.601) = 7.44$ MV/m; Peek's onset $29.8(1 + 0.301/\sqrt{0.5}) = 42.5$ kV/cm $= 4.25$ MV/m. $r = 1.5$ cm: $\ln 666.7 = 6.502$, $E_s = 2.90$ MV/m; onset $29.8(1 + 0.301/\sqrt{1.5}) = 37.1$ kV/cm $= 3.71$ MV/m smooth; stranded, $0.72\times3.71 = 2.67$ MV/m (local) to $0.82\times3.71 = 3.04$ MV/m (decided). The disruptive critical gradient of a seven-strand cable is $0.83$–$0.87\times2.98 = 2.47$–$2.59$ MV/m. The curves cross at $r = 10.8$ mm (smooth), and the surface-field curve lies inside the stranded band from 14.1 to 16.7 mm.

Why it matters, qualitatively. Corona dissipates power continuously along the whole line, and its onset falls as the air gets less dense (Peek's factor $\delta$); Peek's ch. V, "Corona loss", measures the loss. A line must be designed with its surface field comfortably below onset in the conditions it will meet. A single 1.5 cm stranded conductor at 400 kV is not: its 2.90 MV/m is past the point where Peek saw corona start along a cable, and past the gradient at which his loss formula begins. The next slide shows the remedy Peek's ch. III already lists as "split conductors".

Bundles: four thin conductors acting as one thick one

Replace each conductor by a bundle of $N$ subconductors of radius $r$ on a circle of radius $R$, all at one potential. Far away it acts as one conductor of equivalent radius

$$r_{\text{eq}} = \big(N\,r\,R^{N-1}\big)^{1/N}.$$

Each subconductor carries $\lambda/N$, so its surface field, averaged round it, is exactly

$$E_s = \frac{\lambda/N}{2\pi\varepsilon_0r} = \frac{\hat V}{2Nr\ln(D/r_{\text{eq}})}.$$

Four 1.5 cm subconductors on a 45 cm square: $R = 0.318$ m, $r_{\text{eq}} = 0.21$ m, and $E_s = $ 1.22 MV/m (1.39 at the worst point) — a third of the smooth-wire onset, about half the stranded one.

To scale except the subconductors. Four 1.5 cm wires behave, seen from the other phase, like one wire of 21 cm radius — and each of them sees less than half the field a single 1.5 cm wire would.

Full proof

The equivalent radius. Let the bundle carry $\lambda$ per metre, $\lambda/N$ on each subconductor (by symmetry), and let the other bundle be at distance $D \gg R$. The potential on subconductor 1's surface from its own bundle is $-\frac{\lambda/N}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_k\ln d_{1k}$, with $d_{11} = r$ and $d_{1k}$ the centre distances to the others; from the far bundle it is $+\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln D$ as before. So the bundle acts as one conductor of radius $r_{\text{eq}}$ with $\ln r_{\text{eq}} = \frac1N\sum_k\ln d_{1k}$: the geometric mean of the distances, its own radius included. For $N$ points equally spaced on a circle of radius $R$, $\prod_{k\ne1}d_{1k} = NR^{N-1}$ (the product of the distances from one $N$-th root of unity to the others is $|{\textstyle\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dz}}(z^N - 1)|_{z=1} = N$, scaled by $R^{N-1}$). Hence $r_{\text{eq}}^N = r\cdot NR^{N-1}$. For the square, $d_{12} = d_{14} = 0.45$ m and $d_{13} = 0.636$ m, so $r_{\text{eq}} = (0.015\times0.45\times0.45\times0.636)^{1/4} = 0.2097$ m, the same.

The surface field. Gauss's law round one subconductor's surface: the integral of the normal field is its charge $(\lambda/N)/\varepsilon_0$, exactly, so the field averaged round its circumference is $(\lambda/N)/(2\pi\varepsilon_0r)$. With $\lambda = \pi\varepsilon_0\hat V/\ln(D/r_{\text{eq}})$: $E_s = \hat V/(2Nr\ln(D/r_{\text{eq}})) = 565\,685/(8\times0.015\times\ln(10/0.2097)) = 565\,685/(0.12\times3.865) = 1.22$ MV/m.

The worst point. The other three subconductors make a nearly uniform field $E_{\text{ext}}$ at subconductor 1, pointing outward from the bundle's centre, of size $\frac{\lambda/N}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{N-1}{2R}$ (the sum of their fields' outward components, again a roots-of-unity sum). A conducting cylinder in a uniform field has surface field $2E_{\text{ext}}\cos\theta$ added to its own (MR 23, The cylinder beside the sphere; the subconductor's own charge adds its uniform part). So the maximum, on the outside of the bundle, is $E_s(1 + (N - 1)r/R) = 1.22\times(1 + 3\times0.015/0.318) = 1.39$ MV/m. Peek's ch. III, "Split conductors", studies this arrangement experimentally.

07 · limits

Insulation, clearance, and what high voltage costs

Corona is answered by fatter or bundled conductors. At 20 kV a single 5 mm conductor, 1 m from its partner, sees only 0.53 MV/m: no bundle needed.

Insulation. Every conductor hangs from insulators and must be held clear of towers, the ground and the other phases. The voltage those gaps must withstand grows with the line's voltage, so insulators lengthen and clearances widen with every step — larger towers, wider corridors. No figure is given here.

Charging current. A longer, higher-voltage line is also a larger capacitor, and draws reactive current to charge it each cycle: unit 08's line capacitance, and the reason unit 14 turns to direct current for long cables.

Computed with the single-phase estimate. Only the 400 kV single thin wire crosses its smooth-wire mark, though a stranded 15 mm conductor would be over its own lower onset (previous slides); the bundle sits well below both.

The 20 kV number. $\hat V = \sqrt2\times20 = 28.28$ kV, $r = 5$ mm, $D = 1$ m (both assumed for the comparison): $\ln200 = 5.298$, $E_s = 28\,284/(0.01\times5.298) = 0.534$ MV/m, an eighth of Peek's 4.25 MV/m for that radius.

What is not quantified, and why. The clearances a line needs to towers, to the ground and between phases are set by standards for overhead lines — in Europe, EN 50341-1:2012, Overhead electrical lines exceeding AC 1 kV — Part 1: General requirements, whose electrical requirements cover them. None of its figures could be checked here, so the deck states only the direction: they grow with the voltage. The same goes for insulator lengths.

Where it stops. Higher voltage lowers the loss as $1/V^2$; it raises the cost of insulation, towers and right of way, and the line's charging current. Where those balance depends on the distance and the power to be moved, which is why the chain has several levels rather than one. Units 08, 13 and 14 return to long lines: their reactance limits the power they can carry (unit 13), and beyond some length direct current wins (unit 14).
