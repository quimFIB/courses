Transmission lines: telegrapher's equations, characteristic impedance, and reflection

When a circuit is long against the distance a signal covers while it changes, Maxwell's equations reduce to two first-order equations along the line — MR 18's wave equation. Voltage and current then travel, reflect, and settle, and energy is seen in transit.

Unit 02's lumped model cannot answer: it says $12/24 = 0.5$ A at once. Unit 01's field picture says where to look — between the wires — but not when.

A line with transverse fields: in each cross-section the fields are static, so a voltage and a current are defined there. Faraday's law and charge conservation, over a length $\mathrm dz$, give two first-order equations along the line.

08 · telegrapher

A long line, and the hypothesis that fails

a transmission line

Two conductors, parallel to the $z$-axis, of uniform cross-section, in a uniform insulator of permittivity $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$ and permeability $\mu_0$. Here: perfect conductors, lossless insulator.

TEM: transverse fields

$E_z = 0$ and $B_z = 0$ everywhere between the conductors: $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ lie in the cross-section.

What fails. Unit 02's (H3), the circuit small against a wavelength: along $z$ the line may be as long as we like. What survives. Each cross-section is small, so inside the plane of one cross-section the lumped picture still holds.

A coaxial cable, the simplest TEM line. Electric field (solid) radial across the gap; magnetic field (dashed) in circles round the axis, into and out of the page in the side view. Nothing points along $z$.

Why TEM is the case to study. For two perfect conductors in a uniform insulator, fields with $E_z = B_z = 0$ exist and travel at $1/\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}$ at every frequency; that is what this section shows. Other field patterns, with $E_z$ or $B_z$ non-zero, exist too: they are the modes of waveguides, and they propagate only when the cross-section is comparable to a wavelength — stated here, not proved: it is waveguide theory, which this course does not study. Below that frequency only TEM travels.

The hypotheses, one by one. Unit 02's (H3) asks the whole circuit to be small against $\lambda$; along $z$ it is not, and this unit is about what replaces it. (H1) asks that no changing flux thread a loop outside the elements. In a TEM line $\vec B$ lies in the cross-section, so no flux threads a loop drawn in a cross-section: (H1) holds in each plane, which is why a voltage between the conductors is defined there (next slide). A loop with sides along $z$ does enclose flux, so (H1) fails along the line — and that flux is the first telegrapher's equation. (H2) goes the same way: each length of conductor carries a changing surface charge $\lambda\,\mathrm dz$, and that charge is the second equation.

The insulator. Taken as a uniform linear dielectric with $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$ (unit 02, cited from Griffiths ch. 4), and non-magnetic. In a uniform linear dielectric with no free charge, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ (Griffiths, ch. 4: the bound charge density of a uniform linear dielectric vanishes), which is all this section needs from it.

The figure is schematic: the field lines are drawn evenly, not to the $1/r$ law of unit 01.

08 · telegrapher

Each cross-section holds a static field

lemma · fields in one cross-section

In a TEM line, at each $z$ and $t$ the transverse field is a two-dimensional gradient,

$$\vec E = -\nabla_\perp\varphi, \qquad \nabla_\perp^2\varphi = 0,$$

with $\varphi$ constant on each conductor. So the voltage $v(z,t) = \varphi_1 - \varphi_2$ is the same along every path in the cross-section.

proof

Faraday's $z$-component: $(\nabla\times\vec E)_z = -\partial_tB_z = 0$, so $\vec E$ is curl-free in the plane; the annulus's hole is inside a conductor, where $\vec E = \vec 0$, so it is a gradient (notes). $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ with $E_z = 0$ gives Laplace. On a perfect conductor $\vec E$ is normal, so $\varphi$ is constant. $\square$

$\vec B$ likewise is curl- and divergence-free in the plane. The current $i(z,t)$ is the inner conductor's, by Ampère round it.

One cross-section of the coaxial cable, at one instant. Any path between the conductors gives the same $v$; the circle round the inner conductor gives its current $i$ (the displacement current through the plane is zero, since $E_z = 0$).

Full proof. Fix $z$ and $t$. In the cross-section, with $E_z = 0$, $(\nabla\times\vec E)_z = \partial_xE_y - \partial_yE_x$, and Faraday's law makes it $-\partial_tB_z = 0$. A curl-free field need not be a gradient on a region with a hole — MR 04's vortex (Closed, conservative, and the holes between them) — and the insulating region of a coaxial cable is an annulus. But the circulation of $\vec E$ round any loop encircling the inner conductor equals the circulation round a loop just inside the conductor's surface — the two differ by the flux of $(\nabla\times\vec E)_z = 0$ between them — and inside a perfect conductor $\vec E = \vec 0$. So every circulation vanishes, and a field with zero circulation round every loop is a gradient (MR 04, Three conditions, one property): $\vec E = -\nabla_\perp\varphi$. Gauss's law in the insulator, $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$, with $\partial_zE_z = 0$, gives $\nabla_\perp^2\varphi = 0$. At a perfect conductor's surface the tangential $\vec E$ is zero (it is continuous, and zero inside), so $\varphi$ is constant along each conductor's boundary.

The magnetic field. $(\nabla\times\vec B)_z = \mu_0J_z + \mu_0\varepsilon\,\partial_tE_z = 0$ in the insulator (no conduction current there, and $E_z = 0$), and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ with $B_z = 0$ makes it divergence-free in the plane. At a perfect conductor the normal component of $\vec B$ vanishes (it is continuous, and a perfect conductor excludes a changing field). Ampère's law round a loop enclosing the inner conductor, in the plane: $\oint\vec B\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \mu_0 i$ — the conduction current through the loop, with no displacement current, since $E_z = 0$. That defines $i(z, t)$ as the current in the inner conductor; the outer carries $-i$, because $\vec B$ vanishes outside a coaxial cable (unit 02).

So in each plane the problem is unit 01's. Unit 01 solved exactly these two static problems for its cable: $\vec E$ from Laplace in the annulus, $\vec B$ from Ampère. What is new is that $v$ and $i$ may now change with $z$.

08 · telegrapher

Faraday over a length dz: the first equation

Take the loop in the figure: up a radius at $z$, along the outer conductor, down at $z + \mathrm dz$, back along the inner. On the conductors $\vec E$ has no tangential part; the radial sides give $+v(z)$ and $-v(z + \mathrm dz)$. Faraday's law:

$$v(z) - v(z + \mathrm dz) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\Phi'\,\mathrm dz\big),$$

where $\Phi'$ is the flux per metre between the conductors. Unit 02 computed it for the coax: $\Phi' = L'i$ with $L' = (\mu_0/2\pi)\ln(b/a)$. So

the first telegrapher's equation $$\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = -L'\,\frac{\partial i}{\partial t}$$

Units: $\mathrm{V/m}$ against $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,A\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{V\,s\,A^{-1}\,m^{-1}\,A\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{V/m}$ ✓.

The loop for Faraday's law, in a plane containing the axis. Only its two radial sides see a field along them; the flux through it is the cable's magnetic flux over $\mathrm dz$.

Full proof. Orient the loop as drawn: up the radius at $z$ (from inner to outer conductor), along the outer conductor to $z + \mathrm dz$, down, back along the inner. Its circulation of $\vec E$: up at $z$ the integral from $\varphi_1$ to $\varphi_2$ is $\int\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \varphi_1 - \varphi_2 = v(z)$ (the previous slide); along the conductors, zero, since the tangential field vanishes at a perfect conductor; down at $z + \mathrm dz$, $-v(z + \mathrm dz)$. Faraday's law in integral form (MR 24, Faraday's law): $\oint\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = -\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int\vec B\cdot\mathrm d\vec A$, with $\mathrm d\vec A$ oriented by the right-hand rule from the loop's sense. The loop as drawn runs clockwise, so its right-hand normal points into the page, while $\vec B$ — for current $i$ to the right in the inner conductor, in the upper half — points out of it (as in the side view of A long line, and the hypothesis that fails). So the flux is $-\Phi'\mathrm dz$ and $v(z) - v(z + \mathrm dz) = +\partial_t(\Phi'\mathrm dz)$. Divide by $\mathrm dz$.

Why $\Phi' = L'i$. In each cross-section $\vec B$ is the magnetostatic field of current $i$ (previous slide), the same field unit 02 integrated: $B = \mu_0i/2\pi r$, and the flux per metre across the gap is $\int_a^b\mu_0i/(2\pi r)\,\mathrm dr = (\mu_0/2\pi)\ln(b/a)\,i$ (unit 02, Worked: the coaxial cable's $C'$ and $L'$). The insulator is non-magnetic, so $L'$ does not depend on $\varepsilon_r$.

Signs. If $i$ grows, the flux grows, and $v$ falls along $z$: the line's inductance opposes the change, as a lumped inductor's $v = L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$ does, now spread along its length.

Charge over a length dz: the second equation

Enclose the inner conductor between $z$ and $z + \mathrm dz$ in a closed surface. Current $i(z)$ enters, $i(z + \mathrm dz)$ leaves, and the difference stays as surface charge $\lambda\,\mathrm dz$ (unit 00's surface charge):

$$i(z) - i(z + \mathrm dz) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big(\lambda\,\mathrm dz\big).$$

In each cross-section $\lambda$ is the electrostatic charge per metre at voltage $v$: $\lambda = C'v$ with $C' = 2\pi\varepsilon/\ln(b/a)$ (unit 02). So

the second telegrapher's equation $$\frac{\partial i}{\partial z} = -C'\,\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}$$

Units: $\mathrm{A/m}$ against $\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}\,V\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{C\,V^{-1}\,m^{-1}\,V\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{A/m}$ ✓.

What flows in and does not flow out stays on the conductor's surface, and that surface charge ends the electric field across the gap.

Full proof. Charge conservation in integral form (MR 24: the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$, integrated over a volume) applied to the region inside the dashed surface: the rate of change of the charge inside equals the current in minus the current out. Current crosses the surface only where the conductor does — the insulator carries no conduction current — so in is $i(z)$, out is $i(z + \mathrm dz)$. The charge inside is the inner conductor's surface charge on that length, $\lambda(z, t)\,\mathrm dz$ (the conductor's interior is neutral, unit 00).

Why $\lambda = C'v$. In each cross-section $\varphi$ solves Laplace's equation with $\varphi_1 - \varphi_2 = v$ (two slides back), and Gauss's law round the inner conductor gives its charge per metre as $\lambda = \varepsilon\oint\vec E\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dl$: linear in $v$, with the constant the per-metre capacitance. For the coax it is unit 02's $C' = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(b/a)$, multiplied by $\varepsilon_r$ for a filled cable (unit 02, A dielectric multiplies $C$ by $\varepsilon_r$, cited from Griffiths ch. 4).

The surface charge moves. When $v$ changes from place to place, so does $\lambda$, and the current along the conductor is what redistributes it. Unit 00 found surface charge steering a steady current round a bend; here it is the charge on the conductors that ends the field of a travelling wave, and the current is what carries that charge forward.

$L'C' = \mu\varepsilon$ for every TEM line

Unit 02 noticed $L'C' = \mu_0\varepsilon_0$ for the coax, whatever $b/a$. It is true for every cross-section:

theorem

For any two-conductor TEM line in a uniform insulator ($\varepsilon$, $\mu$),

$$L'C' = \mu\varepsilon .$$

Units: $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,F\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{s^2\,m^{-2}}$, and $\mu\varepsilon$ is $\mathrm{s^2/m^2}$ ✓.

proof, in outline

Rotate the electric field a quarter-turn: $\hat z\times\vec E$ is curl-free, divergence-free and tangent to the conductors — everything the magnetic field must be. By uniqueness, $\vec B = k\,\hat z\times\vec E$. Ampère fixes $k = \mu\varepsilon\,i/\lambda$; then the flux is $k\,v$, so $L' = kv/i = \mu\varepsilon/C'$. $\square$ (notes)

Computed for two wires with radius one fifth of the half-spacing. Solid: electric field lines, arcs of circles through the two equivalent line charges. Dashed: magnetic field lines, which are the equipotentials and cross the electric lines at right angles.

Full proof. Fix one cross-section. Let $\vec E$ be its electrostatic field: $\vec E = -\nabla_\perp\varphi$, $\nabla_\perp^2\varphi = 0$, $\varphi = v$ on conductor 1 and $0$ on conductor 2, with charge per metre $\lambda = \varepsilon\oint_1\vec E\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dl$ on conductor 1, so $C' = \lambda/v$. Define $\vec F = \hat z\times\vec E = (-E_y, E_x)$. Then $\nabla\cdot\vec F = -\partial_xE_y + \partial_yE_x = -(\nabla\times\vec E)_z = 0$ and $(\nabla\times\vec F)_z = \partial_xE_x + \partial_yE_y = \nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$. On a conductor $\vec E$ is normal, so $\vec F$ is tangent. And round conductor 1, $\oint\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \oint\vec E\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm dl = \lambda/\varepsilon$ (with $\mathrm d\vec l$ anticlockwise, $\hat z\times$ turns the outward normal into the tangent).

The magnetic field $\vec B$ in the cross-section is curl-free, divergence-free, tangent to the conductors, with $\oint_1\vec B\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = \mu i$ (two slides back). The difference $\vec B - k\vec F$ with $k = \mu\varepsilon i/\lambda$ has all these properties and zero circulation round conductor 1, hence round every loop; so it is a gradient $\nabla\chi$ with $\nabla^2\chi = 0$ and $\partial\chi/\partial n = 0$ on the conductors (and, for an open line like two wires, falling off at infinity). Then MR 23's energy identity (Uniqueness, three ways, proof 2), in two dimensions, gives $\int|\nabla\chi|^2 = \oint\chi\,\partial_n\chi = 0$, so $\chi$ is constant, so $\vec B = k\,\hat z\times\vec E$.

The flux per metre between the conductors, through a ribbon along a path from conductor 1 to conductor 2 with unit tangent $\hat t$ and normal $\hat t\times\hat z$, is $\int k(\hat z\times\vec E)\cdot(\hat t\times\hat z)\,\mathrm dl = -k\int\vec E\cdot\hat t\,\mathrm dl$, whose magnitude is $kv$. So $L' = kv/i = \mu\varepsilon v/\lambda = \mu\varepsilon/C'$. $\square$

The figure. Two line charges $\pm\lambda$ at $x = \pm s$ have equipotentials that are circles (MR 23's two-dimensional potential $-(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon)\ln\rho$, summed); a wire of radius $r$ centred at $\pm D/2$ coincides with one of them when $s^2 = (D/2)^2 - r^2$. The field lines are circles through $\pm s$. Drawn for $D/2 = 110$, $r = 22$ in the figure's units, so $s = 107.8$.

Loss, as a model: R′ and G′

Real conductors have resistance; real insulators leak a little. Model each $\mathrm dz$ as a small circuit:

$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = -R'i - L'\frac{\partial i}{\partial t}, \qquad \frac{\partial i}{\partial z} = -G'v - C'\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}.$$

$R'$ (Ω/m): series resistance of both conductors. $G'$ (S/m): shunt conductance through the insulation.

a model, stated

With resistive conductors the fields are no longer exactly transverse, and $R'$ grows with frequency as current crowds to the surface — branch 10's skin effect. Here $R'$ and $G'$ are given numbers.

Units: $R'i$ and $G'v$ are $\mathrm{V/m}$ and $\mathrm{A/m}$ ✓.

The lossy line as a ladder of tiny sections (rectangles: resistors, the IEC symbol; humps: inductors; plates: capacitors). Kirchhoff's laws on one section, divided by $\mathrm dz$, give the two equations; with $R' = G' = 0$ they are the lossless ones.

From the ladder. Round one section, the voltage law: $v(z) - v(z + \mathrm dz) = R'\mathrm dz\,i + L'\mathrm dz\,\partial_ti$. At the node after the series elements, the current law: $i(z) - i(z + \mathrm dz) = G'\mathrm dz\,v + C'\mathrm dz\,\partial_tv$ (to first order in $\mathrm dz$). Divide by $\mathrm dz$. Each section is small, so unit 02's hypotheses hold within it; the line as a whole is not lumped, because it is made of arbitrarily many sections.

Why "model". Section 1 derived the lossless pair from Maxwell's equations. With finite conductivity there is an axial field $E_z$ at the conductors' surfaces, driving the current; the fields are then not exactly transverse, and the derivation above becomes an approximation, very good when the loss per wavelength is small. Pozar, ch. 2, "Transmission Line Theory", starts from this lumped-element ladder and then checks it against a field analysis; this deck goes the other way round.

The symbols. IEC draws a resistor as a rectangle; the American form is a zigzag. A conductance is a resistor labelled by its conductance. Unit 02 introduced both.

Near the gap, the puzzle's battery wire and bulb wire run side by side, 1 m apart, for 300 000 km: above the gap, a two-wire line; below it, another. Each is joined at its far end.

the two-wire line: $r = 1$ mm, $D = 1$ m, in air

With MR 23's two-line-charge potential (previous slide's figure, notes):

$$C' = \frac{\pi\varepsilon_0}{\operatorname{arccosh}(D/2r)} = 4.027\ \mathrm{pF/m},$$ $$L' = \frac{\mu_0}{\pi}\operatorname{arccosh}\frac{D}{2r} = 2.763\ \mu\mathrm{H/m}.$$

$\operatorname{arccosh}500 = 6.908 \approx \ln(D/r)$. Check: $L'C' = \mu_0\varepsilon_0$ ✓, as the theorem demands.

The near end, not to scale. Battery, upper port, bulb and lower port make one small loop; each port opens on 300 000 km of two-wire line.

Full derivation. Put line charges $+\lambda$ at $x = -s$ and $-\lambda$ at $x = +s$, with $s^2 = (D/2)^2 - r^2$. The potential of the pair (MR 23, Radial solutions, and where the dimension enters: a line charge in two dimensions has potential $-(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln\rho$ plus a constant) is $\varphi = (\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(\rho_2/\rho_1)$, with $\rho_{1,2}$ the distances to the two charges. On the circle of radius $r$ centred at $x = -D/2$ the ratio $\rho_2/\rho_1$ is constant (a circle of Apollonius), equal to $(D/2 + s)/r$ — evaluate at the circle's nearest point to the origin, $x = -D/2 + r$: $\rho_1 = s - D/2 + r$, $\rho_2 = s + D/2 - r$, and $(s + D/2 - r)/(s - D/2 + r) = (D/2 + s)/r$ follows from $s^2 = (D/2)^2 - r^2$. So the wire's surface is an equipotential at $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{D/2 + s}{r}$, the other wire at minus that, and by uniqueness (MR 23) this is the field of the two charged wires. Hence $v = \frac{\lambda}{\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{D/2 + s}{r} = \frac{\lambda}{\pi\varepsilon_0}\operatorname{arccosh}\frac{D}{2r}$, since $\operatorname{arccosh}x = \ln(x + \sqrt{x^2 - 1})$. $L'$ then follows from the theorem, $L' = \mu_0\varepsilon_0/C'$.

Numbers. $\operatorname{arccosh}500 = \ln(500 + \sqrt{249\,999}) = 6.9078$; $\ln1000 = 6.9078$ too (they differ by $10^{-6}$). $C' = \pi\times8.854\times10^{-12}/6.9078 = 4.027\times10^{-12}$ F/m. $L' = 4\times10^{-7}\times6.9078 = 2.763\times10^{-6}$ H/m.

Why the near end is a loop of ports. Each rectangle's long sides are 1 m apart for their whole length, so each is a two-wire line whose near-end terminals are the battery's terminal and the bulb's. The 1 m gap at the near end is where the model is weakest; section 2's warning says how.

08 · waves

The wave equation, and its speed

Differentiate the first equation in $z$, the second in $t$, and eliminate $\partial^2i/\partial z\,\partial t$:

$$\frac{\partial^2v}{\partial z^2} = L'C'\,\frac{\partial^2v}{\partial t^2},$$

and the same for $i$. That is MR 18's string, $u_{tt} = c^2u_{xx}$, with

the speed of a line $$u = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}.$$

Units: $1/\sqrt{\mathrm{s^2\,m^{-2}}} = \mathrm{m/s}$ ✓. Limit: vacuum, $\varepsilon_r = 1$, gives $c$ ✓.

Polyethylene, $\varepsilon_r = 2.25$: $u = c/1.5 = 1.999\times10^8$ m/s. Unit 00's "a large fraction of $c$, set by the insulation", computed.

A forward wave $v = v_+(t - z/u)$: the voltage pattern keeps its shape and moves at $u$.

The elimination. $\partial_z$ of $\partial_zv = -L'\partial_ti$ gives $\partial_z^2v = -L'\partial_z\partial_ti$; $\partial_t$ of $\partial_zi = -C'\partial_tv$ gives $\partial_t\partial_zi = -C'\partial_t^2v$. For $C^2$ fields the mixed derivatives agree, so $\partial_z^2v = L'C'\partial_t^2v$. Eliminating $v$ the other way gives the same equation for $i$.

The speed. By the theorem $L'C' = \mu_0\varepsilon$, so $u = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0} = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$, for every TEM line in that insulator, whatever its cross-section. $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 2.998\times10^8$ m/s with the course's constants; $c/1.5 = 1.999\times10^8$ m/s.

Promises kept. d'Alembert, and the characteristic impedance

MR 18's d'Alembert (cited): every solution is

$$v = v_+\Big(t - \frac zu\Big) + v_-\Big(t + \frac zu\Big),$$

a forward wave and a backward wave. Put it in $\partial_zi = -C'\partial_tv$ and integrate:

$$i = \frac{v_+ - v_-}{Z_0}.$$
the characteristic impedance $$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}$$

The ratio of voltage to current in a forward wave. A real resistance for a lossless line — though nothing in the line dissipates.

Units: $\sqrt{\mathrm{H/F}} = \sqrt{\mathrm{V\,s\,A^{-1}}/\mathrm{A\,s\,V^{-1}}} = \mathrm{V/A} = \Omega$ ✓.

Cited. MR 18, d'Alembert's formula: every $C^2$ solution of the wave equation on the whole line is $F(z - ut) + G(z + ut)$. Writing $F(z - ut) = v_+(t - z/u)$ and $G(z + ut) = v_-(t + z/u)$ is only a relabelling, convenient on a line, where one usually knows the voltage at a fixed place as a function of time.

Full proof of $i = (v_+ - v_-)/Z_0$. $\partial_tv = v_+' + v_-'$, so $\partial_zi = -C'(v_+' + v_-')$. Now $\partial_z[v_+(t - z/u)] = -v_+'/u$ and $\partial_z[v_-(t + z/u)] = v_-'/u$, so $i = C'u\,(v_+ - v_-) + f(t)$ for some function of $t$ alone. The first equation, $\partial_zv = -L'\partial_ti$, gives $-(v_+' - v_-')/u = -L'C'u(v_+' - v_-') - L'f'$; since $L'C'u = 1/u$, $f' = 0$, and $f$ is a constant current, which we set to zero (it would be a steady current with no voltage, possible only in a lossless line and excluded here). Finally $C'u = C'/\sqrt{L'C'} = \sqrt{C'/L'} = 1/Z_0$.

"Impedance", with no $\omega$. For a lossless line $Z_0$ is a plain number of ohms, the same at every frequency, and the relation $v_+ = Z_0i_+$ holds pointwise in time for any waveform. Section 5 adds loss, and then $Z_0$ depends on $\omega$ and is a complex impedance in unit 05's sense. Unit 05 met a quantity of the same form, the characteristic resistance $\sqrt{L/C}$ of a resonator, the ratio of peak voltage to peak current in its free oscillation; this is its per-metre cousin.

Nothing dissipates. A forward wave draws power $v_+i_+ = v_+^2/Z_0$ from whatever launches it, exactly as a resistor of $Z_0$ would. The power is not turned into heat: it goes into building the fields of the wave, which moves on with it.

Switching on: the front

Connect an ideal source $V_0$ to a long line at $t = 0$. A step $v_+ = V_0$ sets off at $u$. Behind its wave front: voltage $V_0$, current $V_0/Z_0$, field. Ahead of it: no charge, no current, no field, no energy.

why the current is $V_0/Z_0$

In time $\mathrm dt$ the front advances $u\,\mathrm dt$, and the new length must be charged to $V_0$: charge $C'V_0u\,\mathrm dt$. So the source supplies $i = C'uV_0 = V_0/Z_0$. $\square$

The charges on the conductors behind the front — unit 00's surface charge, now on the move — are what end the field there. The current flows to the front, to charge it; nothing has to reach the far end first.

The line a moment after switching on. Shaded: where the field is. The current runs along the conductors to the front and stops there, where it charges the next length of line.

The same from the flux. In $\mathrm dt$ the region holding magnetic flux grows by $u\,\mathrm dt$, so the flux linked by the loop "source, upper conductor, front, lower conductor" grows at $L'iu$. By Faraday that needs an EMF $L'iu$ round the loop, which the source supplies: $V_0 = L'iu$, so $i = V_0/(L'u) = V_0/Z_0$, since $L'u = \sqrt{L'/C'}$. Charge and flux give the same current; that they must agree is $L'C'u^2 = 1$.

Where the current goes at the front. On each conductor the current stops at the front, so charge piles up there — which is exactly the surface charge the next length needs. Between the conductors, across the front, the electric field switches on, and $\varepsilon\,\partial_t\vec E$ there is a displacement current that closes the loop: conduction current along the upper conductor, displacement current down across the front, conduction current back along the lower. At the front itself the fields are not transverse, for the instant they switch; the telegrapher's equations hold either side.

Unit 00's surface charge. Unit 00 found that the field inside a conductor carrying current is held in place by charge on its surface. Here the surface charge on the conductors terminates the field between them; behind the front it is $\lambda = C'V_0$ per metre, and the front is where it is being laid down.

Unit 01's "Unit 08 — when". Energy reaches a point on the line when the front does, at $u$, and no sooner — however slowly the electrons themselves drift (unit 00: a fraction of a millimetre per second in the kettle cable).

What the model claims, and what it does not

the model's limits

A TEM line needs its conductor spacing small against the distances over which the fields change. For the first few nanoseconds the 1 m gap is not small against $c$ times the switching time: the near-end loop is not really lumped, and it radiates. How fast the switch closes matters too.

So 7.14 mA and 3.3 ns are the right order of magnitude and the right picture, not precise figures. Nothing more is claimed.

What survives: the current is milliamperes, not 0.5 A; it is set by $2Z_0$, not by the bulb; it starts in nanoseconds, not after a second; and it arrives through the gap, not round the wire.

Computed in the model. Almost all the battery's first output goes into building the fields of the two outgoing waves; the bulb gets a five-thousandth of its rating.

Why the gap is the weak point. The telegrapher's equations were derived for fields that are static in each cross-section. That needs the cross-section — here 1 m — to be crossed by light in a time short compared with the time over which things change. A switch that closes in a nanosecond changes the fields over about 0.3 m, less than the gap; the near end is then a small antenna, and some energy leaves it as radiation (branch 16), which this model leaves out. The lumped near-end loop, the two ports as resistors $Z_0$, and the instant of arrival are all approximations at the nanosecond scale.

What the model is good for. Its picture — a front of field leaving the near end along both lines, a current set by the lines, energy reaching the bulb across the gap — follows from Maxwell's equations without the TEM approximation, and its numbers are the right size. A precise figure for the first few nanoseconds would need a full field calculation, which this course does not attempt.

A load at the end: the reflection coefficient

A line of $Z_0$ ends at $z = l$ in a resistor $R_L$. There $v = R_Li$, and with $v = v_+ + v_-$, $i = (v_+ - v_-)/Z_0$:

the reflection coefficient $$\Gamma_L = \frac{v_-}{v_+} = \frac{R_L - Z_0}{R_L + Z_0}$$

Units: ohms over ohms ✓. $-1 \le \Gamma_L \le 1$ for $0 \le R_L \le \infty$.

energy

Incident $v_+^2/Z_0$; reflected $\Gamma_L^2v_+^2/Z_0$; into the load $v\,i = (1 + \Gamma_L)(1 - \Gamma_L)v_+^2/Z_0 = (1 - \Gamma_L^2)\,v_+^2/Z_0$. They balance ✓.

A matched load, $R_L = Z_0$, gives $\Gamma_L = 0$: the line looks infinitely long.

The load is marked by the passive convention: its current enters at $+$. Below the line, the reflected wave, here drawn for $0 \lt \Gamma_L \lt 1$.

Full proof. At $z = l$, $v_+ + v_- = R_L(v_+ - v_-)/Z_0$ at every instant, where $v_\pm$ are evaluated at $z = l$. Collect terms: $v_-(R_L + Z_0) = v_+(R_L - Z_0)$. Because this holds instant by instant, the backward wave leaving the load is the incident wave scaled by $\Gamma_L$, with the same shape: a resistor has no memory. With $R_L \ge 0$ and $Z_0 \gt 0$, $|R_L - Z_0| \le R_L + Z_0$.

The current is in the line's sign. The line's current $i$ is counted along $+z$ on the upper conductor, which at $z = l$ is the current entering the load's $+$ terminal: the passive convention, so $vi$ is the power the load absorbs.

Energy, term by term. At the load, $v = (1 + \Gamma_L)v_+$ and $i = (1 - \Gamma_L)v_+/Z_0$. Their product is $(1 - \Gamma_L^2)v_+^2/Z_0$. The incident wave brings $v_+^2/Z_0$ (Worked: a wave carries $v_+^2/Z_0$), and the reflected wave carries $v_-^2/Z_0 = \Gamma_L^2v_+^2/Z_0$ back towards $-z$. The net Poynting flux at the load is the difference, all of it absorbed. The general version, for a complex load in the sinusoidal steady state — delivered power as forward minus reflected — is branch 09's.

Matched. With $R_L = Z_0$ the load demands exactly the ratio a forward wave already has, so no backward wave is needed. That is why unit 04's receiver is joined to its antenna by a cable whose $Z_0$ matches: nothing is sent back along the cable.

Open, short, matched: MR 18's ends

Short, $R_L = 0$: $\Gamma = -1$. $v = 0$ at the end — MR 18's fixed end: the pulse returns inverted.

Open, $R_L = \infty$: $\Gamma = +1$. $i = 0$ there, so $\partial_zv = -L'\partial_ti = 0$ — MR 18's free end: the pulse returns upright.

Matched, $R_L = Z_0$: $\Gamma = 0$. No return.

which end is fixed depends on what you watch

$v$ behaves like the string's displacement: zero at a short, zero slope at an open end. The current obeys the same wave equation with the roles swapped — zero at an open end, zero slope at a short — so for $i$ the open end is the fixed one.

The current, at the same ends. At a short, $v = 0$, so the second equation gives $\partial_zi = -C'\partial_tv = 0$: Neumann for $i$. At an open end $i = 0$: Dirichlet. $$v_L = 2v_+\big(1 - e^{-(t - T)/Z_0C}\big).$$

The reflected wave starts at $-v_+$ (an empty capacitor is a short) and ends at $+v_+$ (a full one is open). Its shape is not the incident one.

Units: $\Omega\cdot\mathrm F = \mathrm s$ ✓. 50 Ω and 100 pF: $\tau = 5$ ns.

Computed. A line seen from its end is, to the load, a source $2v_+$ behind $Z_0$ — so a reactive load turns a step into unit 05's exponential.

Full proof. At the load, $v_L = v_+ + v_-$ and $i = (v_+ - v_-)/Z_0 = (2v_+ - v_L)/Z_0$. The capacitor's law $i = C\,\mathrm dv_L/\mathrm dt$ gives $Z_0C\dot v_L + v_L = 2v_+$, with $v_L = 0$ when the step arrives at $t = T$ (the capacitor starts empty). Unit 05's solution (One store, one time constant): $v_L = 2v_+(1 - e^{-(t - T)/\tau})$. The reflected wave $v_- = v_L - v_+ = v_+(1 - 2e^{-(t - T)/\tau})$ crosses zero at $t - T = \tau\ln2 = 0.69\tau$.

Limits. $C\to0$: $\tau\to0$, an open end, $\Gamma = +1$ at once ✓. $C\to\infty$: a short for ever, $\Gamma = -1$ ✓. Energy: the capacitor ends with $\tfrac12C(2v_+)^2$, delivered by the difference of incident and reflected fluxes. An inductor does the opposite: open at first, short at last, the reflection running from $+v_+$ to $-v_+$ with $\tau = L/Z_0$.

No general theory here. For a sinusoid a reactive load reflects with a complex $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$ of modulus 1 — branch 09's subject. In the time domain each reactive load is its own small circuit problem, as this one is.

The puzzle's far ends: $\Gamma = -1$

Each of the puzzle's lines is joined at its far end: a short, $\Gamma = -1$.

The front reaches it after $3\times10^8/c = 1.001$ s and comes back inverted. Behind the returning front the voltage cancels and the current doubles: $1 + \Gamma = 0$, $1 - \Gamma = 2$.

It reaches the near end at $t = 2.00$ s. Only then does the near-end loop learn that the wires are joined — that there is a circuit at all.

the lumped answer starts here

One line, folded flat, not to scale. The inverted reflection cancels the voltage and doubles the current behind it, as at MR 18's fixed end.

The delay. $3\times10^8/2.998\times10^8 = 1.0007$ s each way, so the round trip is $2.0014$ s; the slides round it to 2.00 s.

Why the current doubles. The returning wave has $v_- = -v_+$, and the current of a backward wave is $-v_-/Z_0 = +v_+/Z_0$: the same sign as the forward wave's. So $i = 2v_+/Z_0$ behind the returning front. A short carries current and holds no voltage, and the returning front is spreading that condition back towards the source.

What the near end knows. Until $t = 2.00$ s nothing at the near end depends on whether the far ends are joined, open, or joined through a resistor: the near-end current is $I_0$ in every case. The fields simply have not had time to bring the news. Unit 03 noted that Tellegen's theorem balances power totals and is silent about where energy is; for these two seconds the battery's energy is in transit on the lines, in no element of any lumped circuit. This is the clearest statement of the unit: the lumped model's "a circuit is a closed loop" is a statement about the steady state, reached only after the round trips have happened.

section 4

The bounce diagram

With reflections at both ends, a wave runs back and forth for ever, a little smaller each time. Drawn in the $(z, t)$ plane it is a zigzag; summed, it is a geometric series, and its limit is the lumped circuit.

08 · bounce

Waves as lines in the $(z, t)$ plane

the bounce diagram

Position $z$ across, time $t$ down. Each wave is a diagonal line of slope $\pm1/u$ — MR 18's characteristics — labelled with its amplitude; each reflection is a corner, where the label is multiplied by that end's $\Gamma$.

reading it

The voltage at $(z, t)$ is the sum of the labels of every wave line that has passed $z$ before $t$: the lines crossing the vertical through $z$ above the point. The current is the same sum with each backward wave's label negated, over $Z_0$.

With a one-way delay $T = l/u$, the load sees a new wave at $T, 3T, 5T, \dots$ and the source at $2T, 4T, \dots$

At P, three waves have passed (the dashed vertical crosses three segments above it): $v = v_+ + \Gamma_Lv_+ + \Gamma_s\Gamma_Lv_+$.

Why it works. The telegrapher's equations are linear, so every wave on the line can be added. On a lossless line each wave keeps its shape, so a step launched at one instant stays a step, and its front traces a straight line $z = \pm u(t - t_0)$ in the plane: one of MR 18's characteristics (Characteristics of the wave operator). At each end, the resistive boundary turns an arriving step into a reflected step scaled by $\Gamma$, at the same instant. So the whole history is a sequence of steps, and the bounce diagram is its bookkeeping.

Reading a point. A forward step launched at $t_0$ from $z = 0$ has reached $z$ by time $t$ if $t \gt t_0 + z/u$; a backward step launched from $z = l$ at $t_1$ has reached $z$ if $t \gt t_1 + (l - z)/u$. In the diagram those are the segments that cross the vertical through $z$ above the point. The voltage is the sum of their amplitudes; the current is $(\sum\text{forward} - \sum\text{backward})/Z_0$.

Names. Older texts call it a lattice diagram, or a reflection diagram. With a pulse instead of a step the same construction works if the pulse is short compared with $T$.

08 · bounce

The limit is the lumped answer

theorem

For any $r_i, R_L \ge 0$, not both in $\{0, \infty\}$, the load voltage converges, and

$$v_L(\infty) = \mathcal E\,\frac{R_L}{r_i + R_L},$$

whatever $Z_0$ and $T$.

proof

After $n$ arrivals $v_L = (1 + \Gamma_L)v_+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}(\Gamma_L\Gamma_s)^k$. Since $|\Gamma_L\Gamma_s| \lt 1$ the sum tends to $1/(1 - \Gamma_L\Gamma_s)$, and

$$1 - \Gamma_L\Gamma_s = \frac{2Z_0(r_i + R_L)}{(R_L + Z_0)(r_i + Z_0)} .$$

Multiply by $v_+ = \mathcal EZ_0/(r_i + Z_0)$ and $1 + \Gamma_L = 2R_L/(R_L + Z_0)$: everything but $\mathcal ER_L/(r_i + R_L)$ cancels. $\square$

In the steady state a lossless line is a pair of ideal wires: no drop along it, no current lost from it. $Z_0$ sets how the answer is approached, never what it is.

Full proof. The load voltage after the $n$-th arrival is the sum of $(1 + \Gamma_L)a_k$ for $k \lt n$, with $a_k = (\Gamma_L\Gamma_s)^kv_+$. Each $|\Gamma|$ is at most 1, with equality only for a resistance of $0$ or $\infty$; so if $r_i$ and $R_L$ are not both $0$ or $\infty$, $|\Gamma_L\Gamma_s| \lt 1$ and the geometric series converges. Now $(R_L + Z_0)(r_i + Z_0) - (R_L - Z_0)(r_i - Z_0) = 2Z_0R_L + 2Z_0r_i$, so $1 - \Gamma_L\Gamma_s = 2Z_0(R_L + r_i)/((R_L + Z_0)(r_i + Z_0))$. Then $v_L(\infty) = \frac{\mathcal EZ_0}{r_i + Z_0}\cdot\frac{2R_L}{R_L + Z_0}\cdot\frac{(R_L + Z_0)(r_i + Z_0)}{2Z_0(r_i + R_L)} = \frac{\mathcal ER_L}{r_i + R_L}$. $\square$ (If $R_L = \infty$ the same algebra holds as a limit: $\Gamma_L = 1$, $|\Gamma_s| \lt 1$, and $v_L\to\mathcal E$.)

Why it had to be so. In a steady state nothing depends on $t$, so the telegrapher's equations give $\partial_zv = 0$ and $\partial_zi = 0$: the line carries one voltage and one current along its whole length, and is ideal wire. The theorem shows that the bounces actually reach that state, and how fast.

When it fails. $r_i = 0$ and $R_L = 0$: an ideal source shorted through a lossless line. Then $\Gamma_L\Gamma_s = (-1)(-1) = 1$ and the current grows by $2v_+/Z_0$ every round trip, without limit — the lumped picture is a source across an inductor, whose current ramps for ever. $r_i = 0$, $R_L = \infty$: $\Gamma_L\Gamma_s = -1$, and the line rings for ever, as unit 05's lossless LC does. Long lines, and loss

In the sinusoidal steady state the waves become phasors along $z$. A 400 km power line is a fifteenth of a wavelength — nearly lumped, not quite. Then loss: attenuation, dispersion, and the condition under which a lossy line keeps a pulse's shape.

08 · long lines

Two conventions for one wave

This course (MR 06, unit 05): $v = \operatorname{Re}\big(\sqrt2\,Ve^{i\omega t}\big)$. A forward sinusoid is

$$v = \operatorname{Re}\big(\sqrt2\,V_+e^{i(\omega t - \beta z)}\big),\quad \beta = \frac\omega u .$$

MR 24 writes waves as $\operatorname{Re}\big(E_0e^{i(kz - \omega t)}\big)$.

the translation

$e^{i(kz - \omega t)}$ is the complex conjugate of $e^{i(\omega t - kz)}$, and $\operatorname{Re}\bar w = \operatorname{Re}w$. So the same physical wave has conjugate complex amplitudes in the two conventions, and every complex formula translates by $i\to-i$: an inductor is $i\omega L$ here, $-i\omega L$ there.

What does not change: real fields, speeds, wavelengths, powers, $|\Gamma|$. What does: the sign of every phase and every reactance. This course keeps $e^{+i\omega t}$ throughout.

One real wave. The two conventions turn their phasors opposite ways, and so disagree on the sign of every phase angle and every reactance.

Full proof. For any complex $w$, $\operatorname{Re}\bar w = \operatorname{Re}w$. With $w = \sqrt2V_+e^{i(\omega t - \beta z)}$, $\bar w = \sqrt2\bar V_+e^{i(\beta z - \omega t)}$, which is MR 24's form with $k = \beta$ and complex amplitude $\sqrt2\bar V_+$. Since $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$ acts as $i\omega$ on $e^{i\omega t}$ and as $-i\omega$ on $e^{-i\omega t}$, every impedance, admittance and propagation constant written in one convention is the complex conjugate of the other's. Physics and electrical-engineering texts therefore disagree on the sign of $i$ in reactances, and both are right. Pozar uses $e^{j\omega t}$ with $j = \sqrt{-1}$: this course's convention, with engineers' $j$ (unit 05).

Why this course's choice. Unit 05 chose MR 06's $e^{+i\omega t}$ for the driven oscillator, so that a capacitor is $1/(i\omega C)$ and an inductive load has $Q \gt 0$. The course fixed it once, in unit 05; this slide is the one place where MR 24's waves meet it.

What does not change. Anything real or a modulus: the instantaneous fields, the wavelength $2\pi/\beta$, the speed, $|V|$, $|\Gamma|$, real power. Reactive power and phase angles change sign together, so statements like "the current leads" survive if both are translated.

Sinusoids on a lossless line

With $\partial_t\to i\omega$ the telegrapher's equations become ODEs in $z$, solved by

$$V(z) = V_+e^{-i\beta z} + V_-e^{i\beta z},$$ $$I(z) = \frac{V_+e^{-i\beta z} - V_-e^{i\beta z}}{Z_0},$$

with $\beta = \omega\sqrt{L'C'} = 2\pi/\lambda$.

an open line of length $l$ $$\frac{V(l)}{V(0)} = \frac{1}{\cos\beta l},\qquad I(0) = i\,\frac{\tan\beta l}{Z_0}\,V(0).$$

Units: a ratio; $\Omega^{-1}\mathrm V = \mathrm A$ ✓. Limit $\beta l\to0$: ratio 1, no current — a short open line is a wire ✓.

The far end is higher than the sending end, and the line draws a current leading by 90°: it is a capacitor, charged through its own inductance.

Computed for the 400 km line: $|V(z)| = |V(l)|\cos\beta(l - z)$, rising 9.5% from the sending end to the open end.

Full proof. With $v = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2V(z)e^{i\omega t})$ and the same for $i$, the lossless equations become $V' = -i\omega L'I$ and $I' = -i\omega C'V$, so $V'' = -\omega^2L'C'V = -\beta^2V$, with general solution as on the slide; $I = -V'/(i\omega L') = \beta(V_+e^{-i\beta z} - V_-e^{i\beta z})/(\omega L')$, and $\beta/\omega L' = \sqrt{C'/L'} = 1/Z_0$. Each term is d'Alembert's wave in phasor form: $\operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2V_+e^{i(\omega t - \beta z)})$ moves towards $+z$ at $\omega/\beta = u$.

The open line. $I(l) = 0$ gives $V_-e^{i\beta l} = V_+e^{-i\beta l}$. Then $V(z) = V_+e^{-i\beta l}\big(e^{i\beta(l - z)} + e^{-i\beta(l - z)}\big) = 2V_+e^{-i\beta l}\cos\beta(l - z)$, so $V(l)/V(0) = 1/\cos\beta l$. And $I(0) = (V_+ - V_-)/Z_0 = V_+e^{-i\beta l}(e^{i\beta l} - e^{-i\beta l})/Z_0 = 2iV_+e^{-i\beta l}\sin\beta l/Z_0$, which is $i\tan(\beta l)V(0)/Z_0$. The factor $i$: the current leads the voltage by 90°, a capacitive current (unit 05).

Lumped reading. For small $\beta l$, $1/\cos\beta l\approx1 + (\beta l)^2/2 = 1 + \omega^2(L'l)(C'l)/2$: the line's total capacitance draws current $\omega C'lV$, which flowing through (half) the line's inductance raises the far end. Unit 06 said a long unloaded cable is capacitive; this is how much. Input impedance of a terminated line in general, standing waves and matching are branch 09's.

08 · loss

Loss: the propagation constant

With $R'$ and $G'$, the phasor equations are $V' = -(R' + i\omega L')I$, $I' = -(G' + i\omega C')V$, so $V'' = \gamma^2V$ with

the propagation constant $$\gamma = \sqrt{(R' + i\omega L')(G' + i\omega C')} = \alpha + i\beta ,$$ $$Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{R' + i\omega L'}{G' + i\omega C'}} .$$

Units: $\sqrt{\Omega\,\mathrm m^{-1}\cdot\mathrm S\,\mathrm m^{-1}} = \mathrm m^{-1}$ ✓; $Z_0$ in Ω ✓.

A forward wave is $V_+e^{-\alpha z}e^{-i\beta z}$: the attenuation constant $\alpha$ shrinks it, the phase constant $\beta$ turns it. Both depend on $\omega$: different frequencies fade differently and travel at different speeds $\omega/\beta$ — dispersion, MR 20's phase and group velocity. A pulse shrinks and spreads.

Schematic. Dotted: the pulse if every frequency were attenuated equally and travelled at one speed — smaller, same shape. Solid: what a dispersive line delivers.

The derivation. $V'' = -(R' + i\omega L')I' = (R' + i\omega L')(G' + i\omega C')V$. Solutions $e^{\mp\gamma z}$, with the root taken in the right half-plane so that $\alpha \gt 0$ and the forward wave decays in the direction it travels. The current follows from the first equation: $I = \gamma(V_+e^{-\gamma z} - V_-e^{\gamma z})/(R' + i\omega L')$, and $\gamma/(R' + i\omega L') = 1/Z_0$. With $R' = G' = 0$, $\gamma = i\omega\sqrt{L'C'}$ and $Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$: the lossless case ✓. Pozar, ch. 2, "Transmission Line Theory", treats the lossy line the same way.

Why dispersion spreads a pulse. By MR 20 (Wave packets: phase and group velocity, Packets spread), a pulse is a superposition of frequencies; if $\beta(\omega)$ is not proportional to $\omega$ its components drift out of step and the pulse broadens, and if $\alpha$ depends on $\omega$ its spectrum is reshaped as well. For a telegraph or telephone signal sent a long way, both blur successive symbols into each other.

What is not done here. $R'$ itself depends on frequency through the skin effect (branch 10), which makes real dispersion worse at high frequency. Here $R'$ is a constant, a model.

08 · loss

Heaviside's condition: a lossy line that keeps its shape

theorem · the distortionless condition

If $R'/L' = G'/C'$, then $\alpha = \sqrt{R'G'}$ and $\beta = \omega\sqrt{L'C'}$ at every $\omega$, and $Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$ is real.

proof, by factoring

With $k = R'/L' = G'/C'$: $(R' + i\omega L')(G' + i\omega C') = L'C'(k + i\omega)^2$, so $\gamma = \sqrt{L'C'}\,(k + i\omega)$. $\square$

Every frequency fades equally and travels at $1/\sqrt{L'C'}$: a pulse arrives smaller but the same shape.

A telephone pair has $R'/L' \gg G'/C'$ — for the assumed pair in the figure, $8.3\times10^4$ against $20\ \mathrm s^{-1}$. Raising $L'$ moves them towards the condition; loading coils, inductors inserted at intervals along a line, were later fitted to long telephone lines to approach it.

Computed for an assumed pair: $R' = 50\ \Omega$/km, $L' = 0.6$ mH/km, $C' = 50$ nF/km, $G' = 1\ \mu$S/km; loaded, $L' = 40$ mH/km; distortionless, $L' = R'C'/G' = 2.5$ H/km. Loading flattens both curves.

Full proof. Factor $L'$ from the first bracket and $C'$ from the second: $(R' + i\omega L') = L'(R'/L' + i\omega)$, $(G' + i\omega C') = C'(G'/C' + i\omega)$. If the two ratios are equal to $k$, the product is $L'C'(k + i\omega)^2$, whose root with positive real part is $\sqrt{L'C'}(k + i\omega)$. So $\alpha = k\sqrt{L'C'} = R'\sqrt{C'/L'} = \sqrt{R'G'}$ (using $R' = kL'$, $G' = kC'$), and $\beta = \omega\sqrt{L'C'}$. And $Z_0^2 = L'(k + i\omega)/(C'(k + i\omega)) = L'/C'$. With $\beta/\omega$ constant and $\alpha$ constant, a signal $v(0, t)$ arrives as $e^{-\alpha l}v(0, t - l\sqrt{L'C'})$ exactly.

The numbers (assumed values, per km). Unloaded: $\alpha = 0.048$ Np/km at 300 Hz and 0.137 at 3 kHz — almost three times larger — and phase speed $3.9\times10^4$ against $1.1\times10^5$ km/s. Over 50 km the 3 kHz component arrives at $e^{-6.9} = 0.001$ of its size, the 300 Hz one at $e^{-2.4} = 0.09$. Loaded to 40 mH/km: $\alpha = 0.0271$ and 0.0284 Np/km, speeds $2.14\times10^4$ and $2.23\times10^4$ km/s — both nearly flat, though the condition is far from met. Distortionless: $\alpha = \sqrt{0.05\times10^{-6}}$ per metre $= 0.00707$ Np/km, speed $2.83\times10^3$ km/s, at every frequency. Problem P8 works these. The neper: $e^{-\alpha l}$ is the amplitude ratio; $\alpha$ is in $\mathrm m^{-1}$, called nepers per metre.

History, kept to what is checked. The condition carries Heaviside's name; the deck derives it rather than narrating how he found it. That loading coils were later fitted to long telephone lines to approach it is stated without names or dates, because the account of who did what and when could not be checked against Nahin's biography of Heaviside from here. The transatlantic telegraph cable is not a failure of this condition, and is not told as one.

section 6

Lightning: a line that starts charged

The puzzle's lines start empty and are charged by a front. A lightning channel starts charged, and a front runs up it that discharges it. The energy was in the air before the stroke, and it reaches the channel as a Poynting flux.

08 · lightning

Before the stroke: a charged channel above the ground

What happens (Rakov & Uman, ch. 1, cited). In a stroke that lowers negative charge to ground, a stepped leader grows down from the cloud in steps, depositing negative charge along its path. When it reaches the ground — or an upward connecting leader rising to meet it — a return stroke runs up the same path, from ground towards the cloud.

Where the energy is. Before the return stroke, in the electric field between the charged channel and the ground: in the air, as unit 01's energy was in its cable's insulation.

The model (stated, not sourced): a vertical line charge $Q$ spread over a height $H$, on a charge sheath of radius $r$, above a conducting ground — which MR 23's method of images replaces by an opposite image below.

The model, schematic. The leader channel (negative charge, in its dashed sheath) and the field lines that end on it; below, the image that makes the ground an equipotential.

Cited, and checked. V. A. Rakov and M. A. Uman, Lightning: Physics and Effects (Cambridge University Press, 2003), ch. 1: strokes lowering negative charge to ground are composed of a downward leader and an upward return stroke, the first stroke's leader is stepped, the leader deposits negative charge along its path, and the return stroke traverses that path from ground towards the cloud, and that it may meet an upward connecting leader. Nothing else about the leader — its length, speed or charge — is taken from the book on this slide.

The model. It replaces the channel by a straight, uniformly charged vertical line, which a real channel is not: it is tortuous and branched, and its charge is not uniform. The charge sheath — a radius within which the leader's charge sits, much wider than the hot conducting core — is a term of the lightning literature; here its radius $r$ is simply a parameter of the model, and the next slide shows the answer hardly depends on it. The ground is taken as a perfect conductor, so MR 23's images apply (The method of images, and why it is allowed).

The thread. Unit 01 put a circuit's energy in the insulation around its conductors. Here the "insulation" is kilometres of air, and the "conductors" are a charged channel and the ground.

Worked: the channel's energy, in the model

worked example 5 · the leader channel: $Q = 5$ C, $H = 5$ km, $r = 1$–$10$ m

1. Charge per metre. $\lambda = Q/H = 1.0\times10^{-3}$ C/m.

2. Potential. A long line charge near its image (MR 23):

$$V \approx \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac{2H}{r} = 124\text{–}166\ \mathrm{MV}.$$

3. Energy. $W \approx \tfrac12QV = $ 310–414 MJ.

4. What is what. Computed: steps 1–3. Cited: a first stroke lowers a few coulombs — median 5.2 C in Berger et al.'s measurements — so $Q = 5$ C is of the right size. Assumed: $H$, $r$, and a straight uniform channel.

Check. $\mathrm{C\,m^{-1}}/(\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}}) = \mathrm V$; $\mathrm{C\cdot V} = \mathrm J$ ✓. $r$ enters through a logarithm: ten times wider changes $W$ by a quarter.

Computed in the model. A hundredfold range of $r$ moves $W$ by less than a factor of two; halving $Q$ quarters it.

Numbers. $\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0 = 10^{-3}/(2\pi\times8.854\times10^{-12}) = 1.798\times10^7$ V. $\ln(10^4/1) = 9.210$, $\ln(10^4/10) = 6.908$: $V = 165.6$ and $124.2$ MV; $W = 2.5\times V = 413.9$ and $310.4$ MJ.

How rough the logarithm is. Step 2 is the potential of an infinitely long line charge, $\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln$(distance to its image over its radius), with the "distance" taken as $2H$. For a segment of finite length the potential varies along it, and the energy is half the integral of $\lambda\varphi$ over the channel. Done exactly for a uniform segment and its image, that integral gives 338 MJ at $r = 1$ m and 234 MJ at $r = 10$ m: the same order of magnitude, a fifth to a quarter lower. The model claims only the order — a few hundred megajoules — and is insensitive to its least certain input, $r$.

Source of the charge figure. K. Berger, R. B. Anderson and H. Kröninger, "Parameters of lightning flashes", Electra 41, 23–37 (1975), for the median charge of negative first strokes, 5.2 C, from their measurements. No other charge figure is used: the charge of a whole flash is not quoted, since no figure for it was checked from here.

Not quoted. Published estimates of the energy of a flash disagree widely; this deck quotes none. The number here is a model's, for one stroke, and is labelled as such.

The return stroke: a front running up the channel

When the leader connects, the channel's bottom is suddenly at ground potential. A discharge front runs up the channel — the reverse of the puzzle's switch-on: behind it the channel is near ground potential and carries current; ahead of it, still charged.

Cited. Measured median peak current of negative first return strokes, 30 kA (Berger et al.); return-stroke speed typically a third to a half of $c$ (Rakov, 2007).

In a lossless line charged to $V_0$ and shorted at one end, a discharge front would run away from the short with current $V_0/Z_0$ behind it, flowing towards the short (problem P11). The channel is a lossy line, and its current is not $V_0/Z_0$ of any simple line; the picture carries over, the formula does not.

Schematic. Behind the front the channel carries the return-stroke current and the field's energy flows into it through its side, as into unit 01's resistor; ahead, the leader's charge and field wait.

Sources, checked. Peak current: K. Berger, R. B. Anderson and H. Kröninger (1975), as tabulated in Rakov's reviews: 50% (median) values of 30 kA for negative first strokes and 12 kA for subsequent strokes. Speed: V. A. Rakov, "Lightning return stroke speed", J. Lightning Research 1, 80–89 (2007), whose summary gives the speed of negative return strokes below the cloud base as typically between one-third and one-half of the speed of light.

Lines as models of the stroke. Rakov and Uman, ch. 12, "Modeling of lightning processes", treats return-stroke models; V. A. Rakov and M. A. Uman, "Review and evaluation of lightning return stroke models including some aspects of their application", IEEE Trans. Electromagnetic Compatibility 40, 403–426 (1998), sorts them into classes, two of which are relevant here: distributed-circuit models, which treat the channel as an R-L-C transmission line, and "engineering" models, among them one called the transmission-line (TL) model (M. A. Uman and D. K. McLain, 1969), in which a current wave travels up the channel at constant speed without changing shape. Neither is used quantitatively here.

The slide's own claim is the picture: a channel that starts charged, discharging from the end that touches ground, with a front moving away from the connection. 08 · lightning

Where the energy goes, and what carries it

into the channel, through its side

In the model the field energy within distance $\rho$ of the channel is the fraction $\ln(\rho/r)/\ln(2H/r)$ of the whole: spread over radii from metres to kilometres, almost all of it outside the channel. Poynting's theorem (MR 24): what the channel dissipates must enter through its surface.

Out as: heat in the channel, light, the shock wave heard as thunder, and an electromagnetic pulse — radiation, branch 16. No shares are quoted.

What carries it: not the charges. The charge lowered moves at most the channel's few kilometres, and its carriers lose their energy in collisions as fast as they gain it (unit 00). The field carries the energy; the stroke is the moment it finds a path to be dissipated.

Schematic. Energy arrives from the air as a Poynting flux and leaves in four forms. The proportions are not drawn because no checked source here gives them.

The fraction, derived. For a long line charge the field at distance $\rho$ is $\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0\rho)$, so the energy per metre between radii $r$ and $\rho$ is $\int_r^\rho\frac{\varepsilon_0}2\big(\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0s}\big)^22\pi s\,\mathrm ds = \frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac\rho r$. In the model the field reaches out to distances of order $2H$ (the image cuts it off), so the fraction inside $\rho$ is $\ln(\rho/r)/\ln(2H/r)$. With $r = 1$ m and $2H = 10$ km, the first ten metres hold $\ln10/\ln10^4 = 25\%$, the first hundred metres 50%: the energy is spread over the air for kilometres.

Poynting's theorem. For any volume, $-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\text{field energy inside}) = \oint\vec S\cdot\mathrm d\vec A + \int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV$ (MR 24). Take the volume to be the channel: the field energy that was inside it was a small part of $W$, so the energy it turns into heat and light must, for the most part, have crossed its surface. This is unit 01's resistor, with the resistor a column of ionised air and the cable a few kilometres of atmosphere.

The four outlets. Heat, light and thunder are what a stroke is seen and heard to do; the electromagnetic pulse is what any rapidly changing current radiates (branch 16 computes radiation from a current; here it is only named). How the energy divides among them is not stated: no figure for the shares was checked from here.

The charges. In the model the charge is lowered by at most $H = 5$ km. In a conductor the carriers' kinetic energy is handed to the lattice at every collision (unit 00's Drude picture), so at any moment they hold a negligible part of the energy passing through; unit 01 computed that share for copper as $10^{-21}$. No number is computed for the channel's plasma here; the argument is the structural one — the energy was in the field before any charge moved.

08 · close

The puzzle, answered

Close the switch. Within about 3.3 ns — the time light takes to cross the metre — the field has crossed the gap and 7.14 mA flows in the bulb, set by the two lines' $2Z_0 = 1657\ \Omega$, not by the bulb. That is 1.22 mW: the bulb does not glow. For two seconds nothing changes, while fronts run out along both lines charging them. Then the inverted reflections return from the joined ends, and every 2 s the current steps up, closing 2.9% of the gap to the lumped 0.5 A each time; it passes 90% after 158 s, following unit 05's $L/R = 69$ s.

the thread

Unit 01: where — in the field between the conductors. This unit: when — at the speed the insulation sets, as a front carrying $v^2/Z_0$. The electrons carry neither.

The puzzle's time scales, on logarithmic axes (the lower one continues the upper). Everything after the first nanoseconds is the bounce diagram's staircase.

The answer, as a paragraph to remember. The bulb is in a loop with the battery and the near ends of two transmission lines. Current flows as soon as the field reaches the bulb, about $(1\ \mathrm m)/c$; it is $\mathcal E/(2Z_0 + R)$, because for the first two seconds each line is, to the battery, just a resistor $Z_0$. The lumped 0.5 A arrives only after many round trips to the far ends, at the pace of the loop's $L/R$. The energy reaching the bulb in the first nanoseconds crosses the metre of air from the battery's wires to the bulb's; it does not travel round 600 000 km of copper.

Unit 01's pictures, revisited. Unit 01 drew $\vec S$ flowing from battery to resistor through the insulation of a steady circuit (The whole journey, battery to resistor). Here the same flux is caught in the act of arriving, and it is the field picture alone that gets the timing right. The conveyor-belt picture of charges carrying energy — exact for totals in the steady state, as unit 01 proved — has nothing to say about the first two seconds.

The model, once more. The 7.14 mA and the 3.3 ns are the transmission-line model's, whose near end is not exactly a TEM line (What the model claims, and what it does not); the staircase after 2 s is on firmer ground, since by then the near-end details have long settled.
