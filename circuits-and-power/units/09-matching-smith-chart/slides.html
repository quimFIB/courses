09

Matching on a line: standing waves, the quarter-wave transformer, and the Smith chart as a Möbius map

A length of line turns one impedance into another. Two matches undo the cost, and the Smith chart shows both, because Möbius maps send circles to circles.

needs 08, 06~2 h slides + 3½ h problemsproblems: ./cap problems 09

Unit 08 solved lines in the time domain — waves, reflections, bounce diagrams — and ended on one formula for the sinusoidal steady state. This branch takes that steady state seriously, because it is how radio and microwave work is done: a terminated line of length $d$ turns its load into a different impedance, the forward and reflected waves beat into a standing wave, and a mismatch costs delivered power. Then the mathematics that makes all of it visible: the reflection coefficient $\Gamma = (z - 1)/(z + 1)$ is a Möbius map, it sends unit 05's right half-plane of passive impedances onto the unit disc, walking along the line rotates the disc, and the Smith chart's grid is circles because Möbius maps send circles to circles.

Two promises are kept. Unit 05: "unit 09's Smith chart is a map of the positive-real right half-plane" — the slide The passive half-plane is the disc. Unit 06: "matching over a band is a harder problem (unit 09 begins it, on a line)" — the slide A match is exact at one frequency.

09 · steady state

Unit 08's line, in phasors

Unit 08, in the sinusoidal steady state ($e^{+i\omega t}$, RMS phasors), on a lossless line with $Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$ and speed $u = 1/\sqrt{L'C'}$:

$$V(z) = V_+e^{-i\beta z} + V_-e^{i\beta z},$$ $$I(z) = \frac{V_+e^{-i\beta z} - V_-e^{i\beta z}}{Z_0},$$

with phase constant $\beta = \omega/u = 2\pi/\lambda$: forward waves $e^{i(\omega t - \beta z)}$ go toward $+z$.

at a load $Z_L$, at $z = 0$

$V(0) = Z_LI(0)$ gives $V_+ + V_- = Z_L(V_+ - V_-)/Z_0$, so

$$V_- = \Gamma_LV_+,\qquad \Gamma_L = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$$

Unit 08's $\Gamma_L$, for any complex $Z_L$. Units: $\Omega/\Omega$ ✓.

From here on, $d = -z$ is the distance from the load toward the generator.

The line, its load at $z = 0$, and the distance $d$ measured back from it. $V(d)$ is marked by the passive convention looking toward the load: $I(d)$ enters the line's $+$ conductor there.

What is imported. Unit 08 derived the telegrapher's equations $\partial v/\partial z = -L'\partial i/\partial t$, $\partial i/\partial z = -C'\partial v/\partial t$, solved them with d'Alembert, and found $i = (v_+ - v_-)/Z_0$. In phasors, $\partial/\partial t \to i\omega$, so $\mathrm dV/\mathrm dz = -i\omega L'I$ and $\mathrm dI/\mathrm dz = -i\omega C'V$, whose solutions are exactly the pair above: differentiate $V$ to get $-i\beta(V_+e^{-i\beta z} - V_-e^{i\beta z}) = -i\omega L'I$, and $\beta/\omega L' = \sqrt{L'C'}/L' = 1/Z_0$ ✓. Unit 08 wrote the same formula for its long power line; this unit uses nothing else from the time domain.

The convention. $e^{+i\omega t}$, as in MR 06 and every unit of this course; a forward wave is $\operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2V_+e^{i(\omega t - \beta z)})$, which moves toward $+z$ at $u = \omega/\beta$. Texts written with $e^{-i\omega t}$ (MR 24's $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$) have every phasor complex-conjugated; unit 08 has the translation. Pozar, like engineers generally, writes $j$ for $i$; with that change, and his letters for distances, his ch. 2 formulas are these.

Why $\Gamma_L$ holds for complex loads. Unit 08 derived $\Gamma_L$ for a resistance in the time domain. In phasors the load's law is $V = Z_LI$ for any impedance, and the two-line derivation on the slide never used that $Z_L$ was real. (A reactive load in the time domain reflects a pulse with a changed shape — unit 08's capacitor — because its $\Gamma_L$ depends on frequency; at one frequency it is one complex number.)

Lossless throughout. This unit takes $R' = G' = 0$. With loss, unit 08's propagation constant $\gamma = \alpha + i\beta$ replaces $i\beta$, and the reflection coefficient's size shrinks as $e^{-2\alpha d}$ on the way toward the generator — which is why a long lossy cable hides a bad load. That is the only sentence about loss here.

09 · steady state

The reflection coefficient turns as you walk

With $z = -d$ and $V_- = \Gamma_LV_+$:

$$V(d) = V_+\big(e^{i\beta d} + \Gamma_Le^{-i\beta d}\big),$$ $$I(d) = \frac{V_+}{Z_0}\big(e^{i\beta d} - \Gamma_Le^{-i\beta d}\big).$$
the reflection coefficient at d

The ratio of the reflected wave to the forward wave at $d$:

$$\boxed{\,\Gamma(d) = \frac{\Gamma_LV_+e^{-i\beta d}}{V_+e^{i\beta d}} = \Gamma_Le^{-2i\beta d}\,}$$

Units: $\beta d$ in $\mathrm{m^{-1}\cdot m}$, radians ✓; $\Gamma$ is a ratio of volts ✓.

Then $V(d) = V_+e^{i\beta d}\,(1 + \Gamma(d))$ and $I(d) = (V_+/Z_0)\,e^{i\beta d}\,(1 - \Gamma(d))$. On a lossless line $|\Gamma(d)| = |\Gamma_L|$: walking toward the generator only turns $\Gamma$, clockwise, by $2\beta$ per metre.

The 12.5 Ω load: $\Gamma_L = -0.6$. A quarter wavelength back, $2\beta d = \pi$ and $\Gamma = +0.6$.

Why "turns twice as fast". The forward wave's phase at $d$ is $e^{i\beta d}$ relative to the load and the reflected wave's is $e^{-i\beta d}$: walking a distance $d$ back from the load, the forward wave is met earlier in its journey and the reflected one later, and their ratio picks up the round trip, $2\beta d$. A full turn of $\Gamma$ takes $2\beta d = 2\pi$, so $d = \pi/\beta = \lambda/2$. Everything about the line in this unit therefore repeats every half wavelength.

The running example. $\Gamma_L = (12.5 - 50)/(12.5 + 50) = -37.5/62.5 = -0.6$. At $d = \lambda/8$, $2\beta d = \pi/2$ and $\Gamma = -0.6\,e^{-i\pi/2} = 0.6i$; at $d = \lambda/4$, $\Gamma = -0.6\,e^{-i\pi} = +0.6$. In metres, at 100 MHz on the polyethylene cable, $\lambda = u/f = 1.999\times10^8/10^8 = 1.999$ m, so these are 0.250 m and 0.500 m.

Clockwise. Multiplying by $e^{-2i\beta d}$ turns a complex number by the angle $-2\beta d$, which is clockwise in the usual picture of the plane with the imaginary axis up. Section 5 calls this "toward the generator" and makes it the Smith chart's basic move.

09 · steady state

Input impedance

the input impedance of a terminated line $$Z_{\mathrm{in}}(d) = \frac{V(d)}{I(d)} = Z_0\,\frac{1 + \Gamma(d)}{1 - \Gamma(d)}$$ $$\boxed{\,Z_{\mathrm{in}}(d) = Z_0\,\frac{Z_L + iZ_0\tan\beta d}{Z_0 + iZ_L\tan\beta d}\,}$$

Units: $\Omega\cdot\Omega/\Omega = \Omega$ ✓. At $d = 0$: $Z_L$ ✓.

proof

The first form is the ratio of the last slide's $V$ and $I$. For the second, put in $\Gamma_L$ and multiply through by $Z_L + Z_0$: the numerator becomes $2Z_L\cos\beta d + 2iZ_0\sin\beta d$, the denominator $2Z_0\cos\beta d + 2iZ_L\sin\beta d$. $\square$

At one frequency, a length of line and its load are one impedance at the terminals. It depends on $d$, on $\beta$ — so on frequency — and on $Z_L$.

Divided by $Z_0$, impedances become normalised impedances, $z = Z/Z_0$, and the formula reads

$$z_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{z_L + i\tan\beta d}{1 + iz_L\tan\beta d}.$$

Full proof, written out. With $\Gamma_L = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$, $$\frac{e^{i\beta d} + \Gamma_Le^{-i\beta d}}{e^{i\beta d} - \Gamma_Le^{-i\beta d}} = \frac{(Z_L + Z_0)e^{i\beta d} + (Z_L - Z_0)e^{-i\beta d}}{(Z_L + Z_0)e^{i\beta d} - (Z_L - Z_0)e^{-i\beta d}}.$$ In the numerator the $Z_L$ terms give $Z_L(e^{i\beta d} + e^{-i\beta d}) = 2Z_L\cos\beta d$ and the $Z_0$ terms $Z_0(e^{i\beta d} - e^{-i\beta d}) = 2iZ_0\sin\beta d$. In the denominator the roles swap: $2Z_0\cos\beta d + 2iZ_L\sin\beta d$. Multiplying by $Z_0$ and dividing top and bottom by $2\cos\beta d$ (where it is non-zero; at $\cos\beta d = 0$ take the limit, as section 3 does) gives the boxed form.

Why it matters. To the source, the whole line and its load are one impedance. That is what lets unit 06's circuit results — complex power, conjugate matching — apply at the line's input: the source sees $Z_{\mathrm{in}}$, and nothing else about the line. The price is that $Z_{\mathrm{in}}$ depends on frequency through $\beta = \omega/u$ even when $Z_L$ does not: section 7's bandwidth problem comes from here.

Unit 02's hypothesis (H3), revisited. When $\beta d \ll 1$, $\tan\beta d \approx \beta d$ and $Z_{\mathrm{in}} \approx Z_L$ to first order: a short line is invisible, which is unit 02's lumped approximation. The correction to first order is $Z_{\mathrm{in}} \approx Z_L + i\beta d\,(Z_0^2 - Z_L^2)/Z_0$, the series inductance and shunt capacitance of the length $d$ (a problem on the sheet checks this against $L'd$ and $C'd$).

09 · steady state · example

Worked: three loads, and a reactance that rises

worked example 1 — the input impedance in its three limits

1. Matched, $Z_L = Z_0$. $\Gamma_L = 0$, and the formula gives $Z_0(Z_0 + iZ_0t)/(Z_0 + iZ_0t) = Z_0$ at every $d$: a matched line looks like a resistor $Z_0$, of any length.

2. Short, $Z_L = 0$. $\Gamma_L = -1$ and $Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_0\cdot iZ_0\tan\beta d/Z_0 = iZ_0\tan\beta d$: a pure reactance, inductive for $0 < d < \lambda/4$.

3. Open, $Z_L \to \infty$. $\Gamma_L = +1$; divide top and bottom by $Z_L$: $Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_0/(i\tan\beta d) = -iZ_0\cot\beta d$, capacitive for $0 < d < \lambda/4$.

4. The short as a Foster reactance. At fixed $d$, $X(\omega) = Z_0\tan(\omega d/u)$, so

$$\frac{\mathrm dX}{\mathrm d\omega} = \frac{Z_0\,d}{u}\sec^2\frac{\omega d}{u} = L'd\,\sec^2\beta d > 0 ,$$

with zeros ($\beta d = n\pi$) and poles ($\beta d = \pi/2 + n\pi$) alternating — what unit 05's Foster theorem demands of a lossless one-port.

checks

Units: $Z_0d/u$ is $\Omega\cdot\mathrm m/(\mathrm m/\mathrm s) = \Omega\,\mathrm s$, and $\mathrm dX/\mathrm d\omega$ is $\Omega/\mathrm s^{-1}$ ✓; $Z_0/u = \sqrt{L'/C'}\sqrt{L'C'} = L'$ ✓. Short line, $\beta d \ll 1$: $iZ_0\beta d = i\omega L'd$, the inductance of a shorted loop of length $d$ ✓. Open: $1/(i\omega C'd)$ ✓.

Why Foster's theorem is checked, not applied. Unit 05 proved Foster's reactance theorem for networks of finitely many inductors and capacitors, whose reactance is a rational function of $\omega$. $\tan(\omega d/u)$ is not rational — it has infinitely many poles — so the theorem as proved does not cover it. The conclusion holds anyway, by the direct computation on the slide, and it holds for the reason unit 05 gave: the slope is stored energy.

Full proof that the slope is the stored energy

On the shorted line, $\Gamma_L = -1$, so $V(d') = 2iV_+\sin\beta d'$ and $I(d') = (2V_+/Z_0)\cos\beta d'$ for $0 \le d' \le d$. The mean energies per metre are $\tfrac12C'|V|^2$ and $\tfrac12L'|I|^2$ (RMS phasors, unit 05's convention), so over the length, $$\bar W_C + \bar W_L = 2|V_+|^2\int_0^d\Big(C'\sin^2\beta d' + \frac{L'}{Z_0^2}\cos^2\beta d'\Big)\mathrm dd' = 2C'|V_+|^2d,$$ since $L'/Z_0^2 = C'$ and $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$. The port current is $I = (2V_+/Z_0)\cos\beta d$, and unit 05's $2(\bar W_L + \bar W_C)/|I|^2 = 4C'|V_+|^2d\,Z_0^2/(4|V_+|^2\cos^2\beta d) = L'd\sec^2\beta d$ — the slope on the slide, exactly.

Poles. At $\beta d = \pi/2$ the shorted line has an infinite input impedance — it is a parallel resonance, the distributed version of unit 05's tank. Section 6 uses short-circuited lines as adjustable reactances. On a coaxial line a short is easy to make well, while an open end is never a perfect open: its fields fringe beyond the end and add a little capacitance.

09 · steady state

The power is the same at every point

power along a lossless line

The mean power flowing toward the load past any point $d$ is

$$\boxed{\,P = \operatorname{Re}\big(V(d)I(d)^*\big) = \frac{|V_+|^2}{Z_0}\big(1 - |\Gamma_L|^2\big)\,}$$

— forward power minus reflected power, independent of $d$.

Units: $\mathrm V^2/\Omega = \mathrm W$ ✓.

proof

$VI^* = \frac{|V_+|^2}{Z_0}(1 + \Gamma)(1 - \Gamma^*) = \frac{|V_+|^2}{Z_0}\big(1 - |\Gamma|^2 + (\Gamma - \Gamma^*)\big)$, with $\Gamma = \Gamma(d)$. The bracket's last term is imaginary, and $|\Gamma(d)| = |\Gamma_L|$. $\square$

The 12.5 Ω load, from a matched source with 100 W available: $1 - 0.6^2 = 0.64$.

Energy check, the other way round. At $d = 0$ the formula must be the power the load absorbs. There $V = V_+(1 + \Gamma_L)$ and $I = V_+(1 - \Gamma_L)/Z_0$, and with $Z_L = 12.5\ \Omega$, $|V|^2/Z_L = |V_+|^2(0.4)^2/12.5 = 0.0128|V_+|^2$, while $|V_+|^2(1 - 0.36)/50 = 0.0128|V_+|^2$ ✓. For $|V_+| = 70.71$ V (100 W forward into 50 Ω), both are 64.0 W.

The imaginary part does depend on $d$. $\operatorname{Im}(VI^*) = (|V_+|^2/Z_0)\cdot2\operatorname{Im}\Gamma(d)$ is the reactive power flowing past $d$ (unit 06's $Q$). It changes along the line because each length of line stores energy in its $C'$ and $L'$, and unit 06 showed that $Q$ is twice $\omega$ times the difference of mean magnetic and electric energy. The real part cannot change, because a lossless line has nowhere to put it.

Unit 06's route. Unit 06 proved complex power conserved in any network by Tellegen's theorem with conjugate currents. A length of line between $d_1$ and $d_2$ is (unit 08) the limit of a ladder of $L'\,\mathrm dz$ and $C'\,\mathrm dz$ sections; complex power into the ladder at $d_2$ equals that out at $d_1$ plus what the inductors and capacitors absorb, which is purely imaginary. So the real parts at $d_1$ and $d_2$ agree — the same theorem, without the computation.

Why "forward minus reflected" is not obvious. The forward and reflected waves overlap everywhere on the line, and $|V|^2$ contains their cross-term. The cross-term is what the proof shows to be purely imaginary in $VI^*$: the two waves interfere in $|V|$ (next section) but not in the power.

09 · standing waves

The standing wave, and its ratio

$|V(d)| = |V_+|\,|1 + \Gamma(d)|$, and $\Gamma(d)$ circles at radius $|\Gamma_L|$, so

$$|V_+|(1 - |\Gamma_L|) \le |V(d)| \le |V_+|(1 + |\Gamma_L|),$$

largest where $\Gamma(d)$ is positive real, smallest where it is negative real, half a wavelength apart in each case. The current, $\propto|1 - \Gamma(d)|$, does the opposite. This fixed pattern is a standing wave.

the standing-wave ratio (VSWR) $$\boxed{\,S = \frac{|V|_{\max}}{|V|_{\min}} = \frac{1 + |\Gamma_L|}{1 - |\Gamma_L|}\,}\qquad 1 \le S \le \infty$$

Units: volts over volts ✓. Matched: $S = 1$. Short or open: $S = \infty$.

The 12.5 Ω load: $|V|$ swings between $0.4|V_+|$ and $1.6|V_+|$, so $S = 4$. Minima at the load and every $\lambda/2$; maxima a quarter wavelength from each.

Why "standing". The instantaneous voltage at $d$ is $\operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2V(d)e^{i\omega t})$, a sinusoid in time whose amplitude $\sqrt2|V(d)|$ depends on $d$ but not on $t$. A travelling wave alone has $|V(d)| = |V_+|$ everywhere; the reflected wave adds a ripple that does not move. Its period in $d$ is $\lambda/2$ because $\Gamma(d)$ turns once per half wavelength.

The "V" in VSWR is for voltage: the current's ratio is the same number, with its maxima where the voltage has minima. Engineers usually say "VSWR"; the slide's $S$ is the same thing. It can be measured directly: a probe slid along a slotted section of line reads $|V|$, and $S$ with the position of a minimum gives the load (At a maximum or a minimum the impedance is real, and problem P3).

Inverting it. $|\Gamma_L| = (S - 1)/(S + 1)$. VSWR 1.5 is $|\Gamma| = 0.2$, which reflects $4\%$ of the forward power; section 7 uses it as the edge of an acceptable match. That threshold is this deck's choice for comparing the matches, not a standard's requirement.

Worked: the 12.5 Ω load's standing wave

worked example 2 — the 12.5 Ω load on 50 Ω

1. Reflection. $z_L = 12.5/50 = 0.25$; $\Gamma_L = (0.25 - 1)/(0.25 + 1) = -0.6$.

2. VSWR. $S = 1.6/0.4 = 4$.

3. The forward wave. A matched source ($R_s = Z_0$, so $\Gamma_s = 0$) with 100 W available launches $|V_+|^2/Z_0 = 100$ W, so $|V_+| = \sqrt{5000} = 70.71$ V, and absorbs whatever returns.

4. The pattern. $|V|_{\min} = 0.4\times70.71 = 28.28$ V, at the load and every $\lambda/2$ back; $|V|_{\max} = 1.6\times70.71 = 113.1$ V, at $\lambda/4$, $3\lambda/4$, … — 0.500 m, 1.499 m, … at 100 MHz.

checks

At the load, $|V|^2/R = 28.28^2/12.5 = 64.0$ W ✓. At a maximum, $\Gamma(d) = +0.6$ and $z = 1.6/0.4 = 4$, a resistance of 200 Ω: $113.1^2/200 = 64.0$ W ✓ — the same power, as the theorem said.

The source. Unit 08: a source of EMF $\mathcal E$ behind an internal resistance $R_s$ (unit 08 writes it $r_i$; this unit keeps unit 06's $R_s$) launches a wave $\mathcal EZ_0/(R_s + Z_0)$ and reflects returning waves with $\Gamma_s = (R_s - Z_0)/(R_s + Z_0)$. With $R_s = Z_0$, $\Gamma_s = 0$: the launched wave is $\mathcal E/2$ and nothing that comes back is sent out again, so in the steady state there are exactly two waves, $V_+$ and $\Gamma_LV_+$. The forward power is then $|\mathcal E|^2/4Z_0$, which is unit 06's available power: a matched source puts its whole available power into the forward wave. (In phasors, $\mathcal E$ here is the RMS phasor $V_{\mathrm{th}}$.)

An unmatched source re-reflects part of the returning wave, the forward wave becomes the sum of a geometric series (unit 08's bounce diagram in the steady state), and the power delivered then depends on the line's length. Problem P11 takes that up.

The arithmetic. $\sqrt{5000} = 70.711$; $0.4\times70.711 = 28.284$; $1.6\times70.711 = 113.137$; $28.284^2/12.5 = 64.000$; $113.137^2/200 = 64.000$. Wavelength 1.999 m, so the maxima are at $0.49975 = 0.500$ m and $1.49925 = 1.499$ m.

The figure. At the load the two phasors oppose (because $\Gamma_L$ is negative): $1 - 0.6 = 0.4$. A quarter wavelength back, the forward phasor has turned by $+\pi/2$ and the reflected one by $-\pi/2$; they now point the same way, $1 + 0.6 = 1.6$.

09 · standing waves

At a maximum or a minimum the impedance is real

Where $\Gamma(d) = +|\Gamma_L|$ (a voltage maximum) and where $\Gamma(d) = -|\Gamma_L|$ (a minimum), $z = (1 + \Gamma)/(1 - \Gamma)$ is real:

impedance at the extremes

At a voltage maximum $Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_0S$; at a minimum $Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_0/S$.

Units: $\Omega\times1$ ✓. Proof: $(1 \pm |\Gamma|)/(1 \mp |\Gamma|) = S^{\pm1}$. $\square$

The example: 200 Ω at $\lambda/4$, 12.5 Ω at the load. Between, $Z_{\mathrm{in}}$ is complex.

Reading a load. Given $S$ and the distance $d_{\min}$ from the load to the first minimum, $Z_0/S$ is the impedance at $d_{\min}$, and the input-impedance formula run backwards, with $d \to -d_{\min}$, returns $Z_L$. Problem P3.

The 12.5 Ω load's $z_{\mathrm{in}}(d) = r + ix$ over half a wavelength: real at the load (0.25) and at $\lambda/4$ (4), complex between.

Why those two points. $z = (1 + \Gamma)/(1 - \Gamma)$ is real exactly when $\Gamma$ is real (the map sends the real axis to itself, section 4). On a circle $|\Gamma| = |\Gamma_L|$ there are two real points, $\pm|\Gamma_L|$, and they are the voltage maximum and minimum by the previous slide.

Running backwards. Moving toward the load by $d_{\min}$ is the formula with $d$ replaced by $-d_{\min}$, because the derivation never assumed $d > 0$: $z_L = (z_{\min} - i\tan\beta d_{\min})/(1 - iz_{\min}\tan\beta d_{\min})$ with $z_{\min} = 1/S$.

The figure. Computed from $z_{\mathrm{in}} = (0.25 + i\tan\beta d)/(1 + 0.25i\tan\beta d)$. The reactance is positive (inductive) between the load and $\lambda/4$ and negative beyond; since $x = t(1 - z_L^2)/(1 + z_L^2t^2)$ with $t = \tan\beta d$, its extremes, $\pm1.875$, are at $t = \pm1/z_L = \pm4$, i.e. $d = 0.211\lambda$ and $0.289\lambda$. These are where the stub of section 6 is not placed — the stub needs the admittance's real part to be 1, a different condition.

Total reflection: MR's string

A short: $\Gamma_L = -1$, $|\Gamma| = 1$, $S = \infty$. Then

$$V(d) = V_+(e^{i\beta d} - e^{-i\beta d}) = 2iV_+\sin\beta d,$$

and with $V_+$ real, $v(d, t) = -2\sqrt2\,V_+\sin\beta d\,\sin\omega t$: one shape in $d$, scaled in time, with nodes every $\lambda/2$ from the short.

This is a string held at a fixed end: MR 18's reflection at a fixed end (A fixed end is an odd extension), summed with the incoming wave, gives MR 19's normal mode shape $\sin kx$ (The string's spectrum, and three checks). Voltage plays the string's displacement.

energy

$P = (|V_+|^2/Z_0)(1 - 1) = 0$: a standing wave with $|\Gamma| = 1$ carries no net power. Energy sloshes between $C'$ and $L'$, as in a string's mode between kinetic and potential.

A shorted line: the voltage at four instants (thin) and its envelope (thick). Every point moves in phase or in antiphase — the definition of a mode.

Which end is which. Unit 08 matched the line's ends to MR 18's string: a short ($v = 0$) is a fixed end (displacement zero, $\Gamma = -1$), an open end ($i = 0$, so $\partial v/\partial z = 0$ by the telegrapher's equation) is a free end ($\Gamma = +1$). An open line has $V(d) = 2V_+\cos\beta d$: maxima at the open end, the free string's antinode.

The time dependence. $\operatorname{Re}\big(\sqrt2\cdot2iV_+\sin\beta d\,e^{i\omega t}\big) = 2\sqrt2V_+\sin\beta d\,\operatorname{Re}(ie^{i\omega t}) = -2\sqrt2V_+\sin\beta d\sin\omega t$ for real $V_+$. The current is $I = (2V_+/Z_0)\cos\beta d$, so $i(d, t) = (2\sqrt2V_+/Z_0)\cos\beta d\cos\omega t$: a quarter wavelength and a quarter period away from the voltage. When the voltage is everywhere zero the current is at its peak: all the energy is magnetic; a quarter period later it is all electric.

Partial reflection is a travelling wave plus a standing wave: $V_+e^{i\beta d} + \Gamma_LV_+e^{-i\beta d}$ with real $\Gamma_L$ is $(1 - |\Gamma_L|)$ of a travelling wave plus $|\Gamma_L|$ of a standing one, which is why the minimum of the envelope is not zero. MR 19's string had only the standing kind because both its ends reflected totally.

The quarter-wave line

At one length the input-impedance formula collapses: a quarter wavelength inverts impedance about $Z_0$. A section of the right impedance therefore matches a resistive load — the first match.

09 · quarter-wave

A quarter wavelength inverts impedance

the quarter-wave and half-wave lines

A line of impedance $Z_q$ and length $d$, ending in $Z_L$:

$$d = \frac\lambda4:\quad Z_{\mathrm{in}} = \frac{Z_q^2}{Z_L}$$ $$d = \frac\lambda2:\quad Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_L$$

Units: $\Omega^2/\Omega = \Omega$ ✓.

proof

At $\beta d = \pi/2$, $\tan\beta d \to \infty$; dividing top and bottom by it, $Z_{\mathrm{in}} \to Z_q\cdot iZ_q/(iZ_L) = Z_q^2/Z_L$. At $\beta d = \pi$, $\tan\beta d = 0$. $\square$

Two lengths at which the formula needs no tangent table.

Normalised to $Z_q$: $z \mapsto 1/z$. A short becomes an open, an inductor a capacitor. So a quarter-wave transformer of impedance $Z_q = \sqrt{Z_0Z_L}$ turns a resistive $Z_L$ into $Z_0Z_L/Z_L = Z_0$: matched. A half-wave line is invisible, at one frequency.

"Transformer". An ideal transformer — unit 07, a spine unit this branch does not assume — scales an impedance by the square of its turns ratio; a quarter-wave section multiplies a resistive load by $(Z_q/Z_L)^2$ — here $(25/12.5)^2 = 4$, turning 12.5 Ω into 50 Ω — which is why it borrowed the name. Unlike a transformer, it works at one frequency only, and it inverts rather than scales: a load that rises, at the far end, falls at the near end.

Why only resistive loads. If $Z_L = R + iX$, then $Z_q^2/Z_L$ is complex for real $Z_q$, so no real $Z_q$ makes $Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_0$. The cure is to put the section where the line's own impedance is real: at a voltage maximum or minimum of the existing standing wave (At a maximum or a minimum the impedance is real), where $Z = Z_0S$ or $Z_0/S$. Problem P7 does this.

Inductor to capacitor. $Z_L = i\omega L$ becomes $Z_q^2/(i\omega L) = 1/(i\omega(L/Z_q^2))$, a capacitor $C = L/Z_q^2$ — at the one frequency where the section is a quarter wavelength.

Why it works: two reflections that cancel

The same match, as unit 08 would see it: a sum of reflections, each a wave.

  • At the junction, from the 50 Ω side: $\Gamma_1 = (25 - 50)/(25 + 50) = -1/3$ straight back; $1 + \Gamma_1 = 2/3$ enters.
  • At the load: $\Gamma_2 = (12.5 - 25)/(12.5 + 25) = -1/3$.
  • A round trip through $\lambda/4$ multiplies by $e^{-2i\beta d} = e^{-i\pi} = -1$.
  • Back through the junction from inside: $1 + \Gamma_1' = 1 + 1/3 = 4/3$; and $\Gamma_1' = +1/3$ reflects in again.

The first wave back out is $\tfrac23\cdot(-\tfrac13)(-1)\cdot\tfrac43 = \tfrac8{27}$; each further round trip multiplies by $\tfrac13\cdot(-\tfrac13)(-1) = \tfrac19$:

$$\Gamma = -\tfrac13 + \tfrac{8}{27}\sum_{k\ge0}\big(\tfrac19\big)^k = -\tfrac13 + \tfrac{8}{27}\cdot\tfrac98 = 0 .$$

The quarter wavelength is what makes the second reflection return with the opposite sign.

Partial waves, per unit incident. Unit 08's bounce diagram, in the steady state: every arrow is present at once.

Where the coefficients come from. Unit 08: at a junction of two lines a wave from line 1 reflects with $(Z_2 - Z_1)/(Z_2 + Z_1)$ and is transmitted with $1 + \Gamma$. From the 50 Ω side into 25 Ω: $-1/3$ and $2/3$. From the 25 Ω side into 50 Ω: $+1/3$ and $4/3$. (Transmission coefficients bigger than 1 are allowed: voltage is not power. Power check for the first crossing: $(2/3)^2/25 = 0.01778$ against $1/50 - (1/3)^2/50 = 0.01778$ per unit $|V|^2$ ✓.)

Full proof that the series sums to zero

A wave of 1 arrives. The direct reflection is $-1/3$. The part that enters, $2/3$, makes $k + 1$ round trips before leaving after its $k$-th internal reflection at the junction, each round trip contributing $\Gamma_2e^{-2i\beta d} = (-1/3)(-1) = 1/3$ and each internal reflection $\Gamma_1' = 1/3$. So the emerging total is $$\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac43\cdot\tfrac13\sum_{k\ge0}\big(\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac13\big)^k = \tfrac{8}{27}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - 1/9} = \tfrac13 ,$$ which cancels $-1/3$. At a general frequency the round-trip factor is $-\tfrac13e^{-2i\theta}$ with $\theta = \beta d$, and the same sum gives $\Gamma \ne 0$: section 7's bandwidth curve. The slide writes the round-trip and internal-reflection factors as products, $(-1/3)(-1)$ and $(1/3)(-1/3)(-1)$, to show where each sign comes from.

Why a geometric series is allowed. The ratio is $1/9 < 1$, so it converges; physically, it is the steady state that unit 08's bounce diagram approaches as $t \to \infty$.

section 4

Möbius maps

The map from impedance to reflection coefficient is a fraction of two linear functions. Such maps are built from three moves, send circles to circles, and this one sends the passive half-plane onto the unit disc.

09 · Möbius

The reflection coefficient is a map

With $z = Z/Z_0$, $\Gamma = (Z - Z_0)/(Z + Z_0)$ is

$$\Gamma(z) = \frac{z - 1}{z + 1},\qquad z = \frac{1 + \Gamma}{1 - \Gamma}.$$
a Möbius transformation $$T(z) = \frac{az + b}{cz + d},$$

with $a, b, c, d$ complex and $ad - bc \ne 0$, on the extended plane $\mathbb C\cup\{\infty\}$, with $T(-d/c) = \infty$ and $T(\infty) = a/c$ (or $T(\infty) = \infty$ if $c = 0$).

$\Gamma$ is one: $a = 1$, $b = -1$, $c = d = 1$, $ad - bc = 2$. Its inverse is another. In this section $d$ is a coefficient, not a distance; distances return in section 5.

$z$ and $\Gamma$ are dimensionless ✓.

Where some impedances go. A short ($z = 0$) is $\Gamma = -1$, a match the centre, an open ($z = \infty$) $\Gamma = 1$, a pure reactance $\pm i$ the rim. The 12.5 Ω load: $-0.6$.

Why $ad - bc \ne 0$. $T(z) - T(w) = (ad - bc)(z - w)/((cz + d)(cw + d))$, so the condition is exactly what makes $T$ one-to-one; with $ad - bc = 0$ the fraction is constant. Solving $\Gamma = (az + b)/(cz + d)$ for $z$ gives $z = (d\Gamma - b)/(-c\Gamma + a)$, again Möbius, with the same determinant: every Möbius map is a bijection of the extended plane.

The point at infinity. An open circuit has $Z = \infty$; the extended plane gives it a place. Formally a neighbourhood of $\infty$ is the outside of a large circle, and the rules on the slide make $T$ continuous there. (MR 20 met the extended plane in complex analysis; nothing from it is needed here.)

The letters. $a, b, c, d$ are the standard names, used by Needham (ch. 3, "Möbius Transformations and Inversion", in the reading list). The distance $d$ of sections 1–3 does not appear in this section.

09 · Möbius

Every Möbius map is built from three moves

decomposition

Every Möbius map is a composition of translations $z \mapsto z + b$, dilation-rotations $z \mapsto kz$ ($k \ne 0$), and at most one inversion $z \mapsto 1/z$.

proof

If $c = 0$: $T(z) = (a/d)z + b/d$, a dilation-rotation then a translation. If $c \ne 0$, divide:

$$T(z) = \frac ac + \frac{bc - ad}{c}\cdot\frac{1}{cz + d}.$$

So $z \mapsto cz \mapsto cz + d \mapsto 1/(cz + d) \mapsto \frac{bc - ad}{c}(\cdot) \mapsto \frac ac + (\cdot)$, and $bc - ad \ne 0$. $\square$

A dilation-rotation by $k = |k|e^{i\varphi}$ scales by $|k|$ and turns by $\varphi$.

For $\Gamma$: $c = 1$, so one translation, the inversion, a dilation-rotation by $-2$ (scale 2, turn $\pi$), and a translation.

Check of the division. $\frac ac + \frac{bc - ad}{c(cz + d)} = \frac{a(cz + d) + bc - ad}{c(cz + d)} = \frac{acz + bc}{c(cz + d)} = \frac{az + b}{cz + d}$ ✓.

For $\Gamma$. $a = 1, b = -1, c = 1, d = 1$: $\Gamma = 1 + \frac{-1 - 1}{1}\cdot\frac1{z + 1} = 1 - \frac{2}{z + 1}$. The steps are $z \mapsto w = z + 1 \mapsto u = 1/w \mapsto -2u \mapsto 1 - 2u$.

Why this helps. Any property that each of the three moves has, and that survives composition, every Möbius map has. The next two slides use that for circles. Translations and dilation-rotations are similarities of the plane — they keep shapes, so they send circles to circles and lines to lines. All the work is in the inversion. (Needham ch. 3 proves the same theorem geometrically, by inversion in a circle; the algebraic proof here is self-contained.)

09 · Möbius

Circles and lines are one family

a generalised circle

The set of $z$ with

$$A|z|^2 + B\bar z + \bar Bz + C = 0,$$

with $A, C$ real, $B$ complex and $|B|^2 > AC$.

lemma

These are exactly the circles ($A \ne 0$) and the lines ($A = 0$).

proof

$A \ne 0$: divide by $A$ and complete the square, $|z + B/A|^2 = (|B|^2 - AC)/A^2$: the circle of centre $-B/A$, radius $\sqrt{|B|^2 - AC}/|A|$. Every circle $|z - z_0| = \rho$ is of this form ($A = 1$, $B = -z_0$, $C = |z_0|^2 - \rho^2$). $A = 0$: $2\operatorname{Re}(\bar Bz) = -C$ with $B \ne 0$, a line perpendicular to $B$; every line is one. $\square$

One equation. A line is a circle through $\infty$: let the radius grow with one point held fixed.

Why $|B|^2 > AC$. When $A \ne 0$ it makes the radius real and positive; equality would give a single point, and the reverse inequality the empty set. When $A = 0$ it says $B \ne 0$, without which the equation is $C = 0$: nothing, or everything.

The line case, unpacked. With $B = \beta_1 + i\beta_2$ and $z = x + iy$, $\bar Bz + B\bar z = 2\operatorname{Re}(\bar Bz) = 2(\beta_1x + \beta_2y)$, so the equation is $2\beta_1x + 2\beta_2y + C = 0$: any line in the plane, with normal vector $(\beta_1, \beta_2)$. (Here $\beta_1, \beta_2$ are just the real and imaginary parts of $B$, not the phase constant.)

"A line is a circle through $\infty$." In the extended plane a line is unbounded in both directions, so its closure contains $\infty$; a circle through a fixed point whose radius tends to infinity tends to a line. That is why a map that moves $\infty$ to a finite point can turn a line into a circle, as the inversion is about to.

09 · Möbius

Möbius maps send generalised circles to generalised circles

theorem — the checkpoint's

The image of a generalised circle under a Möbius map is a generalised circle.

proof

By the decomposition it suffices to check the three moves. Translations and dilation-rotations are similarities: they send circles to circles and lines to lines. For $w = 1/z$, put $z = 1/w$ in the equation and multiply by $|w|^2$:

$$A + B w + \bar B\bar w + C|w|^2 = 0,$$

that is, $C|w|^2 + \bar B\bar w + Bw + A = 0$: the same form with $A$ and $C$ swapped and $B$ replaced by $\bar B$, and $|\bar B|^2 > CA$ still. $\square$

The swap says which: a line ($A = 0$) not through 0 ($C \ne 0$) becomes a circle through 0, and the reverse.

Under $w = 1/z$: vertical lines become circles tangent to the imaginary axis at 0, horizontal ones circles tangent to the real axis. Every image passes through $0 = 1/\infty$.

Full proof, the steps written out

Translation $w = z + b_0$: substitute $z = w - b_0$; $|w - b_0|^2 = |w|^2 - \bar b_0w - b_0\bar w + |b_0|^2$, so the equation keeps the form with $A' = A$, $B' = B - Ab_0$, $C' = C + A|b_0|^2 - 2\operatorname{Re}(\bar Bb_0)$. (Or: a translation moves a circle's centre and keeps its radius.)

Dilation-rotation $w = kz$: substitute $z = w/k$; $A|w|^2/|k|^2 + B\bar w/\bar k + \bar Bw/k + C = 0$; multiply by $|k|^2$: $A' = A$, $B' = kB$, $C' = |k|^2C$, and $|B'|^2 - A'C' = |k|^2(|B|^2 - AC) > 0$.

Inversion: as on the slide. The points $z = 0$ and $z = \infty$, where the substitution divides by zero, are handled by the extended plane: $0$ is on the original set iff $C = 0$, and then $\infty$ is on the image, which has $A' = C = 0$ and is a line, consistently.

So every Möbius map, being a composition of these, carries the family of generalised circles to itself. Since the inverse is also Möbius, it is a bijection of the family. Needham ch. 3 has a geometric proof and the further fact — not needed here — that Möbius maps preserve angles.

The figure, computed. $\operatorname{Re}z = a$ is $A = 0$, $B = 1/2$, $C = -a$; its image has $A' = -a$, $B' = 1/2$, $C' = 0$: $|w|^2 - \operatorname{Re}w/a = 0$, the circle of centre $1/2a$ and radius $1/2a$. $\operatorname{Im}z = c$ goes to the circle of centre $-i/2c$ and radius $1/2|c|$.

The passive half-plane is the disc

theorem

$\Gamma = (z - 1)/(z + 1)$ maps $\operatorname{Re}z > 0$ onto $|\Gamma| < 1$, the imaginary axis onto $|\Gamma| = 1$ minus the point 1, and $\infty$ to $1$. It sends $z = 1$ to the centre.

proof

$|z + 1|^2 - |z - 1|^2 = 4\operatorname{Re}z$. So $|\Gamma| < 1 \iff |z - 1| < |z + 1| \iff \operatorname{Re}z > 0$, and likewise with $=$. Onto, because $z = (1 + \Gamma)/(1 - \Gamma)$ inverts it. $\square$

Unit 05 proved every impedance of positive $R$, $L$, $C$ positive-real (Every RLC impedance is positive-real): $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega) \ge 0$. So every passive impedance lands in the closed unit disc — unit 05's "Smith chart is a map of the positive-real right half-plane".

The right half-plane (left) and its image, the disc (right). The grid lines become circles; the 12.5 Ω load goes to $-0.6$.

Geometrically. $|z - 1| < |z + 1|$ says $z$ is nearer to $1$ than to $-1$, and the set of such points is the half-plane to the right of the perpendicular bisector of $-1$ and $1$, the imaginary axis. No algebra needed.

In power terms. The previous section's theorem gives the power into a load as $(|V_+|^2/Z_0)(1 - |\Gamma|^2)$. A load absorbs power iff $|\Gamma| < 1$; a lossless one ($\operatorname{Re}Z = 0$) has $|\Gamma| = 1$ and returns everything. So the disc is the set of loads that absorb, and the theorem is that same fact said about impedance. A negative resistance — an amplifier, a source — has $|\Gamma| > 1$ and lies outside: it returns more than it receives (problem P6).

What is landed. Unit 05 called a rational $Z(s)$ positive-real when $\operatorname{Re}Z(s) \ge 0$ for $\operatorname{Re}s > 0$, and proved it for every network of positive $R$, $L$, $C$ from Tellegen's theorem with conjugate currents. On the imaginary axis, by continuity, $\operatorname{Re}Z(i\omega) \ge 0$ — the closed right half $Z$-plane. The Smith chart is this unit's map of that half-plane, and the chart's disc is unit 05's region, redrawn so that it is bounded and its infinite point (the open circuit) is an ordinary point on the rim.

Constant resistance: circles through Γ = 1

constant-resistance circles

The line $\operatorname{Re}z = r$ ($r \ge 0$) maps to the circle

$$\Big|\Gamma - \frac{r}{1 + r}\Big| = \frac{1}{1 + r}.$$
proof

By the theorem the image is a generalised circle. It contains $\Gamma(\infty) = 1$ and $\Gamma(r) = (r - 1)/(r + 1)$, and it is symmetric about the real axis, since $\Gamma(\bar z) = \overline{\Gamma(z)}$ and the line is. A circle symmetric about the real axis through those two real points has them as a diameter: centre $\tfrac12\big(1 + \frac{r - 1}{r + 1}\big) = \frac{r}{r + 1}$, radius $\tfrac12\big(1 - \frac{r - 1}{r + 1}\big) = \frac1{r + 1}$. $\square$

$r = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4$, with their centres. The 12.5 Ω load is on the $r = 0.25$ circle, at its left end.

Constant reactance: circles centred at 1 + i/x

constant-reactance circles

The line $\operatorname{Im}z = x$ ($x \ne 0$) maps to

$$\Big|\Gamma - \Big(1 + \frac ix\Big)\Big| = \frac1{|x|}.$$

Only the arc inside the disc is used: $\operatorname{Re}z \ge 0$.

proof

With $\Gamma = 1 - 2/(z + 1)$ and $z = t + ix$:

$$\Gamma - 1 - \frac ix = -\frac{x + i(t + 1)}{x\,(t + 1 + ix)},$$

whose modulus is $\frac{\sqrt{x^2 + (t + 1)^2}}{|x|\sqrt{(t + 1)^2 + x^2}} = \frac1{|x|}$ for every $t$. $\square$

$x > 0$ (inductive) above the axis, $x < 0$ (capacitive) below; $x = 0$ is the real axis. Dimensionless throughout ✓.

$x = \pm0.5, \pm1, \pm2$. Each circle touches the real axis at $\Gamma = 1$ and meets the rim again at $\Gamma(ix)$.

The step the slide skips. $\Gamma - 1 - i/x = -2/(z + 1) - i/x = -\frac{2x + i(z + 1)}{x(z + 1)}$. With $z = t + ix$, $2x + i(t + 1 + ix) = 2x - x + i(t + 1) = x + i(t + 1)$. The modulus of $x + i(t + 1)$ equals that of $t + 1 + ix$, which is the whole proof.

By the theorem instead. $\operatorname{Im}z = x$ translates to $\operatorname{Im}w = x$; inverts to the circle of centre $-i/2x$ and radius $1/2|x|$ (the previous section's figure); $\times(-2)$ gives centre $i/x$, radius $1/|x|$; $+1$ gives centre $1 + i/x$. Where it meets the rim. At $t = 0$, $\Gamma(ix) = (ix - 1)/(ix + 1)$, which has modulus 1. For $x = 1$: $(i - 1)/(i + 1) = i$, the top of the disc; the $x = 1$ circle runs from $i$ to $1$ inside the disc, centre $1 + i$, radius 1 — the figure's dashed circle is the rest of it, which belongs to $\operatorname{Re}z < 0$.

Touching at 1. Every $x$-circle has its centre at real part 1 and passes through $\Gamma = 1$, so its tangent there is horizontal: it touches the real axis at 1. Every $r$-circle has its centre on the real axis and passes through 1, so its tangent there is vertical. So at $\Gamma = 1$ every $x$-circle meets every $r$-circle at a right angle, the image of the fact that all the lines meet at $z = \infty$.

The Smith chart

the Smith chart

The unit disc in the $\Gamma$-plane, ruled with the $r$-circles and $x$-arcs. Each point is one passive impedance $z = r + ix$, read off the two families; its distance from the centre is $|\Gamma|$.

Reading it. Centre: matched. Real axis: resistances, $0$ at the left to $\infty$ at the right. Rim: lossless loads. Upper half: inductive. Lower: capacitive.

The 12.5 Ω load, $z = 0.25$: on the real axis, at $\Gamma = -0.6$.

The chart is named after P. H. Smith, who published it as a "transmission line calculator" in Electronics, January 1939.

Computed from the two circle formulas: $r = 0, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, 5$ and $x = \pm0.2, \pm0.5, \pm1, \pm2, \pm5$.

What the chart is for. Before computers, the input-impedance formula with complex numbers and tangents was tedious to evaluate by hand. On the chart it is a rotation (next slide): plot $z_L$, turn, read $z_{\mathrm{in}}$. That was its purpose — Smith's title calls it a calculator — but the reason this unit teaches it is the picture: one disc holding every passive load, on which a line acts by rotation.

Walking along the line turns the chart

Section 1: $\Gamma(d) = \Gamma_Le^{-2i\beta d}$. On the chart, walking a distance $d$ toward the generator is a

rotation

clockwise about the centre, by $2\beta d = 4\pi d/\lambda$: a full turn per $\lambda/2$. The radius $|\Gamma|$, and so the VSWR, does not change.

So $z_{\mathrm{in}}(d)$ is found by plotting $z_L$, turning by $4\pi d/\lambda$ clockwise, and reading the circles. In Möbius terms: $z_L \mapsto \Gamma_L \mapsto e^{-2i\beta d}\Gamma_L \mapsto z_{\mathrm{in}}$, three Möbius maps.

The example at $d = \lambda/8$: $\Gamma = 0.6i$, and $z = (1 + 0.6i)/(1 - 0.6i) = 0.471 + 0.882i$. The formula: $(0.25 + i)/(1 + 0.25i) = 0.471 + 0.882i$ ✓.

The 12.5 Ω load walked back from the load: a quarter wavelength takes it from $z = 0.25$ to $z = 4$, along the circle $|\Gamma| = 0.6$, $S = 4$.

Why the VSWR circle. $|\Gamma| = 0.6$ is the circle of constant standing-wave ratio $S = 4$; it meets the real axis at $z = 1/S = 0.25$ (the minimum) and $z = S = 4$ (the maximum) — section 2's theorem, now visible: the right-hand crossing is the voltage maximum and its $r$-value is the VSWR.

The arithmetic at $\lambda/8$. $(1 + 0.6i)/(1 - 0.6i) = (1 + 0.6i)^2/1.36 = (0.64 + 1.2i)/1.36 = 0.4706 + 0.8824i$. From the formula, $\tan(\pi/4) = 1$: $(0.25 + i)(1 - 0.25i)/1.0625 = (0.5 + 0.9375i)/1.0625 = 0.4706 + 0.8824i$ ✓. In ohms, $23.5 + 44.1i\ \Omega$: 0.250 m of cable makes 12.5 Ω look like a resistance in series with an inductor.

Toward the load is anticlockwise. Printed charts mark both directions round the rim, with a scale in wavelengths from 0 to 0.5.

The quarter-wave line is a half-turn

inversion is a half-turn $$\Gamma(1/z) = \frac{1 - z}{1 + z} = -\Gamma(z).$$

So $z \mapsto 1/z$ is $\Gamma \mapsto -\Gamma$: a rotation by $\pi$ about the centre.

A quarter wavelength turns the chart by $4\pi(\lambda/4)/\lambda = \pi$. That is section 3's $z_{\mathrm{in}} = 1/z_L$ (normalised to the section), seen on the chart: the theorem and the rotation are the same fact.

Match 1 on the chart: normalised to $Z_q = 25\ \Omega$, the load is $z = 0.5$; a half-turn takes it to $2$, which is $2\times25 = 50\ \Omega$ — the centre of the 50 Ω chart.

Diametrically opposite points are reciprocal impedances: $0.25 \leftrightarrow 4$, and $0.5 + 0.5i \leftrightarrow 1 - i$.

Two charts in one match. The quarter-wave section has its own $Z_0 = 25\ \Omega$, so its rotation happens on a chart normalised to 25 Ω; the result is then renormalised to 50 Ω. Renormalising is itself a Möbius map ($z \mapsto kz$ with a real $k$, conjugated by $\Gamma$), which carries the real axis to itself: the point 2 on the 25 Ω chart is the point 1 on the 50 Ω chart.

The check. $1/(0.5 + 0.5i) = (0.5 - 0.5i)/0.5 = 1 - i$ ✓. $\Gamma(0.5 + 0.5i) = (-0.5 + 0.5i)/(1.5 + 0.5i) = -0.2 + 0.4i$ and $\Gamma(1 - i) = (-i)/(2 - i) = 0.2 - 0.4i$: negatives ✓.

Why "about $Z_0$". In ohms, $Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_q^2/Z_L$: the geometric mean of $Z_L$ and $Z_{\mathrm{in}}$ is $Z_q$, so on a logarithmic scale of resistance the section reflects $Z_L$ in the point $Z_q$. The chart's half-turn is the same reflection, drawn in the disc.

09 · the chart

The admittance chart

With normalised admittance $y = 1/z = Y Z_0 = g + ib$, the same reflection coefficient is

$$\Gamma = \frac{z - 1}{z + 1} = \frac{1 - y}{1 + y} = -\frac{y - 1}{y + 1}.$$
the admittance chart

The point $\Gamma$ carries admittance $y$ exactly where the impedance chart carries the value $y$ at $-\Gamma$. So the admittance grid is the impedance grid turned through 180°.

Proof: the half-turn identity on the last slide, $\Gamma(1/z) = -\Gamma(z)$. $\square$

Same disc, same points, same rotations along a line; only the labels change. The load sits where it was, now reading $y = 4$.

Conductance circles $g$ now touch at the left; $g = 1$ (thick) passes through the centre. Positive susceptance (capacitive) is now below the axis.

Why bother. Elements in parallel add admittances, as elements in series add impedances (unit 05). A stub connected across the line — next section — adds its admittance to the line's, so the natural chart there is the admittance chart.

Which way up. On the impedance chart, $x > 0$ is above the axis. On the admittance chart, $b > 0$ is below: a capacitor has $y = i\omega CZ_0$, positive susceptance, and its impedance $-i/(\omega CZ_0)$ is below the axis on the impedance chart. The point does not move; only the reading does.

A common alternative. Many texts keep one impedance grid and instead move the point: to work in admittances they plot $y$ where the impedance chart has that value, i.e. at $-\Gamma$. That is the same half-turn applied to the point rather than to the grid. This deck keeps the point fixed, because the point is the physical $\Gamma$ on the line, and only the labels are a choice.

09 · the stub

A shunt stub

A stub is a length $\ell$ of line, here short-circuited at its far end, connected in parallel with the main line. By worked example 1 it is a pure susceptance:

$$y_{\mathrm{stub}} = \frac{1}{i\tan\beta\ell} = -i\cot\beta\ell .$$
single-stub matching
  1. Walk from the load to a point $d$ where $y(d) = 1 + ib$: on the $g = 1$ circle.
  2. Connect there a stub with $y_{\mathrm{stub}} = -ib$.
  3. Then the line sees $1 + ib - ib = 1$: matched, from the stub back to the source.

Only 50 Ω line is needed, and both $d$ and $\ell$ can be adjusted — the stub works for any load that absorbs power, complex or not.

Match 2 for the 12.5 Ω load: the admittances just on each side of the junction.

Why parallel adds admittance. At the junction the two lines share one voltage, and the current from the source splits between them (unit 03's current law at a node). So $I = (Y_{\text{line}} + Y_{\text{stub}})V$.

Why the $g = 1$ circle is always reached. Walking along the line traces the whole circle $|\Gamma| = |\Gamma_L|$, and the $g = 1$ circle runs from the centre to the rim, at $\Gamma = -1$. Every circle about the centre of radius less than 1 crosses it — twice, unless the load is already matched — so every passive load that is not lossless has exactly two stub positions in each half wavelength.

Stub length is cyclic too. Adding $\lambda/2$ to $\ell$ changes nothing, since $\cot$ has period $\pi$; the shortest stub is usual. An open-circuited stub gives $y = i\tan\beta\ell$, and the same susceptance with a length differing by $\lambda/4$ (problem P8). Pozar, ch. 5, treats stub tuning.

Finding the stub on the chart

1. Plot the load on the admittance chart: $y_L = 1/0.25 = 4$, at $\Gamma = -0.6$.

2. Rotate clockwise along $|\Gamma| = 0.6$ until the $g = 1$ circle, $|\Gamma + \tfrac12| = \tfrac12$. Solving with $|\Gamma| = 0.6$: $|\Gamma|^2 + \operatorname{Re}\Gamma = 0$, so

$$\Gamma = -0.36 \pm 0.48i .$$

3. Read the angle. From $\pi$ to $\arg(-0.36 + 0.48i) = 126.87°$ is $53.13°$ clockwise $= 2\beta d$, so $d = 53.13/720\,\lambda = 0.0738\lambda$. The other crossing is $306.87°$ round: $d = 0.4262\lambda$.

4. Read the admittance: $y = 1 - 1.5i$ at the first, $1 + 1.5i$ at the second. The stub must supply $+1.5i$ or $-1.5i$.

The stub moves the point along $g = 1$ to the centre: its susceptance changes $b$ and leaves $g$ alone.

The intersection. The admittance chart's $g = 1$ circle is the impedance chart's $r = 1$ circle turned through 180°: centre $-\tfrac12$, radius $\tfrac12$. $|\Gamma + \tfrac12|^2 = \tfrac14$ expands to $|\Gamma|^2 + \operatorname{Re}\Gamma = 0$; with $|\Gamma|^2 = 0.36$, $\operatorname{Re}\Gamma = -0.36$ and $\operatorname{Im}\Gamma = \pm\sqrt{0.36 - 0.1296} = \pm0.48$.

The angles. $\arg(-0.36 + 0.48i) = \pi - \arctan(0.48/0.36) = 180° - 53.13° = 126.87°$. A clockwise turn of $53.13°$ is $2\beta d = 0.9273$ rad, $d = 0.9273/(4\pi)\,\lambda = 0.07379\lambda$. The other point is at $-126.87°$, a clockwise turn of $180° + 126.87° = 306.87°$ from the start, $d = 306.87/720\,\lambda = 0.4262\lambda$.

The admittance there. $y = (1 - \Gamma)/(1 + \Gamma) = (1.36 - 0.48i)/(0.64 + 0.48i) = (1.36 - 0.48i)(0.64 - 0.48i)/0.64 = (0.64 - 0.96i)/0.64 = 1 - 1.5i$ ✓. The chart gave $d$ and $y$ in exact arithmetic here only because the circles happen to meet at a 3–4–5 triangle; on a printed chart one reads them to two figures and checks by formula, as the next slide does.

worked example 5 — match 2, checked

1. The condition. With $t = \tan\beta d$ and $y_L = 4$, the input-impedance formula for admittances is $y(d) = (y_L + it)/(1 + iy_Lt)$, whose real part is

checks

Step 1, the algebra. For admittances the input formula has the same form, because $y = 1/z$ and inverting $(z_L + it)/(1 + iz_Lt)$ gives $(1 + iz_Lt)/(z_L + it) = (y_L + it)/(1 + iy_Lt)$ after dividing through by $z_L$. Then $y = (y_L + it)(1 - iy_Lt)/(1 + y_L^2t^2)$ has real part $(y_L + y_Lt^2)/(1 + y_L^2t^2)$. Setting it to 1: $y_L + y_Lt^2 = 1 + y_L^2t^2$, so $t^2(y_L - y_L^2) = 1 - y_L$, so $t^2 = 1/y_L$ (for $y_L \ne 1$).

Step 3. $(4 + 0.5i)(1 - 2i)/5 = (4 - 8i + 0.5i + 1)/5 = (5 - 7.5i)/5 = 1 - 1.5i$. At $t = -\tfrac12$: $1 + 1.5i$.

Step 4. $\operatorname{arccot}1.5 = \arctan(1/1.5) = 0.5880$ rad. $\cot\beta\ell = -1.5$ on $(0, \pi)$: $\beta\ell = \pi - 0.5880 = 2.5536$, $\ell/\lambda = 2.5536/2\pi = 0.4064$; $\times1.999$ m $= 0.812$ m. For $\cot\beta\ell = 1.5$: $\beta\ell = 0.5880$, $\ell/\lambda = 0.0936$, 0.187 m.

Positions. $\arctan\tfrac12 = 0.46365$ rad, $/2\pi = 0.07379$; $(\pi - 0.46365)/2\pi = 0.42621$. Times 1.999 m: 0.1475 m and 0.8520 m.

09 · the real system

An antenna at the end of a feed line

The system, from nothing. A radio transmitter generates power at one frequency. A feed line — usually a coaxial cable like unit 08's — carries it, as a guided wave, to the antenna, which radiates it (branch 16 says how).

To the line, the antenna is a load impedance at the operating frequency. When that is not $Z_0$, part of the forward wave is reflected, as for the 12.5 Ω load.

a matching network

A lossless two-port between line and load that makes the line see $Z_0$ at the operating frequency. Three kinds: a stub (section 6), a quarter-wave section (section 3), or an L-network of one series and one shunt reactance — unit 05's $L$ and $C$ (problem P10).

Lossless matters: a resistor could also make the line see 50 Ω, but would take the power it was meant to deliver.

The chain, and three ways to fill the box. The antenna's 12.5 Ω is the running example's assumed value, not a property of any particular antenna.

Why match at the antenna end. A matching network next to the load leaves the whole line with $\Gamma = 0$: one travelling wave, VSWR 1, $|V| = |V_+|$ everywhere. A match at the transmitter end would also deliver all the power (a lossless line passes it), but the line would carry a standing wave, with voltage peaks $1 + |\Gamma|$ times higher — in the example 1.6 times — which is what the line's insulation must then withstand. That follows from section 2; nothing is claimed here about particular cables' ratings.

The L-network. For a resistive load $R_L < Z_0$, a series reactance $X$ next to the load and a shunt susceptance $B$ across the line side, with $X = \pm R_L\sqrt{Z_0/R_L - 1}$ and $B = \pm\sqrt{Z_0/R_L - 1}/Z_0$ (same signs). For the example: $X = \pm21.65\ \Omega$, $B = \pm34.6$ mS; with an inductor in series and a capacitor across, $L = 34.5$ nH and $C = 55.1$ pF at 100 MHz. Problem P10 derives this and compares its band with the others.

What a real antenna presents. A real antenna's impedance depends on frequency, often strongly, and has a reactive part away from resonance. Branch 16 computes one case. This unit treats the antenna's impedance as a given, fixed number, which is also what the bandwidth computation two slides on assumes, and says so there.

09 · the real system

Where the reflected power goes

The reflected wave travels back to the transmitter. If the transmitter's source impedance is matched to the line ($\Gamma_s = 0$), none of it is re-reflected: the transmitter absorbs it.

In the Thévenin model ($V_{\mathrm{th}}$ behind $R_s = 50\ \Omega$, 100 W available), with $\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}$ at the line's input, the source resistor takes

$$P_{R_s} = \frac{|V_+|^2}{Z_0}\,|1 - \Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}|^2 ,$$

for the example $100\ \mathrm W\times(1.36 + 1.2\cos2\beta d)$ with $d$ the line's length: 16 W to 256 W depending on the line's length, 136 W on average — the matched case's 100 W plus the reflected 36 W.

And the transmitter sees not 50 Ω but $Z_{\mathrm{in}}$, anywhere from 12.5 Ω to 200 Ω: a load other than the one it was designed for.

The Thévenin model's bookkeeping. The load's 64 W does not depend on $d$; the source resistor's share does.

Full proof of the formula

At the line's input, $I = (V_+'/Z_0)(1 - \Gamma_{\mathrm{in}})$, where $V_+'$ is the forward wave there; with a matched source, $|V_+'| = |V_{\mathrm{th}}|/2$, so $|V_+'|^2/Z_0$ is the 100 W available. The resistor takes $|I|^2R_s = |I|^2Z_0 = (|V_+'|^2/Z_0)|1 - \Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}|^2$. With $\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}} = -0.6e^{-2i\beta d}$, $|1 - \Gamma_{\mathrm{in}}|^2 = 1 + 0.36 - 2\operatorname{Re}\Gamma_{\mathrm{in}} = 1.36 + 1.2\cos2\beta d$, where $d$ is the line's length. It is largest, 2.56, when the load is a whole number of half wavelengths away (the transmitter then sees 12.5 Ω directly: $|I| = 141.4/62.5 = 2.263$ A and $2.263^2\times50 = 256$ W ✓), and smallest, 0.16, an odd number of quarter wavelengths away, where it sees 200 Ω. Its mean over a half wavelength of $d$ is $1.36$, i.e. $1 + |\Gamma|^2$.

Energy check. The EMF supplies $\operatorname{Re}(V_{\mathrm{th}}I^*) = 2(|V_+'|^2/Z_0)\operatorname{Re}(1 - \Gamma_{\mathrm{in}})$, and $2(1 - \operatorname{Re}\Gamma) - |1 - \Gamma|^2 = 1 - |\Gamma|^2 = 0.64$: the EMF's power minus the resistor's is the load's 64 W, at every $d$ ✓.

What the model does and does not say. In wave terms the statement is exact: 36 W travels back into the transmitter and, since $\Gamma_s = 0$, does not come out again. How that power is split inside a real transmitter depends on its output stage, which is not a resistor in series with an EMF (unit 04 made the same caution about the Thévenin resistance). What follows from the physics alone is the second paragraph: the transmitter is loaded by $Z_{\mathrm{in}}$, not by $Z_0$. No figures about real transmitters, and nothing about how they protect themselves, are claimed here.

A source not matched to the line re-reflects part of the returning wave, the line rings up to a different steady state, and the load's share then depends on $d$ too — problem P11.

09 · the band

A match is exact at one frequency

Lengths are fixed in metres, so electrical lengths scale with $f$. The quarter-wave section, length $\theta = \tfrac\pi2f/f_0$, gives exactly

$$\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0 + 2i\sqrt{Z_0Z_L}\tan\theta}$$ $$= \frac{-0.6}{1 + 0.8i\tan\theta} .$$

VSWR ≤ 1.5 means $|\Gamma| \le 0.2$: $\tan^2\theta \ge 12.5$, so 0.825 ≤ f/f₀ ≤ 1.175: 35%.

The stubs' $|\Gamma(f)|$, from the same formulas evaluated at each $f$: 0.948–1.037 (8.9%) for the first, 0.965–1.037 (7.3%) for the second.

This is unit 06's warning (The conjugate-matching theorem, in its notes): a reactance cancels another at one frequency only, because by Foster's theorem both rise with $\omega$. Matching over a band is a harder problem; this is where it begins.

Assumes the load stays 12.5 Ω across the band.

$|\Gamma|$ seen from the 50 Ω line. The quarter-wave section uses a quarter wavelength of line; the stubs about half a wavelength each.

Full proof of the quarter-wave formula

$Z_{\mathrm{in}} = Z_q(Z_L + iZ_qt)/(Z_q + iZ_Lt)$ with $t = \tan\theta$ and $Z_q^2 = Z_0Z_L$. Then $Z_{\mathrm{in}} - Z_0 = [Z_qZ_L + iZ_q^2t - Z_0Z_q - iZ_0Z_Lt]/(Z_q + iZ_Lt) = Z_q(Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_q + iZ_Lt)$, since $Z_q^2 = Z_0Z_L$ cancels the $t$ terms; and $Z_{\mathrm{in}} + Z_0 = [Z_q(Z_L + Z_0) + 2iZ_0Z_Lt]/(Z_q + iZ_Lt)$. Dividing, and dividing top and bottom by $Z_q$: $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0 + 2iZ_qt)$ ✓. For the example, divide by 62.5: $-0.6/(1 + 0.8it)$.

The band. $|\Gamma|^2 = 0.36/(1 + 0.64t^2) \le 0.04 \iff t^2 \ge 12.5 \iff |t| \ge 3.536$, i.e. $\theta \in [1.2952, \pi - 1.2952]$, so $f/f_0 = 2\theta/\pi \in [0.8245, 1.1755]$, a fractional bandwidth of $0.3510$. Pozar ch. 5 gives the general formula for the quarter-wave transformer's bandwidth.

The threshold. VSWR 1.5 is this deck's choice for comparing the matches (notes of The standing wave, and its ratio), not a standard's requirement; another threshold moves every band edge.

The stubs. At frequency $f$, $y(d) = (4 + it)/(1 + 4it)$ with $t = \tan(2\pi(d/\lambda_0)f/f_0)$, plus $-i\cot(2\pi(\ell/\lambda_0)f/f_0)$, then $\Gamma = (1 - y)/(1 + y)$. This is an explicit formula; the band edges, where it reaches 0.2, were found by evaluating it on a fine grid of $f$ (they do not depend on how). Results: 0.9479–1.0368 (0.0889) and 0.9649–1.0375 (0.0725). The L-network of problem P10: 0.853–1.128 (0.275).

Why the quarter-wave section is widest, in these three cases: the less line a match uses, the more slowly its phases change with frequency. The quarter-wave section uses $\lambda/4$; the stubs $0.074 + 0.406 = 0.480\lambda$ and $0.426 + 0.094 = 0.520\lambda$, in the order of their bandwidths. This is an observation about these three matches, not a theorem — and it says nothing about lumped matches: the L-network uses no line at all and has 27.5%, narrower than the quarter-wave section's 35%. How well any lossless network can match a given load over a band is a question with a theory of its own, beyond this unit.
