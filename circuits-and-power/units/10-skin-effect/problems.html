The skin effect: why alternating current avoids the middle of the wire

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You derive the skin depth from Maxwell's equations and Ohm's law, get 9.2 mm for copper at 50 Hz and 0.21 mm at 100 kHz, and explain from those two numbers why a substation busbar can be a tube and a wireless charger's coil is wound from many insulated strands.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about three and a half hours. P1, P2, P9 and P11 are the checkpoint's. Conventions as in the deck: RMS phasors with \(e^{+i\omega t}\), so a time average of a product is \(\mathrm{Re}(AB^*)\) with no ½; a good conductor obeys \(\vec J = \sigma\vec E\) with no displacement current; \(\delta = \sqrt{2/\omega\mu\sigma}\); \(\gamma = (1+i)/\delta\) for a flat face and \(k = (1-i)/\delta\) in a round wire; \(R_s = 1/\sigma\delta\).

Constants: copper \(\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\)m and aluminium \(2.65\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\)m (CRC); \(\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\) H/m, \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\) F/m. Unit 07's steel, assumed: \(\rho = 0.5\ \mu\Omega\,\)m, \(\mu_r = 1000\). Where a problem quotes a value of \(J_0\), \(J_1\), \(Y_0\), \(Y_1\) at a complex argument, or a ratio computed from them, it was evaluated numerically and is given; no solution depends on evaluating one by hand.

The diffusion equation and the skin

P1 The diffusion equation and the skin depth medium

exercises: the checkpoint's derivation — Maxwell plus Ohm, the neglected term estimated, the half-space solved, the two numbers

  1. Inside a uniform conductor of conductivity \(\sigma\) and permeability \(\mu\), estimate the ratio of the displacement current to the conduction current for a field varying as \(e^{i\omega t}\), and evaluate it for copper at 50 Hz.
  2. Dropping the displacement current, show that \(\vec B\) obeys \(\partial_t\vec B = (\mu\sigma)^{-1}\nabla^2\vec B\). Which of Maxwell's equations did you use?
  3. A conductor fills \(z \gt 0\); at its face \(\vec H = H_0\hat y\) (RMS phasor). Find \(H(z)\), saying why one solution is rejected, and identify \(\delta\).
  4. Evaluate \(\delta\) for copper at 50 Hz and at 100 kHz.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a ratio of two terms of one equation. Part 2 is a curl of a curl. Part 3 is a second-order linear ODE with constant coefficients; part 4 is arithmetic.

Rung 2

Ampère–Maxwell in phasors: \(\nabla\times\vec H = \sigma\vec E + i\omega\varepsilon_0\vec E\). For part 2 you need \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec F) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec F) - \nabla^2\vec F\), Faraday, and \(\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0\). For part 3, \((1+i)^2 = 2i\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec B) = \mu\sigma\,\nabla\times\vec E = -\mu\sigma\,\partial_t\vec B\).

Worked solution

Idea. In a metal the conduction current outweighs the displacement current by a factor of order \(10^{17}\); without it, Ampère and Faraday combine into a heat equation for the field, and a heat equation driven at one frequency has one length scale.

1. \(|i\omega\varepsilon_0\vec E|/|\sigma\vec E| = \omega\varepsilon_0/\sigma = \omega\varepsilon_0\rho\). Copper, 50 Hz: \(314.16\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times1.68\times10^{-8} = 4.67\times10^{-17}\).

2. Without it, \(\nabla\times\vec B = \mu\sigma\vec E\) (Ampère, with \(\vec B = \mu\vec H\) and Ohm). Curl: \(\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec B) = \mu\sigma\nabla\times\vec E\). Faraday, \(\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t\vec B\), turns the right side into \(-\mu\sigma\partial_t\vec B\). The identity with \(\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0\) makes the left side \(-\nabla^2\vec B\). So \(\partial_t\vec B = (\mu\sigma)^{-1}\nabla^2\vec B\). Used: Ampère (without displacement current), Faraday, no monopoles; plus Ohm's law. \(\square\)

3. In phasors, \(\nabla^2\vec H = i\omega\mu\sigma\vec H\); with \(\vec H = H(z)\hat y\) this is \(H'' = i\omega\mu\sigma H\). Write \(i\omega\mu\sigma = 2i/\delta^2\), defining \(\delta = \sqrt{2/\omega\mu\sigma}\). Trying \(e^{-\gamma z}\): \(\gamma^2 = 2i/\delta^2\), so \(\gamma = \pm(1+i)/\delta\). The root \(-(1+i)/\delta\) gives \(|H| \propto e^{+z/\delta}\), growing without bound into the metal; a field driven from the face cannot grow away from it, so it is rejected. Then \(H(z) = H_0e^{-(1+i)z/\delta}\): amplitude falling as \(e^{-z/\delta}\), phase lagging by \(z/\delta\).

4. \(\delta = \sqrt{\rho/\pi f\mu_0}\). At 50 Hz: \(1.68\times10^{-8}/(\pi\times50\times1.2566\times10^{-6}) = 8.51\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2}\), \(\delta = 9.23\) mm. At 100 kHz: \(9.23\times\sqrt{50/10^5} = 0.206\) mm.

Check. Units: \(\omega\mu\sigma\) is \(\mathrm{s^{-1}\cdot H\,m^{-1}\cdot S\,m^{-1}}\), and \(\mathrm{H\cdot S} = \mathrm s\), so \(\mathrm{m^{-2}}\); \(\delta\) in m ✓. Limits: \(\sigma\to0\) gives \(\delta\to\infty\) (an insulator does not shield) ✓; \(\omega\to0\) gives \(\delta\to\infty\) (DC fills the conductor, unit 00) ✓. Scaling: 2000 times the frequency, \(\sqrt{2000} = 44.7\) times thinner ✓.

P2 Skin depths, from copper to seawater routine

exercises: \(\delta = \sqrt{\rho/\pi f\mu}\) across materials and frequencies, the good-conductor condition, reading \(\delta\) as attenuation

  1. Tabulate \(\delta\) for copper and aluminium at 50 Hz, 1 kHz, 100 kHz, 1 MHz and 2.4 GHz.
  2. Find \(\delta\) for unit 07's steel at 50 Hz, and say which of its two properties makes the difference from copper.
  3. Take seawater to have \(\sigma = 4\) S/m and a relative permittivity below 100 (both assumed for the problem). Check that it is a good conductor up to 1 MHz, and find \(\delta\) at 100 Hz, 1 kHz and 10 kHz.
  4. By what factor does the field's amplitude fall over 100 m of seawater at each of those frequencies? State only what follows from \(\delta\) about reaching a receiver under the sea by radio.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

One formula, many numbers. Do one carefully and scale the rest: \(\delta\propto\sqrt{\rho/f\mu}\).

Rung 2

Copper at 50 Hz is 9.23 mm (P1). Aluminium is \(\sqrt{2.65/1.68}\) times thicker at every frequency. For the good-conductor test the ratio is \(\omega\varepsilon/\sigma\) with \(\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0\). Amplitude over a distance \(L\): \(e^{-L/\delta}\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: seawater's \(\rho = 0.25\ \Omega\,\)m, so at 100 Hz \(\delta^2 = 0.25/(\pi\times100\times1.2566\times10^{-6})\).

Worked solution

Idea. \(\delta\) depends on \(\rho/f\mu\) only, as a square root; everything is scaling from one computed value.

1. Copper: 9.23 mm, 2.06 mm, 0.206 mm, 65.2 μm, 1.33 μm. Aluminium (×1.256): 11.6 mm, 2.59 mm, 0.259 mm, 81.9 μm, 1.67 μm. Copper's good-conductor ratio at 2.4 GHz is \(2.2\times10^{-9}\), aluminium's \(3.5\times10^{-9}\): every entry is within the model.

2. \(\delta = \sqrt{0.5\times10^{-6}/(\pi\times50\times1000\times1.2566\times10^{-6})} = \sqrt{2.533\times10^{-6}} = 1.59\) mm. Its resistivity is 30 times copper's, which alone would make \(\delta\) \(\sqrt{30} = 5.5\) times larger; its \(\mu_r = 1000\) makes it \(\sqrt{1000} = 31.6\) times smaller. The permeability wins: 5.8 times thinner than copper's.

3. \(\omega\varepsilon/\sigma \le 2\pi\times10^6\times100\times8.854\times10^{-12}/4 = 1.4\times10^{-3}\) at 1 MHz, smaller below: a good conductor ✓. With \(\rho = 0.25\ \Omega\,\)m: \(\delta = 25.2\) m at 100 Hz, 7.96 m at 1 kHz, 2.52 m at 10 kHz.

4. \(100/\delta\) = 3.97, 12.6, 39.7, so \(e^{-100/\delta}\) = 0.019, \(3.5\times10^{-6}\), \(5.5\times10^{-18}\) — in decibels (8.69 dB per \(\delta\)) 35, 109 and 345 dB. What follows: only the lowest frequencies penetrate tens of metres of seawater at all, and each tenfold rise in frequency multiplies the attenuation in decibels by \(\sqrt{10}\). That is a statement about the field's penetration only; how such a link is actually built is not this unit's.

Check. Units: \(\Omega\,\mathrm m/(\mathrm{s^{-1}\cdot H\,m^{-1}}) = \mathrm{m^2}\) ✓. Ratios: copper 50 Hz to 100 kHz is \(\sqrt{2000} = 44.7\): \(9.23/0.206 = 44.8\) ✓. Seawater's conductivity is about \(1.5\times10^{7}\) times smaller than copper's, so its \(\delta\) is \(\sqrt{1.5\times10^7} \approx 3900\) times larger at the same frequency: 9.23 mm at 50 Hz becomes 36 m at 50 Hz, consistent with 25.2 m at 100 Hz ✓.

P3 The surface-layer theorem medium

exercises: integrating a complex profile, Ampère for the total current, \(R_s = 1/\sigma\delta\) exactly, and the surface reactance

For the half-space of P1, with \(J_x(z) = \gamma H_0e^{-\gamma z}\):

  1. Show that the total current per unit width is \(K = H_0\), both by integrating \(J_x\) and by Ampère's law.
  2. Show that the mean power dissipated per unit area is exactly \(|K|^2/\sigma\delta\), and that this is what the same current would dissipate spread uniformly through a layer \(\delta\) thick.
  3. Show that \(|\int J_x\,\mathrm dz| \lt \int|J_x|\,\mathrm dz\) and say what the difference means.
  4. Evaluate \(R_s\) for copper at 50 Hz and at 100 kHz.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two integrals of exponentials, one complex and one of a modulus squared. The rest is comparison.

Rung 2

\(|\gamma|^2 = 2/\delta^2\); \(|e^{-\gamma z}| = e^{-z/\delta}\); with RMS phasors the mean power per unit volume is \(|J|^2/\sigma\). For Ampère, take a rectangle with one side outside the face and one deep inside.

Rung 3

First line: \(\int_0^\infty\gamma H_0e^{-\gamma z}\,\mathrm dz = H_0\big[-e^{-\gamma z}\big]_0^\infty = H_0\), since \(\mathrm{Re}\,\gamma \gt 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. The current is fixed by the face field alone (Ampère), and the dissipation integral happens to equal that of a uniform layer exactly — not approximately — because \(\int e^{-2z/\delta} = \delta/2\) and \(|\gamma|^2 = 2/\delta^2\) cancel the 2s.

1. Integration as in rung 3. Ampère: a rectangle in the \(y\)–\(z\) plane, with long sides of length \(\ell\) along \(y\), one just outside the face (field \(H_0\hat y\)) and one deep inside (field 0), is threaded by the current \(K\ell\) flowing along \(x\); the circulation is \(H_0\ell\). So \(K = H_0\).

2. \(p = \int_0^\infty\frac{|J_x|^2}{\sigma}\mathrm dz = \frac{|\gamma|^2|H_0|^2}{\sigma}\int_0^\infty e^{-2z/\delta}\mathrm dz = \frac{2|H_0|^2}{\sigma\delta^2}\cdot\frac\delta2 = \frac{|K|^2}{\sigma\delta}\). Uniform layer: density \(K/\delta\), dissipation \(\frac{|K|^2}{\delta^2\sigma}\cdot\delta = \frac{|K|^2}{\sigma\delta}\). Equal. \(\square\)

3. \(\int_0^\infty|J_x|\,\mathrm dz = |\gamma||H_0|\delta = \sqrt2|H_0|\), against \(|K| = |H_0|\). The ratio \(\sqrt2\) is the cancellation between layers of different phase: deep layers, turned by up to \(180^\circ\) and more, partly carry current against the shallow ones. That is why "the same total \(|J|\)" is the wrong comparison and "the same current, the same heat" is the right one.

4. \(R_s = \rho/\delta\): \(1.68\times10^{-8}/9.226\times10^{-3} = 1.82\ \mu\Omega\) per square at 50 Hz; \(1.68\times10^{-8}/2.063\times10^{-4} = 81.4\ \mu\Omega\) at 100 kHz.

Check. Units: \(\Omega\,\mathrm m/\mathrm m = \Omega\) (per square) ✓. Limit \(\omega\to0\): \(R_s\to0\) for a half-space, correct since an infinitely thick conductor has no DC resistance per square ✓; for a finite conductor the low-frequency limit is \(\rho/t\) (P10). Ratio of the two values: \(\sqrt{2000} = 44.7\) ✓.

P4 Poynting into the skin medium

exercises: the complex Poynting vector, the energy balance of a conductor, the stored magnetic energy as the imaginary part, the flat face against the exact wire

  1. For the half-space, find \(E_x(0)\) and show that \(\mathrm{Re}\,(E_xH_y^*)\) at the face equals the power dissipated per unit area (P3).
  2. Show that \(\mathrm{Im}\,(E_xH_y^*)\) at the face equals \(2\omega\langle W_m\rangle\), where \(\langle W_m\rangle\) is the mean magnetic energy stored per unit area in the metal. Hence the surface impedance is \((1+i)R_s\).
  3. The 100 mm copper bar of the deck carries 3000 A (RMS, 50 Hz, assumed). Treating its surface as flat, find the field at the surface, the power entering per square metre and per metre of bar, and compare with the exact \(I^2R_{\mathrm{ac}}\), \(R_{\mathrm{ac}} = 6.37\ \mu\Omega/\)m.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything is at the face: one field, one product, two integrals already done in P3. Part 3 needs Ampère round the bar.

Rung 2

\(E_x = J_x/\sigma\) and \(J_x(0) = \gamma H_0\). With RMS phasors, \(\langle W_m\rangle = \int\tfrac12\mu|H|^2\,\mathrm dz\). Use \(\omega\mu\sigma\delta^2 = 2\). Round the bar, \(H_0 = I/2\pi a\).

Rung 3

First line: \(E_xH_y^* = \frac{\gamma}{\sigma}|H_0|^2 = \frac{(1+i)|H_0|^2}{\sigma\delta}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Unit 01's statement — what a conductor dissipates enters through its surface — holds here with the energy used up in a skin, and the reactive part measures the field energy stored there.

1. \(E_x(0) = \gamma H_0/\sigma = (1+i)H_0/\sigma\delta\). The flux into the metal is along \(\hat x\times\hat y = \hat z\) ✓, and \(\mathrm{Re}\,(E_xH_y^*) = |H_0|^2/\sigma\delta\), which is P3's \(p\) ✓.

2. \(\langle W_m\rangle = \tfrac12\mu|H_0|^2\int_0^\infty e^{-2z/\delta}\mathrm dz = \mu|H_0|^2\delta/4\), so \(2\omega\langle W_m\rangle = \tfrac12\omega\mu\delta|H_0|^2\). Since \(\omega\mu\sigma\delta^2 = 2\), \(\tfrac12\omega\mu\delta = 1/\sigma\delta\): equal to the imaginary part ✓. So \(Z_s = E_x(0)/H_0 = (1+i)/\sigma\delta = (1+i)R_s\).

3. \(H_0 = 3000/(2\pi\times0.05) = 9549\) A/m. Per square metre: \(R_s|H_0|^2 = 1.821\times10^{-6}\times9549^2 = 166\ \mathrm{W/m^2}\). Per metre of bar (perimeter 0.314 m): 52.2 W/m, i.e. a resistance \(R_s/2\pi a = 5.80\ \mu\Omega/\)m. Exact: \(3000^2\times6.37\times10^{-6} = 57.3\) W/m. The flat estimate is 9% low: it is the \(a/2\delta\) term of the deck's \(a/2\delta + \tfrac14\), and the missing \(\tfrac14\) — the curvature — is worth \(R_{\mathrm{dc}}/4 = 0.53\ \mu\Omega/\)m, which brings it to 6.33, within 0.6% of the exact 6.37.

Check. Units: \(\Omega\cdot(\mathrm{A/m})^2 = \mathrm{W/m^2}\) ✓. Energy: real part in = heat ✓; imaginary part = \(2\omega\langle W_m\rangle\), energy stored in the skin and returned each cycle (what unit 06 calls reactive power, here with no electric energy to speak of) ✓. Numbers: \(\langle W_m\rangle = 0.264\ \mathrm{J/m^2}\), and \(2\times314.16\times0.264 = 166\) ✓.

Round wires

P5 The round wire's two limits medium

exercises: Bessel series of complex argument, the internal impedance's low-frequency limit (units 00 and 02), a cited asymptotic expansion used carefully

A round wire of radius \(a\) has internal impedance per metre \(Z_i = \dfrac{k\rho}{2\pi a}\dfrac{J_0(ka)}{J_1(ka)}\), \(k = (1-i)/\delta\).

  1. Using \(J_0(x) = 1 - \tfrac{x^2}4 + \tfrac{x^4}{64} - \cdots\) and \(J_1(x) = \tfrac x2 - \tfrac{x^3}{16} + \tfrac{x^5}{384} - \cdots\) (MR 09), show that for \(a \ll \delta\) \(Z_i \approx \frac{\rho}{\pi a^2}\big(1 + \frac{a^4}{48\delta^4}\big) + i\omega\frac{\mu_0}{8\pi}\), and name the unit each leading term comes from.
  2. Take as given (DLMF 10.17.3) that for \(|z|\) large with \(|\mathrm{ph}\,z| \lt \pi\), \(J_\nu(z) \approx \sqrt{2/\pi z}\,\big[\cos\omega_\nu - \tfrac{4\nu^2 - 1}{8z}\sin\omega_\nu\big]\), \(\omega_\nu = z - \tfrac{\nu\pi}2 - \tfrac\pi4\). Show that for \(a \gg \delta\), \(R_{\mathrm{ac}}/R_{\mathrm{dc}} \to a/2\delta + \tfrac14\).
  3. Show that the internal inductance at high frequency is \(R_s/(2\pi a\omega)\) and compare it with unit 02's \(\mu_0/8\pi\) for a 1 mm-radius copper wire at 1 MHz.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Both parts are expansions of the one ratio \(J_0/J_1\): in powers of \(x\) for part 1, in powers of \(1/z\) for part 2.

Rung 2

Part 1: divide the series with \(\frac1{1-u} = 1 + u + u^2 + \cdots\); then \(k^2 = -2i/\delta^2\), \(k^4 = -4/\delta^4\). Part 2: \(z = ka\) has \(\mathrm{Im}\,z = -a/\delta \to -\infty\), so \(\cos\omega \approx \tfrac12e^{i\omega}\) and \(\sin\omega \approx \tfrac1{2i}e^{i\omega}\), and \(\omega_0 - \omega_1 = \pi/2\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(J_\nu(z) \approx \sqrt{2/\pi z}\;\tfrac12e^{i\omega_\nu}\Big(1 + i\,\tfrac{4\nu^2 - 1}{8z}\Big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. At low frequency the Bessel ratio is \(2/x\) plus small corrections, which return the DC resistance and the uniform-current internal inductance; at high frequency it tends to \(i\), which returns section 2's flat skin wrapped round the perimeter, plus a curvature term.

1. \(\frac{J_0}{J_1} = \frac2x\cdot\frac{1 - x^2/4 + x^4/64}{1 - x^2/8 + x^4/192}\). With \(u = x^2/8 - x^4/192\), \(\frac1{1-u} = 1 + \frac{x^2}8 + \frac{x^4}{96} + O(x^6)\); times the numerator, \(1 - \frac{x^2}8 - \frac{x^4}{192}\). Then \(Z_i = \frac{\rho}{\pi a^2}\big(1 - \frac{k^2a^2}8 - \frac{k^4a^4}{192}\big) = \frac{\rho}{\pi a^2}\big(1 + \frac{ia^2}{4\delta^2} + \frac{a^4}{48\delta^4}\big)\). The imaginary part: \(\frac{\rho}{4\pi\delta^2} = \frac{\rho\,\omega\mu_0\sigma}{8\pi} = \frac{\omega\mu_0}{8\pi}\). So \(R = \frac{\rho}{\pi a^2}\) — unit 00's \(R = \rho L/A\) per metre — and \(L_{\text{int}} = \frac{\mu_0}{8\pi}\) — unit 02's internal inductance, independent of \(a\).

2. With rung 3, for \(\nu = 0\) the factor is \(1 - \frac{i}{8z}\), for \(\nu = 1\) it is \(1 + \frac{3i}{8z}\), and \(e^{i\omega_0}/e^{i\omega_1} = e^{i\pi/2} = i\). So \(\frac{J_0}{J_1} \approx i\big(1 - \frac i{8z}\big)\big(1 - \frac{3i}{8z}\big) = i\big(1 - \frac{i}{2z}\big) + O(z^{-2}) = i + \frac1{2z}\). Then \(Z_i \approx \frac{k\rho}{2\pi a}\big(i + \frac1{2ka}\big) = \frac{(1+i)\rho}{2\pi a\delta} + \frac{\rho}{4\pi a^2}\), using \(ik = (1+i)/\delta\). Dividing the real part by \(\rho/\pi a^2\): \(\frac a{2\delta} + \frac14\). \(\square\) (The terms dropped are of relative size \(e^{-2a/\delta}\) and \((\delta/a)^2\).)

3. The imaginary part is \(\frac{\rho}{2\pi a\delta} = \frac{R_s}{2\pi a}\), so \(L_{\text{int}} = \frac{R_s}{2\pi a\omega}\). Copper, \(a = 1\) mm, 1 MHz: \(\delta = 65.2\ \mu\)m, \(R_s = 2.58\times10^{-4}\ \Omega\), \(L_{\text{int}} = 2.58\times10^{-4}/(2\pi\times10^{-3}\times6.283\times10^6) = 6.5\ \mathrm{nH/m}\), against 50 nH/m at low frequency: unit 02's "this part shrinks".

Check. Units: \(\omega\mu_0/8\pi\) in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}H\,m^{-1}} = \Omega/\mathrm m\) ✓; \(a/\delta\) dimensionless ✓. The two limits meet sensibly: at \(a = \delta\) the low-frequency form gives 1.0208 against the exact 1.0205, while the high-frequency form gives 0.75, meaningless there. At \(a = 15.3\delta\) both the exact and the asymptotic give 7.92 (P6).

P6 A thick wire at radio frequencies routine

exercises: the high-frequency formula as a number, \(R \propto \sqrt f\), comparison with an exact value

A copper wire of radius 1 mm.

  1. Find its DC resistance per metre.
  2. Using \(R_{\mathrm{ac}}/R_{\mathrm{dc}} \approx a/2\delta + \tfrac14\), find the ratio and \(R_{\mathrm{ac}}\) at 100 kHz and at 1 MHz. The exact Bessel values are 2.69 and 7.92: how large is the error of the formula at each?
  3. By what factor does \(R_{\mathrm{ac}}\) rise from 100 kHz to 1 MHz? Why is it not \(\sqrt{10}\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three numbers from two formulas, then a ratio.

Rung 2

\(R_{\mathrm{dc}} = \rho/\pi a^2\). \(\delta\) at 100 kHz is 0.206 mm (P1); at 1 MHz, \(\sqrt{10}\) times smaller.

Rung 3

First line: \(a/2\delta = 1/(2\times0.2063) = 2.424\) at 100 kHz.

Worked solution

Idea. Many skin depths thick, the wire is a skin round its perimeter plus a small curvature correction.

1. \(R_{\mathrm{dc}} = 1.68\times10^{-8}/(\pi\times10^{-6}) = 5.35\ \mathrm{m\Omega/m}\).

2. 100 kHz: \(2.424 + 0.25 = 2.674\), \(R_{\mathrm{ac}} = 14.3\ \mathrm{m\Omega/m}\); exact 2.69 (14.4 mΩ/m), error 0.7%. 1 MHz: \(\delta = 65.2\ \mu\)m, \(1/(2\times0.06523) + 0.25 = 7.915\), \(R_{\mathrm{ac}} = 42.3\ \mathrm{m\Omega/m}\); exact 7.92 (42.4 mΩ/m), error 0.08%.

3. \(7.92/2.69 = 2.94\), not \(\sqrt{10} = 3.16\): the \(\tfrac14\) does not scale. The \(a/2\delta\) parts alone are in the ratio \(7.665/2.424 = 3.16\) ✓.

Check. Units ✓ (Ω/m throughout). The error shrinks as \(a/\delta\) grows (4.85 then 15.3), as an expansion in \(\delta/a\) should ✓. This \(\sqrt f\) rise is the \(R'\) unit 08 takes as given for its lines at radio frequencies.

P7 Unit 00's kettle cable was right routine

exercises: the low-frequency formula as a number, a design frequency from a tolerance

  1. Unit 00's kettle cable has 2.5 mm² copper conductors. Find \(a\), \(a/\delta\) at 50 Hz, and \(R_{\mathrm{ac}}/R_{\mathrm{dc}} - 1\).
  2. At what frequency would the same conductor's AC resistance be 1% above its DC resistance, by the low-frequency formula? (The exact value there is 1.0099.)
  3. Unit 00 said that at 50 Hz the copper cannot tell the difference from DC. Which two different statements does this problem and unit 00 together establish?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is three short calculations; part 2 inverts the formula; part 3 is about local and global.

Rung 2

\(R_{\mathrm{ac}}/R_{\mathrm{dc}} \approx 1 + (a/\delta)^4/48\). For part 2 solve \((a/\delta)^4 = 0.48\) for \(\delta\), then \(f = \rho/(\pi\mu_0\delta^2)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(a = \sqrt{2.5\times10^{-6}/\pi} = 0.892\) mm.

Worked solution

Idea. A conductor thin against \(\delta\) is filled by the current; the correction starts at the fourth power of \(a/\delta\).

1. \(a = 0.892\) mm; \(a/\delta = 0.892/9.23 = 0.0967\); \(0.0967^4/48 = 8.74\times10^{-5}/48 = 1.8\times10^{-6}\).

2. \(a/\delta = 0.48^{1/4} = 0.832\), so \(\delta = 1.072\) mm and \(f = 1.68\times10^{-8}/(\pi\times1.2566\times10^{-6}\times1.149\times10^{-6}) = 3.7\) kHz.

3. Locally, unit 00's \(\sigma(\omega)\) at 50 Hz equals its DC value: the electrons respond as to DC. Globally, the field the current makes inside the wire redistributes the current by under two parts in a million at that size: the current fills the conductor as at DC. The first is about the material, the second about the wire's size against \(\delta\); a 100 mm bar of the same copper satisfies the first and not the second.

Check. At 3.7 kHz, \(\delta = 9.23\times\sqrt{50/3705} = 1.072\) mm ✓. The exact 1.0099 against the formula's 1.0100: the next term of the series is already small at \(a/\delta = 0.83\) ✓. Units: \(\Omega\,\mathrm m/(\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\cdot m^2}) = \Omega/\mathrm H = \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) ✓.

P8 Proximity, qualitatively routine

exercises: the surface current following the tangential field, the field of two parallel currents, the proximity effect's direction

Two long parallel round copper conductors, each many skin depths thick, sit side by side a little apart.

  1. Show that, where the surface is locally flat on the scale of \(\delta\), the current per unit length of perimeter equals the tangential \(H\) just outside.
  2. Sketch the magnetic field when they carry equal and opposite currents (a go-and-return pair), and say where on each conductor the current crowds.
  3. Repeat for equal currents in the same direction (two strands of one winding).
  4. Why does either case raise the AC resistance above that of an isolated conductor?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is Ampère round a small rectangle; parts 2 and 3 are where two circling fields add and where they cancel.

Rung 2

Each conductor's own field circles it. Between two opposite currents the two circling fields point the same way; between two equal currents they point opposite ways. Current follows field (part 1).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: a rectangle straddling the face, one long side \(\ell\) outside with field \(H_t\), the other a few \(\delta\) inside with field 0, encloses current \(K\ell\).

Worked solution

Idea. On a thick conductor the surface current is the tangential field (P3, applied locally), so the current goes where the total field — the conductor's own and its neighbour's — runs strongest along the surface.

1. As in rung 3: Ampère gives \(H_t\ell = K\ell\), the short sides contributing nothing as they shrink. So \(K = H_t\), point by point, wherever the surface curves slowly on the scale of \(\delta\).

2. Opposite currents, one out of the page on the left and one into it on the right: the left one's field circles anticlockwise, the right one's clockwise; in the gap both point the same way (say downward), so they add, and outside they partly cancel. The field lines crowd through the gap and along the facing surfaces. The current crowds onto the facing sides.

3. Equal currents: in the gap the two fields point opposite ways and cancel; outside they add, the pair looking from afar like one conductor. The current is pushed to the far sides.

4. Either way the current occupies less of each conductor's perimeter, i.e. a smaller cross-section of skin; for a given total current the loss \(\int R_s|H_t|^2\) over the perimeter is larger when \(|H_t|\) is uneven (for a fixed \(\oint H_t = I\), \(\oint|H_t|^2\) is least when \(H_t\) is uniform — Cauchy–Schwarz). So the AC resistance rises.

Check. Sources: the CDA's Copper for Busbars, §2.2.2.1.2, states the facing-surfaces result for round bars and tubes with anti-parallel currents; Sullivan (1999), §II, defines the proximity effect as current flowing in undesirable patterns because of nearby conductors' fields. Limit: as the conductors move far apart the neighbour's field fades and each returns to uniform \(H_t\) ✓.

P10 The optimal slab hard

exercises: a two-point boundary-value problem in the complex, \(\coth\) of a complex argument, a minimisation proved, comparison with the tube

A slab of good conductor occupies \(0 \le z \le t\). The field at \(z = 0\) is \(H_0\hat y\); beyond \(z = t\) there is none.

  1. Find \(H(z)\) and show that the impedance of one square of the face is \(Z = R_s(1+i)\coth\big((1+i)x\big)\), \(x = t/\delta\).
  2. Show that \(\mathrm{Re}\,Z/R_s = f(x) = \dfrac{\sinh2x + \sin2x}{\cosh2x - \cos2x}\).
  3. Prove that \(f\) is least at \(x = \pi/2\), with \(f(\pi/2) = \tanh(\pi/2)\), and find \(f(\pi)\). Check both limits \(x\to0\) and \(x\to\infty\).
  4. The deck's tube optimum is a wall of 14.53 mm in copper at 50 Hz. Compare with \(\pi\delta/2\).
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is \(H'' = \gamma^2H\) with a condition at each end; part 2 is complex algebra; part 3 a derivative whose numerator collapses.

Rung 2

The solution vanishing at \(z = t\) is \(\sinh\gamma(t - z)\). The face's \(E_x = -H'(0)/\sigma\) and the current per width is \(H_0\). For \(\coth(u + iv)\), multiply top and bottom by \(\sinh(u - iv)\).

Rung 3

\(2\cosh w\,\sinh\bar w = \sinh(w + \bar w) - \sinh(w - \bar w)\) and \(2\sinh w\,\sinh\bar w = \cosh(w + \bar w) - \cosh(w - \bar w)\). In the derivative, use \(\cosh^2 - \sinh^2 = 1\) and \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\).

Rung 4

First line of part 3: the numerator of \(f'\) is \((2\cosh2x + 2\cos2x)(\cosh2x - \cos2x) - (\sinh2x + \sin2x)(2\sinh2x + 2\sin2x)\).

Worked solution

Idea. A thin slab has too little copper; a thick one carries counter-flowing layers that add loss. Between them there is a best thickness, and it is exactly where the phase at the far face has turned by \(90^\circ\).

1. \(H'' = \gamma^2H\), \(H(0) = H_0\), \(H(t) = 0\): \(H = H_0\sinh\gamma(t - z)/\sinh\gamma t\). Then \(J_x = -H'\), \(J_x(0) = \gamma H_0\coth\gamma t\), \(E_x(0) = (\gamma/\sigma)H_0\coth\gamma t\), and the current per unit width is \(H(0) - H(t) = H_0\). So \(Z = E_x(0)/H_0 = (\gamma/\sigma)\coth\gamma t = R_s(1+i)\coth\big((1+i)x\big)\), since \(\gamma/\sigma = (1+i)R_s\) and \(\gamma t = (1+i)x\).

2. With \(w = x + ix\): \(\coth w = \frac{\cosh w\sinh\bar w}{|\sinh w|^2} = \frac{\sinh2x - \sinh2ix}{\cosh2x - \cosh2ix} = \frac{\sinh2x - i\sin2x}{\cosh2x - \cos2x}\). Times \(1 + i\), the real part is \(\frac{\sinh2x + \sin2x}{\cosh2x - \cos2x}\).

3. The numerator of \(f'\) (rung 4) is \(2[\cosh^22x - \cos^22x - (\sinh2x + \sin2x)^2] = 2[1 - 1 - 2\sinh2x\sin2x] = -4\sinh2x\sin2x\), so \(f'(x) = -4\sinh2x\sin2x/(\cosh2x - \cos2x)^2\). For \(x \gt 0\), \(\sinh2x \gt 0\), so the sign of \(f'\) is that of \(-\sin2x\): negative on \((0, \pi/2)\), positive on \((\pi/2, \pi)\), and so on. Minima at \(x = \pi/2, 3\pi/2, \dots\), maxima at \(\pi, 2\pi, \dots\). At an odd multiple of \(\pi/2\), \(f = \sinh(n\pi)/(\cosh(n\pi) + 1) = \tanh(n\pi/2)\), increasing in \(n\); so the global minimum is \(f(\pi/2) = \tanh(\pi/2) = 0.917\). \(f(\pi) = \sinh2\pi/(\cosh2\pi - 1) = \coth\pi = 1.0037\). \(\square\) Limits: \(x\to\infty\), \(f\to1\) — the half-space ✓; \(x\to0\), \(f\approx\frac{4x}{4x^2} = \frac1x\), so \(R = R_s\delta/t = \rho/t\), the DC sheet ✓.

4. \(\pi\delta/2 = 1.5708\times9.226 = 14.49\) mm against the tube's 14.53 mm (\(1.575\delta\)): the flat result, bent round a 50 mm radius, moves by 0.3%.

Check. Units: \(R_s\) in Ω per square times a pure number ✓. The 8% saving, \(1 - 0.917\), is what made the 14.5 mm tube beat the solid bar in P9 (6.37 to 5.91 is 7%) ✓. Physical reading: at \(x = \pi/2\) the field at the far face of an infinitely thick conductor would lag the face's by \(90^\circ\); beyond that depth the current starts to oppose the surface's.

P12 Unit 07's laminations, revisited medium

exercises: diffusion from two faces, eliminating the face field for a fixed mean flux, the correction factor and its limits

A steel sheet of thickness \(d\) (\(|z| \le d/2\)) carries flux along its length; the winding fixes its mean flux density \(\bar B\) (RMS phasor).

  1. Find \(H(z)\) with the field \(H_s\) at both faces, and express \(\bar B\) in terms of \(H_s\).
  2. Show that the mean eddy loss per unit volume is unit 07's \(\sigma\omega^2\hat B^2d^2/24\) (\(\hat B = \sqrt2|\bar B|\)) times \(F(x) = \frac3x\frac{\sinh x - \sin x}{\cosh x - \cos x}\), \(x = d/\delta\).
  3. Show \(F\to1\) as \(x\to0\) and \(F\approx3/x\) for large \(x\).
  4. For unit 07's steel at 50 Hz (\(\delta = 1.59\) mm), evaluate \(F\) for \(d\) = 0.3, 1, 3, 5 and 10 mm. Unit 07's P5 estimated 60.5 W/kg for a 5 mm plate from the classical formula: correct it.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

The field is even in \(z\); the loss enters through two faces; the mean flux fixes the face field; then series for small \(x\).

Rung 2

\(H = H_s\cosh\gamma z/\cosh(\gamma d/2)\). Loss per unit volume \(p = \frac2d\mathrm{Re}(E_sH_s^*)\) with \(E_s = J(d/2)/\sigma\). Mean: \(\bar B = \frac\mu d\int H\,\mathrm dz\).

Rung 3

You will need \(\mathrm{Re}(\gamma\tanh u)/|\tanh u|^2 = \mathrm{Re}(\bar\gamma\coth u)\), \(u = \gamma d/2\), and P10's formula \(\coth(v + iv) = \frac{\sinh2v - i\sin2v}{\cosh2v - \cos2v}\) with \(2v = x\). And \(\mu\sigma\omega\delta^2 = 2\).

Rung 4

First line: \(\bar B = \frac{2\mu H_s}{\gamma d}\tanh\frac{\gamma d}2\).

Worked solution

Idea. Unit 07 assumed the flux uniform. In fact it diffuses in from both faces; for a thin sheet that changes nothing, and for a thick one it confines the flux and the eddy currents to two skins.

1. \(H'' = \gamma^2H\), even, \(H(\pm d/2) = H_s\): \(H = H_s\cosh\gamma z/\cosh(\gamma d/2)\). \(\bar B = \frac\mu d\int_{-d/2}^{d/2}H\,\mathrm dz = \frac{2\mu H_s}{\gamma d}\tanh u\), \(u = \gamma d/2\).

2. \(E_s = \frac\gamma\sigma H_s\tanh u\), so \(p = \frac{2}{d\sigma}\mathrm{Re}(\gamma\tanh u)|H_s|^2\). With \(|H_s|^2 = \frac{|\bar B|^2|\gamma|^2d^2}{4\mu^2|\tanh u|^2}\): \(p = \frac{d|\gamma|^2|\bar B|^2}{2\mu^2\sigma}\mathrm{Re}(\bar\gamma\coth u)\). Here \(u = (1+i)x/2\), so \(\coth u = \frac{\sinh x - i\sin x}{\cosh x - \cos x}\), and with \(\bar\gamma = (1-i)/\delta\), \(\mathrm{Re}(\bar\gamma\coth u) = \frac1\delta\frac{\sinh x - \sin x}{\cosh x - \cos x}\). With \(|\gamma|^2 = 2/\delta^2\): \(p = \frac{d|\bar B|^2}{\mu^2\sigma\delta^3}\frac{\sinh x - \sin x}{\cosh x - \cos x}\). Divide by unit 07's \(\sigma\omega^2\cdot2|\bar B|^2d^2/24\): \(\frac{12}{\mu^2\sigma^2\omega^2\delta^3d}\cdot(\cdots) = \frac{12\delta^4}{4\delta^3d}(\cdots) = \frac3x(\cdots)\). \(\square\)

3. \(\sinh x - \sin x = 2(\frac{x^3}{3!} + \frac{x^7}{7!} + \cdots)\) and \(\cosh x - \cos x = 2(\frac{x^2}{2!} + \frac{x^6}{6!} + \cdots)\), so the fraction is \(\frac x3(1 + O(x^4))\) and \(F = 1 + O(x^4)\). For large \(x\), \(\sinh\) and \(\cosh\) dominate and the fraction \(\to1\): \(F\approx3/x\).

4. \(x\) = 0.189, 0.628, 1.885, 3.142, 6.283; \(F\) = 1.0000, 0.9998, 0.981, 0.876, 0.479. The 5 mm plate: \(60.5\times0.876 = 53.0\) W/kg.

Check. Units: \(F\) is a pure number ✓. \(x\to0\) is unit 07's thin-sheet formula ✓ — and that is the proof of unit 07's condition \(d \ll \delta\), stated there "proved in branch 10". For large \(x\) the loss per unit volume grows as \(d^2\cdot\delta/d = d\delta\), not as \(d^2\).
