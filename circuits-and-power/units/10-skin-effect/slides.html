The skin effect: why alternating current avoids the middle of the wire

A changing current induces fields inside its own conductor that push it outward. Maxwell's equations and Ohm's law turn this into a diffusion equation, whose one length — 9.2 mm in copper at 50 Hz, 0.21 mm at 100 kHz — decides how busbars, overhead lines and charging coils are built.

needs 05, 00, 07, MR 09, MR 18, MR 19, MR 24~2 h slides + 3½ h problemsproblems: ./cap problems 10

Unit 00 found that copper answers a 50 Hz field exactly as it answers a steady one — locally, through $\sigma$. This branch shows the other half. Globally, an alternating current makes an alternating magnetic field inside its own conductor; that field induces an electric field which opposes the current in the middle and helps it near the surface, so at high enough frequency, or in a large enough conductor, the middle carries almost nothing. Units 01 and 02 named this twice, and unit 07 used its length scale, the skin depth, without proof as the condition for its laminations. This unit derives it.

10 · diffusion

The half unit 00 left out

Unit 00, locally: at every point, $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$, with the DC value of $\sigma$ up to gigahertz (Alternating fields: σ(ω)).

The other half, globally: the current $i(t)$ makes a field $\vec B$ circling inside the wire. When $i$ grows, so does the flux through the loop $\Gamma$ in the figure, and Faraday's law (MR 24) drives an EMF round $\Gamma$:

$$\oint_\Gamma\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = -\frac{\mathrm d\Phi_\Gamma}{\mathrm dt}.$$
what it does

The induced $\vec E$ runs against the current near the axis and with it near the surface. Through $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ it moves current outward: eddy currents in the wire itself, unit 07's mechanism turned on the conductor that makes the field.

How far outward depends on how fast the field can change compared with how fast it can soak in: the rest of the unit.

Long section, schematic. The flux through $\Gamma$ grows with $i$; the EMF it drives round $\Gamma$ opposes the current on the axis and aids it at the surface (Lenz).

The sign, carefully. Take the current along $+\hat z$ (to the right) and the loop $\Gamma$ in the upper half of the section, in the plane of the page. Above the axis $\vec B$ points out of the page. Orient $\Gamma$ anticlockwise as seen from the reader, so its normal is out of the page and the flux $\Phi_\Gamma$ is positive and growing with $i$. Faraday: $\oint_\Gamma\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = -\dot\Phi_\Gamma \lt 0$, so the induced $\vec E$ circulates clockwise: to the right along the top side (near the surface) and to the left along the bottom side (the axis). Added to the field that drives the current, it weakens the drive on the axis and strengthens it at the surface.

Why this is not in unit 00. Unit 00's Laplace problem for the steady current assumed $\nabla\times\vec E = \vec 0$, so that $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi$. With a changing current that is false inside the conductor itself: $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t\vec B \ne \vec 0$. Unit 00's conclusion — that $\sigma$ at 50 Hz is the DC $\sigma$ — survives untouched; what changes is the field $\vec E$ that $\sigma$ multiplies.

Eddy currents. Unit 07 met them in transformer steel, driven by the flux of the windings. Here the flux is the conductor's own. The name is the same because the mechanism is: Faraday's law inside a conductor, then Ohm's law.

Inside a metal, the displacement current is nothing

Ampère–Maxwell (MR 24) inside a conductor of permeability $\mu$, with Ohm's law, in phasors ($e^{+i\omega t}$, unit 05):

$$\nabla\times\vec H = \sigma\vec E + i\omega\varepsilon_0\vec E .$$
the ratio of the two currents $$\frac{|i\omega\varepsilon_0\vec E|}{|\sigma\vec E|} = \frac{\omega\varepsilon_0}{\sigma}$$

Copper: $4.7\times10^{-17}$ at 50 Hz; $2.2\times10^{-9}$ at 2.4 GHz.

Units: $\mathrm{s^{-1}\cdot F\,m^{-1}}/(\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}}) = \mathrm{s^{-1}\,\Omega\,F} = 1$ ✓.

A conductor with $\omega\varepsilon_0/\sigma \ll 1$ is a good conductor. Drop the term:

$$\nabla\times\vec H = \sigma\vec E,\qquad\text{so}\qquad \nabla\cdot\vec E = 0 .$$

Computed, $\omega\varepsilon_0\rho$ with $\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$. The ratio stays below $10^{-5}$ until Drude's $\sigma$ itself stops being constant (unit 00's knee, 6.4 THz).

The numbers. $\varepsilon_0\rho = 8.854\times10^{-12}\times1.68\times10^{-8} = 1.487\times10^{-19}$ s — unit 00's naive charge-relaxation time, $\varepsilon_0/\sigma$, once more. Times $\omega = 314.16$ rad/s: $4.67\times10^{-17}$. Times $\omega = 2\pi\times2.4\times10^9 = 1.508\times10^{10}$ rad/s: $2.24\times10^{-9}$. The same comparison as unit 00's (The naive answer, and why it is meaningless): there, $\varepsilon_0/\sigma$ was compared with a collision time; here with the period of the drive, and the period is enormously longer.

$\sigma$ is still the DC value. Unit 00 (Alternating fields: σ(ω)) showed $|\sigma(\omega)|$ differs from $\sigma_0$ by $7\times10^{-8}$ at 2.4 GHz ($\omega\tau = 3.8\times10^{-4}$). So both approximations of this unit — Ohm's law with the DC $\sigma$, and no displacement current — hold for copper from DC to microwave frequencies. The plot's right edge is where the first of them starts to fail; the second would fail only near $\omega = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$, $1.1\times10^{18}$ Hz, a frequency at which nothing in this model means anything.

Why $\varepsilon_0$ and not $\varepsilon$. Inside a metal the bound charges' polarisation is not separable from the conduction electrons' response; unit 00's model puts all of the electrons' response into $\sigma$. A factor of a few in the permittivity would change nothing here.

$\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$. Take the divergence of $\nabla\times\vec H = \sigma\vec E$: the left side is identically zero, so $\sigma\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ in a uniform conductor. This is unit 00's result that interior charge vanishes (Neutral inside, and still a field), now holding at every instant of the cycle. Surface charge is allowed and is there: it is what unit 00 found steering the field.

Permeability. For copper and aluminium $\mu = \mu_0$, and $\vec H$ is only the notation $\vec B/\mu_0$ that unit 01 introduced. Section 5 uses unit 07's steel, modelled as linear with $\mu = \mu_r\mu_0$ and $\vec B = \mu\vec H$ (unit 07, Iron, in one slide, citing Griffiths ch. 6). That is why the equations here are written with $\vec H$ and $\mu$.

The diffusion equation

the diffusion equation in a good conductor

With $\nabla\times\vec H = \sigma\vec E$, $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t\vec B$, $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$, $\vec B = \mu\vec H$, uniform $\sigma$, $\mu$:

$$\frac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t} = D\,\nabla^2\vec B,\qquad D = \frac{1}{\mu\sigma},$$

and the same for $\vec E$ and $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$.

Units: $(\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\cdot S\,m^{-1}})^{-1}$; $\mathrm{H\cdot S} = \Omega\,\mathrm s\cdot\Omega^{-1} = \mathrm s$, so $D$ is in $\mathrm{m^2/s}$ ✓.

proof

Curl of Ampère: $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec B) = \mu\sigma\nabla\times\vec E = -\mu\sigma\,\partial_t\vec B$. And $\nabla\times\nabla\times\vec B = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec B) - \nabla^2\vec B = -\nabla^2\vec B$. $\square$

$D$ is the magnetic diffusivity. Copper: $D = 1.34\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$.

Computed: a field switched on at a copper face soaks in as $\mathrm{erfc}\big(z/2\sqrt{Dt}\big)$. Four times the time, twice the depth — diffusion, not a wave.

Full proof

Write Ampère without displacement current as $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu\sigma\vec E$ (multiply $\nabla\times\vec H = \sigma\vec E$ by the constant $\mu$). Its curl is $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec B) = \mu\sigma\,\nabla\times\vec E$, and Faraday turns the right side into $-\mu\sigma\,\partial_t\vec B$. The vector identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec F) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec F) - \nabla^2\vec F$ (the one MR 24 uses for the vacuum wave equation) with $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ gives $\nabla^2\vec B = \mu\sigma\,\partial_t\vec B$. For $\vec E$: curl of Faraday, $\nabla\times\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t\nabla\times\vec B = -\mu\sigma\,\partial_t\vec E$, and $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ (previous slide) gives the same equation. $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ follows, $\sigma$ being constant.

The copper number. $D = \rho/\mu_0 = 1.68\times10^{-8}/(4\pi\times10^{-7}) = 1.337\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$. For comparison it is the same form as a thermal diffusivity: MR 18's $u_t = ku_{xx}$ with $k$ in $\mathrm{m^2/s}$.

The figure. A copper block filling $z \gt 0$, field zero inside until $t = 0$, then held at $B_{\text{face}}$ on the face. The solution is $B = B_{\text{face}}\,\mathrm{erfc}\big(z/2\sqrt{Dt}\big)$, $\mathrm{erfc}(\eta) = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_\eta^\infty e^{-s^2}\mathrm ds$. Check: with $\eta = z/2\sqrt{Dt}$, $\partial_tB = B_{\text{face}}\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-\eta^2}\frac{\eta}{2t}$ and $\partial_z^2B = B_{\text{face}}\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-\eta^2}\frac{2\eta}{(2\sqrt{Dt})^2}$, so $D\partial_z^2B = \partial_tB$ ✓; at $z = 0$ it is $B_{\text{face}}$, and as $t\to0^+$ it tends to $0$ for every $z \gt 0$ ✓. The half-field depth is $0.954\sqrt{Dt}$: 3.5, 7.0 and 13.9 mm after 1, 4 and 16 ms. Section 2 replaces the switch-on by a sinusoid, which is the case that matters.

Worked: copper's diffusion equation, with the neglected term

worked example 1 — the diffusion equation for the copper

1. What is dropped. $\omega\varepsilon_0/\sigma = \omega\varepsilon_0\rho = 314.16\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times1.68\times10^{-8} = 4.67\times10^{-17}$ at 50 Hz. The displacement current is that fraction of the conduction current.

2. What is left. $\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\sigma\vec E$ and $\nabla\times\vec E = -\partial_t\vec B$. Curl the first, use the second and $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$: $\partial_t\vec B = D\nabla^2\vec B$.

3. The coefficient. $D = \rho/\mu_0 = 1.68\times10^{-8}/1.2566\times10^{-6} = 1.34\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$.

4. A length from a time. In a time $1/\omega$ a field diffuses a distance of order $\sqrt{D/\omega}$. At 50 Hz, $\sqrt{2D/\omega} = \sqrt{2\times0.01337/314.16} = 9.23$ mm.

checks

Units. $\rho/\mu_0$: $\Omega\,\mathrm m/(\Omega\,\mathrm s\,\mathrm m^{-1}) = \mathrm{m^2/s}$ ✓. $\sqrt{D/\omega}$: $\sqrt{\mathrm{m^2}} = \mathrm m$ ✓.

Limits. $\sigma\to0$: $D\to\infty$, the field fills everything at once — an insulator does not shield ✓. $\sigma\to\infty$: $D\to0$, a perfect conductor keeps a changing field out entirely ✓.

A size. The 9.23 mm of step 4 is the skin depth section 2 derives: the order-of-magnitude argument already gives the answer.

Step 1 at 2.4 GHz. $\omega = 1.508\times10^{10}$ rad/s, and $\omega\varepsilon_0\rho = 2.24\times10^{-9}$. Still negligible: the diffusion equation holds in copper at microwave frequencies too, which is why section 2's 1.3 μm at 2.4 GHz is trustworthy.

Step 4, the order of magnitude. A diffusion equation $\partial_tB = D\partial_z^2B$ balances $B/T$ against $DB/L^2$ over a time $T$ and length $L$, so $L \sim \sqrt{DT}$. With a field oscillating at $\omega$, the time over which it keeps one sign is of order $1/\omega$. The factor $\sqrt2$ is chosen with hindsight to make it section 2's exact $\delta$; nothing on this slide fixes it. Note what it says: $\delta^2/D = 2/\omega = 6.37$ ms at 50 Hz, a third of the 20 ms period.

Check, $\sigma \to \infty$. The perfect conductor of MR 23's electrostatics excluded $\vec E$; here it excludes a changing $\vec B$ too. A static $\vec B$ is not excluded by $\sigma\to\infty$ alone (excluding that is superconductivity, not this unit).

Parabolic: what the approximation threw away

The type. One time derivative, two space derivatives: MR 18's parabolic type, the heat equation (Three types, in two variables). With the displacement current kept, $\nabla^2\vec B = \mu\sigma\partial_t\vec B + \mu\varepsilon_0\partial_t^2\vec B$ is hyperbolic: a wave equation with damping.

the price: infinite speed

The heat kernel $(4\pi Dt)^{-1/2}e^{-z^2/4Dt}$ is positive at every $z$ for every $t \gt 0$ (MR 18's notes): a disturbance is felt everywhere at once. Real fields cannot outrun light.

Dropping $\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E$ dropped retardation — unit 02's (H3) again, now inside the metal. It costs nothing measurable: after 1 ms the kernel 10 cm away is $10^{-81}$ of its peak, while light has gone 300 km.

Computed for copper, $D = 1.34\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m^2/s}$, relative to a unit-area impulse. The "instant" spread is real mathematics and irrelevant physics.

The classification. In one space dimension $\partial_tu = Du_{zz}$ has principal part $-Du_{zz}$ alone in the variables $(t, z)$: symbol matrix $\mathrm{diag}(0, -D)$, discriminant zero, parabolic (MR 18, The three types, drawn, its warning box "the heat equation, honestly"). The full equation $\mu\varepsilon_0u_{tt} + \mu\sigma u_t = u_{zz}$ has symbol matrix $\mathrm{diag}(\mu\varepsilon_0, -1)$: hyperbolic, with characteristics $z \pm ct$, so its disturbances travel at $c$ exactly. Dropping the small $u_{tt}$ term changes the type — the singular-perturbation situation of MR 21's boundary layers, here harmless because the term dropped is $10^{-17}$ of the one kept.

The kernel. MR 18's notes to Three types, in two variables give $(4\pi kt)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/4kt}$ as a solution positive everywhere for $t \gt 0$. With $D = 0.01337$ and $t = 10^{-3}$ s: $4Dt = 5.35\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, the peak is $(4\pi Dt)^{-1/2} = 77.1\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ ($\log_{10} = 1.89$), and at $z = 0.1$ m the exponent is $-0.01/5.35\times10^{-5} = -187.0$, i.e. a factor $10^{-81.2}$; at 5 cm, $10^{-20.3}$ relative to the peak.

The same trade as unit 02. Unit 02's hypothesis (H3) — the circuit is small against a wavelength, so retardation is ignored — made the fields follow the currents instantly. Inside the metal the same approximation makes the field spread instantly. In both cases the error is set by how small the neglected time (here $\varepsilon_0/\sigma$, there $l/c$) is against the time scale of interest.

10 · half-space

A field at a flat face

Conductor fills $z \gt 0$. At its face the field is $\vec H = H_0\hat y$ (an RMS phasor, $e^{+i\omega t}$). Nothing depends on $x$ or $y$, so $\vec H = H(z)\hat y$ and the diffusion equation is

$$H'' = i\omega\mu\sigma\,H .$$

Try $e^{-\gamma z}$: $\gamma^2 = i\omega\mu\sigma = 2i/\delta^2$, and $(1+i)^2 = 2i$, so $\gamma = \pm(1+i)/\delta$. Bounded as $z\to\infty$: take $+$.

the field in a half-space $$H(z) = H_0\,e^{-(1+i)z/\delta},\qquad \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}} .$$

$\delta$ is the skin depth. The current, $\vec J = \nabla\times\vec H = -H'\hat x$, has the same profile: $J_x = \gamma H$.

Units: $\omega\mu\sigma$ in $\mathrm{s^{-1}\cdot s\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{m^{-2}}$ (previous section), so $\delta$ in m ✓.

Schematic, magnitudes only. The field along the face, $H_0\hat y$, decays into the metal; the current it implies flows along $x$, out of the page, in the same thin layer.

Full proof

Reduction. In phasors $\partial_t \to i\omega$, so the diffusion equation for $\vec H$ ($\vec B = \mu\vec H$ with constant $\mu$) is $\nabla^2\vec H = i\omega\mu\sigma\vec H$. With $\vec H = H(z)\hat y$, $\nabla^2\vec H = H''\hat y$. Also $\nabla\cdot\vec H = \partial_yH = 0$ ✓, so this field is allowed.

Roots. $\omega\mu\sigma = 2/\delta^2$ by the definition of $\delta$, so $\gamma^2 = 2i/\delta^2$. $(1+i)^2 = 1 + 2i - 1 = 2i$, so $\gamma = \pm(1+i)/\delta$. The general solution is $Ae^{-(1+i)z/\delta} + Be^{(1+i)z/\delta}$, and $|e^{(1+i)z/\delta}| = e^{z/\delta}$ grows without bound; a finite field deep in the conductor needs $B = 0$, and $H(0) = H_0$ fixes $A$.

The current. Ampère without displacement current: $\vec J = \nabla\times\vec H$. For $\vec H = H(z)\hat y$, $\nabla\times\vec H = -\partial_zH\,\hat x$, so $J_x = -H' = \gamma H$, with $\gamma = (1+i)/\delta$. The current flows along $x$, perpendicular to the field and parallel to the face, and it is largest at the face: $J_x(0) = (1+i)H_0/\delta$.

Where the face field comes from. In practice from the current itself: section 3's wire carries its own field at its surface, $H = I/2\pi a$. A flat half-space is the limit of any conductor whose radius of curvature is large against $\delta$, which is why this calculation is the template for all the others (RWVD §3.16 does the same calculation, with $j$ for $i$; Griffiths §9.4.1 reaches the same depth from a plane wave entering a conductor, written with $e^{-i\omega t}$; in the good-conductor limit his skin depth $1/\kappa$ is this $\delta$).

Decay and lag

Back in time (RMS phasor, unit 05): $J(z,t) = \sqrt2\,|J_0|\,e^{-z/\delta}\cos(\omega t - z/\delta + \varphi_0)$.

two readings of $e^{-(1+i)z/\delta}$

Decay: the amplitude falls by $e$ per $\delta$ — 8.7 dB — to 5% at $3\delta$.

Lag: the phase falls behind by $z/\delta$ radians — $3\delta$ down, 3 rad, $172^\circ$: there the current flows backwards relative to the face.

A heavily damped wave going in: wavelength $2\pi\delta$, phase speed $\omega\delta$ — in copper at 50 Hz, $58$ mm and $2.9$ m/s — and over one wavelength it decays by $e^{-2\pi} = 1/535$.

Computed, $\mathrm{Re}\,e^{-(1+i)\zeta + i\omega t}$ with $\zeta = z/\delta$, at four instants a quarter period apart. The crests move inward and die.

From the phasor. $J(z) = J_0e^{-z/\delta}e^{-iz/\delta}$ with $J_0 = |J_0|e^{i\varphi_0}$, and the physical current is $\mathrm{Re}\big(\sqrt2J(z)e^{i\omega t}\big) = \sqrt2|J_0|e^{-z/\delta}\cos(\omega t - z/\delta + \varphi_0)$ — the course's convention, RMS phasors with $e^{+i\omega t}$ (unit 05, RMS, and the phasor). At fixed $t$ it is a cosine in $z$ of wavelength $2\pi\delta$; a crest, $\omega t - z/\delta = $ const, moves at $\mathrm dz/\mathrm dt = \omega\delta$, inward.

The numbers. $20\log_{10}e = 8.69$ dB per skin depth. $e^{-3} = 0.0498$. $3\ \mathrm{rad} = 171.9^\circ$. Copper at 50 Hz: $2\pi\delta = 2\pi\times9.226 = 57.97$ mm; $\omega\delta = 314.16\times0.009226 = 2.90$ m/s. $e^{-2\pi} = 1.87\times10^{-3} = 1/535$.

Backwards current. Beyond $z = \pi\delta/2$ the phase lag passes $90^\circ$, so the current at depth $z$ is more often against the current at the face than with it: the time average of their product, $\mathrm{Re}\big(J(z)J_0^*\big) = |J_0|^2e^{-z/\delta}\cos(z/\delta)$, turns negative. Integrated over the depth it is the face's current that dominates, and the next slides do the integral. It is these counter-flowing layers that make section 4's thick tube slightly worse than a thinner one.

The current as a helix of phasors

Draw the phasor $J(z)/J_0 = e^{-z/\delta}e^{-iz/\delta}$ as an arrow standing on the depth axis, at a row of depths. Each arrow is shorter than the last and turned back by a fixed angle: a shrinking helix.

Seen end-on, the tips trace a logarithmic spiral: $|J|$ falls by $e^{-\pi/2} = 0.21$ per quarter turn.

check

$J$ solves $J'' = \gamma^2J$ with $\gamma^2 = 2i/\delta^2$: a pure imaginary $\gamma^2$, so the decay rate and the turning rate are equal — both $1/\delta$. That is why the helix's pitch and its shrinkage are locked.

Computed. Left: arrows every $\delta/4$, real part up, the lag drawn towards the viewer; past $\pi\delta/2$ they point below the axis. Right: the tips seen end-on, dots every $\delta/2$.

How to read it. At an instant, the physical current at depth $z$ is $\sqrt2$ times the projection of that depth's arrow on a direction rotating at $\omega$ (unit 05's rotating-phasor picture). All arrows rotate together; the helix turns rigidly, and what an observer at fixed depth sees is the projection oscillating with that depth's amplitude and phase.

The spiral, end-on. $J/J_0 = e^{-(1+i)\zeta}$ traces $r = e^{-\theta}$ with $\theta = \zeta$ the angle turned: a logarithmic spiral. Per quarter turn $\theta$ grows by $\pi/2$ and $r$ falls by $e^{-\pi/2} = 0.2079$.

The figure's values. At $\zeta = 0.75$: $e^{-0.75}(\cos0.75 - i\sin0.75) = 0.3456 - 0.3220i$; at $\zeta = 1.5$: $0.0158 - 0.2226i$, nearly a quarter turn; at $\zeta = 3$: $-0.0493 - 0.0070i$, almost half a turn and 5% long. 10 · half-space

The surface-layer theorem

theorem

For the half-space, per unit width of face: the total current is $K = H_0$, and the mean power dissipated is

$$p = \frac{|K|^2}{\sigma\delta}\quad\text{exactly:}$$

that of the same current spread uniformly through a layer $\delta$ thick. The surface resistance is $R_s = 1/\sigma\delta$, in ohms per square.

Units: $\mathrm{A^2m^{-2}}/(\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}\cdot m}) = \mathrm{A^2\,\Omega\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$ ✓.

proof

$K = \int_0^\infty J_x\,\mathrm dz = -\int_0^\infty H'\,\mathrm dz = H_0$. And $|J_x|^2 = |\gamma|^2|H_0|^2e^{-2z/\delta}$ with $|\gamma|^2 = 2/\delta^2$, so $\int_0^\infty|J_x|^2/\sigma\,\mathrm dz = \frac{2|H_0|^2}{\sigma\delta^2}\cdot\frac\delta2 = \frac{|K|^2}{\sigma\delta}$. $\square$

The layer's height is $|K|/\delta = |J_0|/\sqrt2$. It is not the area under $|J|$ that matches — the phases cancel part of the current — but the current and the heat, exactly.

Full proof

Current. $J_x = -H'$ (Ampère), so $\int_0^Z J_x\,\mathrm dz = H(0) - H(Z) \to H_0$ as $Z\to\infty$. This is Ampère's law round a rectangle with one long side outside the face (field $H_0$) and one deep inside (field 0): all the current that the face field implies flows inside the conductor.

Power. With RMS phasors the mean power per unit volume is $|J|^2/\sigma$ (unit 05's mean power of a resistor, $|V|^2/R$ with no ½, pointwise). $|J_x(z)|^2 = |\gamma H_0e^{-\gamma z}|^2 = \frac{2}{\delta^2}|H_0|^2e^{-2z/\delta}$, since $|1+i|^2 = 2$ and $|e^{-(1+i)z/\delta}| = e^{-z/\delta}$. $\int_0^\infty e^{-2z/\delta}\mathrm dz = \delta/2$. So $p = |H_0|^2/(\sigma\delta) = |K|^2/(\sigma\delta)$.

The uniform layer. Current $K$ spread uniformly over thickness $\delta$ has density $K/\delta$ and dissipates $\frac{|K|^2}{\delta^2\sigma}\cdot\delta = |K|^2/(\sigma\delta)$ per unit area. Equal. So for the purpose of current and heat, a thick conductor at frequency $\omega$ is a sheet of thickness $\delta$ — that is "the skin".

"Per square". A sheet of resistivity $\rho$ and thickness $t$, of length $L$ and width $W$, has resistance $\rho L/(tW)$ (unit 00): it depends only on $L/W$, so any square has $\rho/t$. With $t = \delta$, $R_s = \rho/\delta = 1/\sigma\delta$. Copper at 50 Hz: $1.68\times10^{-8}/9.226\times10^{-3} = 1.82\ \mu\Omega$ per square. RWVD §3.17–3.18 derive the same internal impedance of a plane conductor.

Why the figure's rectangle is lower than the curve. $|\int J\,\mathrm dz| = |J_0|\delta/|1+i| = |J_0|\delta/\sqrt2$, while $\int|J|\,\mathrm dz = |J_0|\delta$: the deeper layers, turned in phase, partly cancel the shallow ones.

Energy check: the Poynting flux into the skin

At the face, $E_x(0) = J_x(0)/\sigma = (1+i)H_0/\sigma\delta$. The complex Poynting vector (RMS phasors; unit 05's mean of a product, $\mathrm{Re}\,VI^*$, in field form) into the metal is

$$\vec E\times\vec H^* = E_xH_y^*\,\hat z = \frac{(1+i)\,|H_0|^2}{\sigma\delta}\,\hat z .$$
energy balance

Real part $= |H_0|^2/\sigma\delta = p$: what enters is what heats, as in unit 01's wire — but now it is used up within a few $\delta$, since $|\vec S|$ falls as $e^{-2z/\delta}$.

Imaginary part $= 2\omega\langle W_m\rangle$ per area, with $\langle W_m\rangle = \int\tfrac12\mu|H|^2\mathrm dz = \mu|H_0|^2\delta/4$ — the energy stored in the skin's field, sloshing in and out twice a cycle.

So the surface impedance is $Z_s = E_x(0)/H_0 = (1+i)R_s$: as much reactance as resistance.

Above, schematic: the flux arrives through the face and is consumed in the skin. Below, computed: its magnitude against depth.

Full proof

Direction. $\hat x\times\hat y = \hat z$: into the conductor ✓. With RMS phasors the time average of the Poynting vector is $\mathrm{Re}(\vec E\times\vec H^*)$ — no ½, by unit 05's rule for the mean of a product of two RMS phasors (RMS, and the phasor) — and the imaginary part measures the energy that flows in and back out each cycle without being dissipated.

Inside. $E_x(z) = J_x(z)/\sigma = \gamma H(z)/\sigma$, so $E_xH^* = \gamma|H(z)|^2/\sigma = \frac{(1+i)|H_0|^2}{\sigma\delta}e^{-2z/\delta}$. Its real part at depth $z$ is the power still to be dissipated below $z$: differentiate, $-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dz}\frac{|H_0|^2}{\sigma\delta}e^{-2z/\delta} = \frac{2|H_0|^2}{\sigma\delta^2}e^{-2z/\delta} = |J_x|^2/\sigma$ ✓ — Poynting's theorem pointwise, $\nabla\cdot\vec S = -\vec J\cdot\vec E$, as in unit 01's Inside the inner conductor, S points inward.

Reactive part. Mean magnetic energy per unit area with RMS phasors: $\langle W_m\rangle = \int_0^\infty\tfrac12\mu|H|^2\,\mathrm dz = \tfrac12\mu|H_0|^2\cdot\frac\delta2$. Then $2\omega\langle W_m\rangle = \tfrac12\omega\mu\delta|H_0|^2$, and $\tfrac12\omega\mu\delta = \frac{\omega\mu\delta^2}{2\delta} = \frac{1}{\sigma\delta}$ because $\omega\mu\sigma\delta^2 = 2$. So the imaginary part is $|H_0|^2/\sigma\delta$ ✓. This is the field form of what unit 06 (What $Q$ is: stored energy, sloshing) calls reactive power, $Q = 2\omega(\langle W_m\rangle - \langle W_e\rangle)$ — proved here directly, so nothing rests on that unit — with no electric energy to speak of (it is smaller by the factor $\omega\varepsilon_0/\sigma$ of section 1).

The surface impedance $Z_s = (1+i)/\sigma\delta$ is the impedance of one square of the face: RWVD §3.17's "internal impedance per square". Its reactance, $\omega L_s$ with $L_s = \mu\delta/2$, is the internal inductance of the skin: unit 02's $\mu_0/8\pi$, section 3 shows, is its low-frequency cousin.

10 · round wire

The wire's equation is Bessel's

A long straight wire of radius $a$ carries the phasor current $I$ along $z$. By symmetry $\vec E = E_z(r)\hat z$, and the diffusion equation in cylindrical coordinates $(r, \theta, z)$ reads

$$E_z'' + \frac1rE_z' + k^2E_z = 0,\qquad k^2 = -i\omega\mu\sigma,$$

so $k = (1-i)/\delta$. With $x = kr$ this is Bessel's equation of order 0 (MR 09).

the current across a round wire $$J_z(r) = J_z(a)\,\frac{J_0(kr)}{J_0(ka)} .$$

MR 09's series for $J_0$ converges for every complex argument, so nothing changes but the arithmetic. The second solution, $Y_0$, is unbounded at $r = 0$ (MR 09) and is discarded, as on MR 19's drum.

Units: $kr$ is $\mathrm{m^{-1}\cdot m}$, dimensionless ✓.

Cross-section, schematic. $\vec J$ and $\vec E$ point along the axis; $\vec H$ circles it. Only $r$ matters.

Full proof

Symmetry. An infinitely long wire, uniform along $z$ and round, carrying current along $z$: rotating about the axis or sliding along it maps the problem to itself, so $E_z$ can depend only on $r$. A radial $E_r$ inside would need interior charge, and $\nabla\cdot\vec E = 0$ (section 1) with symmetry forbids it; an azimuthal $E_\theta$ would need an axial changing flux, and there is none. So $\vec E = E_z(r)\hat z$, and $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$ likewise.

The equation. The Cartesian component $E_z$ obeys the scalar diffusion equation, $\nabla^2E_z = i\omega\mu\sigma E_z$ in phasors. The Laplacian of a function of $r$ alone in cylindrical coordinates is $E_z'' + E_z'/r$ (MR 19, The drum, separated). Moving the right side over: $E_z'' + E_z'/r - i\omega\mu\sigma E_z = 0$, i.e. $k^2 = -i\omega\mu\sigma = -2i/\delta^2$. And $(1-i)^2 = -2i$, so $k = (1-i)/\delta$ (the other root, $-k$, gives the same functions, $J_0$ being even).

To Bessel. With $x = kr$, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dr} = k\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}$: $k^2(y'' + y'/x + y) = 0$, i.e. $x^2y'' + xy' + x^2y = 0$ — MR 09's Bessel's differential equation with $n = 0$. Its solutions are combinations of $J_0$ and the logarithmic solution (MR 09, The double root, and where the logarithm comes from), whose standard normalisation is Weber's $Y_0$ (MR 09's notes to The logarithmic solution, drawn). A finite current density on the axis discards $Y_0$.

Complex argument. MR 09 (The definition, and a bound, notes) proved the series converges absolutely for every complex $x$, and the termwise derivatives likewise, so $J_0(kr)$ solves the equation with complex $k$ by the same computation as for real $k$. The functions $\mathrm{ber}$ and $\mathrm{bei}$ found in older tables are its real and imaginary parts (Kelvin's functions, DLMF §10.61; RWVD §4.4).

10 · round wire

Internal impedance: the surface field over the current

Faraday (MR 24), $\theta$ component: $-E_z' = -i\omega\mu H_\theta$. Ampère round the surface: $2\pi aH_\theta(a) = I$. With $J_0' = -J_1$ (MR 09) and $i\omega\mu = -k^2/\sigma$:

the internal impedance of a round wire, per metre $$Z_i = \frac{E_z(a)}{I} = \frac{k\rho}{2\pi a}\,\frac{J_0(ka)}{J_1(ka)} .$$

Units: $\mathrm{m^{-1}\cdot\Omega\,m/m} = \Omega/\mathrm m$ ✓.

Why this ratio. The complex power entering a metre of wire through its surface is $2\pi a\,E_z(a)H_\theta(a)^* = E_z(a)I^*$ — unit 01's theorem — so $Z_i|I|^2$ is exactly what the wire absorbs. $\mathrm{Re}\,Z_i$ is the AC resistance; $\mathrm{Im}\,Z_i/\omega$ the internal inductance.

Schematic. Everything the metre of wire absorbs crosses its surface as the Poynting flux $E_z\times H_\theta$; its total is $E_z(a)I^*$.

Full proof

Faraday. For $\vec E = E_z(r)\hat z$, $(\nabla\times\vec E)_\theta = -\partial_rE_z$, and $\nabla\times\vec E = -i\omega\vec B = -i\omega\mu\vec H$, so $H_\theta = E_z'/(i\omega\mu)$. From $E_z(r) = E_z(a)J_0(kr)/J_0(ka)$ and $J_0' = -J_1$: $E_z'(a) = -kE_z(a)J_1(ka)/J_0(ka)$.

Ampère on the circle $r = a$ (just outside or just inside — $H_\theta$ is continuous, there being no surface current in a conductor of finite $\sigma$): $2\pi aH_\theta(a) = I$. So $I = \frac{2\pi a}{i\omega\mu}\cdot\big(-kE_z(a)\tfrac{J_1(ka)}{J_0(ka)}\big)$. With $i\omega\mu = -k^2/\sigma$ (from $k^2 = -i\omega\mu\sigma$): $I = \frac{2\pi a\sigma}{k}E_z(a)\frac{J_1(ka)}{J_0(ka)}$, and $Z_i = E_z(a)/I = \frac{k}{2\pi a\sigma}\frac{J_0(ka)}{J_1(ka)}$. This is RWVD §4.5, eq. (6), in this course's notation.

Why "internal". The voltage between the two ends of a metre of wire, measured along a path on its surface, is $E_z(a)$ — but a voltmeter's leads enclose some of the field outside the wire too, and that part is the external inductance of unit 02 (Worked: the coaxial cable's C′ and L′), which depends on where the return conductor is. $Z_i$ is the part that belongs to the wire alone: what is inside $r = a$. Unit 01's theorem (p = vi, proved) applied to the surface of the wire says the power entering it is the flux through that surface; per metre it is $2\pi a\cdot E_zH_\theta^*$ (the radial flux, $-E_zH_\theta^*\hat r$, points inward) $= E_z(a)\,(2\pi aH_\theta(a))^* = E_z(a)I^*$.

Neighbours: the proximity effect

current follows the tangential field

On a conductor many skin depths thick, the surface-layer theorem's $K = H_0$ holds point by point: the current per unit length of perimeter equals the tangential $H$ just outside.

So the current goes where the field outside runs strongest along the surface — and a neighbour's current changes that field. This is the proximity effect (cited: CDA, Copper for Busbars, §2.2.2.1.2; Sullivan 1999, §II).

  • Opposite currents (go and return): fields add in the gap; the current crowds onto the facing sides.
  • Equal currents (strands of one winding): fields cancel in the gap; the current is pushed to the far sides.

Either way less of the copper is used, and $R_{\mathrm{ac}}$ rises above the isolated wire's. Computing it is beyond this unit.

Schematic, not a computed field. A go-and-return pair: the shaded crescents mark where the current flows.

The local theorem. Section 2's proof used only Ampère round a thin rectangle straddling the face, with one long side outside (tangential field $H_t$) and the other a few skin depths inside (field zero): the current crossing the rectangle per unit length of face is $H_t$. When the conductor's size and radius of curvature are large against $\delta$, the field varies slowly along the surface on the scale of $\delta$, and the same rectangle works locally. So the surface current density follows $H_t$ point by point, and the loss per unit area is $R_s|H_t|^2$.

Why the facing sides, for opposite currents. Between two conductors with opposite currents, each one's field points the same way in the gap (one circles anticlockwise, the other clockwise, and between them they agree), so the tangential field on the facing surfaces is about twice either alone; on the far sides they partly cancel. The current follows. For equal currents the reverse. The CDA publication states exactly this for round bars and tubes carrying anti-parallel currents ("an increase in the current density at those parts of the bar that face each other, so losses are higher"), and tabulates proximity factors from computed fields; Sullivan defines the proximity effect as current flowing in other undesirable patterns because of the fields of nearby conductors.

What is not done here. The self-consistent field around two thick conductors is a two-dimensional boundary-value problem; the CDA's factors come from finite-element computation, by its own account. This unit states the effect and its direction and stops.

A tube: no field in the hole

Hollow the bar: copper for $c \le r \le b$. Inside the hole Ampère's circle encloses no current, so $H_\theta(c) = 0$, i.e. $E_z'(c) = 0$. The middle of the tube no longer needs $J_0$ alone; MR 09's second solution $Y_0$ is allowed, since $r = 0$ is not in the copper:

$$E_z(r) \propto J_0(kr)\,Y_1(kc) - Y_0(kr)\,J_1(kc),$$

with $Y_1 := -Y_0'$. The same steps as for the wire give

internal impedance of a tube, per metre $$Z_i = \frac{k\rho}{2\pi b}\,\frac{J_0(kb)Y_1(kc) - Y_0(kb)J_1(kc)}{J_1(kb)Y_1(kc) - Y_1(kb)J_1(kc)} .$$

Full proof

The equation in the wall is the wire's: Bessel of order 0 in $x = kr$, now on the annulus $c \le r \le b$, where both $J_0(kr)$ and $Y_0(kr)$ are smooth (the logarithm's branch point is at $r = 0$, outside the copper; the principal branch is used throughout). So $E_z = AJ_0(kr) + BY_0(kr)$.

Inner face. For $r \lt c$ there is no conductor and no current; Ampère round a circle of radius $r \lt c$ encloses nothing, so $H_\theta = 0$ there, and $H_\theta$ is continuous at $r = c$. Faraday gives $H_\theta = E_z'/(i\omega\mu)$, so $E_z'(c) = 0$: $-k\big(AJ_1(kc) + BY_1(kc)\big) = 0$, using $J_0' = -J_1$ (MR 09) and the definition $Y_1 := -Y_0'$ (the same relation DLMF §10.6 states for the standard $Y_1$). Take $A = Y_1(kc)$, $B = -J_1(kc)$.

Outer face. As for the wire: $Z_i = E_z(b)/I$ with $I = 2\pi bH_\theta(b) = 2\pi bE_z'(b)/(i\omega\mu) = -\frac{2\pi b\sigma}{k^2}E_z'(b)$, and $E_z'(b) = -k\big(J_1(kb)Y_1(kc) - Y_1(kb)J_1(kc)\big)$. Dividing gives the formula.

The check $c \to 0$. $|Y_1(kc)| \sim 2/(\pi|k|c)\to\infty$ (from MR 09's $Y_0 \sim \frac2\pi\ln$ near 0, differentiated), so both numerator and denominator are dominated by their $Y_1(kc)$ terms, which cancel in the ratio, leaving $J_0(kb)/J_1(kb)$ ✓. And at low frequency the tube's formula must give $\rho/\pi(b^2 - c^2)$, and evaluated for the 10 mm wall at 1 Hz it gives 5.9420 against $\rho/\pi(b^2 - c^2) = 5.9418\ \mu\Omega/\mathrm m$ ✓.

The best wall is $\pi\delta/2$

The flat version: a slab of thickness $t$ fed from one face, with no field at the other (the tube's hole). Its resistance per square is $R_s\,f(t/\delta)$ with

$$f(x) = \frac{\sinh2x + \sin2x}{\cosh2x - \cos2x}$$
theorem: the optimal slab

$f$ is least at $x = \pi/2$, where $f = \tanh(\pi/2) = 0.917$: a slab $\pi\delta/2$ thick has 8% less resistance than an infinitely thick one.

proof

Differentiate and use $\cosh^2 - \sinh^2 = \cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$: $f'(x) = \dfrac{-4\sinh2x\,\sin2x}{(\cosh2x - \cos2x)^2}$, negative on $(0, \pi/2)$, positive on $(\pi/2, \pi)$. $\square$

The tube's optimum, 14.5 mm $= 1.575\delta$, is the slab's $\pi\delta/2 = 1.571\delta = 14.49$ mm, bent round.

Computed $f(x)$. Thinner than $\pi\delta/2$, too little copper; thicker, counter-flowing current. Problem P10 derives $f$.

Full proof

The field. In the slab $0 \le z \le t$, $H'' = \gamma^2H$ with $H(0) = H_0$ and $H(t) = 0$ (no field beyond the far face, as in the tube's hole): $H = H_0\sinh\gamma(t - z)/\sinh\gamma t$. At the face $E_x(0) = J_x(0)/\sigma = -H'(0)/\sigma = (\gamma/\sigma)H_0\coth\gamma t$, and the current per unit width is $H_0$ (Ampère, as in section 2). So the impedance of one square is $Z = (\gamma/\sigma)\coth\gamma t = R_s(1+i)\coth\big((1+i)x\big)$.

Its real part. For $w = u + iv$, $\coth w = \cosh w\,\sinh\bar w/|\sinh w|^2$; $2\cosh w\sinh\bar w = \sinh2u - i\sin2v$ and $2|\sinh w|^2 = \cosh2u - \cos2v$ (product formulas). With $u = v = x$: $\coth\big((1+i)x\big) = \frac{\sinh2x - i\sin2x}{\cosh2x - \cos2x}$, and multiplying by $1 + i$ the real part is $\frac{\sinh2x + \sin2x}{\cosh2x - \cos2x} = f(x)$.

The derivative. Numerator of $f'$: $(2\cosh2x + 2\cos2x)(\cosh2x - \cos2x) - (\sinh2x + \sin2x)(2\sinh2x + 2\sin2x)$ $= 2[\cosh^22x - \cos^22x - \sinh^22x - 2\sinh2x\sin2x - \sin^22x] = 2[1 - 1 - 2\sinh2x\sin2x] = -4\sinh2x\sin2x$. So the stationary points are $x = n\pi/2$: minima at odd $n$, maxima at even. The first minimum is the global one, since later minima have $f = \tanh(n\pi/2)$, increasing in $n$. $f(\pi/2) = \sinh\pi/(\cosh\pi + 1) = \tanh(\pi/2) = 0.9172$; $f(\pi) = \coth\pi = 1.0037$.

Limits. $x\to\infty$: $f\to1$, section 2's half-space ✓. $x\to0$: $f \approx 4x/(4x^2) = 1/x$, i.e. $R = R_s\delta/t = \rho/t$, the DC resistance of a sheet $t$ thick ✓.

Overhead lines: aluminium round a steel core

An overhead line's conductor is stranded: round wires laid in concentric layers, in alternating directions (cited: EN 50182:2001, Conductors for overhead lines — Round wire concentric lay stranded conductors, whose scope covers aluminium, aluminium-alloy and zinc-coated steel wires). Why it works. Aluminium's skin depth at 50 Hz is 11.6 mm. In a conductor whose radius is comparable, the current favours the outer layers (section 3), and that is where the aluminium is. The steel sits where the least current would flow and carries the mechanical tension.

Twisted, so every strand takes every place

Lay 400 insulated strands side by side, joined at the ends, and the bundle is a thick conductor again: the outer strands sit in the strongest field, link the least flux, and take the current — the skin effect, now between strands.

Litz wire

Individually insulated strands, twisted or woven together (cited: Sullivan 1999, §I). Weaving each strand through every position of the bundle along its length, outside and inside, makes every strand link on average the same flux and carry the same share of current: transposition.

Sullivan's §II: simple twisting, which carries each strand round the bundle, is enough to control the proximity effect at the level of the bundle; moving strands between the inside and the outside, which controls the bundle's own skin effect, needs more elaborate weaves.

This is the conductor unit 15's coils are wound from.

Schematic, side views. Above, untwisted: the bundle repeats the solid wire's skin effect between strands. Below, woven: one strand (highlighted) spends equal lengths outside and inside.

Why parallel strands fail. Strands joined at both ends form loops with each other. The voltage along each strand is its own resistance times its current plus the EMF from the flux it links; all strands share the same end voltages. In a straight bundle the strands near the axis link more of the bundle's own internal flux (by Ampère, the field inside the bundle grows outward from zero, so a central strand is encircled by more flux lines) and so see more inductive impedance, exactly as the axis of a solid wire does in section 1's first slide. The current shifts outward. Insulation between strands does not help by itself: it only stops current crossing between strands along the length, while the shift happens through the end joints.

What twisting does, and what it does not. A simple twist carries each strand round the bundle at a fixed distance from its axis: a neighbour's field, which favours one side, is averaged out along the length — the bundle-level proximity effect. But a strand that stays near the axis still links more of the bundle's own flux than one that stays near the outside, so the bundle-level skin effect needs strands that also move in and out, as in the figure's highlighted strand. If each strand spends equal lengths in each position, each links the same flux on average and has the same impedance; with the same end voltages, they carry equal currents. That is the "carefully prescribed pattern" that Sullivan says the term litz wire is sometimes reserved for; he uses it for any insulated grouped strands.

Unit 07's 0.3 and 0.5 mm sheets are exact to $2\times10^{-5}$. Its 5 mm plate (its P5): 53.0 W/kg in place of the classical estimate's 60.5. A 10 mm slab: half the classical loss — the formula would overstate it twofold.

Computed. Below $x \approx 1$ the classical formula is exact for practical purposes; above it the loss grows only as $d$, not $d^2$.

The numbers. $x = d/1.5915$ mm: 0.1885, 0.6283, 1.885, 3.1416, 6.283. $F$: 0.999998, 0.99975, 0.98058, 0.87582, 0.47925. Unit 07's 0.5 mm sheet: $x = 0.314$, $F = 0.999985$.

Unit 07's P5. It found the classical formula would give $0.218\times(5/0.3)^2 = 60.5$ W/kg for a 5 mm plate, "computed for a field that is not there". With the field that is there: $60.5\times0.876 = 53.0$ W/kg. For 10 mm: classical $242$ W/kg, actual $116$ W/kg.

Why less loss, not more. At fixed mean flux the thick sheet's flux crowds into its skins, where it is denser — but the eddy loss depends on the EMF round each loop, which in the classical picture grew as the square of the distance from the mid-plane. With the middle shielded, the long loops round the whole section carry little, and the loss per unit volume grows only as $d$ (the $3/x$ branch: $p \propto d^2\cdot\delta/d$). The classical formula, extrapolated, overstates it.

Steel, again. All these numbers belong to the assumed constant $\mu_r = 1000$. Real steel's permeability varies with the flux density through the cycle (unit 07, Iron, in one slide), so for thick plates this linear calculation is an estimate of the trend, not of the loss. For thin laminations, $x \ll 1$, the result does not depend on $\mu$ at all, and unit 07's numbers stand however $\mu_r$ varies — provided it stays small enough that $x$ stays small.

the unit in four lines

1. Inside a metal, Maxwell plus Ohm is a diffusion equation, diffusivity $1/\mu\sigma$.

2. At one frequency it fixes a depth, $\delta = \sqrt{2/\omega\mu\sigma}$: copper, 9.2 mm at 50 Hz, 0.21 mm at 100 kHz.

3. A conductor much thicker than $\delta$ behaves as a skin $\delta$ thick on its surface: $R_s = 1/\sigma\delta$ per square.

4. So big 50 Hz conductors are hollowed, and high-frequency ones are divided and twisted.

The running example, as the skin depth shapes it. Schematic; the strands are fewer than 400.

What this unit hands on. Unit 15 winds its coils from Litz wire and needs only the last slides of section 4. Unit 08's $R'$ at radio frequencies is section 3's high-frequency resistance, rising as $\sqrt f$. Unit 14's HVDC has "no skin effect" at DC, which is section 3's low-frequency limit taken all the way. Unit 07's laminations are settled by section 5.

What it does not do. The proximity effect is stated and its direction derived, never computed. Magnetic materials are linear with an assumed $\mu_r$. Eddy-current machines and induction heating, which use the same diffusion, are left out.
