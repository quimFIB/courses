Part E · unit 11 · problems

Three-phase power: constant power, fewer conductors, and a field that rotates

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You prove that balanced three-phase power is constant in time, and derive the 75% conductor figure. You compute the workshop's neutral current two ways, by symmetrical components and directly, and get the same number: 36.1 A.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about three and a half hours. P1, P2 and P9 are the checkpoint's. Conventions as in the deck: RMS phasors with \(e^{+i\omega t}\); phase sequence a, b, c with \(V_b = \alpha^2V_a\), \(V_c = \alpha V_a\), \(\alpha = e^{i2\pi/3}\); complex power \(S = VI^*\); the passive sign convention; symmetrical components \(I_0 = \tfrac13(I_a + I_b + I_c)\), \(I_1 = \tfrac13(I_a + \alpha I_b + \alpha^2I_c)\), \(I_2 = \tfrac13(I_a + \alpha^2I_b + \alpha I_c)\). 50 Hz throughout.

The workshop (all loads assumed): 230 V line to neutral, four wires, each conductor 50 m of \(35\ \mathrm{mm^2}\) copper, \(R = 24.0\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\), reactance neglected. Balanced motors of 20 kW at power factor 0.7 lagging (41.41 A per line, \(\varphi = 45.57^\circ\)); a 9.2 kW resistive kiln between a and N (40.0 A); 2.3 kW of resistive lighting between b and N (10.0 A). The street transformer: 400 kVA, 20 kV / 400 V, delta on the 20 kV side and star with earthed neutral on the 400 V side (assumed); load loss \(P_k = 3250\) W at rated load (Regulation (EU) 548/2014, as in unit 07).

Balanced power and conductors

P1 Proof: constant power, and what unbalance does to it routine

exercises: the constant-power theorem, the sum \(1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 = 0\), unit 06's split of \(p(t)\), the 100 Hz part of an unbalanced load

  1. Prove that for \(v_k = \sqrt2V\cos(\omega t - 2\pi k/3)\) and \(i_k = \sqrt2I\cos(\omega t - 2\pi k/3 - \varphi)\), \(k = 0, 1, 2\), the total \(p(t) = \sum_kv_ki_k\) equals \(3VI\cos\varphi\) at every instant, for every \(\varphi\). Deduce \(\sum_k\cos^2(\omega t - 2\pi k/3) = \tfrac32\).
  2. For any three phases with RMS phasors \(V_k\), \(I_k\), show that \(p(t) = \sum_k\operatorname{Re}(V_kI_k^*) + \operatorname{Re}\big(e^{2i\omega t}\sum_kV_kI_k\big)\).
  3. Apply part 2 to the workshop with the kiln and lighting on. Find the mean power and the amplitude of the 100 Hz part, and say which loads contribute to it.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a product-to-sum formula and one sum of cube roots of unity. Part 2 is the same algebra with complex amplitudes. Part 3 ends in a number of a few kilowatts.

Rung 2

For part 2, write \(v = \tfrac{\sqrt2}2(Ve^{i\omega t} + V^*e^{-i\omega t})\) and the same for \(i\), and multiply. For part 3, the motors' \(V_kI_k\) form a balanced set in \(k\) — of which sequence?

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(v_ki_k = VI[\cos\varphi + \cos(2\omega t - 4\pi k/3 - \varphi)]\).

Worked solution

Idea. The power of each phase has a constant part and a part at \(2\omega\). Doubling the frequency doubles the phase shifts, \(4\pi k/3\), which are still three distinct cube roots of unity, so the \(2\omega\) parts cancel. Unbalance spoils the cancellation, and the leftover is one complex number, \(\sum_kV_kI_k\).

1. \(2\cos x\cos(x - \varphi) = \cos\varphi + \cos(2x - \varphi)\), with \(x = \omega t - 2\pi k/3\). Summing, \(p = 3VI\cos\varphi + VI\operatorname{Re}\big[e^{i(2\omega t - \varphi)}\sum_k\alpha^{-2k}\big]\), and \(\sum_k\alpha^{-2k} = 1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 = 0\) (because \(\alpha^{-2} = \alpha\), \(\alpha^{-4} = \alpha^2\)). So \(p = 3VI\cos\varphi\). Set \(\varphi = 0\): \(\sum_k2VI\cos^2x_k = 3VI\), i.e. \(\sum_k\cos^2x_k = \tfrac32\).

2. \(v_ki_k = \tfrac12(V_ke^{i\omega t} + V_k^*e^{-i\omega t})(I_ke^{i\omega t} + I_k^*e^{-i\omega t}) = \tfrac12(V_kI_k^* + V_k^*I_k) + \tfrac12(V_kI_ke^{2i\omega t} + \text{c.c.}) = \operatorname{Re}(V_kI_k^*) + \operatorname{Re}(V_kI_ke^{2i\omega t})\). Sum over \(k\).

3. Mean: \(20 + 9.2 + 2.3 = 31.5\) kW. The 100 Hz amplitude is \(|\sum_kV_kI_k|\). The motors' terms are \(V_kI_k = (\alpha^{-k}V)(\alpha^{-k}I) = \alpha^{-2k}VI\), summing to zero. The kiln: \(230\times40 = 9200\). The lighting: \(V_bI_b = (230\alpha^2)(10\alpha^2) = 2300\alpha^4 = 2300\alpha\). So \(\sum_kV_kI_k = 9200 + 2300\alpha = 230(40 + 10\alpha)\), of modulus \(230\sqrt{1300} = 8293\) W. So \(p(t)\) swings between \(31.5 - 8.29 = 23.21\) and \(39.79\) kW at 100 Hz, and only the single-phase loads cause it.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{V\,A} = \mathrm W\) ✓. The kiln alone would pulse with amplitude 9.2 kW (a resistor's \(p\) swings by its \(P\), unit 06); the lighting alone by 2.3 kW; together less than the sum, \(8.29 \lt 11.5\), because their pulsations are \(240^\circ\) apart. A curiosity worth noticing: \(8293/230 = 36.06\) A, the neutral current's magnitude — because for resistive single-phase loads \(V_kI_k = 230|I_k|\alpha^{-2k}\) and \(|40 + 10\alpha| = |40 + 10\alpha^2|\).

P2 The 75% figure, with and without a neutral routine

exercises: line loss at fixed power, line and phase voltage, comparing systems under stated constraints

  1. Power \(P\) is delivered over a distance \(l\) with total loss \(\mathcal L\) and voltage \(V\) between any two conductors, at a common power factor. Show that a three-wire three-phase line needs 75% of the conductor volume of a two-wire single-phase line.
  2. Repeat for a four-wire system whose neutral has half the cross-section of each line conductor (an assumption). Then for a full-size neutral.
  3. Numbers: 20 kW at unity power factor, 400 V between conductors, 100 m, copper (\(\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\)m), loss 2% of \(P\). Find both cross-sections.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two fractions and two areas in square millimetres. Write each line's loss in terms of \(P\), \(V\) and its cross-section.

Rung 2

Three-phase with line voltage \(V\) has phase voltage \(V/\sqrt3\) and line current \(P/(\sqrt3V\cos\varphi)\). A balanced load's neutral carries nothing, so it adds copper but no loss.

Rung 3

First line: single-phase loss \(2(P/V)^2\rho l/A_1\), three-phase loss \(3(P/\sqrt3V)^2\rho l/A_3 = (P/V)^2\rho l/A_3\).

Worked solution

Idea. At fixed voltage between conductors, three-phase sends the power in three currents each \(1/\sqrt3\) of the single-phase current, and needs no return.

1. Equal loss: \(2/A_1 = 1/A_3\), so \(A_3 = A_1/2\). Volume: \(3A_3l = 1.5A_1l\) against \(2A_1l\): 75%. A power factor \(\cos\varphi\) divides both currents by it, multiplying both losses by \(1/\cos^2\varphi\); the ratio is unchanged.

2. Half-size neutral, balanced load: the neutral carries no current, so the loss and \(A_3\) are unchanged, and the copper is \(3A_3 + A_3/2 = 1.75A_1\): \(1.75/2 = 87.5\%\). Full-size neutral: \(4A_3 = 2A_1\), 100% — no saving in copper, though the four-wire system offers 230 V as well as 400 V.

3. \(\mathcal L = 400\) W. Single-phase: \(I = 50\) A, \(A_1 = 2I^2\rho l/\mathcal L = 2\times2500\times1.68\times10^{-8}\times100/400 = 21.0\ \mathrm{mm^2}\). Three-phase: \(I = 20\,000/(\sqrt3\times400) = 28.87\) A, \(A_3 = 3I^2\rho l/\mathcal L = 10.5\ \mathrm{mm^2}\).

Check. \(A_1/A_3 = 2\) ✓. Units: \(\mathrm{A^2\,\Omega\,m\,m/W} = \mathrm{m^2}\) ✓. The three-phase loss at \(A_3\): \(3\times28.87^2\times(1.68\times10^{-8}\times100/10.5\times10^{-6}) = 3\times833.3\times0.16 = 400\) W ✓.

P3 Why three: \(n\) phases, and two-phase medium

exercises: the \(n\)-phase copper argument, minimising over odd and even \(n\), constant power and the rotating field for two phases in quadrature

  1. For \(n\) phases in star with phase voltage \(V_p\), at fixed power, length and loss, show that the conductor volume is proportional to \(1/V_p^2\), whatever \(n\).
  2. Hold fixed instead the largest voltage between any two conductors. Show that the copper, relative to single-phase, is \(\sin^2(\pi\lfloor n/2\rfloor/n)\), and prove that \(n = 3\) gives the least.
  3. Two-phase: two single-phase circuits, four wires, with voltages \(\sqrt2V\cos\omega t\) and \(\sqrt2V\sin\omega t\), each loaded alike. Show that its total power is constant, and that two identical coils at right angles fed with its currents make a rotating field. What does it cost in copper against single-phase?
  4. Name, without numbers, what besides copper grows with \(n\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1–2 are worked example 2 with every step; part 3 is two short identities; part 4 is a list.

Rung 2

\(|e^{i\theta} - 1| = 2|\sin(\theta/2)|\). For odd \(n\), \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor = (n - 1)/2\). For part 3, \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\), and the two coils' fields are \(\hat B\cos\omega t\) along \(1\) and \(\hat B\sin\omega t\) along \(i\).

Rung 3

First line: each conductor carries \(I = P/(nV_p\cos\varphi)\), and the loss is \(nI^2\rho l/A\).

Worked solution

Idea. More phases spread the current over more wires, but at fixed phase voltage the copper is unchanged; what changes with \(n\) is how large the voltage between two wires is, for a given phase voltage. Three phases have the smallest ratio of largest voltage to phase voltage, \(\sqrt3\), of any \(n\ge2\).

1. Unity power factor for brevity. \(I = P/(nV_p)\); loss \(\mathcal L = nI^2\rho l/A = P^2\rho l/(nV_p^2A)\); so \(A = P^2\rho l/(nV_p^2\mathcal L)\) and volume \(nAl = P^2\rho l^2/(V_p^2\mathcal L)\), independent of \(n\).

2. \(|V_j - V_k| = V_p|1 - e^{-i2\pi(k - j)/n}| = 2V_p\sin(\pi m/n)\), \(m = |j - k|\), largest for \(m = \lfloor n/2\rfloor\). So \(V_p = V_{\max}/(2s_n)\) with \(s_n = \sin(\pi\lfloor n/2\rfloor/n)\), and the volume is \(4s_n^2P^2\rho l^2/(V_{\max}^2\mathcal L)\). For \(n = 2\), \(s_2 = 1\): single-phase, factor 4. Relative copper: \(s_n^2\). Even \(n\): \(\lfloor n/2\rfloor/n = \tfrac12\), \(s_n^2 = 1\). Odd \(n\): \(\pi(n - 1)/2n = \pi/2 - \pi/2n\), so \(s_n^2 = \cos^2(\pi/2n)\), increasing in \(n\) since \(\pi/2n\) falls in \((0, \pi/2)\). Values: \(n = 3\): \(\cos^2(30^\circ) = 0.75\); \(n = 5\): 0.905; \(n = 7\): 0.950. The least is \(n = 3\).

3. Each circuit's load draws \(\sqrt2I\cos(\omega t - \varphi)\) and \(\sqrt2I\sin(\omega t - \varphi)\). Total \(p = 2VI[\cos\omega t\cos(\omega t - \varphi) + \sin\omega t\sin(\omega t - \varphi)] = 2VI\cos\varphi\): constant. Field: \(\hat B(\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t) = \hat Be^{i\omega t}\), of constant magnitude \(\hat B\), turning at \(\omega\). Copper: two independent single-phase circuits, each carrying \(P/2\) at voltage \(V\), each needing half the single-phase copper for half the loss: 100% in total. Seen as \(n = 4\) in star (the four conductors at \(\pm\) each voltage about a common point), it is the even case, also 100%. Two-phase buys constant power and a rotating field, as three-phase does, and saves no copper.

4. More windings in every generator, transformer and motor; more switches and protective devices; more separate conductors to insulate and support. All grow with \(n\); the copper is least at three. That is this model's argument; it makes no historical claim about how three was chosen.

Check. \(n = 3\) in part 2: \(V_{\max} = \sqrt3V_p\), the line voltage, and the ratio \(\tfrac34\) is P2's ✓. Units in part 1: \(\mathrm{W^2\,\Omega\,m\,m^2/(V^2\,W)} = \mathrm{m^3}\) ✓.

Star, delta and the rotating field

P4 A delta of heaters routine

exercises: line and phase quantities in a delta, the \(\sqrt3\) and the \(30^\circ\), what line currents do not determine

  1. Three \(40\ \Omega\) heating elements are connected in delta to the 230/400 V supply (use \(230\sqrt3\) V between lines). Find the phase currents, the line currents with their angles relative to \(V_a\), and the total power.
  2. Replace the delta by its star equivalent and recompute the line current and power.
  3. Show that the line currents of a delta never determine its phase currents uniquely: what can be added to \((I_{ab}, I_{bc}, I_{ca})\) without changing any line current?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two currents of order ten amperes, a power of order ten kilowatts, and in part 3 a one-parameter family.

Rung 2

Each element sees \(V_{ab} = \sqrt3e^{i\pi/6}V_a\). Line current \(I_a = I_{ab} - I_{ca} = \sqrt3e^{-i\pi/6}I_{ab}\). The star equivalent is \(Z_\Delta/3\).

Rung 3

First line: \(|I_{ab}| = 398.4/40 = 9.96\) A, in phase with \(V_{ab}\), i.e. at \(+30^\circ\) to \(V_a\).

Worked solution

Idea. A delta element sees a line voltage; the line current is the difference of two element currents.

1. \(I_{ab} = V_{ab}/40 = 9.96\) A \(\angle{+30^\circ}\) (resistive, in phase with \(V_{ab}\)). \(I_a = \sqrt3e^{-i\pi/6}I_{ab} = 17.25\) A \(\angle{0^\circ}\): in phase with \(V_a\), as a resistive balanced load must be. Power \(3|V_{ab}|^2/40 = 3\times158\,700/40 = 11.90\) kW.

2. Star equivalent \(40/3 = 13.33\ \Omega\) per phase: \(I_a = 230/13.33 = 17.25\) A, power \(3\times230\times17.25 = 11.90\) kW ✓.

3. \(I_a = I_{ab} - I_{ca}\), \(I_b = I_{bc} - I_{ab}\), \(I_c = I_{ca} - I_{bc}\). Adding the same \(J\) to all three phase currents changes no difference: a current \(J\) circulating round the delta is invisible from the lines. It is zero sequence in the phase currents (P12 meets it in the transformer's delta). What fixes it is the elements: with three equal impedances the voltage law round the delta reads \(Z(I_{ab} + I_{bc} + I_{ca}) = V_{ab} + V_{bc} + V_{ca} = 0\), so the circulating part must vanish.

Check. Units ✓. \(\sqrt3\times398.4\times17.25 = 11.90\) kW ✓, the \(\sqrt3V_{\text{line}}I_{\text{line}}\) rule. \(|I_a|/|I_{ab}| = 1.732\) ✓.

P5 Star–delta with complex weights routine

exercises: unit 04's transformation over \(\mathbb C\), \(Z_\Delta = 3Z_Y\), the workshop's motors, when no delta equivalent exists

  1. Eliminate the star point N′ of a star of admittances \(Y_a, Y_b, Y_c\) by the current law, and show that the currents into a, b, c are those of a delta with \(Y_{ab} = Y_aY_b/\Sigma\) and cyclically, \(\Sigma = Y_a + Y_b + Y_c\ne0\). Deduce \(Z_\Delta = 3Z_Y\) for a balanced load.
  2. Find the workshop motors' \(Z_Y\) and \(Z_\Delta\) from their 20 kW at power factor 0.7, and check the delta's complex power.
  3. Find the delta equivalent of the star \(Z_a = 10\ \Omega\), \(Z_b = 10i\ \Omega\), \(Z_c = -10i\ \Omega\). Then explain what goes wrong for \(Y_a = 0\), \(Z_b = 10i\), \(Z_c = -10i\ \Omega\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is three lines of algebra; part 2 two impedances; part 3 three impedances and a division by zero.

Rung 2

The current law at N′ gives its potential as \(\sum_kY_k\varphi_k/\Sigma\). The current into a is then \(Y_a(\varphi_a - \varphi_{N'})\); expand it as a sum over the other two terminals.

Rung 3

First line: \(I_a = Y_a\varphi_a - Y_a\sum_kY_k\varphi_k/\Sigma = \tfrac{Y_aY_b}{\Sigma}(\varphi_a - \varphi_b) + \tfrac{Y_aY_c}{\Sigma}(\varphi_a - \varphi_c)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The star–delta formula is the current law at one node, solved: nothing in it needs real weights.

1. \(\varphi_{N'} = \sum_kY_k\varphi_k/\Sigma\). Then \(I_a = Y_a(\varphi_a - \varphi_{N'}) = \tfrac{Y_a}{\Sigma}\big[(Y_a + Y_b + Y_c)\varphi_a - Y_a\varphi_a - Y_b\varphi_b - Y_c\varphi_c\big] = \tfrac{Y_aY_b}{\Sigma}(\varphi_a - \varphi_b) + \tfrac{Y_aY_c}{\Sigma}(\varphi_a - \varphi_c)\): a delta with \(Y_{ab} = Y_aY_b/\Sigma\), \(Y_{ac} = Y_aY_c/\Sigma\), and likewise at b, c. Balanced: \(Y_{ab} = Y^2/3Y = Y/3\), so \(Z_\Delta = 3Z_Y\).

2. Per phase \(S/3 = 6667 + 6801i\) VA; \(Z_Y = 230^2/(S/3)^* = 3.888 + 3.967i\ \Omega\); \(Z_\Delta = 11.66 + 11.90i\ \Omega\). Delta power: \(3|V_{ab}|^2/Z_\Delta^* = 3\times158\,700/(11.66 - 11.90i) = 20.00 + 20.40i\) kVA ✓.

3. \(Y = (0.1, -0.1i, 0.1i)\) S, \(\Sigma = 0.1\) S. \(Y_{ab} = 0.1(-0.1i)/0.1 = -0.1i\), so \(Z_{ab} = 10i\ \Omega\); \(Y_{bc} = (-0.1i)(0.1i)/0.1 = 0.1\), \(Z_{bc} = 10\ \Omega\); \(Y_{ca} = 0.1i\), \(Z_{ca} = -10i\ \Omega\). A resistor appears between b and c, though the star had none there: the delta's elements need not resemble the star's. With \(Y_a = 0\) and the other two a resonant pair, \(\Sigma = 0\): the star point is joined to b and c by an inductor and a capacitor whose admittances cancel, so the pair b–N′–c is a series circuit at resonance — zero impedance between b and c. The "delta" would need \(Y_{bc} = \infty\), a short circuit, and the formula's division by zero says so.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{S^2/S} = \mathrm S\) ✓. First case, impedance between a and b with c left open: star \(10 + 10i\ \Omega\); delta \(Z_{ab}\parallel(Z_{bc} + Z_{ca}) = 10i(10 - 10i)/10 = 10 + 10i\ \Omega\) ✓. Between b and c with a open: star \(10i - 10i = 0\); delta \(10\parallel(10i - 10i) = 0\) ✓.

P6 Proof: rotating fields, forwards and backwards medium

exercises: the rotating-field identity, negative sequence as a backward field, two coils in quadrature, the field of an unbalanced set

  1. Prove \(\sum_k\cos(\omega t - 2\pi k/3)\alpha^k = \tfrac32e^{i\omega t}\), and that the currents \(\cos(\omega t + 2\pi k/3)\) give \(\tfrac32e^{-i\omega t}\).
  2. Two identical coils with axes at right angles carry \(\sqrt2I\cos\omega t\) and \(\sqrt2I\sin\omega t\). Find their field at the centre.
  3. The three coils of the deck carry any currents \(i_k = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2I_ke^{i\omega t})\). Show that the field at the centre is \(B(t) = \tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}\kappa\big(I_1e^{i\omega t} + I_2^*e^{-i\omega t}\big)\), with \(I_1\), \(I_2\) the positive and negative sequence components. What does the zero sequence contribute?
  4. Describe the path of \(B(t)\) if the coils were fed with the workshop's line currents, and give the ratio of its largest to its smallest magnitude.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything is \(\cos x = \tfrac12(e^{ix} + e^{-ix})\) and sums of powers of \(\alpha\). Part 4: a sum of two circles traversed in opposite senses.

Rung 2

In part 3, \(\sum_kI_k\alpha^k = 3I_1\), and \(\sum_kI_k^*\alpha^k = \big(\sum_kI_k\alpha^{-k}\big)^* = (3I_2)^*\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: \(B = \kappa\sum_ki_k\alpha^k = \tfrac{\kappa}{\sqrt2}\sum_k(I_ke^{i\omega t} + I_k^*e^{-i\omega t})\alpha^k\).

Worked solution

Idea. A pulsating field along a fixed axis is two counter-rotating fields of half the size. The coils' geometry keeps the forward halves of a positive-sequence set and cancels the backward ones.

1. \(\cos(\omega t - 2\pi k/3)\alpha^k = \tfrac12(e^{i\omega t}\alpha^{-k}\alpha^k + e^{-i\omega t}\alpha^k\alpha^k) = \tfrac12(e^{i\omega t} + e^{-i\omega t}\alpha^{2k})\); \(\sum_k\alpha^{2k} = 0\), leaving \(\tfrac32e^{i\omega t}\). With \(+2\pi k/3\): \(\tfrac12(e^{i\omega t}\alpha^{2k} + e^{-i\omega t})\), leaving \(\tfrac32e^{-i\omega t}\).

2. \(B = \kappa\sqrt2I(\cos\omega t + i\sin\omega t) = \hat Be^{i\omega t}\) with \(\hat B = \sqrt2\kappa I\): constant magnitude \(\hat B\), not \(\tfrac32\hat B\).

3. \(i_k = \tfrac{\sqrt2}2(I_ke^{i\omega t} + I_k^*e^{-i\omega t})\). So \(B = \tfrac{\kappa}{\sqrt2}\big(e^{i\omega t}\sum_kI_k\alpha^k + e^{-i\omega t}\sum_kI_k^*\alpha^k\big) = \tfrac{\kappa}{\sqrt2}(3I_1e^{i\omega t} + 3I_2^*e^{-i\omega t})\). The zero sequence contributes \(I_0\sum_k\alpha^k = 0\): no field at the centre.

4. \(|I_1| = 54.39\) A, \(|I_2| = 12.02\) A. The sum of a forward circle and a smaller backward circle is an ellipse, with \(|B|\) between \(\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}\kappa(|I_1| - |I_2|)\) and \(\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}\kappa(|I_1| + |I_2|)\): ratio \(66.41/42.37 = 1.57\). The field still turns forwards on average, but pulsates in size at \(2\omega\) — what the deck's sequence-networks slide calls the backward field dragging on a rotor.

Check. A balanced set (\(I_1 = I_a\), \(I_2 = 0\)) in part 3 gives \(\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}\kappa I_ae^{i\omega t}\), which for real \(I_a = I\) is \(\tfrac32\hat Be^{i\omega t}\) ✓, part 1. Units: \(\kappa\) in T/A times A ✓.

Symmetrical components

P7 Proof: the DFT on ℤ/3 medium

exercises: invertibility of \(F\), uniqueness, the neutral theorem, a line-to-line load's sequences

  1. With \(F_{jk} = \alpha^{-jk}\), prove \(\bar FF = 3\cdot\mathbb 1\). Deduce that every triple \((I_a, I_b, I_c)\) has unique symmetrical components, and write the inverse.
  2. Prove \(|I_a|^2 + |I_b|^2 + |I_c|^2 = 3(|I_0|^2 + |I_1|^2 + |I_2|^2)\).
  3. Prove that the neutral current at a star point is \(3I_0\), and that a load connected only between lines draws no neutral current.
  4. A single-phase load across lines a and b draws \(I\) from a and returns it on b. Find its symmetrical components.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Matrix entries are sums of three powers of a cube root of unity; part 2 is a norm identity; part 4 is three numbers.

Rung 2

\((\bar FF)_{jm} = \sum_k\alpha^{k(j - m)}\). For part 2: \(F/\sqrt3\) is unitary. For part 3: a surface round a load that touches only the lines.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: if \(j = m\) every term is 1; otherwise \(\beta = \alpha^{j - m}\ne1\) has \(\beta^3 = 1\), so \(1 + \beta + \beta^2 = (1 - \beta^3)/(1 - \beta) = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. The columns of \(F\) are orthogonal in \(\mathbb C^3\) with squared length 3: a scaled orthonormal basis.

1. As in rung 3, \(\bar FF = 3\mathbb 1\), so \(F^{-1} = \bar F/3\). The symmetrical components are \((I_0, I_1, I_2) = F^{-1}(I_a, I_b, I_c)\): they exist and are unique. Row \(k\) of \(\bar F\) is \((1, \alpha^k, \alpha^{2k})\), giving the three definitions; the inverse is \(I_a = I_0 + I_1 + I_2\), \(I_b = I_0 + \alpha^2I_1 + \alpha I_2\), \(I_c = I_0 + \alpha I_1 + \alpha^2I_2\).

2. \(U = F/\sqrt3\) has \(U^{\mathsf H}U = \bar FF/3 = \mathbb 1\) (\(F\) is symmetric), so \(\|Us\|^2 = \|s\|^2\). With \(I = Fs = \sqrt3Us\): \(\|I\|^2 = 3\|s\|^2\).

3. The current law at the star point: \(I_N = I_a + I_b + I_c = 3I_0\) by the definition of \(I_0\). For a load touching only the lines, the current law on a closed surface round it gives \(I_a + I_b + I_c = 0\), so \(I_0 = 0\) and \(I_N = 0\).

4. \((I, -I, 0)\): \(I_0 = 0\); \(I_1 = \tfrac13I(1 - \alpha) = \tfrac{I}{\sqrt3}e^{-i\pi/6}\); \(I_2 = \tfrac13I(1 - \alpha^2) = \tfrac{I}{\sqrt3}e^{+i\pi/6}\). Equal positive and negative parts, no zero sequence.

Check. Part 4 against part 2: \(|I|^2 + |I|^2 = 2|I|^2\) and \(3(0 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac13)|I|^2 = 2|I|^2\) ✓. Reconstruct: \(I_1 + I_2 = \tfrac{I}{\sqrt3}\cdot2\cos30^\circ = I\) ✓.

P8 Proof: circulant matrices medium

exercises: rotational symmetry as commuting with the shift, eigenvectors of the shift, sequence impedances of a cable

  1. Let \((Px)_j = x_{j+1}\) (indices mod 3). Prove that a \(3\times3\) matrix \(Z\) satisfies \(PZ = ZP\) if and only if \(Z = c_0\mathbb 1 + c_1P + c_2P^2\), and that then \(F^{-1}ZF = \operatorname{diag}(Z_0, Z_1, Z_2)\) with \(Z_k = c_0 + c_1\alpha^{-k} + c_2\alpha^{-2k}\).
  2. Show that if \(Z\) is also symmetric, \(Z_1 = Z_2\).
  3. A short three-core cable, cores at the corners of an equilateral triangle, has (assumed) \(Z_s = 0.10 + 0.40i\ \Omega\) and \(Z_m = 0.15i\ \Omega\). Find its sequence impedances. If the cores were laid flat, side by side, would the deck's method still diagonalise \(Z\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two short proofs and two complex numbers; the last part is yes or no, with a reason.

Rung 2

\(PZ = ZP\) means \(Z_{j+1,m+1} = Z_{jm}\). The column \(f_k\) of \(F\) satisfies \(Pf_k = \alpha^{-k}f_k\). Symmetric means \(c_1 = c_2\).

Rung 3

First line: \((PZ)_{jm} = Z_{j+1,m}\) and \((ZP)_{jm} = Z_{j,m-1}\), so commuting says \(Z_{j+1,m} = Z_{j,m-1}\) for all \(j, m\).

Worked solution

Idea. Rotational symmetry is commuting with the rotation \(P\); \(P\)'s eigenvectors are the Fourier vectors; anything that commutes with \(P\) and is a polynomial in it shares them.

1. From rung 3, \(Z_{jm}\) depends only on \(m - j\) mod 3; call it \(c_{m-j}\). Since \((P^d)_{jm} = 1\) exactly when \(m = j + d\), \(Z = \sum_dc_dP^d\). Conversely any polynomial in \(P\) commutes with \(P\). With \((f_k)_j = \alpha^{-jk}\): \((Pf_k)_j = \alpha^{-(j+1)k} = \alpha^{-k}(f_k)_j\), so \(Zf_k = (c_0 + c_1\alpha^{-k} + c_2\alpha^{-2k})f_k\). The columns of \(F\) are eigenvectors, hence \(ZF = F\operatorname{diag}(Z_k)\).

2. Symmetric: \(Z_{01} = Z_{10}\), i.e. \(c_1 = c_{-1} = c_2\). Then \(Z_1 = c_0 + c_1(\alpha^{-1} + \alpha^{-2}) = c_0 + c_1(\alpha^2 + \alpha) = c_0 - c_1\) and \(Z_2 = c_0 + c_1(\alpha^{-2} + \alpha^{-4}) = c_0 - c_1\): equal.

3. \(Z_0 = Z_s + 2Z_m = 0.10 + 0.70i\ \Omega\); \(Z_1 = Z_2 = Z_s - Z_m = 0.10 + 0.25i\ \Omega\). Flat: the outer pair are further apart than either neighbouring pair, so their mutual impedance differs; \(Z\) is symmetric but not circulant, and the Fourier vectors no longer diagonalise it — the sequences couple. (Lines can be made symmetric on average by exchanging the conductors' positions along the route, which Bergen & Vittal treat in ch. 3 under transposition; here it is only the remedy the algebra asks for.)

Check. Trace: \(Z_0 + Z_1 + Z_2 = 3Z_s\) ✓ (\(0.30 + 1.20i\)). Limit \(Z_m = 0\): all three equal \(Z_s\) ✓. A balanced current \(I(1, \alpha^2, \alpha)\) through the triangular cable drops \(Z_1I\) per phase: \(Z_sI + Z_m(\alpha^2 + \alpha)I = (Z_s - Z_m)I\) ✓.

P11 The broken neutral medium

exercises: the floating star point by the current law, voltages and powers on an unbalanced star, why the lightest load suffers

  1. Derive the voltage of a star point N′ joined to lines a, b, c by admittances \(Y_a, Y_b, Y_c\) and to the neutral by \(Y_N\).
  2. Resistive loads of 460 W, 2300 W and 1150 W (rated at 230 V) sit on a, b and c. Find the neutral current when it is intact. Then break it, and find \(V_{N'}\), each load's voltage and power, and which appliance is at risk.
  3. With the neutral still broken, the 1150 W load is switched off. What do the other two see now?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A weighted mean; a few amperes in the intact neutral; a star point displaced by a sizeable fraction of 230 V; in part 3, two loads in series across a line voltage.

Rung 2

The current law at N′ with N as reference. The loads' resistances are \(230^2/P\): 115, 23 and 46 \(\Omega\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\sum_k(V_k - V_{N'})Y_k + (0 - V_{N'})Y_N = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. The neutral pins N′ to earth. Without it, N′ goes to the admittance-weighted mean of the three phase voltages, nearest the heaviest load, and the load across the triangle from it gets the most voltage.

1. \(V_{N'} = \sum_kY_kV_k/(Y_a + Y_b + Y_c + Y_N)\).

2. Intact: currents 2, 10, 5 A at the phases' angles; \(I_N = |2 + 10\alpha^2 + 5\alpha| = 7.0\) A. Broken (\(Y_N = 0\)): \(Y = (8.696, 43.48, 21.74)\) mS; \(V_{N'} = 230(8.696 + 43.48\alpha^2 + 21.74\alpha)/73.91 = 94.7\) V \(\angle{-141.8^\circ}\). Loads: a sees \(|230 - V_{N'}| = 310.0\) V and takes \(310.0^2/115 = 835.6\) W, 1.82 times its rating, at 35% over-voltage; b sees 146.3 V, 931.1 W; c sees 260.9 V, 1480.3 W. The 460 W appliance on the lightly loaded phase is the one at risk.

3. Now a and b are in series across \(V_{ab}\), 398.4 V: current \(398.4/138 = 2.887\) A. Load a gets \(2.887\times115 = 332.0\) V (44% over) and 958 W, 2.08 times its rating; load b gets 66.4 V. The lighter the load, the larger its share of the series voltage.

Check. With the neutral broken the three load currents must sum to zero: \(\sum_k(V_k - V_{N'})Y_k = \sum_kY_kV_k - V_{N'}\sum_kY_k = 0\) ✓ by construction. Balanced limit: equal \(Y_k\) give \(V_{N'} = 0\) ✓. Part 3: \(332.0 + 66.4 = 398.4\) V ✓.
