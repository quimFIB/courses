Three-phase power: constant power, fewer conductors, and a field that rotates

Three sinusoids a third of a cycle apart, on three wires. The total power stops pulsing, the copper falls to 75%, three fixed coils make a field that turns — and any unbalance splits, by a Fourier transform on three points, into three balanced parts, only one of which needs the neutral.

11 · balanced sets

Three sinusoids, a third of a cycle apart

a balanced three-phase set

Three RMS phasors of equal magnitude, in phase sequence a, b, c with b lagging a by $120^\circ$:

$$V_a = V,\quad V_b = \alpha^2V,\quad V_c = \alpha V,$$

with $\alpha = e^{i2\pi/3}$. So $v_k(t) = \sqrt2|V|\cos(\omega t - 2\pi k/3)$ for $k = 0, 1, 2$ ↔ a, b, c, with $V$ real.

the operator α $$\alpha^3 = 1,\qquad 1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 = 0 .$$

Proof. $\alpha^3 = e^{i2\pi} = 1$. Then $(1 - \alpha)(1 + \alpha + \alpha^2) = 1 - \alpha^3 = 0$ and $\alpha\ne1$. $\square$

$\alpha^2 = \alpha^{-1} = \bar\alpha = e^{-i2\pi/3}$: multiplying by $\alpha^2$ turns a phasor back by $120^\circ$, which is "lags".

Left: the three voltages over one 20 ms cycle at 230 V RMS (peak 325.3 V). Right: their phasors. The sum of the three at any instant is zero — the right-hand identity, read off the picture.

Why α and not a. Power engineers write the operator $e^{i2\pi/3}$ as $a$ (and $i$ as $j$); Bergen & Vittal do both. This course writes $i$ and $\alpha$, so that $\alpha$ never collides with phase a. The phase sequence, the factor $\alpha^2$ for b, and the 400/230 V values are fixed for the course (AUTHORING.md); unit 05 promised that "the 400/230 V three-phase system is unit 11's".

Why the sum is zero at every instant. $v_a + v_b + v_c = \operatorname{Re}\big(\sqrt2V(1 + \alpha^2 + \alpha)e^{i\omega t}\big) = 0$. On the left of the figure, at every $t$ the three curves add to nothing: where a peaks at $+325$ V, b and c are each at $-162.6$ V.

The real system. The three voltages come from one machine: a generator with three windings set a third of a turn apart round its stator, past which one rotating magnet sweeps (branch 12 builds it). A third of a turn of the rotor is a third of a cycle of each voltage. Unit 07's transformers pass the set down to 400 V without changing its shape.

The other sequence. Swap the labels b and c and the set reads $V$, $\alpha V$, $\alpha^2V$: b now leads. That is the negative sequence of section 6, and section 4 shows it turns a motor backwards.

Star: four wires, two voltages

star connection (Y)

Three sources (or loads) each joined at one end to a common star point. The star point's wire is the neutral, N; the other three are the lines.

  • phase voltage: line to neutral, $V_a$, $V_b$, $V_c$ — 230 V;
  • line voltage: line to line, $V_{ab} = V_a - V_b$ and cyclically — 400 V.

A four-wire system offers both: a house takes one line and N at 230 V; a motor takes the three lines at 400 V.

two meanings of "phase"

"Phase" names a wire (phase b), an angle ($\varphi$), and in "phase voltage" the voltage across one source's winding. The context says which; the deck writes "line a" for the wire when it matters.

A star source. Each phase voltage is marked + at its line and − at N. The line voltage $V_{ab}$ is measured between two lines and never touches N.

The European feeder, from nothing. From the street transformer's 400 V side run three line conductors and one neutral. At the transformer the neutral is joined to the star point of its 400 V windings and to an earth electrode, so the neutral sits near earth potential. Single-phase customers connect between one line and the neutral. That is all the physics here needs; how earthing is arranged downstream is governed by installation rules this deck does not cite.

Kirchhoff's voltage law decides the line voltage. Round the loop N → a → b → N, $V_{ab} = V_a - V_b$: unit 03's voltage law with node potentials $\Phi_a - \Phi_b = (\Phi_a - \Phi_N) - (\Phi_b - \Phi_N)$. No assumption about what is between the lines.

Source symbols. The circle with a sine wave is an AC voltage source, as in unit 05; its phasor is written beside it.

The √3 and the 30°

line voltages of a balanced star $$V_{ab} = V_a - V_b = \sqrt3\,e^{i\pi/6}\,V_a ,$$

and cyclically: the line voltages are a balanced set, $\sqrt3$ times larger, leading by $30^\circ$.

proof

$1 - \alpha^2 = 1 - (-\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}2i) = \tfrac32 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}2i = \sqrt3\big(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2 + \tfrac12i\big) = \sqrt3e^{i\pi/6}$. $\square$

The figure's triangle has sides $V$, $V$ and $|V_{ab}|$, with $120^\circ$ between the first two; the cosine rule gives $|V_{ab}|^2 = V^2 + V^2 + V^2 = 3V^2$.

rounded names

$\sqrt3\times230 = 398.4$ V, not 400. "230/400 V" are the nominal values IEC 60038 lists, each rounded; the deck computes with $230$ and $230\sqrt3$.

$V_{ab}$ drawn from the origin, and dashed as the side of the triangle from the tip of $V_b$ to the tip of $V_a$.

Units. Both sides in volts; $\sqrt3e^{i\pi/6}$ is a pure number ✓. Limit: if $V_b = V_a$ (no phase difference) the line voltage would vanish; at $120^\circ$ it is $\sqrt3$ times larger; at $180^\circ$ (two wires in antiphase, the single-phase centre-tapped case) it would be 2 times larger. Section 3 uses exactly that $2\sin(\theta/2)$ dependence.

Unit 07's 400 kV, landed. Unit 07 said that "a real 400 kV line is three-phase, with 400 kV between any two phases and $400/\sqrt3 = 231$ kV from each to earth". That is this slide at 400 kV: a transmission voltage is named by its line voltage.

The figures to more places. $230\sqrt3 = 398.37$ V; $400/\sqrt3 = 230.94$ V. IEC 60038:2009, Table 1, lists the three-phase four-wire system as 230/400 V. Whichever is taken as exact, the other is rounded by 0.4%. The workshop uses 230 V line to neutral throughout, as unit 06 did.

Delta: phase currents and line currents

delta connection (Δ)

Three sources or loads on the sides of a triangle, each between two lines. No star point, no neutral. Each element sees a line voltage; its own current is a phase current $I_{ab}$, $I_{bc}$, $I_{ca}$; the wire carries a line current $I_a$, $I_b$, $I_c$.

The current law at corner a: $I_a = I_{ab} - I_{ca}$. For a balanced set $I_{bc} = \alpha^2I_{ab}$, $I_{ca} = \alpha I_{ab}$, so

$$I_a = (1 - \alpha)I_{ab} = \sqrt3\,e^{-i\pi/6}I_{ab}.$$

The star's $\sqrt3$ and $30^\circ$, now between currents, and lagging: $1 - \alpha$ is the conjugate of $1 - \alpha^2$.

Star: line current = phase current, line voltage = $\sqrt3\,\times$ phase voltage. Delta: the reverse.

A delta load (IEC rectangles), and its currents' phasors. Reference arrows: $I_a$ into corner a, $I_{ab}$ from a to b.

The current law at a. Two delta elements meet at corner a: $ab$, whose reference current leaves a, and $ca$, whose reference current enters a. The line brings $I_a$ in. Unit 03's current law: $I_a + I_{ca} - I_{ab} = 0$.

Why the phase currents form a balanced set. Each delta element sees a line voltage, and the line voltages are a balanced set (previous slide). Three equal impedances $Z$ then give $I_{ab} = V_{ab}/Z$ and cyclically — a balanced set.

The arithmetic. $1 - \alpha = 1 - (-\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}2i) = \tfrac32 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}2i = \sqrt3e^{-i\pi/6}$. Units: amperes on both sides ✓.

Check by power. A balanced delta load absorbs $3|V_{ab}||I_{ab}|\cos\varphi = 3(\sqrt3V)(|I_a|/\sqrt3)\cos\varphi = 3V|I_a|\cos\varphi$: the same total as a star with phase voltage $V$ and line current $|I_a|$. The total power of a balanced load is $\sqrt3\,V_{\text{line}}I_{\text{line}}\cos\varphi$ however it is connected.

A balanced load's neutral carries nothing

A balanced star load draws a balanced set of line currents, $I_a$, $\alpha^2I_a$, $\alpha I_a$. The current law at its star point sends the rest down the neutral:

$$I_N = I_a + I_b + I_c = (1 + \alpha^2 + \alpha)I_a = 0 .$$

So in a balanced system the neutral is idle. Three wires do the work of three single-phase circuits, which would need six. The returns cancel.

check: instantaneous

$i_a + i_b + i_c = \sqrt2|I_a|\sum_k\cos(\omega t - \varphi - 2\pi k/3) = 0$ at every instant, not just on average — the figure's polygon, rotating.

The workshop's motors are a balanced load: they need no neutral at all. The kiln and the lighting will.

The motors' line currents, 41.4 A each, lagging their voltages by $45.6^\circ$. Head to tail they close: nothing is left for the neutral.

The current law, stated. At the load's star point, three line currents arrive (reference arrows inward) and the neutral current leaves: $I_a + I_b + I_c - I_N = 0$ (unit 03). For a balanced set the left side is $(1 + \alpha^2 + \alpha)I_a = 0$.

Six wires to three. Three separate single-phase loads would each need a go and a return conductor. Join the three returns into one: it carries $I_a + I_b + I_c$, which is zero when balanced, so it can even be left out. That is the whole economic idea of three-phase in one line; section 3 turns it into the 75% figure.

What the neutral is for. A four-wire system keeps the neutral because real loads are not balanced: a house on one phase is a single-phase load. Section 6 proves that only one part of an unbalance — the zero sequence — uses it.

The per-phase equivalent

the per-phase equivalent

A balanced star source feeds a balanced star load $Z_Y$ through three identical lines $Z_\ell$, and a neutral of any impedance $Z_N$ (even an open circuit). Then the two star points are at one potential, $I_N = 0$, and phase a is a single loop: $I_a = V_a/(Z_\ell + Z_Y)$. Phases b and c are the same times $\alpha^2$ and $\alpha$.

proof

Take N as reference and let N′ be at $U$. The current law at N′: $\sum_k(V_k - U)Y + (0 - U)Y_N = 0$ with $Y = 1/(Z_\ell + Z_Y)$. So $U(3Y + Y_N) = Y\sum_kV_k = 0$, and $U = 0$. $\square$

Solve one phase, multiply powers by 3. Unit 13 solves whole grids this way.

Above, the balanced three-phase circuit. Below, its per-phase equivalent: a line-to-neutral source, one line, one load.

Full proof

Nodes: N (the source star point, reference, potential 0), the three line ends at the source (potentials $V_k$, set by the ideal sources), and N′ (the load star point, potential $U$). Each phase $k$ is a series path from the source terminal to N′ of impedance $Z_\ell + Z_Y$, carrying $(V_k - U)Y$ towards N′. The neutral carries $(0 - U)Y_N$ towards N′ (with $Y_N = 0$ for an open neutral). Unit 03's current law at N′, all currents counted inward: $\sum_k(V_k - U)Y - UY_N = 0$. Hence $U = Y\sum_kV_k/(3Y + Y_N)$, and the balanced sum vanishes. Denominator non-zero: $3Y + Y_N$ has positive real part for passive elements (unit 05), except in the degenerate case where all are pure reactances tuned to cancel, which is set aside. The formula for $U$ is section 7's floating star point; here its numerator vanishes.

Where the hypothesis enters. Balance is used twice: equal $Y$ in all three phases (so $Y$ factors out of the sum), and a balanced source (so the sum is zero). Break either and N′ moves — section 7 measures how far.

11 · constant power

Balanced three-phase power does not pulse

theorem · constant power

Phase $k$ carries $v_k = \sqrt2V\cos(\omega t - \tfrac{2\pi k}3)$ and $i_k = \sqrt2I\cos(\omega t - \tfrac{2\pi k}3 - \varphi)$. Then

$$\boxed{\,p(t) = \sum_{k=0}^2v_ki_k = 3VI\cos\varphi\,}\quad\text{at every }t.$$
proof

$2\cos x\cos(x - \varphi) = \cos\varphi + \cos(2x - \varphi)$ (unit 06). With $x = \omega t - 2\pi k/3$, the oscillating terms sum to $VI\operatorname{Re}\big[e^{i(2\omega t - \varphi)}(1 + \alpha^{-2} + \alpha^{-4})\big]$, and $1 + \alpha^{-2} + \alpha^{-4} = 1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 = 0$. $\square$

At $\varphi = 0$: $\sum_k\cos^2(\omega t - 2\pi k/3) = \tfrac32$. Units: $\mathrm{V\,A} = \mathrm W$ ✓. The mean is unit 06's $P$, three times; what is new is that there is nothing else.

The motors, computed: each phase's power swings from −2.86 to +16.19 kW at 100 Hz, a third of a 50 Hz cycle behind the last. Their sum never moves.

Full proof

$v_ki_k = 2VI\cos x_k\cos(x_k - \varphi)$ with $x_k = \omega t - 2\pi k/3$. Product to sum: $= VI[\cos\varphi + \cos(2x_k - \varphi)]$. Sum over $k$: $3VI\cos\varphi + VI\sum_k\cos(2\omega t - \varphi - 4\pi k/3)$. Write the cosine as a real part: $\sum_k\cos(2\omega t - \varphi - 4\pi k/3) = \operatorname{Re}\big[e^{i(2\omega t - \varphi)}\sum_ke^{-i4\pi k/3}\big]$, and $e^{-i4\pi k/3} = \alpha^{-2k}$. Since $\alpha^3 = 1$, $\alpha^{-2} = \alpha$ and $\alpha^{-4} = \alpha^{-1} = \alpha^2$; the sum is $1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 = 0$. $\square$

Why it works, in words. The pulsation of $p_k$ is at $2\omega$. Shifting phase $k$ by $2\pi k/3$ in $\omega t$ shifts its pulsation by $4\pi k/3$ in $2\omega t$: the three pulsations form a balanced set themselves (in the other sequence), and a balanced set sums to zero. The trick needs the phase shifts to stay distinct after doubling: with two phases at $180^\circ$ (the single-phase centre-tapped supply), doubling gives $360^\circ$ and the pulsations add instead.

Energy check. The mean of $p(t)$ must be the sum of the three phases' $P$, each $VI\cos\varphi$ by unit 06 ✓. A limit: $\varphi = \pm\pi/2$, a balanced pure reactance, gives $p(t) = 0$ at every instant — next slide.

The figure's numbers. Per phase $P/3 = 6.667$ kW and $|S|/3 = 9.524$ kVA, so $p_k$ runs between $6.667 - 9.524 = -2.857$ and $16.190$ kW. Scale 6 px per kW; the curves were computed, not sketched.

11 · constant power

Where the reactive power went

Unit 06 split each phase's power in two. For phase $k$, with $\theta_k = 2\omega t - 4\pi k/3$:

$$p_k = \tfrac P3(1 + \cos\theta_k) + \tfrac Q3\sin\theta_k .$$

Both oscillating parts sum to zero over $k$. Each phase still has its reactive power $Q/3$: its own fields still fill and empty twice a cycle. But the three do it in turn, so the energy passes from phase to phase, and none returns to the source in total.

stored energy is constant

For three equal inductances $L$: $\sum_k\tfrac12Li_k^2 = \tfrac12L\cdot2I^2\cdot\tfrac32 = \tfrac32LI^2$, constant — the $\cos^2$ identity again.

Q survives

$Q = 3VI\sin\varphi = 20.40$ kvar is still defined per phase and still costs current: 41.41 A, not $20\,000/690 = 28.99$ A. Correcting the power factor still pays.

The motors' reactive parts, $Q/3 = 6.80$ kvar each, computed. Each phase's fields fill and empty; the three together hold a constant total.

The decomposition. Unit 06's slide $p(t)$ splits in two proved $p = VI\cos\varphi(1 + \cos2\omega t) + VI\sin\varphi\sin2\omega t$ for $v = \sqrt2V\cos\omega t$. Replace $\omega t$ by $\omega t - 2\pi k/3$, so $2\omega t$ becomes $2\omega t - 4\pi k/3$, and use $VI\cos\varphi = P/3$, $VI\sin\varphi = Q/3$. The sums $\sum_k\cos(2\omega t - 4\pi k/3)$ and $\sum_k\sin(2\omega t - 4\pi k/3)$ are the real and imaginary parts of $e^{i2\omega t}(1 + \alpha + \alpha^2) = 0$.

The stored energy, in the workshop's numbers. Model each phase of the motors as the series $R$–$L$ of $Z_Y = 3.888 + 3.967i\ \Omega$: $L = 3.967/314.16 = 12.63$ mH. Each phase stores $\tfrac12Li_k^2$, swinging between 0 and $\tfrac12L(\sqrt2I)^2 = LI^2 = 21.7$ J at 100 Hz, with mean 10.8 J; the total is $\tfrac32\times0.01263\times41.41^2 = 32.5$ J, constant. Unit 06's theorem said $\langle W_m\rangle - \langle W_e\rangle = Q/2\omega = 32.5$ J for the single-phase workshop, pushed in and out a hundred times a second by the supply. Here the same 32.5 J sits in the motors permanently, handed round the three phases.

The general statement. For any balanced load (any network per phase), unit 06's $Q = 2\omega(\langle W_m\rangle - \langle W_e\rangle)$ holds phase by phase, and the instantaneous total of the stored energies is constant: the total input $p(t)$ is constant and so is the total dissipation (each resistor's $Ri^2$ summed over the three phases is a $\cos^2$ sum), so their difference, the rate of change of stored energy, is zero.

Energy flow. Unit 01's proof that the Poynting flux into a component is $vi$ summed over the leads in its step 5; with four leads, under the same quasi-static hypotheses, it gives $\sum_k\varphi_ki_k$ over the four, and since the four currents sum to zero (the same proof's step 4) the potentials may be measured from the neutral: $\sum_kv_ki_k = p(t)$. So for the balanced motors the total Poynting flux into any closed surface round them is constant in time — draw the surface across the four-core cable and the energy arrives along it steadily, never reversing. In unit 06's single-phase cable it ran back towards the supply for $\varphi/\pi = 25.3\%$ of each cycle. With the kiln and lighting on, $p(t)$ pulses again (problem P1), and so does this flux.

section 3

Fewer conductors

Deliver the same power, over the same distance, with the same loss and the same voltage between conductors. Two wires need a third more copper than three. Why three, and not five or six, is a minimisation.

11 · fewer conductors

Three wires, 75% of the copper

Power $P$ at unity power factor, length $l$, resistivity $\rho$, voltage $V$ between conductors, total loss $\mathcal L$.

  • Single-phase, two wires of section $A_1$: $I = P/V$; $\mathcal L = 2I^2\rho l/A_1 = 2P^2\rho l/(V^2A_1)$.
  • Three-phase, three wires of section $A_3$, line voltage $V$: $I = P/(\sqrt3V)$; $\mathcal L = 3I^2\rho l/A_3 = P^2\rho l/(V^2A_3)$.
the 75% figure

Equal loss gives $A_3 = A_1/2$. Copper: $3A_3l = \tfrac32A_1l$ against $2A_1l$:

$$\frac{\text{three-phase copper}}{\text{single-phase copper}} = \frac34 .$$

Units: $\mathrm{W^2\,\Omega\,m\,/\,(V^2\,m^2)} = \mathrm W$ ✓. Any common power factor scales both currents alike; the ratio stands.

A four-wire system adds a neutral that, balanced, carries nothing — and copper of its own.

Cross-sections to scale: equal power, length, loss and voltage between any two conductors. Three wires of half the area: 75% of the copper.

The comparison, stated fairly. The previous section's "a sixth" compared at equal phase voltage and equal conductor size — not equal anything that matters to a line's designer. Here the constraints are the ones that set a line's cost: the same power, delivered over the same distance, with the same loss, and with the same voltage between conductors, which is what the insulation and clearances must withstand (unit 07's section on what limits voltage).

The three-phase current. Line voltage $V$ means phase voltage $V/\sqrt3$; each phase delivers $P/3$, so $I = (P/3)/(V/\sqrt3) = P/(\sqrt3V)$. Then $3I^2 = P^2/V^2$.

Energy check. Both lines dissipate the same $\mathcal L$ by construction, and both deliver $P$; the sources supply $P + \mathcal L$ in each case. A limit: as $\mathcal L\to0$ both cross-sections grow without bound, in the same ratio.

The neutral. A four-wire low-voltage system carries a neutral, which carries no current when the load is balanced and returns the unbalance otherwise (section 6). How large it is made is a design choice this deck does not claim from a source; problem P2 computes the copper fraction for an assumed half-size neutral (87.5%).

Worked: why three, and not two or six

worked example 2 — $n$ phases in star, at fixed power, loss and largest voltage

1. Current. $n$ wires at phase voltage $V_p$, phasors $V_pe^{-i2\pi k/n}$: each carries $I = P/(nV_p)$.

2. Copper. Loss $nI^2\rho l/A = P^2\rho l/(nV_p^2A)$; at fixed loss $\mathcal L$, $A = P^2\rho l/(nV_p^2\mathcal L)$, and the copper $nAl = P^2\rho l^2/(V_p^2\mathcal L)$: independent of $n$, $\propto1/V_p^2$.

3. Insulation. $|V_j - V_k| = 2V_p\sin(\pi|j - k|/n)$, largest at $|j - k| = \lfloor n/2\rfloor$: $V_{\max} = 2V_p\sin(\pi\lfloor n/2\rfloor/n)$.

4. At fixed $V_{\max}$, copper $\propto4\sin^2(\pi\lfloor n/2\rfloor/n)$: 4 for every even $n$ (single-phase is $n = 2$); 3 for $n = 3$; 3.618 for 5; 3.802 for 7.

5. Minimum. For odd $n$, $\lfloor n/2\rfloor = (n - 1)/2$ and the factor is $4\cos^2(\pi/2n)$, increasing in $n$. So $n = 3$ is the least copper of all.

Copper relative to single-phase, computed from step 4. Even $n$ pairs every wire with its opposite and gains nothing.

Step 3. $V_j - V_k = V_pe^{-i2\pi j/n}(1 - e^{-i2\pi(k - j)/n})$, and $|1 - e^{i\theta}| = 2|\sin(\theta/2)|$. With $m = |j - k|\in\{1, …, n - 1\}$, $\sin(\pi m/n)$ is largest at the $m$ closest to $n/2$, i.e. $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ (or $\lceil n/2\rceil$, which gives the same sine). For $n = 3$: $2\sin(\pi/3) = \sqrt3$, the section 1 result.

Step 5. $\pi(n - 1)/2n = \pi/2 - \pi/2n$, and $\sin(\pi/2 - x) = \cos x$. As $n$ grows, $\pi/2n$ falls and $\cos^2(\pi/2n)$ rises towards 1. Even $n$ gives exactly 4. So the factor is at least 3 for every $n\ge2$, with equality only at $n = 3$. Percentages: $3/4 = 75\%$; $3.618/4 = 90.5\%$; $3.802/4 = 95.0\%$.

Check against the previous slide. $n = 2$: two wires at $\pm V_p$ about a star point, $V_{\max} = 2V_p$ — the single-phase line with $V = V_{\max}$. $n = 3$: $V_{\max} = \sqrt3V_p$ is the line voltage, and the ratio is $3/4$ ✓.

What is not copper. More phases need more windings in every generator and transformer, more switches, more insulators and towers' worth of separate conductors. Those costs rise with $n$; the copper is least at $n = 3$; so three is the natural choice on this argument. That is a statement about this model's costs. No historical claim about who chose three, or why, is made here. section 4

A field that rotates

Three fixed coils, fed with a balanced set of currents, make a field of constant size turning at $\omega$. One line of complex arithmetic proves it, and the same line shows why swapping two wires reverses a motor.

11 · rotating field

Three coils, one rotating field

Three identical coils, axes in one plane at $0^\circ$, $120^\circ$, $240^\circ$ through a common centre. Identify the plane with $\mathbb C$. By Biot–Savart (MR 23) coil $k$'s field at the centre lies along its axis and is linear in its current: $\vec B_k = \kappa\,i_k(t)\,e^{i2\pi k/3}$. Feed $i_k = \sqrt2I\cos(\omega t - 2\pi k/3)$ and write $\hat B = \sqrt2\kappa I$.

the rotating field $$B(t) = \hat B\sum_k\cos\!\big(\omega t - \tfrac{2\pi k}3\big)\alpha^k = \tfrac32\hat B\,e^{i\omega t}.$$
proof

$\cos x = \tfrac12(e^{ix} + e^{-ix})$: the terms are $\tfrac12\hat B\big(e^{i\omega t} + e^{-i\omega t}\alpha^{2k}\big)$, and $\sum_k\alpha^{2k} = 1 + \alpha^2 + \alpha^4 = 0$. $\square$

Constant magnitude $\tfrac32\hat B$, turning anticlockwise at $\omega$. Units: $\kappa$ in T/A, $B$ in T ✓.

Three coils seen edge-on. Each makes a field at the centre along its own axis, pulsating with its current; the arrows are the axes' unit vectors $e^{i2\pi k/3}$.

The linearity, from MR 23. MR 23's slide Biot–Savart, and the two equations it hides gives $\vec B$ as an integral linear in $\vec J$, so a coil's field is its current times a fixed vector field, and the fields of several coils add. On the axis of a coil that is symmetric under rotation about that axis, the field points along the axis: a transverse component would pick out a direction the coil does not have. So $\vec B_k(\text{centre}) = \kappa i_k\hat e_k$ with $\hat e_k$ the axis and $\kappa$ a constant set by the coil's geometry and distance, the same for three identical coils. The currents change at 50 Hz, slowly enough that the field at each instant is the magnetostatic one (unit 02's lumped hypotheses).

What is idealised. No iron, and only the field at one point. A machine shapes the field across a whole air gap with iron and distributed windings; that the same $\tfrac32$ and the same rotation appear there is branch 12's business, which turns this field into torque.

Energy check. The field at the centre has constant magnitude, so its energy density $B^2/2\mu_0$ there is constant; the coils' total stored energy $\tfrac12L\sum i_k^2$ (uncoupled coils) is $\tfrac32LI^2$, constant — section 2's identity again. The rotating field is maintained without the supply pumping energy in and out of it.

Speed. One turn per cycle: at 50 Hz, 50 turns a second, 3000 a minute, for this arrangement of one coil per phase. Arrangements with more poles turn slower; branch 12 derives how.

Swap two wires, and the field turns back

Exchange the supply leads of coils b and c. Coil $k$ now carries $\sqrt2I\cos(\omega t + 2\pi k/3)$, and the same expansion gives

$$B(t) = \tfrac12\hat B\sum_k\big(e^{i\omega t}\alpha^{2k} + e^{-i\omega t}\big) = \tfrac32\hat B\,e^{-i\omega t}.$$

The same field, turning clockwise. A motor whose rotor follows the field (branch 12) runs backwards: swapping any two of the three supply wires reverses a three-phase motor. Swapping all three cyclically (a→b→c→a) only delays the field by a third of a turn.

11 · symmetrical components

Three phasors are a vector in ℂ³

symmetrical components

For phasors $(I_a, I_b, I_c)$: the zero sequence $I_0 = \tfrac13(I_a + I_b + I_c)$, the positive sequence $I_1 = \tfrac13(I_a + \alpha I_b + \alpha^2I_c)$, the negative sequence $I_2 = \tfrac13(I_a + \alpha^2I_b + \alpha I_c)$.

the discrete Fourier transform on ℤ/3

With $F_{jk} = \alpha^{-jk}$ ($j$ the phase, $k$ the sequence, both mod 3), $(I_a, I_b, I_c)^{\mathsf T} = F\,(I_0, I_1, I_2)^{\mathsf T}$:

$$\begin{aligned}I_a &= I_0 + I_1 + I_2,\\ I_b &= I_0 + \alpha^2I_1 + \alpha I_2,\\ I_c &= I_0 + \alpha I_1 + \alpha^2I_2 .\end{aligned}$$

$\bar FF = 3\cdot\mathbb 1$, so $F^{-1} = \bar F/3$: every triple splits, uniquely.

Proof. $(\bar FF)_{jm} = \sum_k\alpha^{k(j - m)}$: 3 if $j = m$; otherwise $1 + \beta + \beta^2 = 0$ with $\beta = \alpha^{\pm1}$. $\square$

The columns of $F$. Positive: a balanced a-b-c set. Negative: a balanced set in the other order, a-c-b. Zero: three equal phasors.

Full proof

$F$ is symmetric, so $\bar F = F^{\mathsf H}$, and $(\bar FF)_{jm} = \sum_k\overline{F_{jk}}F_{km} = \sum_k\alpha^{jk}\alpha^{-km} = \sum_k\alpha^{k(j - m)}$. If $j = m$ every term is 1. If $j\ne m$, $\beta = \alpha^{j - m}$ is $\alpha$ or $\alpha^2$, a cube root of unity other than 1, and $1 + \beta + \beta^2 = (1 - \beta^3)/(1 - \beta) = 0$. So $\bar FF = 3\mathbb 1$, $F$ is invertible, and $(I_0, I_1, I_2) = \tfrac13\bar F(I_a, I_b, I_c)$ — row $k$ of $\bar F$ is $(1, \alpha^k, \alpha^{2k})$, which gives the three definitions. Uniqueness is invertibility. $\square$

Why "Fourier". The phases are indexed by $\mathbb Z/3$, the integers mod 3, and the columns of $F$ are its characters $j\mapsto\alpha^{-jk}$: the discrete Fourier transform of a function on three points. $F/\sqrt3$ is unitary, so it is a change of orthonormal basis of $\mathbb C^3$ — Parseval: $|I_a|^2 + |I_b|^2 + |I_c|^2 = 3(|I_0|^2 + |I_1|^2 + |I_2|^2)$.

Reading the columns. Column 1 is $(1, \alpha^{-1}, \alpha^{-2}) = (1, \alpha^2, \alpha)$, section 1's balanced set; column 2 is $(1, \alpha, \alpha^2)$, b leading — the swapped set of section 4; column 0 is $(1, 1, 1)$.

History. The decomposition is named after C. L. Fortescue, who introduced it in 1918 ("Method of symmetrical co-ordinates applied to the solution of polyphase networks", Trans. AIEE 37, part II, 1027–1140), for polyphase networks in general. The deck proves what it uses and cites the paper for the name only. Bergen & Vittal, ch. 12, "Unbalanced System Operation", is the textbook account; it writes $a$ for $\alpha$ and $j$ for $i$, and may order or scale the transform differently, so match its definitions before comparing formulas.

11 · symmetrical components

Only zero sequence returns through the neutral

theorem · the neutral

At a star point with a neutral, the current law gives

$$I_N = I_a + I_b + I_c = 3I_0 .$$

Positive and negative sequences sum to zero over the three lines ($1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 = 0$): only zero sequence uses the neutral.

Corollary 1. A load connected only between lines draws no neutral current, however unbalanced: the current law on a surface round it gives $I_a + I_b + I_c = 0$, so $I_0 = 0$.

Corollary 2. A balanced load draws only positive sequence: its currents are $I_a(1, \alpha^2, \alpha)$, a multiple of $F$'s column 1, and the decomposition is unique.

So the neutral sees the kiln and the lighting, never the motors.

Left: the star point. Right: a load across two lines puts $+I$ on one and $-I$ on the other: zero sequence $\tfrac13(I - I + 0) = 0$.

Corollary 1, in full. Draw a closed surface round the load that cuts only the three line conductors. Unit 03's current law (charge conservation under the lumped hypotheses) says the currents entering sum to zero: $I_a + I_b + I_c = 0$, whatever is inside. So $I_0 = 0$. A delta load is such a load; so is a single-phase load connected between two lines at 400 V.

Corollary 2, in full. The decomposition is a bijection (previous slide). A balanced set equals $I_a$ times column 1 of $F$, so it is its own decomposition: $I_1 = I_a$, $I_0 = I_2 = 0$. Checked directly: $I_0 = \tfrac13I_a(1 + \alpha^2 + \alpha) = 0$, $I_2 = \tfrac13I_a(1 + \alpha^2\alpha^2 + \alpha\alpha) = \tfrac13I_a(1 + \alpha + \alpha^2) = 0$.

Triplen harmonics. One sentence, as promised elsewhere: a current at three times the supply frequency in each phase is in step in all three, so it is zero sequence and returns through the neutral; unit 14 meets such currents.

Symmetric networks are diagonal in the sequence basis

Three lines with self impedance $Z_s$ and equal mutual impedances $Z_m$: the drops are $\Delta V = ZI$ with

$$Z = \begin{pmatrix}Z_s&Z_m&Z_m\\Z_m&Z_s&Z_m\\Z_m&Z_m&Z_s\end{pmatrix}.$$
theorem · circulant matrix

A $3\times3$ $Z$ unchanged by relabelling a→b→c→a is $c_0\mathbb 1 + c_1P + c_2P^2$, $P$ the cyclic shift. The columns $f_k$ of $F$ are its eigenvectors: $Zf_k = Z_kf_k$, with sequence impedances $Z_k = c_0 + c_1\alpha^{-k} + c_2\alpha^{-2k}$.

proof

$(Pf_k)_j = (f_k)_{j+1} = \alpha^{-(j+1)k} = \alpha^{-k}(f_k)_j$. So $P^nf_k = \alpha^{-nk}f_k$. $\square$

The cable: $Z_0 = Z_s + 2Z_m$, $Z_1 = Z_2 = Z_s - Z_m$ (since $\alpha + \alpha^2 = -1$).

A three-core cable with its cores at the corners of an equilateral triangle: rotating it by $120^\circ$ maps it to itself, so its $Z$ commutes with $P$.

Full proof

Symmetric means circulant. Let $(Px)_j = x_{j+1}$, indices mod 3. "Unchanged by relabelling" means $P^{-1}ZP = Z$, i.e. $Z_{j+1,m+1} = Z_{jm}$ for all $j, m$: each entry depends only on $m - j$ mod 3. Write $c_d = Z_{j,j+d}$. Then $Z = c_0\mathbb 1 + c_1P + c_2P^2$, because $(P^d)_{jm} = 1$ exactly when $m = j + d$.

The eigenvalues. With $(f_k)_j = \alpha^{-jk}$, $(Pf_k)_j = \alpha^{-(j+1)k} = \alpha^{-k}(f_k)_j$. Hence $Zf_k = (c_0 + c_1\alpha^{-k} + c_2\alpha^{-2k})f_k$, and $F^{-1}ZF = \operatorname{diag}(Z_0, Z_1, Z_2)$. For the cable $c_0 = Z_s$, $c_1 = c_2 = Z_m$: $Z_0 = Z_s + 2Z_m$; for $k = 1, 2$, $\alpha^{-k} + \alpha^{-2k} = \alpha + \alpha^2 = -1$, so $Z_1 = Z_2 = Z_s - Z_m$. $\square$

When $Z_1\ne Z_2$. If $c_1\ne c_2$ the matrix is circulant but not symmetric, and $Z_1\ne Z_2$. A passive reciprocal network has a symmetric $Z$ (unit 02's mutual inductance is symmetric), so $Z_1 = Z_2$; rotating machines are where the two differ (branch 12's territory).

Units and limits. All in ohms ✓. No coupling, $Z_m = 0$: $Z_0 = Z_1 = Z_2 = Z_s$, three independent lines ✓. A balanced star load without neutral impedance is $Z\mathbb 1$, the simplest circulant.

The mutual impedance. At 50 Hz $Z_m$ is $i\omega M$ for the mutual inductance $M$ between two cores (unit 02's Neumann formula). Equal $M$ for all pairs needs the symmetric layout of the figure. No value of $Z_m$ for any real cable is claimed here.

Sequence networks: three problems, uncoupled

In the sequence basis $\Delta V_k = Z_kI_k$, $k = 0, 1, 2$, separately. An unbalanced load on a symmetric network becomes three sequence networks, each a single-phase problem, coupled only where the unbalance is.

The workshop's cable with its neutral: the drop in phase $k$ to neutral is $RI_k + R_NI_N$, a circulant with $c_0 = R + R_N$, $c_1 = c_2 = R_N$. So $Z_1 = Z_2 = R = 24$ mΩ and $Z_0 = R + 3R_N = 96$ mΩ.

check: the loss, by sequence

Uses. Chiefly fault analysis — a short on one line is an extreme unbalance — which this course names and does not do. And negative sequence makes a field turning backwards (section 4), which drags on a motor's rotor and heats it (branch 12).

One network per sequence. A balanced source drives only the positive one; the workshop's unbalance injects current into all three.

The circulant, derived. Take the neutral bar at the transformer as reference. The voltage of line $k$ at the workshop, measured from the workshop's neutral bar, is the source's $V_k$ minus the drop along line $k$, $RI_k$, minus the rise along the neutral from the workshop back to the transformer, $R_NI_N$, with $I_N = \sum_jI_j$. So the drop matrix has $R + R_N$ on the diagonal and $R_N$ off it. Here $R_N = R = 24$ mΩ (the four cores are equal).

The loss check, in full. The lines lose $R\sum_k|I_k|^2 = 3R\sum_k|I_k|^2$ over the three sequences $k = 0, 1, 2$, by Parseval (the previous slides' $F/\sqrt3$ is unitary); the neutral loses $R_N|3I_0|^2 = 9R_N|I_0|^2$. Delta on the 20 kV side stops zero sequence

On each limb, ampere-turns balance: the delta winding of limb $k$ carries the LV current divided by $N = 86.6$. At HV corner A, the line takes the difference of two windings' currents:

$$I_A = \frac{I_a - I_c}{N},\quad I_B = \frac{I_b - I_a}{N},\quad I_C = \frac{I_c - I_b}{N}.$$
theorem

Zero-sequence LV currents produce no 20 kV line current: $I_0 - I_0 = 0$ at every corner. They circulate inside the delta, $I_0/N$ round the triangle.

The workshop alone on the transformer: $I_0/N = 0.139$ A circulates; the 20 kV lines carry 1.044, 1.323 and 0.940 A, summing to zero.

The 20 kV network never sees the workshop's neutral current.

Equal currents in all three delta windings: each corner receives one and passes it on. Line current zero.

Full proof

Limb a carries the HV winding from A to B and the LV winding from a to n (previous section). Ideal transformer on each limb (unit 07): the winding currents are in the ratio $1/N$, so with reference directions chosen to match, the HV winding of limb $k$ carries $I_k/N$ — from A to B for limb a, B to C for b, C to A for c. The current law at corner A: the line brings $I_A$ in; winding $ab$ takes $I_a/N$ away; winding $ca$ brings $I_c/N$ in. So $I_A = (I_a - I_c)/N$, and cyclically. Write $I_k = I_0 + (\text{positive and negative parts})$: the $I_0$ cancels in every difference. Conversely any sum $I_A + I_B + I_C$ is zero, as the current law on a surface round the delta requires. $\square$

The zero-sequence ampere-turns on each limb's LV winding are balanced by an equal circulating current in its HV winding, so the core sees no unbalanced zero-sequence magnetisation, and the 20 kV lines see none of it. 11 · broken neutral

The star point floats

Loads of admittance $Y_a$, $Y_b$, $Y_c$ from the lines to a common point N′, joined to the transformer's N by a neutral of admittance $Y_N$. The current law at N′, as for the per-phase equivalent:

$$\boxed{\,V_{N'} = \frac{\sum_kY_kV_k}{Y_a + Y_b + Y_c + Y_N}\,}$$

— the formula known as Millman's theorem. Units: $\mathrm{S\,V/S} = \mathrm V$ ✓.

  • Intact neutral, $Y_N\to\infty$: $V_{N'} = 0$. Each load sees its 230 V, balanced or not.
  • Balanced loads: $V_{N'} = 0$ whatever $Y_N$ (section 1).
  • Broken neutral, $Y_N = 0$, unbalanced: $V_{N'}\ne0$. The loads' voltages $V_k - V_{N'}$ are no longer 230 V.

Three single-phase loads on three phases, with the neutral broken between them and the transformer.

Full proof

Take N as reference. Load $k$ carries $(V_k - V_{N'})Y_k$ into N′; the neutral carries $(0 - V_{N'})Y_N$ into N′. Unit 03's current law: $\sum_k(V_k - V_{N'})Y_k - V_{N'}Y_N = 0$, so $V_{N'}(\sum_kY_k + Y_N) = \sum_kY_kV_k$. $\square$ The per-phase slide was the case $Y_k$ all equal.

Limits. $Y_N\to\infty$ (a perfect neutral) sends $V_{N'}\to0$ ✓. One load only, say $Y_b = Y_c = 0$, neutral broken: $V_{N'} = V_a$, the load sees nothing — there is no return path ✓.

The name. Engineering texts call this Millman's theorem; it is used here only as a name for a formula derived on the slide.
