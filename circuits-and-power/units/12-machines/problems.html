the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You derive a reluctance motor's mean torque from co-energy, \(\tfrac14\hat I^2L_2\sin2\delta\); you find the speed of a 40-pole synchronous generator on a 50 Hz grid, 150 rpm; and you locate the pump motor's maximum torque on its torque–slip curve: 164 N·m at a slip of 0.187.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about four hours. P4, P6 and P10 are the checkpoint's. Conventions as in the deck: flux linkage \(\Lambda\); stored energy \(W(\Lambda, \theta)\) and co-energy \(W'(i, \theta) = i\Lambda - W\); torque \(T\) on the rotor in the direction of increasing \(\theta\); RMS phasors with \(e^{+i\omega t}\); complex power \(S = VI^*\); the synchronous generator in the generator convention (\(I\) leaves the + terminal), everything else passive; \(p\) is the number of poles, so \(n_s = 120f/p\) rpm; slip \(s = (n_s - n)/n_s\).

Torque from co-energy

P1 Proof: energy, co-energy, and the half-and-half split medium

exercises: the energy balance of a lossless coupling, the change of variables to co-energy, the energy check on a small displacement

A lossless coupling has one electrical port and one angle \(\theta\). Its flux linkage is a smooth function \(\Lambda(i, \theta)\), strictly increasing in \(i\) at each \(\theta\).

  1. Starting from the energy balance \(i\,\mathrm d\Lambda = \mathrm dW + T\,\mathrm d\theta\), prove that \(T = -\partial W/\partial\theta|_\Lambda = +\partial W'/\partial\theta|_i\) at every state, where \(W' = i\Lambda - W\).
  2. For a linear coupling, \(\Lambda = L(\theta)i\), let the rotor turn by \(\mathrm d\theta\) at constant current. Show that the electrical input is twice the mechanical work, and that the other half is stored.
  3. Repeat part 2 at constant flux linkage. Where does the mechanical work come from now? Check that the torque is the same.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a comparison of coefficients in two differentials. Parts 2 and 3 are three numbers each, all proportional to \(\mathrm dL = L'\,\mathrm d\theta\).

Rung 2

\(W\) is a function of the state \((\Lambda, \theta)\) because nothing is lost. Compute \(\mathrm d(i\Lambda)\) and subtract \(\mathrm dW\). For parts 2 and 3, write \(W\) in the variables held fixed: \(\tfrac12L i^2\) at fixed \(i\), \(\Lambda^2/2L\) at fixed \(\Lambda\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(\mathrm dW' = \Lambda\,\mathrm di + i\,\mathrm d\Lambda - \mathrm dW = \Lambda\,\mathrm di + T\,\mathrm d\theta\).

Worked solution

Idea. A state has one torque; the two formulas are two coordinate systems for the same differential.

1. Since the coupling is lossless, \(W\) depends only on the state, which \((\Lambda, \theta)\) fixes. So \(\mathrm dW = W_\Lambda\,\mathrm d\Lambda + W_\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\). The balance says \(\mathrm dW = i\,\mathrm d\Lambda - T\,\mathrm d\theta\) for all independent \(\mathrm d\Lambda, \mathrm d\theta\), so \(W_\Lambda = i\) and \(W_\theta = -T\). Because \(\Lambda\) is strictly increasing in \(i\), \((i, \theta)\) also fixes the state, and \(W'(i, \theta) = i\Lambda(i, \theta) - W(\Lambda(i, \theta), \theta)\) is a function of it, with \[\mathrm dW' = \Lambda\,\mathrm di + i\,\mathrm d\Lambda - (i\,\mathrm d\Lambda - T\,\mathrm d\theta) = \Lambda\,\mathrm di + T\,\mathrm d\theta ,\] so \(\partial W'/\partial\theta|_i = T\). Both equal the \(T\) of the balance at that state. \(\square\)

2. At fixed \(i\): \(\mathrm d\Lambda = i\,\mathrm dL\), so the input is \(i\,\mathrm d\Lambda = i^2\,\mathrm dL\). Stored: \(W = \tfrac12Li^2\) changes by \(\tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL\). Work: \(T\,\mathrm d\theta = \tfrac12i^2L'\,\mathrm d\theta = \tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL\). Input \(=\) stored \(+\) work: \(i^2\,\mathrm dL = \tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL + \tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL\) ✓.

3. At fixed \(\Lambda\): the input \(i\,\mathrm d\Lambda = 0\). \(W = \Lambda^2/2L\) changes by \(-\Lambda^2\,\mathrm dL/2L^2 = -\tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL\). So the work \(T\,\mathrm d\theta = -\mathrm dW = \tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL\) comes entirely out of the stored energy. The torque, \(\tfrac12i^2L'\), is the same as in part 2, as part 1 requires.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{A^2\,H} = \mathrm J\) for every term ✓. A limit: for \(L' = 0\) (no dependence on angle) every term is zero and there is no torque ✓. The signs: at constant current the field grows as the rotor moves towards larger \(L\), and at constant flux it shrinks, yet the force is towards larger \(L\) in both — the same pull, paid for differently.

P2 A plunger routine

exercises: force from co-energy for a sliding part, which way the force points, the work done checked against the co-energy

A solenoid pulls an iron plunger towards it across an air gap of length \(x\). Assume \(L(x) = L_0/(1 + x/g)\), with \(L_0 = 50\) mH and \(g = 2\) mm, and a steady current \(i = 3\) A.

  1. Find the force on the plunger, \(F = \partial W'/\partial x|_i\), as a function of \(x\). Which way does it point?
  2. Evaluate it at \(x = 2\) mm, 1 mm and 0.
  3. Find the work the force does as the gap closes from 2 mm to 0 at constant current, two ways.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is a function of \(x\) in newtons; its sign says whether it increases or decreases \(x\).

Rung 2

With \(x\) in place of \(\theta\), the linear case's formula is \(F = \tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL/\mathrm dx\). Part 3: integrate \(F\), or use the fact that at constant current the work equals the change in co-energy.

Rung 3

First line: \(\mathrm dL/\mathrm dx = -L_0/\big(g(1 + x/g)^2\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. A linear coupling with a sliding coordinate: \(F = \tfrac12i^2L'(x)\), towards larger inductance.

1. \(W' = \tfrac12L(x)i^2\), so \[F = \tfrac12i^2\frac{\mathrm dL}{\mathrm dx} = -\frac{i^2L_0}{2g(1 + x/g)^2} .\] It is negative: it decreases \(x\), closing the gap, towards larger \(L\). The plunger is pulled in.

2. \(i^2L_0/2g = 9\times0.05/0.004 = 112.5\) N. At \(x = 2\) mm, \((1 + 1)^2 = 4\): \(F = -28.1\) N. At 1 mm, \(1.5^2 = 2.25\): \(F = -50.0\) N. At 0: \(F = -112.5\) N. The pull grows as the gap closes.

3. By co-energy: at constant current the work done equals the change in \(W'\), \(\tfrac12i^2(L(0) - L(2\ \mathrm{mm})) = \tfrac12\times9\times(0.050 - 0.025) = 0.1125\) J. By integration: the work is \(\int F\,\mathrm dx\) from 2 mm to 0, \[\int_{0.002}^{0}\frac{-112.5}{(1 + x/g)^2}\,\mathrm dx = 112.5\int_0^{0.002}\frac{\mathrm dx}{(1 + x/g)^2} = 112.5\,g\Big[1 - \frac{1}{1 + x/g}\Big]_{x = 0.002} = 112.5\times0.002\times\tfrac12 = 0.1125\ \mathrm J .\] The same ✓. The source meanwhile supplied \(i^2\,\Delta L = 0.225\) J, twice as much: P1's half-and-half split.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{A^2\,H/m} = \mathrm{J/m} = \mathrm N\) ✓. A limit: for \(x \gg g\), \(F \to -i^2L_0g/2x^2\), falling as the gap widens ✓. The values are assumed; 28 N is about the weight of 2.9 kg.

P3 Proof: the Lagrangian of two coupled coils medium

exercises: Lagrange's equations with generalised forces (MR 12), magnetic energy as kinetic energy, the energy function as a check

Two coils, a fixed one (charge \(q_1\), resistance \(R_1\), source \(v_1\)) and one on a rotor (charge \(q_2\), resistance \(R_2\), shorted), have self-inductances \(L_1\), \(L_2\) independent of \(\theta\) and mutual inductance \(M(\theta)\). The rotor has inertia \(J\) and drives a load torque \(T_{\mathrm{load}}\).

  1. Write the Lagrangian \(\mathcal L(q_1, q_2, \theta, \dot q_1, \dot q_2, \dot\theta)\) and the generalised forces.
  2. Derive the three equations of motion. Identify the two circuit equations and the torque.
  3. Show that the torque is \(\partial W'/\partial\theta|_{i_1, i_2}\) for \(W' = \tfrac12\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}\mathsf L(\theta)\mathbf i\).
  4. Prove the energy balance: source power equals resistive loss plus the rate of change of magnetic and kinetic energy plus the power to the load.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The Lagrangian is unit 02's energy of two loops, written in \(\dot q\)'s, plus the rotor's kinetic energy. The equations are two voltage laws and one Newton's law for rotation.

Rung 2

The generalised forces: \(v_1 - R_1\dot q_1\) on \(q_1\), \(-R_2\dot q_2\) on \(q_2\), \(-T_{\mathrm{load}}\) on \(\theta\). For part 4, multiply each equation by its velocity and add.

Rung 3

First line: \(\mathcal L = \tfrac12L_1\dot q_1^2 + M(\theta)\dot q_1\dot q_2 + \tfrac12L_2\dot q_2^2 + \tfrac12J\dot\theta^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. Magnetic energy is quadratic in the currents, the velocities of the charges, so it enters \(\mathcal L\) as kinetic energy; everything dissipative or external is a generalised force (MR 12, From d'Alembert to Lagrange's equations).

1. \(\mathcal L = \tfrac12L_1\dot q_1^2 + M(\theta)\dot q_1\dot q_2 + \tfrac12L_2\dot q_2^2 + \tfrac12J\dot\theta^2\). Generalised forces \(Q_1 = v_1 - R_1\dot q_1\), \(Q_2 = -R_2\dot q_2\), \(Q_\theta = -T_{\mathrm{load}}\).

2. \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q_1 = L_1i_1 + Mi_2 = \Lambda_1\), \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial q_1 = 0\): \(\dfrac{\mathrm d\Lambda_1}{\mathrm dt} = v_1 - R_1i_1\), the voltage law for coil 1. Likewise \(\dfrac{\mathrm d\Lambda_2}{\mathrm dt} = -R_2i_2\) with \(\Lambda_2 = Mi_1 + L_2i_2\): the shorted rotor coil. For \(\theta\): \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\theta = J\dot\theta\), \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\theta = M'(\theta)i_1i_2\), so \[J\ddot\theta = i_1i_2M'(\theta) - T_{\mathrm{load}} .\] The electrical torque is \(i_1i_2M'\).

3. \(W' = \tfrac12L_1i_1^2 + Mi_1i_2 + \tfrac12L_2i_2^2\), and at fixed currents \(\partial W'/\partial\theta = i_1i_2M'\) ✓, the same as part 2, with the same sign.

4. Multiply the three equations by \(i_1\), \(i_2\), \(\dot\theta\) and add: \(i_1\dot\Lambda_1 + i_2\dot\Lambda_2 + J\dot\theta\ddot\theta - i_1i_2M'\dot\theta = v_1i_1 - R_1i_1^2 - R_2i_2^2 - T_{\mathrm{load}}\dot\theta\). Now \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}W' = L_1i_1\dot i_1 + M(\dot i_1i_2 + i_1\dot i_2) + M'\dot\theta i_1i_2 + L_2i_2\dot i_2\), and \(i_1\dot\Lambda_1 + i_2\dot\Lambda_2 = L_1i_1\dot i_1 + M(i_1\dot i_2 + \dot i_1i_2) + 2M'\dot\theta i_1i_2 + L_2i_2\dot i_2\). So \(i_1\dot\Lambda_1 + i_2\dot\Lambda_2 - i_1i_2M'\dot\theta = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}W'\), and with \(W' = W\) in value for a linear coupling, \[v_1i_1 = R_1i_1^2 + R_2i_2^2 + \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(W + \tfrac12J\dot\theta^2\big) + T_{\mathrm{load}}\dot\theta .\ \square\]

Check. Units: \(M'\) in H/rad, times \(\mathrm A^2\), is N·m ✓. A limit: with the rotor clamped (\(\dot\theta = 0\)) the circuit equations are unit 07's coupled pair, and the energy balance is unit 02's ✓. With \(M\) independent of \(\theta\), no torque ✓.

P4 The reluctance motor medium

exercises: torque from co-energy with an alternating current, averaging a product of sinusoids, why a synchronous motor cannot start itself (the checkpoint's)

The coil of the deck, \(L(\theta) = 0.8 + 0.4\cos2\theta\) H, carries \(i = \hat I\cos\omega t\) with \(\hat I = 2\) A at 50 Hz.

  1. With the rotor at \(\theta = \omega t - \delta\), derive the mean torque \(\langle T\rangle = \tfrac14\hat I^2L_2\sin2\delta\) from co-energy. Find its maximum.
  2. The rotor drives a load of 0.20 N·m. Find \(\delta\) and the mechanical power. How fast does it turn?
  3. Prove that at any constant speed \(\omega_r \notin \{0, \pm\omega\}\) the mean torque is zero, and say what happens at \(\omega_r = 0\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 ends in a sinusoid of \(2\delta\) times a constant in N·m; part 2 is an angle, a power and a speed; part 3 is a statement about which frequencies appear in \(T(t)\).

Rung 2

\(T = \tfrac12i^2L'(\theta) = -i^2L_2\sin2\theta\). Use \(\cos^2\omega t = \tfrac12(1 + \cos2\omega t)\) and the product-to-sum formula; the mean of \(\sin(\Omega t + c)\) is zero unless \(\Omega = 0\).

Rung 3

First line: \(T(t) = -\tfrac12\hat I^2L_2(1 + \cos2\omega t)\sin(2\omega_rt - 2\delta)\), with \(\omega_r = \omega\) for part 1.

Worked solution

Idea. Co-energy gives the instantaneous torque; averaging keeps only the terms whose frequency is zero, and that happens only in step with the supply.

1. \(W' = \tfrac12L(\theta)i^2\), so \(T = \tfrac12i^2L'(\theta) = -i^2L_2\sin2\theta\). With \(i^2 = \tfrac12\hat I^2(1 + \cos2\omega t)\) and \(2\theta = 2\omega t - 2\delta\), \[T = -\tfrac12\hat I^2L_2\big[\sin(2\omega t - 2\delta) + \tfrac12\sin(4\omega t - 2\delta) + \tfrac12\sin(-2\delta)\big].\] The first two terms average to zero; the last is constant. So \(\langle T\rangle = \tfrac14\hat I^2L_2\sin2\delta\). Its maximum is \(\tfrac14\times4\times0.4 = 0.40\) N·m, at \(\delta = 45°\).

2. \(0.40\sin2\delta = 0.20\): \(\sin2\delta = 0.5\), \(2\delta = 30°\), \(\delta = 15°\) (the smaller root; the other, \(\delta = 75°\), lies past the maximum). The rotor turns once per supply period: \(\omega = 314.16\) rad/s, 3000 rpm. Power \(0.20\times314.16 = 62.8\) W.

3. With \(\theta = \omega_rt - \delta\), \(T = -\tfrac12\hat I^2L_2\big[\sin(2\omega_rt - 2\delta) + \tfrac12\sin(2(\omega_r + \omega)t - 2\delta) + \tfrac12\sin(2(\omega_r - \omega)t - 2\delta)\big]\). Each term is a sinusoid of constant amplitude at angular frequency \(2\omega_r\), \(2(\omega_r + \omega)\) or \(2(\omega_r - \omega)\). Its long-time mean is zero unless that frequency is zero. So \(\langle T\rangle = 0\) unless \(\omega_r \in \{0, \omega, -\omega\}\). \(\square\) At \(\omega_r = 0\), \(\theta = -\delta\) is fixed and \(\langle T\rangle = -\tfrac12\hat I^2L_2\sin2\theta\): an aligning torque that turns the rotor to \(\theta = 0\) and holds it there. At rest the motor does not turn; at any speed short of synchronous its mean torque is zero; so it cannot run itself up to speed.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{A^2\,H} = \mathrm{N\,m}\) per radian ✓. Energy: over a period the stored energy returns to its value, so the mean electrical input equals \(\langle T\rangle\omega\), 62.8 W in part 2. A limit: \(L_2 = 0\) (a round rotor) gives no torque at all ✓.

Back-EMF and the synchronous machine

P5 The starter's machine constant routine

exercises: the one constant \(k\) of a DC machine, unit 04's starter at speed, maximum power with the back-EMF as the load

Model unit 04's starter as \(R_s = 45\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\) in series with \(e = k\omega\), \(k\) constant. Assume that, with nothing to turn and no friction, it runs up to 4000 rpm on the battery.

  1. Find \(k\), in both of its units.
  2. Find the stall torque, and the speed and torque when the current is 110 A.
  3. Find the speed at which the starter's mechanical power is greatest, and that power.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

\(k\) is a number in V·s/rad, which is the same as N·m/A. The rest are torques in N·m, speeds in rpm, a power in W.

Rung 2

With no load and no friction the torque is zero, so the current is zero, and the starter's terminals see the battery's whole EMF. The circuit's total resistance is 60 mΩ. Mechanical power is \(ei\): a parabola in \(e\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\omega_0 = 4000\times2\pi/60 = 418.9\) rad/s, and \(k\omega_0 = 12.6\) V.

Worked solution

Idea. One measurement at no load fixes \(k\); the same \(k\) then gives every torque.

1. At no load \(T = ki = 0\), so \(i = 0\) and \(e = \mathcal E = 12.6\) V. \(k = 12.6/418.88 = 0.03008\) V·s/rad \(= 0.03008\) N·m/A.

2. Stalled, \(i = 210\) A (unit 04), \(T = 0.03008\times210 = 6.32\) N·m. At 110 A, \(e = 12.6 - 110\times0.060 = 6.0\) V, so \(\omega = 6.0/0.03008 = 199.5\) rad/s \(= 1905\) rpm, and \(T = 0.03008\times110 = 3.31\) N·m.

3. \(P = ei = e(12.6 - e)/0.060\), greatest at \(e = 6.3\) V (unit 04's maximum-power theorem, with the back-EMF as the load: the source resistance takes half the EMF). There \(i = 105\) A, \(\omega = 6.3/0.03008 = 209.4\) rad/s \(= 2000\) rpm — half the no-load speed — and \(P = 6.3\times105 = 661.5\) W, with \(T = 3.16\) N·m.

Check. Energy at 110 A: \(T\omega = 3.309\times199.47 = 660\) W \(= ei = 6\times110\) ✓; the battery delivers \(12.6\times110 = 1386\) W, of which \(110^2\times0.060 = 726\) W heats the resistances ✓. Units: \(\mathrm{V\,s/rad}\times\mathrm{rad/s} = \mathrm V\) ✓. The 4000 rpm is an assumption; a real starter has friction, and its field may not be constant.

P6 Synchronous speeds routine

exercises: \(n_s = 120f/p\) from the rotating field with \(p/2\) pole pairs (the checkpoint's)

  1. Derive \(n_s = 120f/p\) from the air-gap field \(B_r = \tfrac32\hat B\cos(\tfrac p2\varphi - \omega t)\).
  2. Find the synchronous speeds for 2, 4 and 40 poles at 50 Hz and at 60 Hz.
  3. A water turbine is to turn at 200 rpm on a 50 Hz grid. How many poles must its generator have? And on 60 Hz, at the nearest allowed speed?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is the speed at which a crest of the pattern moves round the bore. Parts 2 and 3 are arithmetic with an even integer \(p\).

Rung 2

Follow the crest \(\tfrac p2\varphi - \omega t = 0\). Convert rad/s to rpm with \(60/2\pi\).

Rung 3

First line: the crest is at \(\varphi = \omega t/(p/2)\), so it moves at \(\omega/(p/2)\) rad/s.

Worked solution

Idea. A \(p\)-pole pattern repeats \(p/2\) times round the bore, so one electrical cycle moves it by \(2/p\) of a turn.

1. The crest moves at \(\omega/(p/2) = 2\pi f/(p/2)\) rad/s, which is \(f/(p/2)\) turns per second, \(60f/(p/2) = 120f/p\) per minute.

2. At 50 Hz, \(6000/p\): 3000, 1500 and 150 rpm. At 60 Hz, \(7200/p\): 3600, 1800 and 180 rpm.

3. \(p = 6000/200 = 30\) poles at 50 Hz. On 60 Hz, \(7200/p = 200\) gives \(p = 36\), an even integer, so 36 poles and exactly 200 rpm.

Check. Units: cycles/s \(\times\) s/min \(\div\) pole pairs per turn \(=\) turns/min ✓. A limit: \(p = 2\) gives one turn per cycle, which is unit 11's two-pole field ✓. The 40-pole machine of the checkpoint is hypothetical, and so is this turbine's speed.

P7 A generator's P and Q medium

exercises: the power–angle characteristic from \(S = VI^*\), the field current as the control of reactive power

A round-rotor generator, per unit: \(V = 1.0\), \(X_s = 1.0\), generator convention.

  1. Derive \(P = EV\sin\delta/X_s\) and \(Q = (EV\cos\delta - V^2)/X_s\).
  2. With \(E = 1.2\), find \(P_{\max}\), the angle for \(P = 0.8\), the current, and \(Q\) there.
  3. At \(P = 0.8\), find the \(E\) that makes \(Q = 0\), and the \(E\) that makes \(Q = +0.3\). Which way must the field current move?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a complex power. Parts 2 and 3 are angles and per-unit numbers; at fixed \(P\), \(E\sin\delta\) is fixed.

Rung 2

\(I = (E - V)/(iX_s)\) with \(V\) real and \(E = Ee^{i\delta}\); in the generator convention, \(VI^*\) is the power delivered. In part 3 use \(E\sin\delta = 0.8\) and \(E\cos\delta = 1 + Q\).

Rung 3

First line: \(VI^* = V\,(Ee^{-i\delta} - V)/(-iX_s) = iV(Ee^{-i\delta} - V)/X_s\).

Worked solution

Idea. The turbine fixes \(P\), so \(E\sin\delta\); the field current fixes \(E\), so the remaining freedom, \(E\cos\delta\), and that is \(Q\).

1. \(VI^* = iV(E\cos\delta - iE\sin\delta - V)/X_s = [EV\sin\delta + i(EV\cos\delta - V^2)]/X_s\).

2. \(P_{\max} = 1.2\) at \(90°\). \(\sin\delta = 0.8/1.2\): \(\delta = 41.81°\). \(E = 0.8944 + 0.8i\), \(I = (E - 1)/i = 0.8 + 0.1056i\), magnitude 0.807 at \(+7.5°\). \(Q = 1.2\cos41.81° - 1 = -0.106\): the machine absorbs a little reactive power, and its current leads its voltage.

3. \(Q = 0\): \(E\cos\delta = 1\), \(E = \sqrt{1 + 0.64} = 1.281\), \(\delta = \arctan0.8 = 38.66°\). \(Q = 0.3\): \(E\cos\delta = 1.3\), \(E = \sqrt{1.69 + 0.64} = 1.526\), \(\delta = 31.61°\). Both need more field current than \(E = 1.2\): raising \(i_f\) raises \(E\) in proportion (\(|E| = \omega M_fi_f/\sqrt2\)), and at fixed \(P\) that raises \(Q\).

Check. \((P, Q + V^2/X_s)\) lies on the circle of radius \(EV/X_s\): at \(E = 1.526\), \(0.8^2 + 1.3^2 = 2.33 = 1.526^2\) ✓. Energy: the reactance absorbs no real power, so \(\mathrm{Re}(EI^*) = \mathrm{Re}(VI^*) = 0.8\) at every \(E\) — the turbine is not asked for more ✓. At \(Q = 0\), \(|I| = P/V = 0.8\) ✓.

P8 Proof: rotating field to torque medium

exercises: torque as \(\tfrac12\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}\mathsf L'\mathbf i\) for several coils, the \(1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 = 0\) cancellation of unit 11, the shaft's power as the EMFs' power

Three stator phases \(k = 0, 1, 2\) carry \(i_k = \sqrt2I\cos(\omega t - \tfrac{2\pi k}{3} - \gamma)\). A round rotor with \(p\) poles carries a field current \(i_f\); its mutual inductances to the phases are \(M_{kf}(\theta) = M_f\cos(\tfrac p2\theta - \tfrac{2\pi k}{3})\), and no other inductance depends on \(\theta\).

  1. Show that the torque is \(T = i_f\sum_ki_k\,\partial M_{kf}/\partial\theta\).
  2. With \(\tfrac p2\theta = \omega_et + \theta_0\), show that \(T = -\tfrac{3\sqrt2}{2}\tfrac p2M_fi_fI\sin\big((\omega_e - \omega)t + \theta_0 + \gamma\big)\): constant at synchronous speed, zero mean otherwise.
  3. Show that \(T\dot\theta = \sum_ke_ki_k\), where \(e_k\) is the voltage the rotor induces in phase \(k\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is the linear case's formula with only the \(\theta\)-dependent entries of \(\mathsf L\). Part 2 is unit 11's rotating-field calculation, done on a product of two cosines.

Rung 2

In \(\tfrac12\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}\mathsf L'\mathbf i\), each \(M_{kf}\) appears twice (row \(k\), column \(f\), and the reverse). Use \(\sin A\cos B = \tfrac12[\sin(A + B) + \sin(A - B)]\) and \(\sum_k e^{\mp i4\pi k/3} = 0\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\partial M_{kf}/\partial\theta = -\tfrac p2M_f\sin(\tfrac p2\theta - \tfrac{2\pi k}{3})\).

Worked solution

Idea. The torque is a product of stator and rotor currents with the mutual inductance's slope; the three phases sum, as in unit 11, to a single term whose frequency is the difference between the field's speed and the rotor's.

1. \(W' = \tfrac12\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}\mathsf L(\theta)\mathbf i\) with \(\mathbf i = (i_0, i_1, i_2, i_f)\). Only \(M_{kf} = M_{fk}\) depend on \(\theta\), and each appears in the quadratic form as \(2\cdot\tfrac12M_{kf}i_ki_f\). So \(T = \partial W'/\partial\theta = i_f\sum_ki_kM_{kf}'(\theta)\).

2. \(T = -\tfrac p2M_fi_f\sqrt2I\sum_k\sin(\omega_et + \theta_0 - \tfrac{2\pi k}3)\cos(\omega t - \tfrac{2\pi k}3 - \gamma)\). Each product is \(\tfrac12[\sin((\omega_e + \omega)t + \theta_0 - \gamma - \tfrac{4\pi k}3) + \sin((\omega_e - \omega)t + \theta_0 + \gamma)]\). The second term does not depend on \(k\); the first, summed over \(k\), is the imaginary part of \(e^{i(\cdots)}(1 + e^{-i4\pi/3} + e^{-i8\pi/3}) = 0\). So \[T = -\tfrac p2M_fi_f\sqrt2I\cdot\tfrac32\sin\big((\omega_e - \omega)t + \theta_0 + \gamma\big).\] If \(\omega_e = \omega\), the rotor turns at \(\omega/(p/2)\), synchronous speed, and \(T\) is constant, set by the angle \(\theta_0 + \gamma\). Otherwise \(T\) is a sinusoid at \(\omega_e - \omega\), with zero mean. \(\square\)

3. The rotor's flux in phase \(k\) is \(M_{kf}(\theta)i_f\); with \(i_f\) constant its contribution to the voltage is \(e_k = i_f\,\mathrm dM_{kf}/\mathrm dt = i_fM_{kf}'\dot\theta\). Then \(\sum_ke_ki_k = \dot\theta\,i_f\sum_ki_kM_{kf}' = T\dot\theta\). \(\square\) The shaft's power is the power the induced EMFs absorb, which is the deck's "mechanical power equals \(P\)".

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{H\cdot A\cdot A}\) per radian \(=\) N·m ✓. A limit: \(I = 0\) or \(i_f = 0\) gives no torque — a round rotor has no reluctance torque ✓. The factor \(\tfrac32\) is unit 11's \(\tfrac32\hat B\).

P9 Proof: the induction motor's equivalent circuit medium

exercises: referring the rotor (unit 07), the \(R_2'/s\) step, the split of the air-gap power into rotor loss and mechanical power

  1. A rotor phase, referred to the stator, would have EMF \(E\) at standstill. Explain why at slip \(s\) it has EMF \(sE\) at frequency \(sf\) and leakage reactance \(sX_2'\), and show that its current obeys \(E = (R_2'/s + iX_2')I_2'\).
  2. Prove that the rotor's copper loss is \(sP_{\mathrm{ag}}\) and its mechanical power \((1 - s)P_{\mathrm{ag}}\), where \(P_{\mathrm{ag}} = 3|I_2'|^2R_2'/s\).
  3. Show that \(T = P_{\mathrm{ag}}/\omega_s\), and evaluate everything for the pump motor at \(s = 0.03\), given \(|I_2'| = 14.19\) A.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is Faraday's law in the rotor's frame. Part 2 is an energy balance across the air gap. Part 3 is a power divided by a speed.

Rung 2

The flux pattern passes the rotor at \(sn_s\), so everything induced in it is \(s\) times slower. In the rotor's own frame the power its EMF delivers is \(3\,\mathrm{Re}(sE\,I_2'^*)\), and a reactance absorbs none of it.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(3\,\mathrm{Re}(EI_2'^*) = 3\,\mathrm{Re}\big((R_2'/s + iX_2')|I_2'|^2\big) = 3|I_2'|^2R_2'/s = P_{\mathrm{ag}}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The circuit at supply frequency is the rotor's own circuit with every term divided by \(s\); the resistor \(R_2'/s\) then absorbs \(1/s\) times the rotor's real loss, and the excess is the shaft's power.

1. The air-gap field turns at \(n_s\) and the rotor at \((1 - s)n_s\), so the pattern passes the rotor at \(sn_s\). By Faraday's law each rotor phase's EMF is proportional to the rate at which flux sweeps it: \(s\) times its standstill value, \(sE\), at \(s\) times the frequency, \(sf\). The rotor's leakage inductance \(L_2'\) has reactance \(2\pi sf L_2' = sX_2'\). Kirchhoff round the rotor phase: \(sE = (R_2' + isX_2')I_2'\). Divide by \(s\): \(E = (R_2'/s + iX_2')I_2'\). The rotor currents' own field turns at \(sn_s\) relative to the rotor, so at \(n_s\) relative to the stator: seen from the stator they are supply-frequency currents, which is why the divided equation may be drawn in the stator's circuit.

2. The power crossing the gap into the rotor branch is \(3\,\mathrm{Re}(EI_2'^*) = P_{\mathrm{ag}}\). In its own frame the rotor's EMF delivers \(3\,\mathrm{Re}(sEI_2'^*) = sP_{\mathrm{ag}}\); the leakage reactance absorbs no real power, so all of it is dissipated in \(R_2'\): \(3|I_2'|^2R_2' = sP_{\mathrm{ag}}\) (directly: \(s\times3|I_2'|^2R_2'/s\) ✓). The coupling is lossless with steady stored energy, so the rest, \((1 - s)P_{\mathrm{ag}}\), leaves by the shaft. Equivalently, \(R_2'/s = R_2' + R_2'(1 - s)/s\), and the second resistor absorbs exactly the mechanical power. \(\square\)

3. \(T = P_{\text{mech}}/\omega_m = (1 - s)P_{\mathrm{ag}}/((1 - s)\omega_s) = P_{\mathrm{ag}}/\omega_s\). Pump: \(P_{\mathrm{ag}} = 3\times14.19^2\times15 = 9067\) W (with the unrounded 14.195 A); rotor copper \(0.03\times9067 = 272\) W; mechanical \(8795\) W; \(T = 9067/157.08 = 57.7\) N·m.

Check. Limits: at \(s = 1\) (standstill) the mechanical resistor is zero and all of \(P_{\mathrm{ag}}\) heats the rotor ✓; at \(s \to 0\) the rotor branch opens and no torque is made ✓. Units: \(\mathrm{W/(rad/s)} = \mathrm{N\,m}\) ✓. The two torque routes agree: \(8795/(0.97\times157.08) = 57.7\) N·m ✓.

P10 The maximum torque medium

exercises: Thévenin's theorem with phasors (units 04 and 05), the maximum-power theorem for a resistive load on a complex source (unit 06), the pump motor's numbers (the checkpoint's)

  1. Find the Thévenin equivalent of the pump motor's stator side as seen by the rotor branch.
  2. Prove that the torque is greatest where \(R_2'/s = |R_{\mathrm{th}} + i(X_{\mathrm{th}} + X_2')|\), and find \(T_{\max}\) in general.
  3. Evaluate \(s_{\max T}\), the speed there and \(T_{\max}\). What would you get by (wrongly) dropping the magnetising branch?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a complex EMF and a complex impedance. Part 2 maximises a function of one positive variable \(R = R_2'/s\). Part 3 is a slip, a speed and a torque near 160 N·m.

Rung 2

Open-circuit voltage: a divider between \(R_1 + iX_1\) and \(iX_m\). Impedance: the two in parallel. Torque \(= P_{\mathrm{ag}}/\omega_s\), and \(P_{\mathrm{ag}}\) is the power in \(R\) behind \(V_{\mathrm{th}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{th}} + iX\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dR}\dfrac{R}{(a + R)^2 + X^2} = \dfrac{a^2 + X^2 - R^2}{[(a + R)^2 + X^2]^2}\), with \(a = R_{\mathrm{th}}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The slip is a knob on one resistor behind a fixed source; the most power goes into it, and so the most torque comes out, when the resistor equals the magnitude of everything in series with it.

1. \(V_{\mathrm{th}} = 230.94\times30i/(0.5 + 31.2i)\): magnitude \(230.94\times30/31.204 = 222.0\) V. \(Z_{\mathrm{th}} = 30i(0.5 + 1.2i)/(0.5 + 31.2i) = 0.462 + 1.161i\ \Omega\).

2. \(T = 3V_{\mathrm{th}}^2f(R)/\omega_s\) with \(f(R) = R/[(a + R)^2 + X^2]\), \(a = R_{\mathrm{th}}\), \(X = X_{\mathrm{th}} + X_2'\). The derivative in rung 3 is positive for \(R \lt \sqrt{a^2 + X^2}\) and negative beyond: one maximum, at \(R = \sqrt{a^2 + X^2}\). There \((a + R)^2 + X^2 = 2R(R + a)\), so \(f = 1/(2(a + R))\) and \[T_{\max} = \frac{3V_{\mathrm{th}}^2}{2\omega_s\big(R_{\mathrm{th}} + \sqrt{R_{\mathrm{th}}^2 + X^2}\big)},\qquad s_{\max T} = \frac{R_2'}{\sqrt{R_{\mathrm{th}}^2 + X^2}} .\] This is unit 06's best resistive load on a complex source (When only the resistance can change), there proved by AM–GM; with \(X = 0\) it is unit 04's theorem, load equal to source resistance. \(\square\)

3. \(X = 2.361\ \Omega\); \(\sqrt{0.462^2 + 2.361^2} = 2.406\ \Omega\); \(s_{\max T} = 0.45/2.406 = 0.187\); \(n = 1500\times0.813 = 1219\) rpm; \(T_{\max} = 3\times222.03^2/(2\times157.08\times2.868) = 164.1\) N·m. Dropping the magnetising branch — 230.9 V behind \(0.5 + 1.2i\) — gives \(s = 0.45/|0.5 + 2.4i| = 0.184\) and \(T = 3\times230.94^2/(2\times157.08\times(0.5 + 2.4515)) = 172.6\) N·m: 5% too high, because the magnetising branch shunts part of the supply.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{V^2}/(\mathrm{s^{-1}\,\Omega}) = \mathrm{W\,s} = \mathrm{N\,m}\) per radian ✓. Evaluating the full \(T(s)\) at \(s = 0.187\) gives the same 164.1 N·m ✓, and at \(s = 0.15\) and \(0.25\) it gives less (160.8 and 158.5 N·m) ✓.

P11 Stability of the operating point medium

exercises: a one-dimensional flow and the sign of its derivative (MR 02), the torque–speed curve against a rising and a level load

The pump motor drives a load \(T_{\mathrm{load}}(n)\); its rotor obeys \(J\dot\omega = T(\omega) - T_{\mathrm{load}}(\omega)\).

  1. Prove that an equilibrium is stable if \(\mathrm d(T - T_{\mathrm{load}})/\mathrm d\omega \lt 0\) there, and unstable if it is \(\gt 0\).
  2. The pump: \(T_{\mathrm{load}} = k_Ln^2\), sized for 57.7 N·m at 1455 rpm. Prove that the curves cross exactly once, and that the crossing is stable.
  3. A conveyor (assumed) asks a constant 100 N·m. Find the crossings and classify them.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is linearisation in one dimension. Part 2 splits the speed range at the motor's maximum, 1219 rpm. Part 3 is two speeds, one of each kind.

Rung 2

Below the maximum, the motor's torque rises from 66.1 N·m; how large is the pump's there? Above it, the motor's torque falls and the pump's rises. For part 3, solve \(T(s) = 100\) on each side of \(s = 0.187\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: with \(\xi = \omega - \omega^*\), \(J\dot\xi \approx \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\omega}(T - T_{\mathrm{load}})\big|_{\omega^*}\,\xi\).

Worked solution

Idea. Where the motor's surplus torque falls as the speed rises, a small speed-up is braked and a slow-down is pushed back.

1. Let \(F = (T - T_{\mathrm{load}})/J\), so \(\dot\omega = F(\omega)\), with \(F(\omega^*) = 0\). Linearised, \(\dot\xi = F'(\omega^*)\xi\), so \(\xi = \xi_0e^{F'(\omega^*)t}\): decaying if \(F' \lt 0\), growing if \(F' \gt 0\). In one dimension a non-zero \(F'\) decides the question for the nonlinear flow too (MR 02): if \(F' \lt 0\), \(F\) is positive just below \(\omega^*\) and negative just above, so the speed moves towards \(\omega^*\) from both sides. \(\square\)

2. \(k_L = 57.72/1455^2 = 2.727\times10^{-5}\) N·m/rpm². Below 1219 rpm: as the speed rises from 0 to 1219 rpm, \(R_2'/s\) rises from 0.45 Ω to 2.406 Ω, where (P10) \(f(R)\) is increasing; so the motor's torque rises from 66.1 to 164.1 N·m, while the pump's is at most \(k_L\times1219^2 = 40.5\) N·m. No crossing. Above 1219 rpm: the motor's torque falls (to 0 at 1500) and the pump's rises (to 61.4 N·m), so their difference is strictly decreasing and changes sign once: one crossing, at 1455 rpm by construction. There \(\mathrm dT/\mathrm dn = -1.149\) N·m per rpm and \(\mathrm dT_{\mathrm{load}}/\mathrm dn = 2k_L\times1455 = +0.079\): the difference has slope \(-1.23 \lt 0\), stable.

3. \(T(s) = 100\) at \(s = 0.0581\) (1413 rpm) and \(s = 0.602\) (597 rpm). At 1413 rpm \(\mathrm dT/\mathrm dn = -0.852\) N·m per rpm with a level load: stable. At 597 rpm \(\mathrm dT/\mathrm dn = +0.082\): unstable — a little faster and the motor accelerates on to 1413 rpm, a little slower and it decelerates to a stall. Started from rest, the motor (66.1 N·m) cannot even begin to turn this load.

Check. At 1455 rpm the motor gives 57.72 N·m and the pump asks \(2.727\times10^{-5}\times1455^2 = 57.72\) N·m ✓. Units of the slopes: N·m per rpm, both sides ✓. For any load above \(T_{\max} = 164.1\) N·m there is no crossing at all.

P12 Doubling the rotor resistance routine

exercises: reading \(T_{\max}\) and \(s_{\max T}\) off their formulas, what a rotor resistance trades away

The pump motor's rotor is rebuilt with \(R_2' = 0.90\ \Omega\) (doubled); nothing else changes.

  1. Prove that the maximum torque is unchanged and moves to twice the slip. Give the numbers.
  2. Find the new starting torque and starting current.
  3. Find the new operating point against the pump, and its rotor copper loss, compared with 272 W before.
  4. Why might a motor whose rotor resistance could be raised only while starting be useful? (Qualitative.)
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is about which formula contains \(R_2'\). Parts 2 and 3 are the circuit at a new resistance; the answers are a torque near 110 N·m, a current near 85 A, and a slip near 0.056.

Rung 2

\(T(s)\) depends on \(R_2'\) and \(s\) only through \(R_2'/s\). At \(s = 1\), \(R_2'/s = 0.90\ \Omega\). For part 3, solve \(T(s) = k_Ln^2\) with \(n = 1500(1 - s)\); rotor loss is \(sP_{\mathrm{ag}} = sT\omega_s\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(T_{\max} = 3V_{\mathrm{th}}^2/(2\omega_s(R_{\mathrm{th}} + |R_{\mathrm{th}} + iX|))\) contains no \(R_2'\).

Worked solution

Idea. The torque curve is a function of \(R_2'/s\) alone, so doubling \(R_2'\) stretches it along the slip axis by two without changing its height.

1. \(T\) depends on \(R_2'/s\) only, so \(T_{\text{new}}(s) = T_{\text{old}}(s/2)\): the curve is stretched by a factor of two in slip. \(T_{\max} = 164.1\) N·m still, now at \(s = 2\times0.187 = 0.374\), 939 rpm.

2. At \(s = 1\), \(R_2'/s = 0.90\ \Omega\), the old curve's value at \(s = 0.5\): \(T = 114.0\) N·m, against 66.1 before. The circuit at \(s = 1\): \(Z_2 = 0.9 + 1.2i\), in parallel with \(30i\), plus \(0.5 + 1.2i\), gives \(|Z_{\text{in}}| = 2.725\ \Omega\) and \(I_1 = 84.7\) A, against 91.2 A.

3. Solving \(T(s) = k_L(1500(1 - s))^2\) gives \(s = 0.0564\), 1415 rpm, 54.6 N·m. \(P_{\mathrm{ag}} = 54.6\times157.08 = 8580\) W, rotor copper \(0.0564\times8580 = 484\) W — 78% more than the 272 W before — and the shaft gets 8.10 kW instead of 8.80 kW.

4. High rotor resistance gives more starting torque for less starting current (part 2); low rotor resistance gives small slip and small rotor loss in running (part 3). A rotor whose resistance can be raised for starting and lowered for running gets both. A wound rotor — three-phase windings in place of the cage, their ends brought out through slip rings, continuous rings on the shaft touched by fixed brushes like section 2's, to resistors outside the machine — allows exactly that. No claim is made here about how often it was or is done; Fitzgerald §6.7, "Effects of Rotor Resistance; Wound and Double-Squirrel-Cage Rotors", is where to read more.

Check. Units ✓ (as P10). A limit: \(R_2' \to 0\) would put the maximum at \(s \to 0\) with the same height, and give no starting torque at all, as \(T(1) \to 0\) ✓. The loss trade: at fixed torque near synchronous speed, \(s \propto R_2'\), so rotor loss \(sT\omega_s\) roughly doubles; here the operating torque also fell a little, so the factor is 1.78.
