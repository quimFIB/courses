section 1

Torque from co-energy

An energy balance for a lossless coupling gives the force or torque as a derivative of stored energy. Changing variables to the co-energy makes it a derivative at constant current; Lagrange's equations give the same answer; and an iron rotor in a coil turns by it.

12 · co-energy

A lossless coupling: one electrical port, one shaft

the model: a lossless coupling

A coil whose flux linkage $\Lambda$ depends on its current $i$ and on the position of a moving part, an angle $\theta$. Its resistance is drawn outside it. Inside, energy is only stored, $W$, or passed to the shaft as work, torque $T$ through angle $\mathrm d\theta$.

the energy balance

In time $\mathrm dt$ the port delivers $vi\,\mathrm dt = i\,\mathrm d\Lambda$ (Faraday, $v = \mathrm d\Lambda/\mathrm dt$). So

$$i\,\mathrm d\Lambda = \mathrm dW + T\,\mathrm d\theta .$$

Units: $\mathrm{A\cdot Wb} = \mathrm{A\cdot V\,s} = \mathrm J$; $\mathrm{N\,m\cdot rad} = \mathrm J$ ✓.

The coupling is everything that is neither the winding's resistance nor the shaft's friction: a field, storing energy, whose shape changes as the shaft turns.

Why lossless. A real machine loses energy in its winding resistance, in its iron (unit 07's hysteresis and eddy currents) and in friction. Each is drawn outside the coupling: a resistance in series with the port, a load on the shaft. What is left stores energy in a magnetic field and passes it on, and that is what has a force law. This is Fitzgerald's method (ch. 3, from §3.2, "Energy Balance"), and every machine in this deck is built from it.

The electrical input. Under the passive convention the port absorbs $p = vi$ (unit 02). The voltage across the coupling is $v = \mathrm d\Lambda/\mathrm dt$ by Faraday's law for a coil (unit 02, where $\Lambda$ is the flux linkage), whether $\Lambda$ changes because the current changes or because the rotor moves. So in time $\mathrm dt$ the energy in is $vi\,\mathrm dt = i\,\mathrm d\Lambda$, with no reference to how $\Lambda$ changed.

The mechanical output. $T$ is the torque the field exerts on the rotor, in the direction of increasing $\theta$; the work it does as the rotor turns by $\mathrm d\theta$ is $T\,\mathrm d\theta$. For a part that slides, replace $\theta$ by $x$ and $T$ by a force $F$: everything below goes through word for word, and the plunger of problem P2 is that case.

The stored energy is a function of state. Since nothing is lost, the energy stored depends only on where the system is, $(\Lambda, \theta)$, not on how it got there: otherwise a closed cycle could create energy. Two variables of the three ($i$, $\Lambda$, $\theta$) fix the state, because for each angle the coil's $\Lambda$–$i$ relation is a curve (a line, if there is no iron in saturation).

12 · co-energy

Torque at constant flux linkage

torque from the stored energy

With $W(\Lambda, \theta)$ a function of state, $\mathrm dW = i\,\mathrm d\Lambda - T\,\mathrm d\theta$ says

$$i = \frac{\partial W}{\partial\Lambda}\bigg|_\theta,\qquad T = -\frac{\partial W}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_\Lambda .$$
computing $W$

Integrate along a path where $T$ does no work: hold $\theta$ fixed and raise $\Lambda$ from 0.

$$W(\Lambda, \theta) = \int_0^\Lambda i(\Lambda', \theta)\,\mathrm d\Lambda' ,$$

the area between the $\Lambda$–$i$ curve and the $\Lambda$ axis.

At constant $\Lambda$ the shaft's work comes out of the field: $T\,\mathrm d\theta = -\mathrm dW$.

Schematic: one angle's $\Lambda$–$i$ curve, bending over where iron saturates. The stored energy at P is the shaded area, $\int i\,\mathrm d\Lambda$ — the area to the left of the curve.

Full proof

The balance $i\,\mathrm d\Lambda = \mathrm dW + T\,\mathrm d\theta$ holds for every small change of state. Since $W$ is a function of $(\Lambda, \theta)$, its differential is $\mathrm dW = (\partial W/\partial\Lambda)\,\mathrm d\Lambda + (\partial W/\partial\theta)\,\mathrm d\theta$. Comparing with $\mathrm dW = i\,\mathrm d\Lambda - T\,\mathrm d\theta$ for independent $\mathrm d\Lambda$ and $\mathrm d\theta$ gives the two coefficients. The torque here is the torque in the state $(\Lambda, \theta)$; it does not depend on what is held fixed in a motion, since a state has one torque. "At constant $\Lambda$" says only which partial derivative computes it.

The path for $W$. Start from $\Lambda = 0$, $i = 0$, where there is no field and no torque, at any angle; turning the rotor there costs nothing. Then, at the final angle, raise $\Lambda$: $\mathrm d\theta = 0$, so $\mathrm dW = i\,\mathrm d\Lambda$, and integrating gives the formula. Since $W$ is a function of state, any other path gives the same number.

A check on a known case. For a coil with no moving part, $\Lambda = Li$, so $W = \int_0^\Lambda(\Lambda'/L)\,\mathrm d\Lambda' = \Lambda^2/2L = \tfrac12Li^2$: unit 02's inductor energy ✓.

The sign. At constant $\Lambda$ the port delivers nothing ($i\,\mathrm d\Lambda = 0$), so any work the shaft receives is paid for by the field: $T\,\mathrm d\theta = -\mathrm dW$. The field pushes the rotor whichever way lowers its energy at fixed flux. That is the minus sign. It is a correct result and an awkward one, because a machine is fed at a known current rather than a known flux linkage; the next slide changes variables.

12 · co-energy

Co-energy, and torque at constant current

the co-energy $$W'(i, \theta) = i\Lambda - W ,$$ $$\mathrm dW' = \Lambda\,\mathrm di + T\,\mathrm d\theta .$$

The Legendre transform of $W$ in its first variable (MR 14).

theorem: torque from co-energy $$T = +\frac{\partial W'}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_i = -\frac{\partial W}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_\Lambda$$

at the same state, and $W'(i, \theta) = \int_0^i\Lambda(i', \theta)\,\mathrm di'$.

proof

$\mathrm dW' = \Lambda\,\mathrm di + i\,\mathrm d\Lambda - (i\,\mathrm d\Lambda - T\,\mathrm d\theta) = \Lambda\,\mathrm di + T\,\mathrm d\theta$; read off the coefficient of $\mathrm d\theta$. $\square$

The same curve and point. The co-energy is the area under the curve, $\int\Lambda\,\mathrm di$; together the two areas fill the rectangle $i\Lambda$, which is the definition.

Full proof

Torque. Define $W' = i\Lambda - W$, regarded as a function of $(i, \theta)$: the state is the same, only labelled by current instead of flux linkage. Its differential is $\mathrm d(i\Lambda) - \mathrm dW = \Lambda\,\mathrm di + i\,\mathrm d\Lambda - i\,\mathrm d\Lambda + T\,\mathrm d\theta = \Lambda\,\mathrm di + T\,\mathrm d\theta$. So $\partial W'/\partial i|_\theta = \Lambda$ and $\partial W'/\partial\theta|_i = T$.

The two give the same torque. Both are the coefficient of $\mathrm d\theta$ in differentials of functions of the same state, and both equal the one torque $T$ that appears in the balance. Directly: write $\Lambda = \Lambda(i, \theta)$ and differentiate $W'(i, \theta) = i\Lambda(i, \theta) - W(\Lambda(i, \theta), \theta)$ in $\theta$ at fixed $i$: $\partial_\theta W'|_i = i\,\partial_\theta\Lambda - (\partial_\Lambda W)\,\partial_\theta\Lambda - \partial_\theta W|_\Lambda = i\,\partial_\theta\Lambda - i\,\partial_\theta\Lambda - \partial_\theta W|_\Lambda = -\partial_\theta W|_\Lambda$, using $\partial_\Lambda W = i$ from the last slide. $\square$

The integral. Along $\theta$ fixed, $\mathrm dW' = \Lambda\,\mathrm di$, and $W' = 0$ at $i = 0$ (where $W = 0$ and $i\Lambda = 0$). So $W' = \int_0^i\Lambda\,\mathrm di'$: the area between the curve and the $i$ axis. The figure's rectangle is the identity $W + W' = i\Lambda$, read as areas.

The Legendre transform. MR 14 (The Legendre transform, as convex duality) defines it by a supremum and shows that for a smooth, strictly convex function it is "$xp - f$ with gradients inverse". Here $W(\Lambda)$ at fixed $\theta$ is convex when $i$ rises with $\Lambda$, which is all that is used: the one line above. The pair $(W, W')$ is the pair (Hamiltonian, Lagrangian) in two slides' time.

Reading. Fitzgerald §3.4 and §3.5, "Determination of Magnetic Force and Torque from Energy" and "… from Coenergy", treat force and torque from the energy and from the co-energy, as here; §3.6 and §3.7 go on to several windings and to permanent magnets.

12 · co-energy

The linear case: $T = \tfrac12 i^2\,\mathrm dL/\mathrm d\theta$

linear coupling

If $\Lambda = L(\theta)\,i$ (no saturation), then $W' = \tfrac12L(\theta)i^2 = W$ and

$$\boxed{\,T = \tfrac12\,i^2\,\frac{\mathrm dL}{\mathrm d\theta}\,}$$

Several coils, $\boldsymbol\Lambda = \mathsf L(\theta)\,\mathbf i$ with $\mathsf L$ unit 02's symmetric inductance matrix:

$$W' = \tfrac12\,\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}\mathsf L(\theta)\,\mathbf i ,\qquad T = \tfrac12\,\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}\frac{\mathrm d\mathsf L}{\mathrm d\theta}\,\mathbf i .$$

Units: $\mathrm{A^2\cdot H/rad} = \mathrm{J/rad} = \mathrm{N\,m}$ ✓.

The pump motor is six such coils — three on the stator, three on the rotor — whose mutual inductances depend on the rotor's angle. Its torque is this formula; section 4 evaluates it through a circuit.

Linear: each angle's curve is a line of slope $L(\theta)$, and the rectangle splits into two equal triangles, $W = W'$. They are equal as numbers, not as functions: $W = \Lambda^2/2L$, $W' = Li^2/2$.

Full proof

One coil: $W' = \int_0^iL(\theta)i'\,\mathrm di' = \tfrac12L(\theta)i^2$, and $\partial W'/\partial\theta|_i = \tfrac12i^2L'(\theta)$. Several coils: along the straight path $\mathbf i(t) = t\,\mathbf i$, $0 \le t \le 1$, at fixed $\theta$, $W' = \int\boldsymbol\Lambda\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf i = \int_0^1 t\,\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}\mathsf L\,\mathbf i\,\mathrm dt = \tfrac12\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}\mathsf L\mathbf i$; the integral of $\boldsymbol\Lambda\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf i$ is path-independent because $\mathsf L$ is symmetric (unit 02: $M_{12} = M_{21}$, from Neumann's formula), so $\boldsymbol\Lambda = \mathsf L\mathbf i$ is the gradient of $\tfrac12\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}\mathsf L\mathbf i$. Differentiating in $\theta$ at fixed $\mathbf i$ gives the torque.

The same as $-\partial W/\partial\theta|_\Lambda$. One coil: $W = \Lambda^2/2L(\theta)$, so $-\partial_\theta W|_\Lambda = \Lambda^2L'/2L^2 = \tfrac12i^2L'$ ✓. The two energies are equal in value and differ as functions, and the derivatives at different held variables come out equal, as the theorem said they must.

Two coils written out. $W' = \tfrac12L_1i_1^2 + Mi_1i_2 + \tfrac12L_2i_2^2$ — unit 02's energy of two loops, with every inductance now a function of $\theta$ — and $T = \tfrac12i_1^2L_1' + i_1i_2M' + \tfrac12i_2^2L_2'$. The middle term is the one every motor in this deck except the reluctance motor runs on: a torque proportional to the product of two currents.

Dimensions. An angle is dimensionless, so $\mathrm{J/rad}$ is a joule per radian of turn: the work per radian, which is a torque in newton-metres.

12 · co-energy

Half to the field, half to the shaft

the linear case's signature

A linear coupling turns by $\mathrm d\theta$ at constant current $i$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}\text{electrical in:}\quad i\,\mathrm d\Lambda &= i^2\,\mathrm dL\\ \text{stored:}\quad \mathrm dW &= \tfrac12 i^2\,\mathrm dL\\ \text{work:}\quad T\,\mathrm d\theta &= \tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL\end{aligned}$$

Half of what the source delivers is stored, half goes to the shaft.

proof

$\mathrm d\Lambda = i\,\mathrm dL$ at fixed $i$. $W = \tfrac12Li^2$ changes by $\tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL$. $T\,\mathrm d\theta = \tfrac12i^2L'\,\mathrm d\theta = \tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL$. Sum: $i^2\,\mathrm dL$ ✓. $\square$

At constant $\Lambda$ instead, the source gives nothing and the work comes out of the field: $\mathrm dW = -T\,\mathrm d\theta$.

The strip of width $i$ and height $\mathrm d\Lambda$ is what the source delivers; the thin triangle, half of it, is the work; the other half is the growth of $W$. Not to scale: $\mathrm dL$ is drawn large.

Reading the figure. In the $\Lambda$–$i$ plane the state moves from the point on the lower line to the point on the upper one, straight up, at fixed $i$. The work done on the shaft is the change of co-energy at fixed current, the area under the line: it grows by the triangle between the two lines. The electrical input is $\int i\,\mathrm d\Lambda$ along the vertical step, the rectangle of width $i$. Its area is twice the triangle's, since the triangle has the same base $\mathrm d\Lambda$ and height $i$. The rest of the strip, the other half, is the growth of $W$, the area to the left of the line.

The energy check, as a balance. $i^2\,\mathrm dL = \tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL + \tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL$: input equals stored plus work. This is this unit's version of the course's energy check, and it holds for every result below. In a machine running steadily the stored energy returns to the same value each revolution, so over a revolution the half-and-half bookkeeping nets to "electrical in equals work out" — section 2's $ei = T\omega$, and section 3's $P = T\omega$.

Constant flux linkage. If instead $\Lambda$ is held fixed (the coil's terminals shorted by an ideal wire, say), $i\,\mathrm d\Lambda = 0$ and $W = \Lambda^2/2L$ changes by $-\Lambda^2\,\mathrm dL/2L^2 = -\tfrac12i^2\,\mathrm dL$: the field gives up exactly the work done. Same torque, different energy source. Problem P1 proves both cases in general.

12 · Lagrangian

The same torque from a Lagrangian

Generalised coordinates: the charge $q$ that has passed through the coil, with $\dot q = i$, and the angle $\theta$ (MR 12). Write $\mathcal L$ for the Lagrangian, to keep $L$ for inductance:

$$\mathcal L(q, \theta, \dot q, \dot\theta) = \tfrac12L(\theta)\,\dot q^2 + \tfrac12J\dot\theta^2 .$$

Non-potential forces enter as generalised forces: on $q$, the source voltage less the resistive drop, $Q_q = v_s - R\dot q$; on $\theta$, the load, $Q_\theta = -T_{\mathrm{load}}$.

Lagrange's equations give $$\begin{aligned}v_s &= Ri + \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(L(\theta)\,i\big),\\ J\ddot\theta &= \tfrac12i^2L'(\theta) - T_{\mathrm{load}} .\end{aligned}$$

Units of $Q_q$: $\mathrm{J/C} = \mathrm V$ ✓. The torque term is $\partial W'/\partial\theta$ — the same sign.

Two coordinates, one circuit and one rotor. The inductance of the coil depends on where the iron rotor points; that dependence is the whole coupling.

Full proof

MR 12 (From d'Alembert to Lagrange's equations) gives, for each coordinate, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot q_a} - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial q_a} = Q_a$ with $Q_a$ the generalised force of everything not in $\mathcal L$. Take that form as the model of the electromechanical system; its justification is that its equations are the ones already derived.

The $q$ equation. $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q = L(\theta)\dot q = L(\theta)i$; $\partial\mathcal L/\partial q = 0$. So $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(L(\theta)i) = v_s - Ri$: Kirchhoff's voltage law round the loop, with the coil's voltage $\mathrm d\Lambda/\mathrm dt$ including the term $iL'(\theta)\dot\theta$ from the rotor's motion ✓.

The $\theta$ equation. $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\theta = J\dot\theta$; $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\theta = \tfrac12L'(\theta)\dot q^2$. So $J\ddot\theta - \tfrac12i^2L'(\theta) = -T_{\mathrm{load}}$: Newton for a rotor, with the electrical torque $\tfrac12i^2L'$ — the co-energy slide's $\partial W'/\partial\theta|_i$ ✓.

Energy, checked. Multiply the first equation by $i$ and the second by $\dot\theta$ and add: $v_si = Ri^2 + \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\tfrac12Li^2 + \tfrac12J\dot\theta^2\big) + T_{\mathrm{load}}\dot\theta$, using $i\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(Li) - \tfrac12i^2L'\dot\theta = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\tfrac12Li^2)$. Source power equals resistive loss plus growth of magnetic and kinetic energy plus work on the load. This is MR 12's energy function (The energy function) with its non-conservative forces.

What is a model here. A charge as a coordinate is not a particle's position, and MR 12's derivation from d'Alembert does not apply to it literally. What makes the Lagrangian legitimate is that it reproduces Faraday's law for the circuit and the energy balance of the coupling, both already proved. Problem P3 repeats this with a second coil.

12 · Lagrangian

Magnetic energy is kinetic

the sign, explained

$\tfrac12Li^2 = \tfrac12L\dot q^2$ is quadratic in a velocity, so it enters $\mathcal L = T - V$ with a plus sign, as kinetic energy does, and its $\theta$-derivative enters the equation of motion with a plus sign:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\theta} = +\tfrac12\dot q^2L'(\theta) = +\frac{\partial W'}{\partial\theta}\bigg|_i .$$
the wrong sign, and how it fails

Treated as a potential, $V = \tfrac12Li^2$ would give $J\ddot\theta = -\tfrac12i^2L'$: iron pushed out of a coil, towards low inductance. Iron is pulled in.

The Legendre pair $(W', W)$ is the pair (Lagrangian, energy function): constant current is constant velocity, constant flux linkage is constant momentum.

Why the pairing holds. The momentum conjugate to $q$ is $p_q = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q = L(\theta)\dot q = \Lambda$. The magnetic part of MR 12's energy function is $\dot q\,p_q - \tfrac12L\dot q^2 = \Lambda i - W' = W$: exactly the co-energy slide's Legendre transform, read backwards. MR 14 calls the energy function, written in momenta, the Hamiltonian; so the stored energy $W(\Lambda, \theta)$ is the magnetic part of a Hamiltonian, and $T = -\partial W/\partial\theta|_\Lambda$ is Hamilton's $\dot p_\theta = -\partial H/\partial\theta$. The two torque formulas of this section are the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian forms of one equation.

The failed sign, as a check against experiment. A solenoid pulls an iron core into itself: that is how a relay or a door lock's solenoid closes. Inductance is larger with the iron in (unit 07: iron multiplies a path's inductance by $\mu_r$), so the observed force points towards larger $L$, which the plus sign gives and the minus sign does not.

Where the analogy stops. Capacitor energy $\tfrac12q^2/C$ depends on the coordinate $q$, not its velocity, and it enters $\mathcal L$ as a potential, with a minus sign. A machine built on electric fields would have the opposite-looking signs; this unit's machines are all magnetic.

12 · reluctance

An iron rotor in a coil: reluctance torque

A bar of iron turns inside a fixed coil. The coil's inductance is largest when the bar lies along its axis (least air in the flux path) and smallest across it. The bar is the same after a half-turn, so $L$ has period $\pi$. Assume the simplest such form:

$$L(\theta) = L_0 + L_2\cos2\theta .$$
reluctance torque $$T = \tfrac12i^2L'(\theta) = -\,i^2L_2\sin2\theta$$

At any constant current the rotor turns towards $\theta = 0$, the aligned position, where $L$ is greatest. It needs no current in the rotor.

Assumed: $L = 0.8 + 0.4\cos2\theta$ H. At 2 A DC the peak torque is $4\times0.4 = 1.6$ N·m, at $\theta = \mp45°$.

Computed for the assumed $L(\theta)$. The torque is always towards the nearest maximum of $L$: aligned positions (green) are stable, the crossed one (red) is not.

Why $L$ depends on angle. The coil's flux has to cross the bore, through the iron bar where it can and through air where it cannot. Unit 07 found that a path through iron carries $\mu_r$ times the flux per ampere-turn of the same path in air. With an air gap in an iron path the two add like resistances in series: Ampère's law round the path gives $H_{\text{iron}}l + H_{\text{gap}}g = Ni$, and the same $B$ crosses both, so $B = \mu_0Ni/(l/\mu_r + g)$ — a gap of length $g$ counts as much as $\mu_r g$ of iron. Turning the bar changes how much air the flux must cross, and so changes $L$. The reciprocal of the flux per ampere-turn is the path's reluctance (unit 07's remark); "reluctance torque" is the torque towards least reluctance, greatest inductance.

The form of $L(\theta)$. The period $\pi$ is derived: the bar is unchanged by a half-turn, so $L(\theta + \pi) = L(\theta)$. It is also unchanged by reflection in the coil's axis, $L(-\theta) = L(\theta)$. A function with both properties has a Fourier series $L_0 + L_2\cos2\theta + L_4\cos4\theta + \dots$ (MR 19); keeping the first two terms is the assumption. The numbers 0.8 and 0.4 H are assumed for the example.

Stability. At $\theta = 0$, $\mathrm dT/\mathrm d\theta = -2i^2L_2 \lt 0$: turned slightly, the rotor is pushed back. At $\theta = \pi/2$ the sign reverses. A compass needle behaves the same way, for a related reason.

Saturation, in one sentence. At large currents the iron saturates (unit 07's curve), $\Lambda$ stops being linear in $i$, and the co-energy must be computed as an area under a curve as on Co-energy, and torque at constant current; nothing else changes, and this deck stays linear.

12 · reluctance · example

Worked: the reluctance motor's mean torque

alternating current, rotor turning with it

Feed $i = \hat I\cos\omega t$ and let the rotor turn at $\omega$, lagging by $\delta$: $\theta = \omega t - \delta$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}T(t) &= -\hat I^2L_2\cos^2\!\omega t\,\sin2\theta\\ &= -\tfrac12\hat I^2L_2\,(1 + \cos2\omega t)\sin2\theta ,\end{aligned}$$

with $2\theta = 2\omega t - 2\delta$.

Average over a period: $\langle\sin(2\omega t - 2\delta)\rangle = 0$ and $\langle\cos2\omega t\,\sin(2\omega t - 2\delta)\rangle = \tfrac12\sin(-2\delta)$. So

$$\boxed{\,\langle T\rangle = \frac{\hat I^2L_2}{4}\,\sin2\delta\,}$$

With $\hat I = 2$ A and $L_2 = 0.4$ H: at most $\mathbf{0.40}$ N·m, at $\delta = 45°$ — a quarter of the 1.6 N·m peak static torque at 2 A DC.

Units: $\mathrm{A^2\,H} = \mathrm J = \mathrm{N\,m}$ per radian ✓.

Computed for the assumed values. The rotor lagging the current ($\delta \gt 0$) is driven; leading, it is braked and returns energy to the source. Past 45° a larger load cannot be held: the rotor falls out of step.

Full proof

$T = \tfrac12i^2L'(\theta) = -i^2L_2\sin2\theta$ with $i^2 = \hat I^2\cos^2\omega t = \tfrac12\hat I^2(1 + \cos2\omega t)$ and $2\theta = 2\omega t - 2\delta$. The average of $\sin(2\omega t - 2\delta)$ over a period $\pi/\omega$ is zero. By the product-to-sum formula, $\cos2\omega t\sin(2\omega t - 2\delta) = \tfrac12[\sin(4\omega t - 2\delta) + \sin(-2\delta)]$, whose average is $-\tfrac12\sin2\delta$. So $\langle T\rangle = -\tfrac12\hat I^2L_2\cdot(-\tfrac12\sin2\delta) = \tfrac14\hat I^2L_2\sin2\delta$.

Numbers. $\hat I^2L_2/4 = 4\times0.4/4 = 0.40$ N·m. The peak static torque at a steady 2 A is $i^2L_2 = 1.6$ N·m. The factor of four is two factors of two: the mean of $\cos^2$ is a half, and only half of the remaining product survives averaging. (A steady 2 A and a sinusoid of peak 2 A are compared; the sinusoid's RMS value is 1.41 A.)

Energy. The mean power to the shaft is $\langle T\rangle\omega$: at 50 Hz and $\delta = 45°$, $0.40\times314.16 = 126$ W. Over a cycle the stored energy returns to its starting value, so by the energy balance this is exactly the mean electrical power the coil takes in. For $\delta \lt 0$ both are negative: the same machine, driven, is a generator.

Speed. The rotor turns once per supply period, so at 50 Hz it turns at 3000 rpm. Two lobes on the bar make it, in the language of section 3, a two-pole machine.

12 · reluctance

A reluctance motor runs only at synchronous speed

Let the rotor turn at any other constant rate, $\theta = \omega_rt - \delta$. Then $T$ is a sum of sinusoids at frequencies $2\omega_r$ and $2\omega_r \pm 2\omega$, and

$$\langle T\rangle = 0\quad\text{unless}\quad\omega_r \in \{0, \pm\omega\} .$$

At $\omega_r = 0$ the mean torque only pulls the rotor to the aligned position and holds it there. So the motor cannot start itself: it must be brought to speed, and then it locks to the supply, at the synchronous speed.

the checkpoint's first part

From co-energy, $\langle T\rangle = \tfrac14\hat I^2L_2\sin2\delta$: 0.40 N·m at most for the assumed coil.

Computed: $T(t)$ in N·m for $\hat I = 2$ A and the assumed $L(\theta)$, same vertical scale in both panels. Locked to the supply, the pulsing torque has a mean; slipping, its mean over the beat is zero.

Full proof

$T = -\tfrac12\hat I^2L_2(1 + \cos2\omega t)\sin(2\omega_rt - 2\delta)$. Expand $\cos2\omega t\,\sin(2\omega_rt - 2\delta) = \tfrac12[\sin(2(\omega_r + \omega)t - 2\delta) + \sin(2(\omega_r - \omega)t - 2\delta)]$. So $T$ is a sum of three sinusoids, at angular frequencies $2\omega_r$, $2(\omega_r + \omega)$ and $2(\omega_r - \omega)$, each with a constant amplitude. The long-time mean of $\sin(\Omega t + c)$ is zero for $\Omega \ne 0$ and $\sin c$ for $\Omega = 0$. So $\langle T\rangle = 0$ unless one of the three frequencies vanishes: $\omega_r = 0$, $\omega_r = -\omega$ or $\omega_r = \omega$. $\square$

At $\omega_r = \pm\omega$: the previous slide's $\tfrac14\hat I^2L_2\sin2\delta$, turning either way — a single coil's field pulses rather than rotates, and a pulsating field is two fields rotating in opposite directions (unit 11's negative sequence is the backward one), so the rotor can lock to either. At $\omega_r = 0$: $\theta = -\delta$ is fixed and $\langle T\rangle = -\tfrac12\hat I^2L_2\sin2\theta$, the static aligning torque with $i^2$ replaced by its mean: it turns the rotor to $\theta = 0$ and holds it.

The figure's means. Computed over the plotted windows: 0.400 N·m for the locked rotor and zero (to $10^{-16}$) for the slipping one, whose window holds a whole number of beats.

What it offers. A reluctance motor that has been brought to speed turns at a speed locked exactly to the supply frequency, with no current in its rotor; how it is brought up to speed is a design question this unit leaves out. Fitzgerald ch. 8, "Variable-Reluctance Machines and Stepping Motors", treats such machines.

12 · back-EMF

The commutator: a switch that keeps the torque one-signed

A DC machine: a fixed magnetic field, and a turning coil, the armature. The flux the field sends through the coil goes as $\cos\theta$, so its rate of change with angle reverses every half-turn.

The commutator — a split ring on the shaft, touched by fixed brushes — reverses the coil's connections every half-turn. Seen from the terminals, $\partial\Lambda/\partial\theta$ then keeps one sign.

the model

With many coils and segments the ripple is small. The idealisation: between terminals,

$$\frac{\partial\Lambda}{\partial\theta} = k ,\quad\text{a constant.}$$

Schematic machine, computed curves. One commutated coil gives rectified half-sines; eight coils spaced round the armature, in series, give a ripple of 1.9% (both normalised to their peak).

The system, from nothing. The field is made by magnets or by a winding carrying a steady current; this unit only names permanent magnets, whose materials are out of scope. The armature is a set of coils on an iron rotor. A coil turning in a fixed field sees its flux linkage change as $\cos\theta$, and by Faraday's law it generates an alternating voltage. The commutator is a ring cut into segments, each joined to a coil end, sliding under fixed contacts, the brushes. As the shaft turns, the brushes pass from one segment to the next, and each coil's connection to the terminals is reversed at the moment its $\partial\Lambda/\partial\theta$ changes sign.

The curves. One coil, commutated: $|\sin\theta|$. Eight identical coils spaced $\pi/8$ apart and each commutated, in series: $\sum_{k=0}^{7}|\sin(\theta - k\pi/8)|$, which varies between 5.027 and 5.126 — a ripple of 1.9% of its peak. More coils shrink it further. The deck's model is the limit: $k$ constant.

Not modelled. Commutation itself — the switching of a coil's current while it is shorted by a brush — is a design problem (Fitzgerald §7.2, "Commutator Action", and §7.8, "Commutation and Interpoles"), and the slight dependence of $\Lambda$ on $i$ through the coil's own inductance $L$ is kept only as a constant $L$ on the next slide.

12 · back-EMF

$e = k\omega$ and $T = ki$: one constant

theorem

If $\Lambda = k\theta + Li$ with $k$, $L$ constant, then turning at $\omega = \dot\theta$ the armature generates the back-EMF, and carrying $i$ it makes a torque,

$$e = k\omega,\qquad T = ki ,$$

with the same $k$, the machine constant. And $ei = T\omega$ exactly.

proof

Faraday: $v = \dot\Lambda = k\omega + L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$. Co-energy: $W' = \int_0^i\Lambda\,\mathrm di' = k\theta i + \tfrac12Li^2$, so $T = \partial W'/\partial\theta|_i = ki$. Then $T\omega = ki\omega = ei$. $\square$

Units: $[k] = \mathrm{V\,s/rad} = \mathrm{Wb/rad}$ and $\mathrm{N\,m/A} = \mathrm{J/(A\,rad)} = \mathrm{V\,s/rad}$ ✓ — the same unit.

The DC machine as a circuit. The coupling is an EMF $k\omega$ under the passive convention: current into its + terminal is power absorbed, and it leaves by the shaft.

Full proof

The flux linkage. With $k$ constant, the field's contribution to the armature's flux linkage between commutations is $k\theta$ plus a constant, which is dropped (it changes neither $\dot\Lambda$ nor $\partial W'/\partial\theta$). The armature's own field adds $Li$. So $\Lambda(i, \theta) = k\theta + Li$ is linear in $i$ with an offset — not the $L(\theta)i$ of section 1, which is why the co-energy is computed from its integral, not from $\tfrac12Li^2$.

Faraday. $v = \mathrm d\Lambda/\mathrm dt = k\dot\theta + L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$. In a steady state the second term vanishes, and the coil shows $e = k\omega$.

Co-energy. $W'(i, \theta) = \int_0^i(k\theta + Li')\,\mathrm di' = k\theta i + \tfrac12Li^2$. At fixed $i$, $\partial W'/\partial\theta = ki$. Section 1's theorem applies because it needed only a lossless coupling with $\Lambda$ a function of $(i, \theta)$.

Why one constant is forced. The energy balance $i\,\mathrm d\Lambda = \mathrm dW + T\,\mathrm d\theta$ at steady current gives $ik\,\mathrm d\theta = 0 + T\,\mathrm d\theta$ (the stored $\tfrac12Li^2$ does not change). A machine whose torque constant differed from its EMF constant would make or destroy energy. In a motor's data the two are the same number in two units: V per rad/s and N·m per A.

The field winding. If the field is made by a winding whose current is the armature's own, $k$ grows with $i$ and the model changes; this deck keeps $k$ constant, an assumption.

Unit 11's field, with more poles

Unit 11: three coils 120° apart, fed 120° apart, make a field of constant magnitude turning at $\omega$, $\tfrac32\hat B\,e^{i\omega t}$. Idealised as uniform across the bore (notes), its radial part at angle $\varphi$ is

one north and one south region — two poles.

$$\boxed{\,B_r(\varphi, t) = \tfrac32\hat B\cos\big(\tfrac p2\varphi - \omega t\big)\,}$$

Computed: $B_r$ at 24 points round the bore at $t = 0$. With four poles the pattern repeats twice; in one supply cycle it moves on by half a turn.

Full proof

Two poles, from unit 11. Unit 11's field at the centre is the complex number $\tfrac32\hat B e^{i\omega t}$: a vector of fixed length pointing at angle $\omega t$. Unit 11 computed it at one point only; take it as uniform across the bore — the idealisation of this slide. The bore is the circle round the centre, and a uniform field $\vec B$ at angle $\omega t$ has radial component $|\vec B|\cos(\varphi - \omega t)$ at the point of the circle at angle $\varphi$. That is the two-pole pattern: outward where $\cos \gt 0$, the north region, inward on the other side. (The field in a real gap is shaped by the iron round it; this deck assumes the radial component has this sinusoidal pattern round the bore. $p$ poles. Model phase $k$'s winding as making a radial field $\hat B\cos(\tfrac p2(\varphi - \varphi_k))\,\cos(\omega t - \tfrac{2\pi k}{3})$ with $\varphi_k = \tfrac{2\pi k}{3}\big/\tfrac p2$: the two-pole pattern compressed so that it repeats $p/2$ times round the bore. 12 · synchronous machine

A rotor that locks to the field

A synchronous machine: the stator of the last slides, and a rotor carrying a steady field — a field winding fed with direct current $i_f$ (or magnets), with the same $p$ poles.

torque between two fields

By co-energy, the torque on the rotor is the cross term $i_f\sum_ki_k\,\partial M_{kf}/\partial\theta$. With the rotor turning at $\omega_s$ and its field lagging the stator's by a fixed electrical angle, it is constant, proportional to $\sin$ of that angle. At any other speed its mean is zero.

So the rotor turns at exactly $n_s$, and the angle between the fields adjusts to the load.

Schematic, two poles, as a motor: the stator's field leads, the rotor's lags by a fixed angle, and the pull between them is a steady torque. As a generator the order reverses: the rotor's field is ahead.

Full proof (sketch here; in full in problem P8)

Six coupled coils would be the full model; take the three stator phases $k = 0, 1, 2$ and the field winding $f$. With a round rotor the stator's self- and mutual inductances do not depend on the rotor's angle, and neither does the field winding's self-inductance. Only the mutual inductances depend on it: with sinusoidal windings, $M_{kf} = M_f\cos(\tfrac p2\theta - \tfrac{2\pi k}3)$, where $\theta$ is the rotor's mechanical angle. By The linear case, the torque is $T = i_f\sum_ki_k\,\partial M_{kf}/\partial\theta = -\tfrac p2M_fi_f\sum_k i_k\sin(\tfrac p2\theta - \tfrac{2\pi k}3)$.

Feed $i_k = \sqrt2I\cos(\omega t - \tfrac{2\pi k}3 - \gamma)$ and let $\tfrac p2\theta = \omega_et + \theta_0$. The product-to-sum formula turns each term into $\tfrac12[\sin(\omega_et + \theta_0 + \omega t - \tfrac{4\pi k}3 - \gamma) + \sin(\omega_et + \theta_0 - \omega t + \gamma)]$. Summed over $k$, the first kind cancels ($1 + \alpha + \alpha^2 = 0$) and the second adds up to $\tfrac32\sin((\omega_e - \omega)t + \theta_0 + \gamma)$. So $T = -\tfrac{3\sqrt2}{2}\tfrac p2M_fi_fI\,\sin((\omega_e - \omega)t + \theta_0 + \gamma)$. If $\omega_e = \omega$ — the rotor at $\omega/(p/2)$, synchronous — this is a constant set by an angle. Otherwise it is a sinusoid with zero mean. $\square$

Energy. Multiply by $\dot\theta$: $T\dot\theta = \sum_ki_k\,(i_f\,\partial M_{kf}/\partial\theta)\,\dot\theta = \sum_ki_ke_k$, where $e_k = i_f\,\mathrm dM_{kf}/\mathrm dt$ is the voltage the turning rotor induces in phase $k$. The shaft's power is exactly the electrical power the induced EMFs absorb. This is the identity behind the next slides' "mechanical power equals $P$".

Unit 11 promised that this unit turns its rotating field into torque. This is it: the field's crest pulls the rotor's pole along, steadily, only when the two turn together.

An EMF behind a reactance

Phase $a$'s flux linkage has two parts. From the rotor: $\Lambda_{af} = M_fi_f\cos(\omega t + \delta_0)$, which induces a sinusoidal EMF of RMS value

$$|E| = \omega M_fi_f/\sqrt2 .$$

From the stator's own currents: $L_{aa}i_a + M_{ab}(i_b + i_c) = (L_{aa} - M_{ab})\,i_a$, since $i_a + i_b + i_c = 0$. That is an inductance; at $\omega$, the synchronous reactance $X_s = \omega(L_{aa} - M_{ab})$.

the round-rotor model, per phase $$V = E - iX_sI$$

in the generator convention: $I$ leaves the + terminal. Resistance and saturation are left out: an assumption of the model.

The synchronous generator, per phase (unit 11's per-phase equivalent). $E$ comes from the rotor's field and is set by $i_f$; $X_s$ is the stator's own field acting back on it.

Full proof

The rotor's EMF. The rotor turns at synchronous speed, so its electrical angle is $\tfrac p2\theta = \omega t + \delta_0$ and $\Lambda_{af} = M_fi_f\cos(\omega t + \delta_0)$ (previous slide's notes). Faraday's law (unit 02): its contribution to the voltage is $-\omega M_fi_f\sin(\omega t + \delta_0)$, a sinusoid of peak $\omega M_fi_f$, RMS $\omega M_fi_f/\sqrt2$. It is proportional to the field current and to the speed.

The stator's own field. With a round rotor, phase $a$'s self-inductance $L_{aa}$ and its mutual inductance to $b$ and $c$, $M_{ab} = M_{ac}$ (by symmetry), do not depend on the rotor's angle. Balanced currents sum to zero, so $L_{aa}i_a + M_{ab}(i_b + i_c) = (L_{aa} - M_{ab})i_a$: phase $a$ sees only its own current, through an inductance $L_s = L_{aa} - M_{ab}$. Since the phases' axes are 120° apart, $M_{ab}$ is negative, and $L_s \gt L_{aa}$. For ideal sinusoidal windings with no leakage $M_{ab} = L_{aa}\cos120° = -\tfrac12L_{aa}$ and $L_s = \tfrac32L_{aa}$ — unit 11's factor $\tfrac32$ again: the three phases' fields add.

The equation. Under the passive convention for the phase, with $v_a = \mathrm d\Lambda_a/\mathrm dt$ and no resistance, the phasor form is $V = iX_sI_p + E$. The generator convention takes the reference current out of the + terminal, $I = -I_p$, so that a generator delivering power has $\mathrm{Re}(VI^*) \gt 0$. Then $V = E - iX_sI$. This is the one place the deck departs from the course's passive convention, because a generator's power is more naturally counted as delivered; the two differ only by the sign of $I$.

What is assumed. The stator's resistance and the iron's saturation are left out; that is this deck's assumption, not a result, and the circuit above is derived only under it. Fitzgerald ch. 5 builds the same equivalent circuit at length (§5.2, "Synchronous-Machine Inductances; Equivalent Circuits"). Salient poles, transient reactances and the d–q transformation are left out here; for salient poles and the d–q variables, see Fitzgerald §5.6–5.7 and appendix C, "The dq0 Transformation".

12 · induction motor

A cage in the rotating field must slip

An induction motor's rotor is a squirrel cage: bars in slots, joined by a ring at each end. No current is fed to it.

  • If the rotor turned at $n_s$, the field would be still relative to it: no flux change through its loops, no current, no torque.
  • So it turns slower, at $n$. The slip is $s = (n_s - n)/n_s$.
  • The field passes the rotor at $sn_s$, so the rotor's currents alternate at $sf$.
the pump at its operating point

$n = 1455$ rpm, $n_s = 1500$: $s = 45/1500 = 0.03$. Rotor currents at $0.03\times50 = 1.5$ Hz.

Schematic. Any two bars and the ring segments between them form a shorted loop; as the field slips past, its flux through the loop changes, and current flows round it.

The system, from nothing. The cage's bars lie in slots in a laminated iron rotor, and the end rings short all of them together. It has no brushes, no commutator, no winding to insulate and no connection to the supply; this is what makes the induction motor robust. "Squirrel cage" is what the bars and rings look like with the iron removed, as drawn.

Why it must slip. In the rotor's frame the field moves past at $(n_s - n)$ rpm. Every rotor loop sees its flux change at that rate, and by Faraday's law (unit 02) an EMF proportional to it drives current round the loop. At $n = n_s$ the field is stationary in the rotor's frame: nothing changes, no EMF, no current, no torque. So a motor carrying any load runs below synchronous speed, and the slip grows with the load.

The rotor's frequency. A $p$-pole pattern passing at $sn_s$ rpm brings $(p/2)\cdot sn_s/60 = sf$ pole pairs past each bar per second: the rotor current alternates at $sf$. The pump at 3% slip: 1.5 Hz.

Negative sequence (unit 11). A backward-rotating field, from an unbalanced supply, passes the rotor at $n_s + n$, a slip of $(n_s + n)/n_s = 2 - s$ relative to it: rotor currents near $2f$ = 100 Hz, and a torque opposing the motion. The motor gets little torque from it and a lot of rotor heating — unit 11's "negative sequence heats motors".

The rotor as a transformer secondary at frequency $sf$

Per phase, referred to the stator as in unit 07: the stator's flux induces $E$ in a winding at standstill. Turning at slip $s$, the rate of change is $s$ times smaller, so the rotor sees $sE$ at frequency $sf$, and its leakage reactance is $sX_2'$:

$$sE = (R_2' + isX_2')\,I_2' .$$
the step: divide by $s$ $$E = \Big(\frac{R_2'}{s} + iX_2'\Big)I_2'$$

— a circuit at the supply frequency with resistance $R_2'/s$. Same current, same phase relation to $E$.

The rotor's own circuit (above) and the same equation divided by $s$ (below). The resistance $R_2'/s$ is where the shaft's power hides.

Full proof

Referring. Unit 07 replaced a secondary winding by its equivalent seen through the turns ratio: voltages times $n$, currents divided by $n$, impedances times $n^2$. Do the same with the rotor, and mark the referred quantities with a prime. Let $E$ be the RMS EMF the air-gap flux would induce in a (referred) rotor phase at standstill, at frequency $f$.

At slip $s$. The flux pattern passes the rotor $s$ times as fast, so by Faraday's law the induced EMF is $sE$, at frequency $sf$. The rotor's leakage inductance $L_2'$ (flux that links the rotor but not the stator, unit 07's leakage) has reactance $2\pi sf\,L_2' = sX_2'$, where $X_2' = 2\pi fL_2'$ is its value at supply frequency. Kirchhoff round the rotor: $sE = (R_2' + isX_2')I_2'$.

Dividing by $s$. $E = (R_2'/s + iX_2')I_2'$ is the same complex equation; nothing about the current has changed. Why it may be drawn at frequency $f$ beside the stator: the rotor's currents, at $sf$ in the rotor's frame, make their own rotating field turning at $s\omega_s$ relative to the rotor, and the rotor itself turns at $(1 - s)\omega_s$. So their field turns at $\omega_s$ in the stator's frame — in step with the stator's — and induces voltages in the stator at frequency $f$. Seen from the stator, the rotor current is a supply-frequency phasor of the same magnitude and phase relative to $E$.

Air-gap power: $s$ to the rotor's copper, $1 - s$ to the shaft

the split of the air-gap power

$P_{\mathrm{ag}} = 3|I_2'|^2R_2'/s$, the power crossing from stator to rotor. Then

$$P_{\text{rotor Cu}} = sP_{\mathrm{ag}},\quad P_{\text{mech}} = (1 - s)P_{\mathrm{ag}},$$ $$T = \frac{P_{\mathrm{ag}}}{\omega_s}.$$
proof

In the rotor's frame its EMF is $sE$, so the rotor receives $3\,\mathrm{Re}(sE\,I_2'^*) = s\cdot3\,\mathrm{Re}(EI_2'^*) = sP_{\mathrm{ag}}$, and all of it heats $R_2'$. Energy: the rest, $(1 - s)P_{\mathrm{ag}}$, leaves by the shaft. At speed $(1 - s)\omega_s$ that is a torque $P_{\mathrm{ag}}/\omega_s$. $\square$

Computed for the pump at $s = 0.03$; bar heights 1 px per 100 W, losses drawn as the branches leaving at the top (not to the same scale). Losses beyond copper are not modelled.

Full proof

The air-gap power. Everything to the right of the magnetising branch absorbs $3\,\mathrm{Re}(EI_2'^*)$. By the divided equation, $E = (R_2'/s + iX_2')I_2'$, so $3\,\mathrm{Re}(EI_2'^*) = 3|I_2'|^2R_2'/s$: that is $P_{\mathrm{ag}}$, the power that crosses the gap. (The magnetising branch is a pure reactance and absorbs no real power; the stator's $R_1$ is upstream.)

The rotor's share. In its own frame the rotor is a circuit with EMF $sE$, resistance $R_2'$ and reactance $sX_2'$, carrying $I_2'$. The power its EMF delivers is $3\,\mathrm{Re}(sEI_2'^*) = sP_{\mathrm{ag}}$, and a reactance absorbs none, so all of it is dissipated: $3|I_2'|^2R_2' = sP_{\mathrm{ag}}$. Check directly: $3|I_2'|^2R_2' = s\cdot3|I_2'|^2R_2'/s$ ✓.

The shaft's share. The coupling is lossless and its stored energy is steady, so what crosses the gap and is not dissipated in the rotor leaves by the shaft: $(1 - s)P_{\mathrm{ag}} = 3|I_2'|^2R_2'(1 - s)/s$ — the power in the second resistor of the split. That is why the split was made. The torque is the mechanical power over the mechanical speed: $(1 - s)P_{\mathrm{ag}}/((1 - s)\omega_s) = P_{\mathrm{ag}}/\omega_s$. Read the other way: the field, turning at $\omega_s$, exerts torque $T$ on the rotor and so delivers $T\omega_s$ across the gap; the rotor, turning slower, takes out only $T(1 - s)\omega_s$; the difference is the slip loss.

The numbers. $P_{\mathrm{ag}} = 9067$ W, $0.03\times9067 = 272$ W, $0.97\times9067 = 8795$ W, $\omega_s = 157.08$ rad/s, $T = 57.7$ N·m. Stator copper loss $3\times16.38^2\times0.5 = 402$ W. Input $9470$ W $= 402 + 9067$ ✓.

The co-energy torque. Section 1 promised that this torque is $\tfrac12\mathbf i^{\mathsf T}(\mathrm d\mathsf L/\mathrm d\theta)\mathbf i$ for the six coils; the proof is the same as for the synchronous machine (problem P8): the shaft's power equals the power absorbed by the motional EMFs, and that is what $P_{\text{mech}}$ counts.

$T = P_{\mathrm{ag}}/\omega_s$, and $P_{\mathrm{ag}}$ is the power into a resistance $R = R_2'/s$ behind a fixed source impedance $R_{\mathrm{th}} + iX$, $X = X_{\mathrm{th}} + X_2'$ — unit 06's resistive load on a complex source. $$R^2 = R_{\mathrm{th}}^2 + X^2:\qquad \frac{R_2'}{s_{\max T}} = |R_{\mathrm{th}} + iX| .$$

There, the bracket is $2R(R + R_{\mathrm{th}})$, and

$$T_{\max} = \frac{3V_{\mathrm{th}}^2}{2\omega_s\big(R_{\mathrm{th}} + |R_{\mathrm{th}} + iX|\big)} .$$

Pump: $|0.462 + 2.361i| = 2.406\ \Omega$, so $s_{\max T} = 0.45/2.406 = \mathbf{0.187}$ and

$$T_{\max} = \frac{3\times222.0^2}{2\times157.08\times2.868} = \mathbf{164.1\ N\,m}$$

Computed: the same torque, now against the load resistance on a log scale. Slowing down moves left. The peak is where the load resistance equals the magnitude of everything in series with it.

Full proof

Let $f(R) = R/[(a + R)^2 + X^2]$ with $a = R_{\mathrm{th}} \ge 0$. Then $f'(R) = [(a + R)^2 + X^2 - 2R(a + R)]/[\cdot]^2 = [a^2 + X^2 - R^2]/[\cdot]^2$, positive for $R^2 \lt a^2 + X^2$ and negative beyond: a single maximum at $R = \sqrt{a^2 + X^2}$. There $(a + R)^2 + X^2 = a^2 + 2aR + R^2 + X^2 = 2R^2 + 2aR = 2R(R + a)$, so $f = 1/(2(R + a))$ and $T_{\max} = 3V_{\mathrm{th}}^2/(2\omega_s(a + R))$.

Landing units 04 and 06. Unit 04's maximum-power theorem: a resistive load on a resistive source takes the most power when the two are equal. Unit 06 extended it to conjugate matching for a load that may be any impedance, and then, on When only the resistance can change, to exactly this case: a load that can only be a resistance takes the most power when it equals the magnitude of the source impedance, $|V_{\mathrm{th}}|^2/2(R_s + |Z_s|)$ — proved there by AM–GM, and by calculus above. The slip is the knob that makes the rotor's resistor variable. With $X = 0$ it is unit 04's.

Numbers. $X = 1.1613 + 1.2 = 2.3613\ \Omega$; $\sqrt{0.4622^2 + 2.3613^2} = 2.4061\ \Omega$; $s = 0.45/2.4061 = 0.18703$; $n = 1500(1 - 0.18703) = 1219.5$ rpm. $T_{\max} = 3\times222.03^2/(2\times157.080\times2.8683) = 164.13$ N·m.

What does not depend on $R_2'$. $T_{\max}$ contains no $R_2'$: changing the rotor resistance moves the slip at which the maximum occurs ($s_{\max T} \propto R_2'$), not its height. Problem P12 uses this.

12 · induction motor

Which side of the maximum is stable

The rotor obeys $J\dot\omega = T(\omega) - T_{\mathrm{load}}(\omega)$, a one-dimensional flow. An equilibrium $\omega^*$ is stable when

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\omega}\big(T - T_{\mathrm{load}}\big)\Big|_{\omega^*} \lt 0$$

(MR 02, linearisation in one dimension).

  • Above the maximum (small slip), $T$ falls as speed rises; with any load that rises or stays level, stable operating point.
  • Below it, $T$ rises with speed: a level load gives an unstable crossing.

A constant 100 N·m: crossings at 1413 rpm (stable) and 597 rpm (unstable). A load above 164.1 N·m: none — the motor stalls.

Computed, against an assumed constant load. Where the motor's torque exceeds the load the rotor speeds up, and where it falls short it slows: the arrows on the axis show the flow.

Full proof

Write $F(\omega) = (T(\omega) - T_{\mathrm{load}}(\omega))/J$, so $\dot\omega = F(\omega)$. At an equilibrium $F(\omega^*) = 0$. Linearising (MR 02, Linearisation: what it is, and what it claims), $\xi = \omega - \omega^*$ obeys $\dot\xi = F'(\omega^*)\xi$, which decays if $F'(\omega^*) \lt 0$ and grows if $F'(\omega^*) \gt 0$; in one dimension the sign of $F'$ decides stability whenever it is not zero (MR 02, Stability, defined). Speed is $\omega = (1 - s)\omega_s$, so $\mathrm dT/\mathrm d\omega = -(1/\omega_s)\,\mathrm dT/\mathrm ds$.

The two sides. For $s \lt s_{\max T}$, $T$ increases with $s$, so it decreases with $\omega$: $\mathrm dT/\mathrm d\omega \lt 0$. With $\mathrm dT_{\mathrm{load}}/\mathrm d\omega \ge 0$ (a load that does not ease off as it speeds up), $F' \lt 0$: stable. For $s \gt s_{\max T}$, $\mathrm dT/\mathrm d\omega \gt 0$, and the crossing is stable only if the load rises faster still.

The numbers. Constant 100 N·m: $T(s) = 100$ at $s = 0.0581$ (1412.8 rpm) and $s = 0.6019$ (597.2 rpm). Slopes there: $\mathrm dT/\mathrm dn = -0.852$ and $+0.082$ N·m per rpm. A load above $T_{\max} = 164.1$ N·m has no crossing: the rotor decelerates through the maximum to standstill — it stalls, and draws its 91.2 A until something trips.

The real system. The pump's load is not constant; the closing slide puts the motor against it.
