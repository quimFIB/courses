Part E · unit 13 · problems

Power flow and stability: the grid as a pendulum

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. On paper and without notes, you derive the two-bus transfer formula \(P = V_1V_2\sin\delta/X\) from \(S = VI^*\), and its maximum \(V_1V_2/X\). You then use the equal-area criterion to find the critical clearing angle for a machine running at \(\delta_0 = 30°\) when a fault cuts the transfer to zero: about 79.6°.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about four and a half hours. P1 and P8 are the checkpoint's. Conventions as in the deck: RMS phasors with \(e^{+i\omega t}\); complex power \(S = VI^*\); in power-flow formulas \(V_k\) is a bus voltage's magnitude and \(\delta_k\) its angle, so the phasor is \(\bar V_k = V_ke^{i\delta_k}\); \(S_k\) is the power injected into the network at bus \(k\); lines are lossless reactances; 50 Hz throughout, \(\omega_s = 2\pi\times50 = 314.16\) rad/s, and \(\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\) H/m.

Data, all assumed unless a source is named. The two-area model: Iberia, one machine of \(H = 4\) s on 50 GW, exporting 2000 MW over a tie of \(40\ \Omega\) per phase at 400 kV to Continental Europe, an infinite bus. Continental Europe for frequency: 300 GW online, \(H = 5\) s. The three-bus example, per unit on 100 MVA: \(X_{12} = 0.1\), \(X_{13} = 0.2\), \(X_{23} = 0.25\); bus 1 slack \(1\angle0\); bus 2 a generator with \(P = 1.0\), \(V = 1.0\); bus 3 a load of \(1.5 + 0.5i\). Unit 07's line geometry: bundles of equivalent radius \(r_{\text{eq}} = 0.2097\) m, phases 10 m apart. The street transformer: 400 kVA, 20 kV / 400 V, 4% (IEC 60076-5, Table 1). Regulation (EU) 2017/1485 for Continental Europe: reference incident 3000 MW (Art. 153); maximum instantaneous frequency deviation 800 mHz and maximum steady-state deviation 200 mHz (Annex III, Table 1); frequency containment reserve fully active within 30 s and at 200 mHz (Annex V).

Two buses and a reactance

P1 Proof: the two-bus transfer routine

exercises: complex power \(S = VI^*\) on a reactance; the maximum; the reactive-power energy check; the three-phase form (the checkpoint's first half)

Two buses with phasor voltages \(\bar V_1 = V_1e^{i\delta_1}\) and \(\bar V_2 = V_2e^{i\delta_2}\) are joined by a lossless series reactance \(X\), per phase. Let \(\delta = \delta_1 - \delta_2\).

  1. From \(S = VI^*\), derive the real power \(P\) sent from bus 1, the reactive power \(Q_1\) sent from bus 1 into the reactance, and the reactive power \(Q_2\) arriving at bus 2.
  2. Show that \(P\) is the same at both ends, and that \(Q_1 - Q_2 = |I|^2X\).
  3. Find the maximum of \(P\) over \(\delta\) and where it occurs.
  4. In a balanced three-phase system each phase carries this \(S\). Show that the total \(P\) is given by the same formula with line-to-line voltage magnitudes.
  5. Check your formulas on the tie of the two-area model: 400 kV at both ends, \(X = 40\ \Omega\), \(\delta = 30°\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three formulas, each a function of \(V_1, V_2, \delta, X\); then two identities; then an extremum; then a factor of 3 against a factor of \(\sqrt3\) squared; then numbers. Everything comes from one line: the current through the reactance.

Rung 2

The current from bus 1 to bus 2 is \(I = (\bar V_1 - \bar V_2)/(iX)\). Conjugate it — remember \((1/i)^* = -1/i = i\) — and multiply by \(\bar V_1\) for \(S_1\), by \(\bar V_2\) for \(S_2\). For part 2, the law of cosines turns \(V_1^2 + V_2^2 - 2V_1V_2\cos\delta\) into \(|\bar V_1 - \bar V_2|^2\).

Rung 3

First line: \(S_1 = \bar V_1I^* = \bar V_1\cdot\dfrac{i(\bar V_1^* - \bar V_2^*)}{X} = \dfrac iX\big(V_1^2 - V_1V_2e^{i\delta}\big)\). Now expand \(e^{i\delta}\) and separate real and imaginary parts.

Worked solution

Idea. One current, two products with it. Everything else is algebra on \(e^{i\delta}\).

1. \(I = (\bar V_1 - \bar V_2)/(iX)\), so \(I^* = (\bar V_1^* - \bar V_2^*)/(-iX) = i(\bar V_1^* - \bar V_2^*)/X\). Then

\[ S_1 = \bar V_1I^* = \frac iX\big(V_1^2 - V_1V_2e^{i\delta}\big) = \frac iX\big(V_1^2 - V_1V_2\cos\delta\big) + \frac{V_1V_2\sin\delta}{X}, \]

using \(\bar V_1\bar V_2^* = V_1V_2e^{i\delta}\). So

\[ P = \frac{V_1V_2\sin\delta}{X},\qquad Q_1 = \frac{V_1^2 - V_1V_2\cos\delta}{X}. \]

At bus 2 the power arriving from the line is \(S_2 = \bar V_2I^* = \frac iX\big(V_1V_2e^{-i\delta} - V_2^2\big) = \frac{V_1V_2\sin\delta}X + i\,\frac{V_1V_2\cos\delta - V_2^2}X\), so \(Q_2 = (V_1V_2\cos\delta - V_2^2)/X\).

2. The real parts of \(S_1\) and \(S_2\) are both \(V_1V_2\sin\delta/X\): a reactance absorbs no real power. And

\[ Q_1 - Q_2 = \frac{V_1^2 + V_2^2 - 2V_1V_2\cos\delta}{X} = \frac{|\bar V_1 - \bar V_2|^2}{X} = \frac{X^2|I|^2}{X} = |I|^2X, \]

by the law of cosines in the triangle \(0, \bar V_1, \bar V_2\), and \(|\bar V_1 - \bar V_2| = |iXI| = X|I|\). That is unit 06's \(Q\) of a reactance: the reactive power that goes in, less what comes out, is what the reactance holds.

3. \(\mathrm dP/\mathrm d\delta = V_1V_2\cos\delta/X\) vanishes at \(\delta = \pm90°\); the maximum is at \(\delta = 90°\):

\[ P_{\max} = \frac{V_1V_2}{X}. \]

4. With phase magnitudes \(V_k\) and line magnitudes \(U_k = \sqrt3V_k\) (unit 11), the three phases together carry \(3V_1V_2\sin\delta/X = U_1U_2\sin\delta/X\). The angle is the same, since both buses' line voltages are their phase voltages turned by the same \(30°\).

5. \(V^2/X = (4\times10^5)^2/40 = 4\times10^9\) W. \(P = 4000\sin30° = 2000\) MW. \(Q_1 = 4000(1 - \cos30°) = 535.9\) Mvar; \(Q_2 = -535.9\) Mvar: bus 2 also supplies 535.9 Mvar. \(Q_1 - Q_2 = 1071.8\) Mvar. Directly: \(|\bar U_1 - \bar U_2| = 2\times400\sin15° = 207.06\) kV, and \(207.06^2/40 = 1071.8\) Mvar ✓.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{V^2/\Omega} = \mathrm W\) ✓. Limits: \(\delta = 0\) and \(V_1 = V_2\) give \(I = 0\) and \(P = Q_1 = Q_2 = 0\) ✓; \(\delta = 90°\), \(V_1 = V_2 = V\): \(Q_1 = V^2/X\), \(Q_2 = -V^2/X\), and \(|I|^2X = 2V^2/X\) ✓.

P2 Transfer limits routine

exercises: \(P_{\max} = V^2/X\) at three voltages; the resistive cap \(V^2/4R\); a line's reactance from its geometry

  1. Take unit 07's 10 Ω line as a lossless reactance, with equal voltages at both ends. Find \(P_{\max}\) at 20, 132 and 400 kV, and set each beside unit 04's resistive cap \(V^2/4R\) for a 10 Ω resistance at the same voltage. Can 100 MW be sent at 20 kV?
  2. Unit 07's 400 kV line: \(r_{\text{eq}} = 0.2097\) m, phases at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side 10 m. Compute the per-phase inductance per kilometre, the reactance of 400 km at 50 Hz, and \(P_{\max}\).
  3. At what angle does that line carry 1000 MW? How long may it be and still carry 2000 MW at all?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Six powers, then an inductance, a reactance and a power, then an angle and a length. Every formula is in the deck's section 1.

Rung 2

The reactive limit has no factor 4: it is \(V^2/X\), at \(\delta = 90°\). The per-phase inductance of three conductors with balanced currents is \((\mu_0/2\pi)\ln(D/r)\), with \(r_{\text{eq}}\) for \(r\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\ln(10/0.2097) = \ln47.69 = 3.865\), so \(L' = 2\times10^{-7}\times3.865\) H/m.

Worked solution

Idea. Both limits scale as \(V^2\); they differ in what limits — phase for the reactance, heat and drop for the resistance — and by a factor of four.

1.

\(V\)\(V^2/X\), reactive\(V^2/4R\), resistive
20 kV40 MW10 MW
132 kV1742.4 MW435.6 MW
400 kV16 000 MW4000 MW

For instance \((2\times10^4)^2/10 = 4\times10^7\) W. At 20 kV, 100 MW is beyond both limits: no.

2. \(L' = (\mu_0/2\pi)\ln(D/r_{\text{eq}}) = 2\times10^{-7}\times3.8647 = 7.729\times10^{-7}\) H/m \(= 0.773\) mH/km. \(X' = 2\pi\times50\times7.729\times10^{-4} = 0.2428\ \Omega\)/km; over 400 km, \(X = 97.1\ \Omega\). \(P_{\max} = (4\times10^5)^2/97.13 = 1647\) MW.

3. \(\sin\delta = 1000/1647.3 = 0.6071\), \(\delta = 37.4°\). For 2000 MW the line needs \(X \le 1.6\times10^{11}/2\times10^9 = 80\ \Omega\), i.e. a length of at most \(80/0.2428 = 329.5\) km.

Check. \(V^2\) scaling: \((400/132)^2 = 9.18 = 16\,000/1742.4\) ✓. Units of \(L'\): \(\mu_0\) is H/m, the logarithm is a pure number ✓. The three-phase \(L'\) is half the two-wire loop's \(1.55\) mH/km, as the deck proved ✓.

P3 Per unit, and the 4% routine

exercises: base impedance; the ideal transformer vanishing in per unit; changing base; the 4% as a fault current

  1. Prove that with base voltages in the turns ratio, \(V_{b1} = nV_{b2}\), and one base power on both sides, an ideal transformer is a wire in per unit.
  2. The street transformer, in unit 07's single-phase model: compute the base impedances on its two sides for its own 400 kVA. Its 4% is a per-unit impedance: what is it in ohms on each side? What is the ratio, and why?
  3. Re-express the 0.04 pu on a 100 MVA base, voltages unchanged.
  4. Short the 400 V terminals with the 20 kV side held at 1 pu. Find the current in per unit on both bases and in amperes, and check that they agree.
  5. The 400 km line of P2 in per unit on 100 MVA and 400 kV: its reactance, and \(P_{\max}\) in per unit and in MW.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A proof of three equalities (\(v_1 = v_2\), \(i_1 = i_2\), \(z_1 = z_2\)), then numbers. In the single-phase model the base current is \(S_b/V_b\); in a three-phase system with line voltages, \(S_b/\sqrt3V_b\).

Rung 2

Write the ideal transformer's laws (unit 07) and the base quantities on each side; divide. To change base, convert to ohms with the old base and back with the new.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(Z_{b,20\,\mathrm{kV}} = (2\times10^4)^2/(4\times10^5) = 1000\ \Omega\).

Worked solution

Idea. The bases obey the same scaling laws as the transformer, so dividing by them removes the transformer.

1. Unit 07: \(V_1 = nV_2\), \(I_1 = I_2/n\) (with \(I_2\) delivered on side 2), and an impedance \(Z_2\) on side 2 is seen from side 1 as \(n^2Z_2\). The bases: \(V_{b1} = nV_{b2}\), \(I_{b1} = S_b/V_{b1} = I_{b2}/n\), \(Z_{b1} = V_{b1}^2/S_b = n^2Z_{b2}\). Hence \(v_1 = nV_2/nV_{b2} = v_2\), \(i_1 = (I_2/n)/(I_{b2}/n) = i_2\), and \(n^2Z_2/n^2Z_{b2} = Z_2/Z_{b2}\). In per unit the transformer imposes \(v_1 = v_2\) and \(i_1 = i_2\): a wire. \(\square\)

2. \(Z_b = 1000\ \Omega\) at 20 kV and \(400^2/4\times10^5 = 0.4\ \Omega\) at 400 V. \(0.04\times1000 = 40\ \Omega\) and \(0.04\times0.4 = 16\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\). The ratio \(1000/0.4 = 2500 = 50^2\): impedances reflect by \(n^2\), and the bases were chosen to do the same. (Unit 07 measured \(|8.13 + 39.2i| = 40.0\ \Omega\) ✓.)

3. \(Z = 40\ \Omega\) at 20 kV; the new base is \((2\times10^4)^2/10^8 = 4\ \Omega\); so \(z = 10\) pu. Equivalently \(0.04\times(100/0.4) = 10\).

4. On 400 kVA: \(i = 1/0.04 = 25\) pu; the base current at 400 V is \(4\times10^5/400 = 1000\) A, so 25 kA. On 100 MVA: \(i = 1/10 = 0.1\) pu, with base current \(10^8/400 = 250\) kA, so 25 kA ✓. Same current, two bases.

5. \(Z_b = (4\times10^5)^2/10^8 = 1600\ \Omega\); \(x = 97.13/1600 = 0.0607\) pu; \(P_{\max} = 1\cdot1/0.0607 = 16.47\) pu \(= 1647\) MW ✓ (P2).

Check. A per-unit impedance is independent of the side it is computed on (part 2), and a fault current is independent of the base it is computed in (part 4) — both are what the proof in part 1 promises.

Many buses

P4 Proof: the bus admittance matrix, and Kron medium

exercises: \(Y_{\text{bus}} = A\operatorname{diag}(y)A^{\mathsf T}\) from Kirchhoff's laws; symmetry and zero row sums; shunts; Kron reduction with complex weights

  1. A grid has incidence matrix \(A\) (unit 03's signs) and branch admittances \(y_e\). With \(I\) the currents injected into the buses from outside, prove \(I = Y_{\text{bus}}\bar V\) with \(Y_{\text{bus}} = A\operatorname{diag}(y_e)A^{\mathsf T}\).
  2. Prove that \(Y_{\text{bus}}\) is symmetric and that its rows sum to zero. For lossless branches show \(Y_{\text{bus}} = -iB\) with \(B\) a real weighted Laplacian.
  3. Add a capacitor bank of admittance \(0.2i\) pu from bus 3 of the three-bus example to the neutral. What changes in \(Y_{\text{bus}}\), and which row no longer sums to zero?
  4. Without the bank, suppose bus 2 injected nothing. Kron-reduce the three-bus network to buses 1 and 3 and find the equivalent reactance. Check it by series and parallel.
  5. Show that Kron-reducing \(Y_{\text{bus}} = -iB\) gives \(-i\) times the Kron reduction of \(B\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two proofs about a matrix product, a matrix entry, a \(2\times2\) Schur complement, and a scaling identity. Unit 04 did the real case of all of it.

Rung 2

Branch voltages are \(A^{\mathsf T}\bar V\), branch currents \(\operatorname{diag}(y_e)A^{\mathsf T}\bar V\), and the current law with injections is \(A\cdot(\text{branch currents}) = I\). A shunt is a branch to the neutral, which is grounded. For part 4, order the buses \((1, 3 \mid 2)\).

Rung 3

First line of part 4: \(B_{\text{red}} = \begin{pmatrix}15&-5\\-5&9\end{pmatrix} - \dfrac1{14}\begin{pmatrix}-10\\-4\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-10&-4\end{pmatrix}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Unit 04's \(L = AGA^{\mathsf T}\), with complex weights. Nothing in its algebra used that the weights were real, except positivity, which only the lossless case keeps.

1. The voltage law with phasors: branch voltages \(v = A^{\mathsf T}\bar V\) (unit 03). Each branch is linear: \(i_e = y_ev_e\), so \(i = \operatorname{diag}(y_e)A^{\mathsf T}\bar V\). The current law at each bus, with \(I_k\) entering from outside: the branch currents leaving bus \(k\) sum to \(I_k\), i.e. \(Ai = I\) (unit 03's sign convention: \(A_{ke} = +1\) when \(e\) leaves \(k\)). So \(I = A\operatorname{diag}(y_e)A^{\mathsf T}\bar V\). \(\square\)

2. \((ADA^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = AD^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T} = ADA^{\mathsf T}\) for diagonal \(D\): symmetric (not Hermitian — no conjugate appears). Each column of \(A\) has one \(+1\) and one \(-1\), so \(A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1 = 0\) and \(Y_{\text{bus}}\mathbf 1 = 0\): rows sum to zero. For \(y_e = 1/(iX_e) = -i/X_e\), \(Y_{\text{bus}} = -iA\operatorname{diag}(1/X_e)A^{\mathsf T} = -iB\), and \(B\) is unit 04's Laplacian with positive weights \(1/X_e\). \(\square\)

3. The bank is a branch from bus 3 to the neutral. Including the neutral as a node and then grounding it (deleting its row and column) leaves one extra term: \(+0.2i\) on the diagonal entry \((3,3)\). So \(Y_{33} = -9i + 0.2i = -8.8i\), and row 3 sums to \(0.2i\), the bank's admittance; rows 1 and 2 still sum to zero.

4. \(B_{\text{red}} = \begin{pmatrix}15 - 100/14&-5 - 40/14\\-5 - 40/14&9 - 16/14\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}7.857&-7.857\\-7.857&7.857\end{pmatrix}\). Rows sum to zero ✓. The equivalent line has weight \(1/X_{\text{eq}} = 7.857\), so \(X_{\text{eq}} = 0.1273\). Series and parallel: the path \(1\to2\to3\) is \(0.1 + 0.25 = 0.35\) in series, in parallel with the direct \(0.2\): \(0.2\times0.35/0.55 = 0.1273\) ✓.

5. \(Y_{KK} - Y_{KE}Y_{EE}^{-1}Y_{EK} = (-i)B_{KK} - (-i)B_{KE}\,(-i)^{-1}B_{EE}^{-1}\,(-i)B_{EK} = -i\big(B_{KK} - B_{KE}B_{EE}^{-1}B_{EK}\big)\). \(\square\)

Check. Part 4's answer is unit 04's theorem in action: the reduced matrix is again a Laplacian, and it agrees with the elementary reduction. Dimensions: per-unit admittances throughout ✓.

P5 The DC power flow routine

exercises: \(P = B\delta\) as unit 04's Kirchhoff problem; line flows; the slack; the first Newton step

  1. Solve the DC power flow for the three-bus example: \(\delta_2\), \(\delta_3\), the three line flows and the slack's injection.
  2. Generator 2 is raised to \(P_2 = 1.5\), the load unchanged. Repeat. What does the slack do now?
  3. Prove that for lossless lines the angles of the first Newton step from a flat start are the DC power flow's.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two \(2\times2\) linear systems and a short proof about a Jacobian at one point.

Rung 2

Strike the slack's row and column of \(B\) (diagonal 15, 14, 9; off-diagonal \(-10, -5, -4\)). Line flows are \((\delta_k - \delta_j)/X_{kj}\). For part 3, differentiate \(P_k = \sum_jb_{kj}V_kV_j\sin\delta_{kj}\) and evaluate at \(V = 1\), \(\delta = 0\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\begin{pmatrix}14&-4\\-4&9\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\delta_2\\\delta_3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1.0\\-1.5\end{pmatrix}\), determinant 110.

Worked solution

Idea. Angles are potentials, the weights \(1/X\) conductances, powers currents: unit 04's grounded Laplacian, solved by Cramer.

1. \(\delta_2 = (9\times1 - 4\times1.5)/110 = 3/110\) rad \(= 1.5626°\); \(\delta_3 = (14\times(-1.5) + 4\times1)/110 = -17/110\) rad \(= -8.8548°\). Flows: \(1\to2\): \(10(0 - 0.02727) = -0.2727\); \(1\to3\): \(5\times0.15455 = 0.7727\); \(2\to3\): \(4\times0.18182 = 0.7273\). The slack injects \(-0.2727 + 0.7727 = 0.5\).

2. Right-hand side \((1.5, -1.5)\): \(\delta_2 = (13.5 - 6)/110 = 0.06818\) rad \(= 3.906°\); \(\delta_3 = (-21 + 6)/110 = -0.13636\) rad \(= -7.813°\). Flows: \(1\to2\): \(-0.6818\); \(1\to3\): \(0.6818\); \(2\to3\): \(4\times0.20455 = 0.8182\). The slack injects \(-0.6818 + 0.6818 = 0\): generator 2 now covers the whole load, and the slack merely passes 0.68 through from line 12 to line 13.

3. \(\partial P_k/\partial\delta_j = -b_{kj}V_kV_j\cos\delta_{kj} \to -b_{kj} = B_{kj}\) for \(j \ne k\), and \(\partial P_k/\partial\delta_k = \sum_jb_{kj}V_kV_j\cos\delta_{kj} \to B_{kk}\), at \(V = 1\), \(\delta = 0\). The derivatives \(\partial P_k/\partial V_j\) carry \(\sin\delta_{kj} = 0\), as do \(\partial Q_k/\partial\delta_j\): the Jacobian is block-diagonal and its \(P\)–\(\delta\) block is \(B\) without the slack. At the flat start every computed \(P_k\) is 0, so the mismatch is \(-P_{\text{spec}}\) and the Newton step solves \(B\,\Delta\delta = P_{\text{spec}}\): the DC power flow. \(\square\)

Check. Part 1: bus 3 receives \(0.7727 + 0.7273 = 1.5\) ✓; bus 2 sends \(0.2727 + 0.7273 = 1.0\) ✓. Part 2: bus 3 receives \(0.6818 + 0.8182 = 1.5\) ✓; bus 2 sends \(0.6818 + 0.8182 = 1.5\) ✓. Every angle difference is below 0.21 rad, so \(\sin x \approx x\) is within 1% ✓.

P6 Newton–Raphson by hand medium

exercises: the power-flow equations of a three-bus grid; the Jacobian; one Newton iteration; the mismatch after it

For the three-bus example, with unknowns \(x = (\delta_2, \delta_3, V_3)\) and mismatch \(F(x)\) = computed minus specified \((P_2, P_3, Q_3)\):

  1. Write \(P_2\), \(P_3\) and \(Q_3\) as functions of \(x\).
  2. Compute \(F\) and the Jacobian at the flat start.
  3. Take one Newton step. Give \(\delta_2\), \(\delta_3\) in degrees and \(V_3\).
  4. Compute the mismatch at the new point. By how much has the largest entry fallen?
  5. (Optional, in any tool you like.) Take a second step, and compare the mismatch with the square of the last.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three trigonometric expressions, a vector and a \(3\times3\) matrix of numbers, a linear solve, three numbers, three more.

Rung 2

Each \(P_k\) and \(Q_k\) is a sum of section 1's two-bus terms, one per line at the bus: \(P_k = \sum_jb_{kj}V_kV_j\sin\delta_{kj}\), \(Q_k = \sum_jb_{kj}(V_k^2 - V_kV_j\cos\delta_{kj})\), with \(b = 10, 5, 4\) and \(V_1 = V_2 = 1\), \(\delta_1 = 0\). At the flat start the Jacobian is block-diagonal (P5).

Rung 3

First line: \(P_2 = 10\sin\delta_2 + 4V_3\sin(\delta_2 - \delta_3)\), \(P_3 = 5V_3\sin\delta_3 + 4V_3\sin(\delta_3 - \delta_2)\), \(Q_3 = 9V_3^2 - V_3\big(5\cos\delta_3 + 4\cos(\delta_3 - \delta_2)\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Linearise the mismatch, solve, re-evaluate. At the flat start the linearisation is the DC power flow plus one decoupled equation for \(V_3\).

1. As in rung 3. (Bus 3's \(Q\): \(5(V_3^2 - V_3\cos\delta_3) + 4(V_3^2 - V_3\cos(\delta_3 - \delta_2))\).)

2. At \(x_0 = (0, 0, 1)\): \(P_2 = P_3 = Q_3 = 0\), so \(F_0 = (0 - 1.0,\ 0 - (-1.5),\ 0 - (-0.5)) = (-1.0, 1.5, 0.5)\). The Jacobian:

\[ J_0 = \begin{pmatrix}14&-4&0\\-4&9&0\\0&0&9\end{pmatrix}, \]

from \(\partial P_2/\partial\delta_2 = 10 + 4 = 14\), \(\partial P_2/\partial\delta_3 = -4\), \(\partial P_3/\partial\delta_3 = 5 + 4 = 9\), \(\partial Q_3/\partial V_3 = 18V_3 - 9 = 9\), and every cross term carrying a \(\sin0 = 0\).

3. \(J_0\Delta x = -F_0 = (1, -1.5, -0.5)\): the angle block is P5's system, \(\Delta\delta_2 = 3/110\), \(\Delta\delta_3 = -17/110\); and \(\Delta V_3 = -0.5/9 = -0.0556\). New point: \(\delta_2 = 1.5626°\), \(\delta_3 = -8.8548°\), \(V_3 = 0.9444\).

4. With \(\delta_2 = 0.027273\), \(\delta_3 = -0.154545\), \(\delta_2 - \delta_3 = 0.181818\), \(V_3 = 0.944444\):

  • \(P_2 = 10(0.027270) + 4(0.94444)(0.180818) = 0.27269 + 0.68309 = 0.95578\); \(F_1 = -0.04422\).
  • \(P_3 = 5(0.94444)(-0.153931) + 4(0.94444)(-0.180818) = -0.72690 - 0.68309 = -1.40999\); \(F_2 = 0.09001\).
  • \(Q_3 = 9(0.891975) - 0.94444\,(5\times0.988081 + 4\times0.983517) = 8.02778 - 8.38145 = -0.35367\); \(F_3 = 0.14633\).

The largest mismatch fell from 1.5 to 0.146, a factor of ten.

5. The Jacobian at the new point (rows \(P_2, P_3, Q_3\); columns \(\delta_2, \delta_3, V_3\)) is

\[ J_1 = \begin{pmatrix}13.712&-3.716&0.723\\-3.716&8.381&-1.493\\0.683&-1.410&8.126\end{pmatrix}, \]

and \(J_1\Delta x = -F_1\) gives \(\Delta x = (0.00046, -0.01419, -0.02051)\): \(\delta_2 = 1.5891°\), \(\delta_3 = -9.6677°\), \(V_3 = 0.9239\). The swing equation and equal areas

P7 Proof: the swing equation medium

exercises: Newton for rotation in power form; the inertia constant; the first integral; MR 02's classification of equilibria

  1. A rotor of moment of inertia \(J\) with \(n_p\) pole pairs obeys \(J\dot\omega_m = T_m - T_e\). With \(H = \tfrac12J\omega_{sm}^2/S\) and the electrical rotor angle \(\delta = n_p\theta_m - \omega_st\), derive \((2H/\omega_s)\ddot\delta = P_m - P_e\) in per unit, stating the approximation made.
  2. With \(P_e = P_{\max}\sin\delta\), prove that \(\mathcal H = (H/\omega_s)\dot\delta^2 - P_m\delta - P_{\max}\cos\delta\) is constant along solutions.
  3. For \(P_m < P_{\max}\), find the equilibria in \((-\pi, \pi]\) and classify them, saying which tool from MR 02 settles each.
  4. A two-pole 500 MVA generator has \(H = 5\) s. Find \(J\). Find its natural frequency of small swings at \(\delta_0 = 30°\) with \(P_{\max} = 1\) pu on its rating.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A derivation with one approximation, a one-line proof, two equilibria with their types, and two numbers.

Rung 2

Multiply Newton's law by \(\omega_m\), and replace \(\omega_m\) by \(\omega_{sm}\) where it multiplies. \(\dot\delta = n_p(\omega_m - \omega_{sm})\) and \(\omega_s = n_p\omega_{sm}\). For part 2, multiply the swing equation by \(\dot\delta\). For part 3, linearise and compute trace and determinant (MR 02, Planar linear systems: two numbers decide).

Rung 3

First line: \(J\omega_m\dot\omega_m = P_m - P_e\), and near synchronous speed \(J\omega_{sm}\dot\omega_m = \dfrac{2HS}{\omega_{sm}}\dot\omega_m\).

Worked solution

Idea. Newton's law for one rotational degree of freedom, rewritten in the variables a grid cares about: power, and angle against a rotating reference.

1. \(J\omega_m\dot\omega_m = \omega_mT_m - \omega_mT_e = P_m - P_e\). Approximation: \(\omega_m \approx \omega_{sm}\) in the factor on the left, with relative error equal to the relative speed deviation (about a per cent or less here). By the definition of \(H\), \(J\omega_{sm} = 2HS/\omega_{sm}\). With \(\ddot\delta = n_p\dot\omega_m\) and \(\omega_s = n_p\omega_{sm}\): \(\frac{2HS}{\omega_{sm}}\frac{\ddot\delta}{n_p} = \frac{2HS}{\omega_s}\ddot\delta = P_m - P_e\). Divide by \(S\). \(\square\)

2. \(\dot{\mathcal H} = \frac{2H}{\omega_s}\dot\delta\ddot\delta - P_m\dot\delta + P_{\max}\sin\delta\,\dot\delta = \dot\delta\Big[\frac{2H}{\omega_s}\ddot\delta - P_m + P_{\max}\sin\delta\Big] = 0\). \(\square\)

3. Equilibria: \(\dot\delta = 0\), \(\sin\delta = P_m/P_{\max}\): \(\delta_0 = \arcsin(P_m/P_{\max})\) and \(\pi - \delta_0\). The linearisation has matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-k\cos\delta^*&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(k = \omega_sP_{\max}/2H\): trace 0, determinant \(k\cos\delta^*\). At \(\pi - \delta_0\) the determinant is negative: a hyperbolic saddle, and Hartman–Grobman (MR 02) makes it a saddle of the nonlinear system. At \(\delta_0\) it is positive: a linear centre, which linearisation cannot certify (MR 02, When linearisation lies). But \(\mathcal H\) has a strict minimum there (\(\partial_\delta^2\mathcal H = P_{\max}\cos\delta_0 > 0\) and the \(\dot\delta^2\) term is positive), so its level sets near \(\delta_0\) are small closed curves and the equilibrium is a nonlinear centre — MR 02's argument for the undamped pendulum.

4. A two-pole rotor turns at \(\omega_{sm} = \omega_s = 314.16\) rad/s (3000 rpm). \(J = 2HS/\omega_{sm}^2 = 2\times5\times5\times10^8/314.16^2 = 5.07\times10^4\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Small swings: \(\omega_n = \sqrt{\omega_sP_{\max}\cos\delta_0/2H} = \sqrt{314.16\times0.866/10} = 5.216\) rad/s, \(f_n = 0.830\) Hz.

Check. Units: \(\tfrac12J\omega^2\) is J, over VA gives s ✓; \(2HS/\omega^2\) is \(\mathrm{s\cdot W\cdot s^2} = \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) ✓. Limit: \(P_m \to 0\) gives \(\delta_0 \to 0\) and the saddle \(\to\pi\), the unforced pendulum ✓.

P8 The equal-area criterion medium

exercises: accelerating and decelerating areas; the critical clearing angle (the checkpoint's second half); a partial fault; a fault that need never be cleared

A machine runs at \(\delta_0 = 30°\), so \(P_m = \tfrac12P_{\max}\). After the fault is cleared the network is as before.

  1. A fault cuts the transfer to zero. Find the critical clearing angle \(\delta_c\), and check it by computing both areas.
  2. A fault only halves the transfer: \(P_e = \tfrac12P_{\max}\sin\delta\) while it lasts. Find \(\delta_c\).
  3. A fault leaves \(P_e = 0.6P_{\max}\sin\delta\). Show that the machine stays in step even if the fault is never cleared, and find how far it swings. Does clearing it at any moment keep it in step?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three angles (or a proof that none is needed). The upper limit of the braking area is always the post-fault saddle, \(\pi - \delta_0 = 150°\).

Rung 2

Accelerating area: \(\int_{\delta_0}^{\delta_c}(P_m - P_{e,\text{fault}})\,\mathrm d\delta\). Decelerating: \(\int_{\delta_c}^{\pi - \delta_0}(P_{\max}\sin\delta - P_m)\,\mathrm d\delta\). Set them equal. For part 3, the faulted curve crosses \(P_m\) at \(\arcsin(5/6)\): the faulted system has its own centre and saddle.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(P_m(\delta_c - \delta_0) = P_{\max}(\cos\delta_c - \cos150°) - P_m(150° - \delta_c)\), angles in radians, which gives \(\cos\delta_c = \tfrac12(\pi - 2\delta_0) + \cos150°\).

Worked solution

Idea. The criterion is the first integral: the energy gained while faulted must be absorbable before the post-fault saddle. Work in units of \(P_{\max}\), angles in radians: \(\delta_0 = 0.5236\), \(\pi - \delta_0 = 2.6180\).

1. \(\cos\delta_c = 0.5\times2.0944 - 0.8660 = 0.1812\), \(\delta_c = 79.56° = 1.3886\) rad. Areas: \(A_1 = 0.5(1.3886 - 0.5236) = 0.4325\); \(A_2 = (0.1812 + 0.8660) - 0.5(2.6180 - 1.3886) = 1.0472 - 0.6147 = 0.4325\) ✓.

2. \(A_1 = \int_{\delta_0}^{\delta_c}(0.5 - 0.5\sin\delta)\,\mathrm d\delta = 0.5(\delta_c - \delta_0) - 0.5(\cos\delta_0 - \cos\delta_c)\). Setting \(A_1 = A_2\), the \(\delta_c\) terms in \(P_m\) cancel again:

\[ (1 - 0.5)\cos\delta_c = 0.5(\pi - 2\delta_0) - 0.5\cos\delta_0 + \cos(\pi - \delta_0) = 1.0472 - 0.4330 - 0.8660 = -0.2518, \]

so \(\cos\delta_c = -0.5037\) and \(\delta_c = 120.24°\), below 150°, so it is a genuine clearing angle. The faulted curve \(\tfrac12\sin\delta\) never exceeds \(P_m = \tfrac12\) (it touches at 90°), so the rotor accelerates throughout the fault, but more slowly than in part 1.

3. While faulted, \(\mathcal H_f = \tfrac H{\omega_s}\dot\delta^2 - 0.5\delta - 0.6\cos\delta\) is conserved. The faulted system has a centre at \(\arcsin(0.5/0.6) = 56.44°\) and a saddle at \(123.56°\). Starting from rest at \(30°\), the energy to reach the faulted centre is the accelerating area \(\int_{30°}^{56.44°}(0.5 - 0.6\sin\delta)\,\mathrm d\delta = 0.5\times0.4615 - 0.6(0.8660 - 0.5528) = 0.0428\); the braking area available up to the faulted saddle is \(\int_{56.44°}^{123.56°}(0.6\sin\delta - 0.5)\,\mathrm d\delta = 0.6\times1.1055 - 0.5\times1.1715 = 0.0776\). Since \(0.0428 < 0.0776\) the orbit is closed: the machine swings about \(56.44°\) for ever, in step. Its far turning point solves \(0.5(\delta - \delta_0) + 0.6(\cos\delta - \cos\delta_0) = 0\): \(\delta = 1.611\) rad \(= 92.3°\).

Clearing at any moment: along the faulted orbit \(\mathcal H_f = -0.5\times0.5236 - 0.6\times0.8660 = -0.7814\) (units of \(P_{\max}\), with \(H/\omega_s\) absorbed in the scaling), and the post-fault \(\mathcal H = \mathcal H_f - 0.4\cos\delta\). For \(\delta\) between \(30°\) and \(92.3°\), \(-0.4\cos\delta \le 0.016\), so \(\mathcal H \le -0.765\), below the post-fault saddle's \(-0.5\times2.6180 - \cos150° = -0.443\), with \(\delta < 150°\). So the state is inside the post-fault separatrix whenever the fault is cleared: yes.

Check. The more of the transfer the fault leaves, the later the critical angle: 79.56°, then 120.24°, then none ✓. Limit: a fault that leaves \(P_e\) unchanged gives \(A_1 = 0\) ✓.

P9 Critical clearing times routine

exercises: integrating the faulted swing equation; \(t_c\) for one machine and for the two-area model; how \(t_c\) scales with inertia

  1. With the transfer cut to zero, derive \(t_c = \sqrt{4H(\delta_c - \delta_0)/\omega_sP_m}\).
  2. Evaluate it for one machine with \(H = 5\) s at \(P_m = 0.5\) pu, and for the two-area model (Iberia: \(H = 4\) s on 50 GW, exporting 2000 MW over a 4000 MW tie). Use P8's \(\delta_c\).
  3. Explain the ratio of the two times in one sentence, and find the \(H\) the single machine would need to survive a fault of 0.5 s.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A constant acceleration, so a parabola; then two numbers; then a square root.

Rung 2

Faulted, \((2H/\omega_s)\ddot\delta = P_m\). For Iberia, put everything in per unit on its own 50 GW: \(P_m = 0.04\), \(P_{\max} = 0.08\), so \(\delta_0 = 30°\) again.

Rung 3

First line: \(\delta(t) = \delta_0 + \dfrac{\omega_sP_m}{4H}t^2\), so \(\delta_c - \delta_0 = \dfrac{\omega_sP_m}{4H}t_c^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. During the fault the rotor is a body under constant torque; the clearing time is how long it takes to coast from \(\delta_0\) to \(\delta_c\).

1. \(\ddot\delta = \omega_sP_m/2H\), constant, from rest relative to synchronous speed: \(\delta - \delta_0 = \tfrac12\ddot\delta t^2 = \omega_sP_mt^2/4H\). Solve for \(t\) at \(\delta_c\). \(\square\)

2. \(\delta_c - \delta_0 = 1.3886 - 0.5236 = 0.8650\) rad. One machine: \(\ddot\delta = 314.16\times0.5/10 = 15.71\) rad/s², \(t_c = \sqrt{2\times0.8650/15.71} = 0.332\) s. Iberia: \(\ddot\delta = 314.16\times0.04/8 = 1.571\) rad/s², \(t_c = \sqrt{2\times0.8650/1.571} = 1.05\) s.

3. Iberia's export is 4% of its rotating rating against the single machine's 50%, so with \(H\) 4 s against 5 s its acceleration is ten times smaller and its time \(\sqrt{10} = 3.16\) times longer: \(1.0495/0.3319 = 3.16\) ✓. Since \(t_c \propto \sqrt H\), surviving 0.5 s needs \(H = 5\times(0.5/0.3319)^2 = 11.3\) s.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm s\cdot\mathrm{rad}/(\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}})\) under the root, s² ✓. Limit: \(H\to\infty\) gives \(t_c\to\infty\) ✓; \(P_m\to0\) too, since an unloaded machine does not accelerate ✓.

P10 Losing 1 GW routine

exercises: the aggregate swing equation; rate of change of frequency; the dip of a ramp model, in closed form

Continental Europe, 300 GW online with \(H = 5\) s, loses 1000 MW of generation.

  1. Find the initial rate of change of frequency.
  2. With no reserves at all, how long would the frequency take to fall 200 mHz, and 800 mHz?
  3. Now let the frequency containment reserve ramp linearly to 1000 MW over 30 s, and neglect load damping. Find the lowest frequency and when it occurs.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A rate in Hz/s, two times, and a minimum of a quadratic.

Rung 2

\((2HS/f_0)\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt = -\Delta P + R(t)\) with \(R = \Delta P\,t/T\) for \(t \le T\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt = -f_0\Delta P/2HS = -50\times1000/(2\times5\times300\,000)\) Hz/s.

Worked solution

Idea. The whole synchronous area is one rotor of stored energy \(HS = 1500\) GJ; a lost gigawatt is drawn from it until the reserves take over.

1. \(-50\times1000/3\times10^6 = -0.0167\) Hz/s.

2. At a constant rate: \(0.2/0.0167 = 12\) s and \(0.8/0.0167 = 48\) s.

3. \(\Delta f(t) = -\frac{f_0\Delta P}{2HS}\big(t - t^2/2T\big)\) for \(t \le T\); its derivative \(-\frac{f_0\Delta P}{2HS}(1 - t/T)\) vanishes at \(t = T = 30\) s, where \(\Delta f = -\frac{f_0\Delta P\,T}{4HS} = -0.0167\times15 = -0.25\) Hz. After that the reserve matches the loss and, in this model, the frequency stops falling.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{Hz\cdot W/(s\cdot W)} = \mathrm{Hz/s}\) ✓. The dip is a third of the 3000 MW case's 0.75 Hz, as linearity requires ✓. Limit \(T\to0\): no dip ✓.

P11 The reference incident medium

exercises: the 3000 MW design case against the regulation's 800 mHz; droop and the 200 mHz steady state; how the dip scales with inertia

Continental Europe as in P10 loses the regulation's reference incident, 3000 MW.

  1. Find the initial rate of change of frequency and the dip with the reserve ramping to 3000 MW over 30 s (no load damping). Compare with 800 mHz.
  2. The reserve is delivered by droop, sized so that the full 3000 MW is out at 200 mHz. Find the droop gain and the steady-state frequency. Then add load damping of 1% of a 300 GW load per hertz and find it again.
  3. Below what inertia constant would the ramp model's dip exceed 800 mHz?
  4. (Optional, numerical.) Integrate the ramp model with the load damping of part 2. Find the minimum and when it occurs.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

P10 three times larger; then a steady state, where the left side of the aggregate swing equation is zero; then solve the dip formula for \(H\).

Rung 2

Droop: \(R = K|\Delta f|\). Load damping \(D\) is in MW per Hz: 1% of 300 GW. In steady state \(\Delta P = R + D|\Delta f|\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(K = 3000\ \mathrm{MW}/0.2\ \mathrm{Hz}\); then \(0 = -\Delta P + K|\Delta f|\).

Worked solution

Idea. Two simple models for two questions: how deep, if the reserve arrives along an assumed ramp; and where it settles, once it is out.

1. \(-50\times3000/3\times10^6 = -0.050\) Hz/s; dip \(0.050\times30/2 = 0.75\) Hz at 30 s — inside 800 mHz, with 50 mHz to spare.

2. \(K = 15\) GW/Hz. Steady state: \(3000 = 15\,000|\Delta f|\), \(|\Delta f| = 0.2\) Hz, exactly the regulation's maximum steady-state deviation. With \(D = 0.01\times300\,000 = 3000\) MW/Hz: \(|\Delta f| = 3000/18\,000 = 0.167\) Hz.

3. \(f_0\Delta P\,T/4HS = 0.8\) gives \(H = 50\times3000\times30/(4\times300\,000\times0.8) = 4.69\) s.

4. \((2HS/f_0)\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt = -3000(1 - \min(t/30, 1)) - 3000\,\Delta f\), with \(2HS/f_0 = 60\,000\) MW·s/Hz. It is linear, so it can be solved by hand. With \(\tau = 60\,000/3000 = 20\) s and \(\Delta f\) in Hz, for \(t \le 30\) s it reads \(\tau\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt = -(1 - t/30) - \Delta f\). Try \(\Delta f = a + bt + Ce^{-t/\tau}\): matching terms gives \(b = 1/30\) Hz/s and \(a = -1 - \tau/30 = -5/3\) Hz, and \(\Delta f(0) = 0\) gives \(C = 5/3\) Hz. The minimum is where \(\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt = 0\), i.e. \(\tfrac1{30} = \tfrac{5/3}{20}e^{-t/20}\), \(e^{-t/20} = 0.4\), \(t = 20\ln2.5 = 18.3\) s; there the equation itself gives \(\Delta f = -(1 - t/30) = -0.389\) Hz. So the minimum is \(-0.39\) Hz at 18.3 s, earlier than 30 s because load damping already carries part of the deficit; after 30 s the reserve matches the loss and \(\tau\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt = -\Delta f\) returns the frequency to 50 Hz. Any numerical integration should reproduce these two numbers.

Check. Units: \(K\) in MW/Hz, \(\Delta P/K\) in Hz ✓. Load damping helps in both questions ✓. These are a simple model's numbers; the regulation's figures are what the system is operated to, and nothing here says how they were set.

P12 Reactive power stays local routine

exercises: \(\Delta V \approx QX/V\); the price of sending \(Q\) against the price of sending \(P\); the tie's reactive bill

  1. On the 400 km line of P2 (\(X = 97.1\ \Omega\)) at 400 kV, how large a voltage difference is needed to send 200 Mvar? As a fraction of 400 kV?
  2. How large an angle is needed to send 200 MW instead, with equal voltages?
  3. With \(\delta = 0\) and 200 Mvar sent, find the receiving voltage exactly, the reactive power that arrives, and what the line absorbs.
  4. Explain in two sentences why capacitor banks and generators supply reactive power near where it is used.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A voltage, an angle, three powers, and an argument from the decoupling.

Rung 2

\(Q_1 \approx V_1(V_1 - V_2)/X\) and \(P \approx V_1V_2\delta/X\), three-phase with line voltages. At \(\delta = 0\) the \(Q_1\) formula is exact.

Rung 3

First line: \(\Delta V = QX/V = 2\times10^8\times97.1/4\times10^5\) V.

Worked solution

Idea. Across a reactance, \(Q\) is paid for in voltage difference and \(P\) in angle — and only angle is free.

1. \(\Delta V = 2\times10^8\times97.13/4\times10^5 = 48.6\) kV, 12.1% of 400 kV.

2. \(\delta \approx PX/V^2 = 2\times10^8\times97.13/1.6\times10^{11} = 0.1214\) rad \(= 6.96°\), with no voltage difference.

3. \(V_2 = 400 - 48.6 = 351.4\) kV. \(Q_2 = (V_1V_2 - V_2^2)/X = V_2(V_1 - V_2)/X = 351.4\times10^3\times48.6\times10^3/97.13 = 175.7\) Mvar arrives; the line absorbs \(200 - 175.7 = 24.3\) Mvar. 4. Moving reactive power needs a voltage difference proportional to how much and how far, and voltages must stay within narrow limits everywhere; so \(Q\) cannot be sent far, while real power, which needs only an angle, can. Reactive power is therefore made close to where it is consumed — by capacitor banks (unit 06) and by generators' field control (branch 12).

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{var\cdot\Omega/V} = \mathrm V\) ✓. Scaling: halving the distance halves \(X\) and \(\Delta V\) ✓. The tie of the two-area model is the same story: at 2000 MW each end must make 535.9 Mvar for it (P1).
