Two areas, one tie, and three questions

the running example: Iberia and the rest of Europe

13 · two buses

Per unit, and a transformer that disappears

the per-unit system

Choose one base power $S_b$ (here 100 MVA) and, on each voltage level, a base voltage $V_b$ (line-to-line). Then $I_b = S_b/\sqrt3V_b$ and the base impedance is $Z_b = V_b^2/S_b$. Every quantity is divided by its base: $z = Z/Z_b$, and so on. Units: $\mathrm{V^2/VA} = \Omega$ ✓; per-unit values are pure numbers.

the ratio disappears

With bases $V_{b1}/V_{b2} = n$ across an ideal transformer of ratio $n$, the transformer is a plain wire in per unit.

proof

Unit 07: $V_1 = nV_2$, $I_1 = I_2/n$, and $Z$ seen through it is $n^2Z$. The bases obey the same laws: $Z_{b1} = V_{b1}^2/S_b = n^2Z_{b2}$. So $v_1 = v_2$, $i_1 = i_2$, $z_1 = z_2$. $\square$

Unit 07's 4% is a per-unit impedance: 0.04 pu on the street transformer's own 400 kVA.

The street transformer, ideal core plus series impedance. In ohms it is 40 Ω or 16 mΩ depending on the side; in per unit it is 0.04 on either, and the ratio is gone.

Full proof

Let the transformer be ideal with $V_1 = nV_2$ and $I_1 = I_2/n$ (unit 07's limit, with $I_2$ the current delivered on side 2). Choose bases with $V_{b1} = nV_{b2}$ and the same $S_b$ on both sides. Then $I_{b1} = S_b/(\sqrt3V_{b1}) = I_{b2}/n$ and $Z_{b1} = n^2Z_{b2}$. Dividing: $v_1 = V_1/V_{b1} = nV_2/(nV_{b2}) = v_2$, $i_1 = (I_2/n)/(I_{b2}/n) = i_2$, and an impedance $Z_2$ on side 2, seen from side 1 as $n^2Z_2$ (unit 07), has per-unit value $n^2Z_2/(n^2Z_{b2}) = Z_2/Z_{b2}$. So in per unit the ideal transformer imposes $v_1 = v_2$, $i_1 = i_2$: a wire. Only its leakage and magnetising branches remain, as ordinary impedances.

Three-phase or one. With $V_b$ line-to-line and $S_b$ three-phase, $Z_b = V_b^2/S_b = (V_b/\sqrt3)^2/(S_b/3)$, the same as the per-phase base built from the phase voltage and a third of the power (unit 11's per-phase equivalent). So per-unit impedances do not care which one is meant, and a per-unit voltage is the same number whether line or phase voltages are divided.

The street transformer. Unit 07 modelled it as one single-phase 400 kVA transformer: $Z_b = (20\ \mathrm{kV})^2/400\ \mathrm{kVA} = 1000\ \Omega$ on the 20 kV side, and $0.4\ \Omega$ on the 400 V side; the ratio of the bases is $2500 = 50^2$. Its 4% is the short-circuit impedance, $|R + iX| = |8.13 + 39.2i| = 40.0\ \Omega$ seen from 20 kV, $16.0\ \mathrm{m\Omega}$ from 400 V: 0.04 of the base on either side. That is unit 07's "4%" landed: a nameplate's percentage impedance is a per-unit impedance on the machine's own rating.

Changing base. A system study uses one $S_b$ for everything; $z_{\text{new}} = z_{\text{old}}(S_{b,\text{new}}/S_{b,\text{old}})(V_{b,\text{old}}/V_{b,\text{new}})^2$, which is just $Z$ divided by the new base. On 100 MVA the street transformer is $0.04\times100/0.4 = 10$ pu: a small transformer is a large impedance on a large base.

Reading. Fitzgerald, Kingsley and Umans, §2.9, "The Per-Unit System" — unit 07 deferred it to unit 11, which deferred it here. Bergen and Vittal, ch. 5, for the same in power-system language.

13 · two buses

Two buses, a reactance, and $S = VI^*$

Per phase: bus voltages $V_1e^{i\delta_1}$ and $V_2e^{i\delta_2}$ joined by a lossless series reactance $X$. Let $\delta = \delta_1 - \delta_2$ and $I = (V_1e^{i\delta_1} - V_2e^{i\delta_2})/iX$. Unit 06's $S_1 = V_1e^{i\delta_1}I^*$ gives

the two-bus transfer $$P = \frac{V_1V_2\sin\delta}{X},$$ $$Q_1 = \frac{V_1^2 - V_1V_2\cos\delta}{X},$$ $$Q_2 = \frac{V_1V_2\cos\delta - V_2^2}{X}.$$

$P$ leaves bus 1 and arrives at bus 2; $Q_1$ leaves bus 1, $Q_2$ arrives at bus 2. Units: $\mathrm{V^2/\Omega} = \mathrm W$ ✓.

One phase of a balanced three-phase link, in unit 11's per-phase equivalent. A bus is a node; its voltage is measured to the neutral.

notation

From here $V_k$ is a bus voltage's magnitude and $\delta_k$ its angle; the phasor is $V_ke^{i\delta_k}$. Power texts write it so, and it keeps $P = V_1V_2\sin\delta/X$ short.

Full proof

Write $\bar V_k = V_ke^{i\delta_k}$ for the phasors. The current from bus 1 to bus 2 is $I = (\bar V_1 - \bar V_2)/(iX)$, so $I^* = (\bar V_1^* - \bar V_2^*)/(-iX) = i(\bar V_1^* - \bar V_2^*)/X$. The complex power leaving bus 1 into the line is

$$S_1 = \bar V_1I^* = \frac{i}{X}\big(V_1^2 - V_1V_2e^{i\delta}\big) = \frac{i}{X}\big(V_1^2 - V_1V_2\cos\delta - iV_1V_2\sin\delta\big) = \frac{V_1V_2\sin\delta}{X} + i\,\frac{V_1^2 - V_1V_2\cos\delta}{X}.$$

The complex power arriving at bus 2 from the line is $S_2 = \bar V_2I^* = \frac iX(V_1V_2e^{-i\delta} - V_2^2) = \frac{V_1V_2\sin\delta}{X} + i\,\frac{V_1V_2\cos\delta - V_2^2}{X}$. Real and imaginary parts are the slide's three formulas.

Three phases. In a balanced system each phase carries the same $S$ (unit 11), so the total is $3S$ with phase voltages. With line-to-line magnitudes $U_k = \sqrt3V_k$, $3V_1V_2 = U_1U_2$, so $P_{3\phi} = U_1U_2\sin\delta/X$: the same formula, with line voltages and three-phase power. The angle $\delta$ is the same either way, since the line voltages of a balanced set are the phase voltages turned by the same $30°$ at both buses (unit 11). This is why "400 kV" can be put straight into $P = V_1V_2\sin\delta/X$.

Signs. $P > 0$ when $\delta > 0$: power flows from the bus whose voltage leads. $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ follow unit 06's convention, $S = VI^*$ with $I$ the current leaving the bus into the line.

13 · two buses

The phasor diagram, and the energy check

$iXI = \bar V_1 - \bar V_2$ is the chord between the two phasors, so $I$ is at right angles to it. The height of $\bar V_1$ above $\bar V_2$ is $V_1\sin\delta$, and that is what drives $P$.

energy check

Real power: $P$ is the same at both ends — a reactance absorbs none (unit 06).

Reactive power:

$$\begin{aligned}Q_1 - Q_2 &= \frac{V_1^2 + V_2^2 - 2V_1V_2\cos\delta}{X}\\ &= \frac{|\bar V_1 - \bar V_2|^2}{X} = |I|^2X,\end{aligned}$$

exactly the reactance's own $Q$ (unit 06: $Q = |I|^2X$ for a reactance). Law of cosines, then $|\bar V_1 - \bar V_2| = X|I|$. $\square$

Limits. $\delta = 0$, $V_1 = V_2$: no current, $P = Q = 0$ ✓. $X \to 0$ with $\delta \ne 0$: $P \to \infty$ — two stiff sources shorted together ✓.

To scale for $V_1 = V_2$ and $\delta = 30°$. The current lies along the bisector, $\delta/2 = 15°$ from each voltage, so each end's power factor angle is $15°$ — and each end supplies the same $Q$.

Full proof

$Q_1 - Q_2 = \frac1X\big[(V_1^2 - V_1V_2\cos\delta) - (V_1V_2\cos\delta - V_2^2)\big] = \frac1X\big(V_1^2 + V_2^2 - 2V_1V_2\cos\delta\big)$. By the law of cosines in the triangle of the figure, $V_1^2 + V_2^2 - 2V_1V_2\cos\delta = |\bar V_1 - \bar V_2|^2$. And $\bar V_1 - \bar V_2 = iXI$, so $|\bar V_1 - \bar V_2|^2 = X^2|I|^2$. Hence $Q_1 - Q_2 = X|I|^2$: the reactive power bus 1 sends in, less what bus 2 takes out, is what the reactance absorbs. That is unit 06's conservation of complex power on a network of three elements — two buses and a reactance — with real and reactive parts balancing separately.

Equal voltages. With $V_1 = V_2 = V$: $Q_1 = V^2(1 - \cos\delta)/X$ and $Q_2 = -Q_1$. Both ends supply the same reactive power to the line, each half of its $|I|^2X$. The figure shows why: the current bisects the angle, so it lags $\bar V_1$ by $\delta/2$ and leads $\bar V_2$ by $\delta/2$. The tie is this case.

The figure. 300 px per unit voltage; $\bar V_2$ along the axis, $\bar V_1$ at $30°$; the current drawn to an arbitrary length along $15°$.

13 · two buses

Real power follows angle; reactive power follows voltage

Linearise about the unloaded state, $\delta \approx 0$ and $V_1 \approx V_2$:

$$P \approx \frac{V_1V_2}{X}\,\delta, \qquad Q_1 \approx \frac{V_1(V_1 - V_2)}{X}.$$

$P$ depends to first order only on the phase difference, $Q$ only on the magnitude difference: $\partial P/\partial V_k$ and $\partial Q_1/\partial\delta$ vanish at $\delta = 0$. That is the decoupling every power-flow method exploits.

unit 06's promise

On a network, reactive power and voltage magnitude go together: to push $Q$ across a reactance you need a voltage difference, and $Q$ arriving at a bus raises its voltage.

Computed. Left: $\sin\delta$ against its tangent $\delta$, 4.5% apart at $30°$. Right: $Q_1$ against $V_2/V_1$ — a straight line through zero at equal voltages; a $10°$ angle shifts it by only $1 - \cos10° = 1.5\%$.

Full proof

Partial derivatives of the two-bus formulas: $\partial P/\partial\delta = V_1V_2\cos\delta/X$, $\partial P/\partial V_2 = V_1\sin\delta/X$; $\partial Q_1/\partial\delta = V_1V_2\sin\delta/X$, $\partial Q_1/\partial V_2 = -V_1\cos\delta/X$. At $\delta = 0$ the cross terms $\partial P/\partial V_2$ and $\partial Q_1/\partial\delta$ vanish, and the diagonal ones are $V_1V_2/X$ and $-V_1/X$. So the first-order change is $\Delta P = (V_1V_2/X)\Delta\delta$ and $\Delta Q_1 = -(V_1/X)\Delta V_2$ (and $+(2V_1 - V_2)/X\cdot\Delta V_1$, which is $V_1/X\cdot\Delta V_1$ at $V_1 = V_2$), from which the slide's formulas follow about the point $P = Q = 0$. The coupling returns at second order: at $\delta \ne 0$, $\partial Q_1/\partial\delta = P$, and a heavily loaded line couples the two again.

Unit 06, landed. Unit 06 noticed, for its workshop's cable, that quadrature current through a resistance costs heat but almost no voltage, and "through a reactance it is the other way round", and named that as a second reason utilities care about $Q$. Here it is in general: across a reactance, $Q$ and the voltage magnitude move together, and $P$ and the angle move together. Real power can be sent a long way at the price of an angle. Reactive power is sent at the price of a voltage drop, which the voltage limits forbid; the slide Reactive power stays near where it is used quantifies it.

Numbers. $\sin30° = 0.5$, $30° = 0.5236$ rad, ratio 1.047. $1 - \cos10° = 0.0152$.

13 · two buses · example

Worked: unit 07's 20 kV line, as a reactance

worked example 1 — unit 07's 10 Ω line, now reactive

Assume the 10 Ω is a lossless reactance and both ends are held at 20 kV line-to-line (unit 07's line was resistive; this is the AC counterpart).

Limit. $P$ is greatest at $\delta = 90°$, where $\sin\delta = 1$:

$$P_{\max} = \frac{V_1V_2}{X} = \frac{(20\ \mathrm{kV})^2}{10\ \Omega} = 40\ \mathrm{MW}.$$

100 MW is impossible — two and a half times the limit.

Against unit 04's resistive cap $V^2/4R = 10$ MW at 20 kV through 10 Ω: both say "not at 20 kV". A resistive line limits by heat and drop, a reactive one by phase, and the second reason holds even for a line with no resistance at all.

At 400 kV: $400^2/10 = 16\,000$ MW. At 132 kV: 1742 MW.

checks

Units ✓. Scaling as $V^2$: $(400/20)^2 = 400 = 16\,000/40$ ✓. $\delta \to 0$: $P \to 0$ ✓. $Q_1$ at $90°$: $V^2/X = 40$ Mvar from each end.

The arithmetic. $(2\times10^4)^2/10 = 4\times10^7$ W. $(1.32\times10^5)^2/10 = 1.7424\times10^9$ W. $(4\times10^5)^2/10 = 1.6\times10^{10}$ W. Unit 04's cap, from unit 07: $(2\times10^4)^2/40 = 10^7$ W.

Unit 07, landed. Unit 07 found that at 20 kV the "loss" of a 100 MW transfer exceeded the power delivered, and that this meant the operating point could not be reached at all; it promised that unit 13 "makes this precise for AC, where the line's reactance sets the limit". Here it is: whatever the receiving end does, the power through a lossless reactance between two buses held at 20 kV cannot exceed 40 MW.

Why the two limits differ by four. The resistive cap comes from a load free to choose its current against a fixed sending voltage, with half the power lost in the line at the optimum. The reactive limit holds both voltages fixed and lets only the angle move; nothing is lost. Different constraints, different numbers; the same verdict.

What it costs at the limit. At $\delta = 90°$ with equal voltages, $Q_1 = V^2/X$ and $Q_2 = -V^2/X$: each end supplies 40 Mvar, and the reactance absorbs $|I|^2X = 2V^2/X = 80$ Mvar. A line at its limit eats as much reactive power as it carries real power, twice over.

13 · two buses

The static limit, and the two crossings

The power-angle curve $P(\delta) = P_{\max}\sin\delta$ rises to $P_{\max} = V_1V_2/X$ at $90°$ and falls again. A demand $P < P_{\max}$ crosses it twice, at $\delta_0$ and $180° - \delta_0$.

  • At $\delta_0 < 90°$ the slope $\mathrm dP/\mathrm d\delta$ is positive: a little more angle sends a little more power. This is where grids run.
  • At $180° - \delta_0$ the slope is negative: more angle sends less. Section 4 shows it is a saddle.

$P_{\max}$ is the static stability limit. For the tie: $400^2/40 = 4000$ MW, and 2000 MW sits at $\sin\delta_0 = \tfrac12$, $\delta_0 = 30°$.

Computed, at 400 kV. The same 2000 MW is half the tie's limit and beyond the 400 km line's: a demand above the curve has no operating point.

Why "static". The name is for the limit found by asking only where an operating point exists, with no dynamics. Slow increases of transfer move the operating point up the curve until it reaches the top; there, the two crossings merge and vanish. That is MR 02's saddle-node bifurcation, met again in section 4 (MR 02's pendulum with a constant torque).

The positive slope, physically. Suppose the receiving side is a stiff grid and the sending side's machine is pushed slightly ahead, $\delta$ growing. On the rising side, it then delivers more power than its turbine supplies and is braked back; on the falling side it delivers less, is accelerated further, and runs away. Section 4 makes this precise with the swing equation.

Numbers. $(4\times10^5)^2/40 = 4\times10^9$ W; $\arcsin(2000/4000) = 30°$. The 400 km line's 1647 MW is derived on the next two slides.

A line's reactance, from the field of a wire

Three long conductors of radius $r$ at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side $D$, carrying balanced currents: $i_a + i_b + i_c = 0$. MR 23's long wire gives $B = \mu_0 i/2\pi\rho$, so the flux per metre between radii $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ is $(\mu_0i/2\pi)\ln(\rho_2/\rho_1)$.

per-phase series inductance $$L' = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac{D}{r}$$

per metre of line, per phase. Units: $\mathrm{H/m}$ ✓. It is half the two-wire loop's $(\mu_0/\pi)\ln(D/r)$.

Proof in the figure: the currents sum to zero, so the far radius $R$ drops out.

The flux linking conductor $a$, per metre: collected on a strip from its surface out to a distant radius $R$. Each other conductor contributes from distance $D$ outwards.

Full proof

Count the flux, per metre of line, through a strip in the plane of the figure running from the surface of conductor $a$ (radius $r$) out to a distance $R \gg D$, where the return path is taken to close. Conductor $a$'s own field, $\mu_0i_a/2\pi\rho$ (MR 23, A long straight wire), contributes $(\mu_0i_a/2\pi)\ln(R/r)$. Conductor $b$'s field crosses the strip at distances from $b$ running from about $D$ to about $R$, contributing $(\mu_0i_b/2\pi)\ln(R/D)$ — exactly for a strip pointing directly away from $b$, and up to terms that vanish as $R \to \infty$ otherwise, since the flux of a line current through a strip depends only on the distances from the current to the strip's two ends. The same for $c$. Summing, with $i_b + i_c = -i_a$:

$$\lambda_a = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\Big[i_a\ln\frac Rr + (i_b + i_c)\ln\frac RD\Big] = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\,i_a\Big[\ln\frac Rr - \ln\frac RD\Big] = \frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\,i_a\ln\frac Dr .$$

$R$ has gone, which is why the far return path does not matter: balanced currents return through the other two phases. $L' = \lambda_a/i_a$. For a two-wire loop (unit 08's line, unit 07's single-phase estimate) the same count gives $(\mu_0/\pi)\ln(D/r)$ for the loop, twice this: a loop's inductance counts the flux linked by both wires, a phase's only its own.

What is neglected. (1) The flux inside each conductor. For a uniform current in a round wire of radius $a$, MR 23's Ampère gives $B = \mu_0i\rho/2\pi a^2$ inside, with energy $\int B^2/2\mu_0\,\mathrm dA = \mu_0i^2/16\pi$ per metre, which is $\tfrac12L_{\text{int}}i^2$ with $L_{\text{int}} = \mu_0/8\pi$. For a bundle of $N$ subconductors this adds $\mu_0/(8\pi N)$, the same as replacing $\ln(D/r_{\text{eq}})$ by $\ln(D/r_{\text{eq}}) + 1/(4N)$: for $N = 4$, $3.865 + 0.0625$, raising $L'$ by 1.6%. Not used here. (2) A real line is seldom equilateral; the textbooks' remedy, transposing the phases along the route, is not used here. (3) The bundle: unit 07's equivalent radius $r_{\text{eq}}$ replaces $r$, by the same geometric-mean argument unit 07 made for the potential, now made for the flux.

The 400 km line: 97 Ω, and 1647 MW

Unit 07's geometry: $D = 10$ m, $r_{\text{eq}} = 0.2097$ m, so $\ln(D/r_{\text{eq}}) = 3.865$ and

$$L' = 2\times10^{-7}\times3.865 = 0.773\ \mathrm{mH/km}.$$

At 50 Hz, $X' = \omega L' = 0.243\ \Omega$/km; over 400 km, $X = 97.1\ \Omega$, and

$$P_{\max} = \frac{(400\ \mathrm{kV})^2}{97.1\ \Omega} = 1647\ \mathrm{MW}.$$

$P_{\max} \propto 1/l$: the longer the line, the less it can carry. 2000 MW needs a line of at most 329.5 km.

Reactive power stays near where it is used

From $Q_1 \approx V_1(V_1 - V_2)/X$: sending $Q$ across $X$ costs a voltage difference

$$\Delta V \approx \frac{QX}{V}.$$

Units: $\mathrm{var\cdot\Omega/V} = \mathrm V$ ✓ (line-to-line voltages with three-phase $Q$, as for $P$).

On the 400 km line, 200 Mvar needs $\Delta V = 200\times10^6\times97.1/400\times10^3 = $ 48.6 kV, 12% of the voltage. 200 MW needs only $\delta \approx PX/V^2 = 6.96°$, with no voltage difference at all.

Voltages must stay within a few per cent everywhere, so $Q$ cannot travel far and $P$ can. That is why capacitor banks (unit 06) and generators' field control (branch 12) supply $Q$ locally.

Both to scale. The same line, two jobs: reactive power must be pushed by a difference in length, real power by a difference in angle.

The arithmetic. $2\times10^8\times97.13/4\times10^5 = 48\,565$ V. As a fraction of 400 kV, 12.1%. For $P$: $\delta \approx 2\times10^8\times97.13/(4\times10^5)^2 = 0.1214$ rad $= 6.96°$.

The nose: where voltage collapses

A stiff source $\mathcal E$ feeds a load at power factor 1 through a lossless $X$. With the load's voltage $V$ as reference, $I = P/V$ is real and $\mathcal E e^{i\delta} = V + iXP/V$, so

$$V^4 - \mathcal E^2V^2 + X^2P^2 = 0,$$ $$V^2 = \tfrac{\mathcal E^2}{2} \pm \sqrt{\tfrac{\mathcal E^4}{4} - X^2P^2}.$$

Two voltages for each $P$, meeting at $P = \mathcal E^2/2X$, $V = \mathcal E/\sqrt2$: the nose curve. Beyond it no voltage exists. The upper branch is where a grid runs; the nose is voltage collapse.

Its subject is voltage stability (Kundur, the chapter "Voltage Stability"). This unit stops here.

Computed, in units of $\mathcal E$ and $\mathcal E^2/X$. At $P = 0.4$ the load may sit at $0.894\,\mathcal E$ or $0.447\,\mathcal E$; only the first is the grid's.

Full proof

Take the load's voltage $V$ real and positive. At power factor 1 the current is in phase: $I = P/V$. The source is $\mathcal Ee^{i\delta} = V + iXI = V + iXP/V$, so $\mathcal E^2 = V^2 + X^2P^2/V^2$, i.e. $V^4 - \mathcal E^2V^2 + X^2P^2 = 0$, a quadratic in $V^2$. Real roots need $\mathcal E^4/4 \ge X^2P^2$, so $P \le \mathcal E^2/2X$, with equality at $V^2 = \mathcal E^2/2$. At the nose $\tan\delta = XP/V^2 = 1$: $\delta = 45°$, and $P = \mathcal EV\sin\delta/X$ checks: $\mathcal E(\mathcal E/\sqrt2)(1/\sqrt2)/X = \mathcal E^2/2X$ ✓. The limit is half the two-bus $P_{\max}$ because the load's voltage is not held up.

Numbers. With $\mathcal E = X = 1$ and $P = 0.4$: $V^2 = 0.5 \pm 0.3$, $V = 0.894$ or $0.447$.

Why the upper branch. On it, a load that draws more current (lower resistance $V^2/P$) receives more power. On the lower branch, it receives less: it is the far side of unit 04's maximum-power theorem, past the match. What happens dynamically there depends on how loads respond to their voltage — the subject the slide names. Kundur's chapter "Voltage Stability" is the reading.

13 · many buses

The bus admittance matrix is unit 04's Laplacian

A grid of $n$ buses and $m$ branches (lines and transformers), incidence matrix $A$ (unit 03), branch admittances $y_e$. Injected currents $I$ and bus voltages $\bar V$ obey $I = Y_{\text{bus}}\bar V$ with

the bus admittance matrix $$Y_{\text{bus}} = A\,\operatorname{diag}(y_e)\,A^{\mathsf T}.$$
lossless lines, no shunts

With $y_e = 1/(iX_e)$: $Y_{\text{bus}} = -iB$, where $B = A\operatorname{diag}(1/X_e)A^{\mathsf T}$ is a real weighted Laplacian — symmetric, positive semi-definite, rows summing to zero, kernel the constants.

proof

$\operatorname{diag}(1/(iX_e)) = -i\operatorname{diag}(1/X_e)$ and the weights $1/X_e$ are positive: unit 04's theorem, word for word. $\square$

The three-bus example (per unit). Weights are $b_e = 1/X_e$, the size of each line's susceptance $-1/X_e$ (notes); every row of $B$ sums to zero, and $Y_{\text{bus}} = -iB$.

Full proof

$I = Y_{\text{bus}}\bar V$. Branch voltages are $A^{\mathsf T}\bar V$ (unit 03's voltage law, with phasors); branch currents are $\operatorname{diag}(y_e)A^{\mathsf T}\bar V$ (each branch's admittance, unit 05); the current law at the buses, with $I$ the current injected at each bus from outside the network (generators in, loads out), is $A\cdot(\text{branch currents}) = I$. Together: $I = A\operatorname{diag}(y_e)A^{\mathsf T}\bar V$. This is unit 04's $L\varphi = s$, with complex weights; unit 04 said so ("Unit 13 reuses this matrix with complex weights") and unit 05 named it.

Properties. Symmetric for any weights: $(A\,D\,A^{\mathsf T})^{\mathsf T} = A\,D\,A^{\mathsf T}$ for diagonal $D$. It is not Hermitian unless the weights are real. Rows sum to zero because $A^{\mathsf T}\mathbf 1 = 0$ (each column of $A$ has one $+1$ and one $-1$), so $Y_{\text{bus}}\mathbf 1 = 0$. For lossless branches, $Y_{\text{bus}} = -iA\operatorname{diag}(1/X_e)A^{\mathsf T} = -iB$ with real positive weights $1/X_e$, and unit 04's theorem (Symmetric, positive semi-definite, and its kernel) applies to $B$: $\delta^{\mathsf T}B\delta = \sum_e(\delta_{\text{tail}} - \delta_{\text{head}})^2/X_e \ge 0$, kernel the constants on each component.

Shunts break the row sums. A capacitor bank at a bus, or a line's charging capacitance (unit 08 for those who took it), is a branch from the bus to the neutral. Treating the neutral as a grounded node and deleting its row adds the shunt admittance to the bus's diagonal entry, and that row no longer sums to zero. This unit uses no shunts.

A sign to watch. The course writes admittance as $Y = G + iB$, with $B$ the susceptance; an inductive branch has susceptance $-1/X$. The matrix $B$ here collects $+1/X_e$, so that it is a Laplacian, and $Y_{\text{bus}} = -iB$ carries the sign.

The numbers. $b_{12} = 10$, $b_{13} = 5$, $b_{23} = 4$. Diagonal: $10 + 5 = 15$, $10 + 4 = 14$, $5 + 4 = 9$. So $Y_{\text{bus}}$ has diagonal $-15i, -14i, -9i$ and off-diagonal $10i, 5i, 4i$.

13 · many buses

The power-flow equations: complex power, bus by bus

At bus $k$, the complex power injected into the network is $S_k = \bar V_kI_k^*$, and $I = Y_{\text{bus}}\bar V$. So

the power-flow equations $$S_k = \bar V_k\sum_jY_{kj}^*\,\bar V_j^*, \qquad k = 1,\dots,n.$$

For lossless lines, $b_{kj} = 1/X_{kj}$ over neighbours $j$:

$$P_k = \sum_jb_{kj}V_kV_j\sin\delta_{kj},$$ $$Q_k = \sum_jb_{kj}\big(V_k^2 - V_kV_j\cos\delta_{kj}\big),$$

$\delta_{kj} = \delta_k - \delta_j$: section 1's two-bus formula, once per line at the bus.

One bus. What generation less load puts in, the lines take away — complex power, so real and reactive parts each balance.

unit 06's promise

Summed over buses: $\sum_kP_k = 0$ and $\sum_kQ_k = \sum_e|I_e|^2X_e$. What the buses inject, the lines absorb — Tellegen, bus by bus.

Full proof

The equations. $I_k = \sum_jY_{kj}\bar V_j$, so $S_k = \bar V_kI_k^* = \bar V_k\sum_jY_{kj}^*\bar V_j^*$. For lossless branches $Y_{kj} = -iB_{kj}$, so $Y_{kj}^* = iB_{kj}$ and $S_k = i\sum_jB_{kj}V_kV_je^{i\delta_{kj}}$. The diagonal term is $iB_{kk}V_k^2 = i\sum_jb_{kj}V_k^2$; the off-diagonal ones are $-ib_{kj}V_kV_j(\cos\delta_{kj} + i\sin\delta_{kj})$. Real part: $\sum_jb_{kj}V_kV_j\sin\delta_{kj}$. Imaginary part: $\sum_jb_{kj}(V_k^2 - V_kV_j\cos\delta_{kj})$. Each term is section 1's $P$ and $Q_1$ for the line from $k$ to $j$, so $S_k = \sum_jS_{kj}$ — the figure.

The sums. $\sum_kS_k = \bar V^{\mathsf T}I^* = \bar V^{\mathsf T}Y_{\text{bus}}^*\bar V^* = (A^{\mathsf T}\bar V)^{\mathsf T}\operatorname{diag}(y_e^*)(A^{\mathsf T}\bar V)^* = \sum_ey_e^*|v_e|^2$, with $v_e$ the branch voltages. For $y_e = -i/X_e$, $y_e^* = i/X_e$ and $|v_e| = X_e|I_e|$: $\sum_kS_k = i\sum_e|I_e|^2X_e$. So $\sum_kP_k = 0$ and $\sum_kQ_k = \sum_e|I_e|^2X_e$. This is unit 06's theorem, $\sum S = 0$ over all elements, with the buses' injections as one set of elements and the branches as the other; unit 06 proved it from Tellegen's theorem. Unit 06's promise — "Unit 13's power-flow equations are the conservation of complex power at every bus of a grid" — is the line $S_k = \sum_jS_{kj}$.

Sign convention. $S_k$ is the power injected into the network at bus $k$: generation counts positive, load negative. That is the opposite of the passive convention for a bus seen as a component, and the convention every power-flow text uses.

With resistance. $Y_{kj} = G_{kj} + iB'_{kj}$ in general and $P_k$, $Q_k$ pick up cosine and sine terms in $G$; Bergen and Vittal, ch. 10, "Power Flow Analysis", writes them out. Nothing here uses them.

Slack, PV and PQ: counting knowns against unknowns

Each bus has four numbers, $P_k, Q_k, V_k, \delta_k$; the equations fix two. Which two are given depends on what is there:

the count

With $n_{PV}$ generator and $n_{PQ}$ load buses: unknowns $\delta$ at $n - 1$ buses and $V$ at $n_{PQ}$; equations $P$ at $n - 1$ buses and $Q$ at $n_{PQ}$. Both number $n - 1 + n_{PQ}$. $\square$

The slack exists because the angles are defined only up to a constant — unit 04's kernel — and because the losses are not known until the flows are.

The three-bus example: unknowns $\delta_2, \delta_3, V_3$ (coloured) against equations $P_2, P_3, Q_3$ — three and three. The grey unknowns follow afterwards from their buses' equations.

Why these types. A generator's governor sets its real output and its excitation holds its terminal voltage (branch 12 names both), so $P$ and $V$ are what is known; its $Q$ is whatever is needed to hold $V$. A load is described by what it draws, $P$ and $Q$. The slack is a generator chosen to make up the difference — the losses, which in a real grid with resistance are not known until the flows are — and its angle is the reference, since adding a constant to every $\delta$ changes no $\delta_{kj}$ (the kernel of $B$, unit 04).

The count, in full. Unknowns: the $n - 1$ non-slack angles and the $n_{PQ}$ load-bus magnitudes. Equations: $P_k$ given at every non-slack bus ($n_{PV} + n_{PQ} = n - 1$ equations) and $Q_k$ given at every PQ bus ($n_{PQ}$ equations). Equal numbers, so the system is square; whether it has a solution, and how many, is another matter (the nose of section 1 is a case with two, or none). Once solved, the slack's $P, Q$ and the PV buses' $Q$ are computed from their own equations.

Limits. A real generator has a range of $Q$ it can supply; at the end of it, a PV bus becomes a PQ bus. Power-flow programs handle that switching; Bergen and Vittal, ch. 10, is the reading. Not used here.

The DC power flow is unit 04's Kirchhoff problem

Take all $V_k = 1$ pu, small angle differences ($\sin\delta_{kj} \approx \delta_{kj}$), lossless lines. Then $P_k = \sum_jb_{kj}(\delta_k - \delta_j)$:

the DC power flow $$P = B\,\delta, \qquad P_{kj} = \frac{\delta_k - \delta_j}{X_{kj}}.$$

Linear. A solution exists iff $\sum_kP_k = 0$, and is unique once $\delta_{\text{slack}} = 0$.

proof

It is unit 04's $L\varphi = s$ with angles for potentials, the weights $1/X$ for conductances and powers for currents. Unit 04's theorem: solvable iff the injections sum to zero; unique up to the kernel, the constants. $\square$

"DC" because it is the circuit of a resistive network — nothing to do with direct current.

One linear problem, two readings. Real power flows "downhill" in angle through each line's $1/X$, as current flows downhill in potential through each resistor's $G$.

Full proof

From the lossless power-flow equation, $P_k = \sum_jb_{kj}V_kV_j\sin\delta_{kj}$. Set $V_k = V_j = 1$ and $\sin\delta_{kj} = \delta_{kj}$: $P_k = \sum_jb_{kj}(\delta_k - \delta_j) = B_{kk}\delta_k + \sum_{j\ne k}B_{kj}\delta_j = (B\delta)_k$, using $B_{kk} = \sum_jb_{kj}$ and $B_{kj} = -b_{kj}$. The flow on line $kj$ is its two-bus $P$, linearised: $b_{kj}(\delta_k - \delta_j)$. Unit 04's existence and uniqueness theorem (When the potentials exist, and how unique they are) applies verbatim to a connected grid: $B\delta = P$ is solvable iff $\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T}P = 0$, and two solutions differ by a constant. Lossless lines make $\sum P_k = 0$ exact, the slack supplying whatever the others leave over; fixing $\delta_{\text{slack}} = 0$ is unit 04's grounding.

What it gets right and wrong. It keeps the network and the angles and throws away voltage magnitudes, reactive power and losses. The next section shows it is not an arbitrary simplification: at a flat start it is exactly the first step of Newton's method for the full equations.

Why it is used. Being linear, it can be solved for many cases quickly, and its flows add by superposition — unit 03's linearity again.

Newton–Raphson

The power-flow equations are nonlinear. Linearise, solve, repeat: Newton's method, whose error squares at every step once it is close — and the first step from a flat start is the DC power flow.

13 · Newton–Raphson

Newton's method, and the theorem that makes it fast

To solve $F(x) = 0$, $F:\mathbb R^N\to\mathbb R^N$: replace $F$ by its linearisation at the current guess and solve that,

$$x_{k+1} = x_k - J(x_k)^{-1}F(x_k), \qquad J = \frac{\partial F}{\partial x}.$$
local quadratic convergence

If $F(x^*) = 0$, $J$ is Lipschitz on a ball about $x^*$, $\|J(x) - J(y)\| \le \gamma\|x - y\|$, and $J(x^*)$ is invertible, then for $x_0$ close enough to $x^*$ the iterates converge and

$$\|x_{k+1} - x^*\| \le \gamma\|J(x^*)^{-1}\|\,\|x_k - x^*\|^2.$$

The error squares: correct digits roughly double each step. Proof in the notes; Kelley's book (SIAM, 2003) treats the method at length.

One dimension, computed for $F(x) = x^3 - 2x - 5$: $x_0 = 3$, $x_1 = 2.36$, $x_2 = 2.127$; the root is $2.0946$. Errors $0.905$, $0.265$, $0.033$: each roughly the square of the one before, times a constant.

Full proof

Let the Lipschitz bound hold on the ball $\|x - x^*\| \le \rho$; write $e = x - x^*$ and $J_* = J(x^*)$, and take $x$ in that ball, so the segment from $x^*$ to $x$ lies in it too. Step 1, the linearisation error. By the fundamental theorem of calculus along the segment, $F(x) = F(x) - F(x^*) = \int_0^1J(x^* + te)\,e\,\mathrm dt$, so $J(x)e - F(x) = \int_0^1\big[J(x) - J(x^* + te)\big]e\,\mathrm dt$, whose norm is at most $\int_0^1\gamma(1 - t)\|e\|^2\mathrm dt = \tfrac12\gamma\|e\|^2$.

Step 2, the inverse stays bounded. If $\|J(x) - J_*\| \le 1/(2\|J_*^{-1}\|)$ then $J(x) = J_*(I - M)$ with $M = J_*^{-1}(J_* - J(x))$, $\|M\| \le \tfrac12$. The series $\sum_kM^k$ converges (its terms are bounded by $2^{-k}$) and is $(I - M)^{-1}$, with norm at most 2. So $\|J(x)^{-1}\| \le 2\|J_*^{-1}\|$. By the Lipschitz condition this holds whenever $\|e\| \le \varepsilon := \min\big(\rho,\ 1/(2\gamma\|J_*^{-1}\|)\big)$.

Step 3. The Newton step gives $e_+ = e - J(x)^{-1}F(x) = J(x)^{-1}\big[J(x)e - F(x)\big]$, so $\|e_+\| \le 2\|J_*^{-1}\|\cdot\tfrac12\gamma\|e\|^2 = K\|e\|^2$ with $K = \gamma\|J_*^{-1}\|$. For $\|e\| \le \varepsilon$, $K\|e\| \le \tfrac12$, so $\|e_+\| \le \tfrac12\|e\|$: the next iterate is again within $\varepsilon$, and by induction the iterates converge, each error at most $K$ times the square of the last. $\square$

What it does not say. It is local: "close enough" is not given in advance, and from a poor guess Newton's method may wander or diverge. And it needs $J(x^*)$ invertible — at the nose of section 1 it is not, which is why Newton's method can fail to converge near it. Kelley, Solving Nonlinear Equations with Newton's Method (SIAM, 2003), is the reading for the theory and its practical variants; its exact statement of this theorem was not checked from here, and the proof above stands on its own.

The one-dimensional numbers. $F(3) = 16$, $F'(3) = 25$, $x_1 = 3 - 0.64 = 2.36$; $F(2.36) = 3.4241$, $F'(2.36) = 14.709$, $x_2 = 2.12720$. Errors from $x^* = 2.09455$: $0.905$, $0.265$, $0.0327$. Ratios $e_{k+1}/e_k^2$: $0.32$, $0.46$, $0.55$, tending to $F''/2F' = 3x^*/(3x^{*2} - 2) = 0.56$.

The running example, as a power flow. The tie is the smallest power-flow problem there is: one unknown angle, one equation, $F(\delta) = \sin\delta - \tfrac12$ in units of $P_{\max}$. From the flat start $\delta = 0$, Newton gives $0.5$ rad $= 28.65°$ — the DC power flow's $PX/V^2$, as the next slide proves in general — then $29.9912°$, then $29.99999961°$. 13 · Newton–Raphson

At a flat start, the Jacobian is $B$

Unknowns $x = (\delta_2, \delta_3, V_3)$; the mismatch $F(x)$ = computed minus specified $(P_2, P_3, Q_3)$. A flat start is $x_0$: every $V = 1$, every $\delta = 0$.

theorem

For lossless lines, at a flat start the Jacobian is block-diagonal: its $P$–$\delta$ block is $B$ with the slack struck out, its $Q$–$V$ block is $B$'s PQ rows and columns, and the cross blocks vanish. So the first Newton step's angles are the DC power flow's.

proof

$\partial P_k/\partial\delta_j = -b_{kj}V_kV_j\cos\delta_{kj} \to -b_{kj} = B_{kj}$, and the diagonal $\to B_{kk}$. Every $\partial P/\partial V$ and $\partial Q/\partial\delta$ carries a $\sin\delta_{kj} = 0$. At the start $P = 0$, so the step solves $B\,\Delta\delta = P_{\text{spec}}$. $\square$

The three-bus example's Jacobian at the flat start. Decoupled exactly — section 1's linearisation, for a whole grid.

Full proof

From $P_k = \sum_jb_{kj}V_kV_j\sin\delta_{kj}$ and $Q_k = \sum_jb_{kj}(V_k^2 - V_kV_j\cos\delta_{kj})$, sums over neighbours $j$ of $k$:

  • $\partial P_k/\partial\delta_k = \sum_jb_{kj}V_kV_j\cos\delta_{kj}$; $\partial P_k/\partial\delta_j = -b_{kj}V_kV_j\cos\delta_{kj}$ ($j \ne k$).
  • $\partial P_k/\partial V_k = \sum_jb_{kj}V_j\sin\delta_{kj}$; $\partial P_k/\partial V_j = b_{kj}V_k\sin\delta_{kj}$.
  • $\partial Q_k/\partial\delta_k = \sum_jb_{kj}V_kV_j\sin\delta_{kj}$; $\partial Q_k/\partial\delta_j = -b_{kj}V_kV_j\sin\delta_{kj}$.
  • $\partial Q_k/\partial V_k = \sum_jb_{kj}(2V_k - V_j\cos\delta_{kj})$; $\partial Q_k/\partial V_j = -b_{kj}V_k\cos\delta_{kj}$.

At $V = 1$, $\delta = 0$: $\partial P_k/\partial\delta_k = \sum_jb_{kj} = B_{kk}$, $\partial P_k/\partial\delta_j = -b_{kj} = B_{kj}$; the $\sin$ terms vanish; $\partial Q_k/\partial V_k = \sum_jb_{kj} = B_{kk}$ and $\partial Q_k/\partial V_j = B_{kj}$. So the $P$–$\delta$ block is $B$ restricted to the non-slack buses and the $Q$–$V$ block is $B$ restricted to the PQ buses. At the start every computed $P_k$ and $Q_k$ is zero, so $F(x_0) = -(\text{specified})$ and the Newton step $\Delta x = -J^{-1}F$ solves $B\,\Delta\delta = P_{\text{spec}}$: the DC power flow, exactly. $\square$

For the example. $B_{33} = 9$; $\partial Q_3/\partial V_3 = (5 + 4)(2\times1 - 1) = 9$ ✓.

The fast decoupled method. Keeping these constant blocks at every iteration, instead of recomputing $J$, gives a cheaper iteration that converges more slowly; Bergen and Vittal, ch. 10, is the reading. Not used here.

13 · swing equation

The synchronous machine, stated

A synchronous machine, in two sentences. Its rotor carries a steady magnetic field and turns locked to the grid's frequency, so it induces in the stator windings an EMF $\mathcal E$ whose phase is the rotor's position. The stator's own currents make a field that acts back on the stator as a reactance $X$.

cited · the machine on a bus

So the machine is $\mathcal E$ behind $X$, and joined to a bus of voltage $V$ it delivers, per phase,

$$P_e = \frac{\mathcal EV\sin\delta}{X},$$

with $\delta$ the rotor angle: how far $\mathcal E$, and so the rotor, leads the bus. Section 1's formula, with $\mathcal E$ for $V_1$.

Stated, not derived: Fitzgerald, Kingsley and Umans, ch. 5, §5.4. Branch 12 derives it from the machine's co-energy.

Schematic, not to scale. The rotor's lead $\delta$ over the grid's rotating reference is the angle of its EMF ahead of the bus. Two poles drawn; with more, electrical angles are a multiple of mechanical ones (notes).

What is stated, and where it is proved. Fitzgerald, Kingsley and Umans, Electric Machinery (6th ed.), ch. 5, "Synchronous Machines": §5.2 builds the equivalent circuit, an EMF behind the synchronous reactance, and §5.4, "Steady-State Power-Angle Characteristics", gives $P = \mathcal EV\sin\delta/X$ for a machine joined to a bus through a reactance. Branch 12 of this course derives the same from co-energy and unit 11's rotating field. A learner who skipped branch 12 needs only the two sentences on the slide: a rotor's field induces $\mathcal E$, and the stator's own field acts as a reactance.

Which reactance. The steady-state model uses the synchronous reactance. For the first second or so after a disturbance, the textbooks' "classical model" of a machine uses a smaller transient reactance behind a constant EMF; Kundur's chapter "Synchronous Machine Representation in Stability Studies" is the reading. This unit's examples fold whatever reactance the machine has into the network's, an assumption said each time.

Poles. A rotor with $n_p$ pairs of poles ($n_p = p/2$ in branch 12's count of poles) induces $n_p$ electrical cycles per revolution, so electrical angle $= n_p\times$ mechanical angle, and at 50 Hz a two-pole rotor turns at 3000 rpm, a four-pole one at 1500 rpm. Everything below is written in electrical angles.

Notation. $\mathcal E$ is the course's symbol for an EMF; Fitzgerald writes $E_{af}$, and branch 12 writes $E$ behind $X_s$, the synchronous reactance — this deck's $X$.

13 · swing equation

Newton for the rotor: the swing equation

The rotor, moment of inertia $J$, is driven by the turbine's torque $T_m$ and braked by the electrical torque $T_e$: $J\dot\omega_m = T_m - T_e$. Multiply by $\omega_m$ to get powers, $\omega_m T = P$.

the inertia constant

$H = \dfrac{\tfrac12J\omega_{sm}^2}{S}$: the kinetic energy at synchronous speed $\omega_{sm}$ over the rating $S$. Units: $\mathrm{J/VA} = \mathrm s$ ✓.

the swing equation $$\frac{2H}{\omega_s}\,\ddot\delta = P_m - P_e \quad\text{(per unit)},$$

$\delta$ the rotor angle in electrical radians against a reference turning at $\omega_s = 2\pi\times50$. Units: $\mathrm{s\cdot s^{-2}/s^{-1}}$ — a pure number, as per-unit power is ✓.

The turbine drives the rotor forward, the stator's field holds it back. If they balance it turns at synchronous speed and $\delta$ is constant.

Full proof

Rotation about a fixed axis: $J\dot\omega_m = T_m - T_e$ (MR 16, Euler's equations: for a body turning about a principal axis held fixed by its bearings, the component along that axis reads $J\dot\omega = T$). Multiply by $\omega_m$: $J\omega_m\dot\omega_m = P_m - P_e$, since torque times angular speed is power. The rotor never strays far from synchronous speed — about a per cent or less in the events studied here (0.75 Hz is 1.5% of 50 Hz) — so replace the factor $\omega_m$ on the left by $\omega_{sm}$, an approximation with relative error equal to that fraction. Then $J\omega_{sm} = 2HS/\omega_{sm}$ by the definition of $H$.

The electrical rotor angle is $\delta = n_p\theta_m - \omega_st$, with $\omega_s = n_p\omega_{sm}$. So $\dot\delta = n_p(\omega_m - \omega_{sm})$ and $\ddot\delta = n_p\dot\omega_m$. Substituting, $\frac{2HS}{\omega_{sm}}\cdot\frac{\ddot\delta}{n_p} = \frac{2HS}{\omega_s}\ddot\delta = P_m - P_e$. Dividing by $S$ gives the per-unit form. $\square$

The card's $M$. The curriculum writes $M\delta'' = P_m - P_{\max}\sin\delta$: $M = 2H/\omega_s$ is the inertia coefficient, in seconds squared per radian.

Values of $H$. This deck uses only assumed values (5 s and 4 s), and states no published range, since none was checked from here. Kundur's chapter "Synchronous Machine Parameters" is the reading.

Damping. Real machines also feel a torque proportional to $\dot\delta$ (from currents induced in the rotor, and from loads whose power rises with frequency). It is left out: with it, the centre found next becomes MR 02's stable spiral, and the equal-area estimates of section 5 become conservative.

13 · swing equation

MR 02's pendulum with a constant torque

With $P_e = P_{\max}\sin\delta$ and time scaled by $\sqrt{2H/\omega_sP_{\max}}$, the swing equation is

$$\delta'' + \sin\delta = \frac{P_m}{P_{\max}},$$

MR 02's $\ddot\theta + \gamma\dot\theta + \sin\theta = T$ with $\gamma = 0$ and $T = P_m/P_{\max}$ — "The pendulum with a constant torque", and "The torque, drawn: where $\sin\theta = T$".

equilibria, classified

$\sin\delta = P_m/P_{\max} < 1$ has two roots on the circle: $\delta_0 = \arcsin(P_m/P_{\max})$, a centre, and $\pi - \delta_0$, a saddle.

proof

Linearise, $\delta = \delta^* + \varepsilon$: $\tfrac{2H}{\omega_s}\ddot\varepsilon = -P_{\max}\cos\delta^*\,\varepsilon$. At $\delta_0$, $\cos > 0$: imaginary eigenvalues, and the first integral (next slide) makes it a true centre (MR 02). At $\pi - \delta_0$, $\cos < 0$: real eigenvalues of opposite sign, a saddle. $\square$

Computed, as MR 02 drew it: the crossings of $P_m$ with the power-angle curve. Left of $\delta_0$, $P_m > P_e$ and the rotor speeds up; between the crossings it slows. At $P_m = P_{\max}$ they merge — MR 02's saddle-node — the static limit of section 1.

Full proof

The scaling. Let $\tau = t\sqrt{\omega_sP_{\max}/2H}$. Then $\ddot\delta = (\omega_sP_{\max}/2H)\,\delta''$ and the swing equation becomes $P_{\max}\delta'' = P_m - P_{\max}\sin\delta$.

The classification. As a first-order system $(\delta, \dot\delta)$, the Jacobian at an equilibrium $\delta^*$ is $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-\omega_sP_{\max}\cos\delta^*/2H&0\end{pmatrix}$, with trace 0 and determinant $\omega_sP_{\max}\cos\delta^*/2H$ (MR 02, Planar linear systems: two numbers decide). Determinant positive at $\delta_0$: a linear centre, which linearisation alone cannot certify for the nonlinear system (MR 02, When linearisation lies); the conserved quantity on the next slide has a strict local minimum there, so nearby orbits are closed level curves and the equilibrium is a nonlinear centre, stable (MR 02, The undamped pendulum: a centre, proved stable). Determinant negative at $\pi - \delta_0$: a hyperbolic saddle, and Hartman–Grobman applies (MR 02).

Static stability, again. The centre is where $\mathrm dP_e/\mathrm d\delta > 0$: section 1's rising side. The power engineer's "synchronising power coefficient" $P_{\max}\cos\delta_0$ is the determinant above, times $2H/\omega_s$. At the static limit it is zero.

Running example. Iberia, in its own per unit on 50 GW: $P_m = 2000/50\,000 = 0.04$, $P_{\max} = 4000/50\,000 = 0.08$, so $P_m/P_{\max} = \tfrac12$ and $\delta_0 = 30°$, the same picture.

13 · swing equation

The tilted washboard, and the first integral

first integral $$\mathcal H(\delta, \dot\delta) = \frac{H}{\omega_s}\dot\delta^2 - P_m\delta - P_{\max}\cos\delta$$

is constant along every solution.

proof

Multiply the swing equation by $\dot\delta$: $\frac{2H}{\omega_s}\dot\delta\ddot\delta = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac{H}{\omega_s}\dot\delta^2$, $P_m\dot\delta = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}P_m\delta$, $P_{\max}\sin\delta\,\dot\delta = -\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}P_{\max}\cos\delta$. So $\dot{\mathcal H} = 0$. $\square$

Kinetic energy of the swing plus a potential $U(\delta) = -P_m\delta - P_{\max}\cos\delta$: a washboard tilted by the turbine. The rotor sits in a well; over the lip it slips a pole and keeps sliding — it has lost synchronism.

Computed for $P_m = \tfrac12P_{\max}$, in units of $P_{\max}$. The well's lip is $0.685$ above its floor: the most energy a disturbance may give the rotor.

Energy, in what units. The swing equation is a balance of powers, so $\mathcal H$ has units of power times angle. Dividing by $\omega_s$ turns it into energy: in watts, $\int P\,\mathrm d\delta/\omega_s$ is the work a torque does on the rotor relative to the synchronously turning frame, since $\mathrm d\delta = n_p\,\mathrm d(\theta_m - \omega_{sm}t)$ and $P = T\omega_m \approx Tn_p^{-1}\omega_s$ near synchronous speed. The kinetic term is then $HS\dot\delta^2/\omega_s^2 = \tfrac12J(\omega_m - \omega_{sm})^2$: the energy of the swing in that frame, not the rotor's whole kinetic energy. Section 5 uses this to read areas in the $P$–$\delta$ plane as joules.

The depth. $U(\pi - \delta_0) - U(\delta_0) = 2P_{\max}\cos\delta_0 - P_m(\pi - 2\delta_0)$. At $\delta_0 = 30°$, $P_m = \tfrac12P_{\max}$: $1.7321 - 0.5\times2.0944 = 0.6849\,P_{\max}$.

MR 02's picture. MR 02 drew the untilted pendulum's level curves (The undamped pendulum, drawn as level curves) and the torque's equilibria; the tilt is what the constant torque adds. Its state space is a cylinder, $\delta$ mod $2\pi$; drawn unrolled, the washboard repeats every $2\pi$, one step lower each time.

"Slipping a pole". Each $2\pi$ of electrical angle gained is one pole pitch of the rotor past the stator's field. A machine that does this is out of step with the grid, swinging its power violently; protection disconnects it.

The phase portrait: closed swings, and the separatrix

Level curves of $\mathcal H$ are the orbits (MR 02's method, no integration).

  • Below the saddle's level, near $\delta_0$: closed orbits, the rotor swinging and returning.
  • At the saddle's level: the separatrix, a loop leaving and re-entering the saddle.
  • Outside: orbits that run on in $\delta$ — slipping poles.

A disturbance moves the state; whether it lies inside the separatrix when the disturbance ends decides whether the machine stays in step. Section 5 turns this into a criterion.

Computed for $P_m = \tfrac12P_{\max}$, $\dot\delta$ in units of $\sqrt{\omega_sP_{\max}/2H}$. The tilted cousin of MR 02's level-curve portrait.

The curves. In scaled units, $\tfrac12\delta'^2 - \tfrac12\delta - \cos\delta = c$. Closed orbits: $c$ between the centre's value $-\tfrac12\delta_0 - \cos\delta_0 = -1.1278$ and the saddle's $-\tfrac12(\pi - \delta_0) - \cos(\pi - \delta_0) = -0.4430$. The separatrix is $c = -0.4430$: it leaves the saddle to the left, turns at the point $\delta_L$ where $U(\delta_L) = U(\pi - \delta_0)$, and returns. The dashed orbit has $c = 0.757$.

Why a separatrix cannot be crossed. Orbits do not cross (MR 02, Orbits cannot cross); the separatrix is made of orbits; so a state inside stays inside while the equation holds. That is the whole of the equal-area criterion's logic.

Damping again. With a small damping term the closed orbits become inward spirals and the separatrix splits; the qualitative picture — inside returns, outside slips — survives, and the undamped boundary is a slightly pessimistic estimate of the true one.

Small swings: Iberia against Europe, every 3.8 s

Units: $\mathrm{s^{-1}}\cdot1/\mathrm s$ under the root, so $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$ ✓.

The two areas, per unit on Iberia's 50 GW: $P_{\max} = 0.08$, $\cos30° = 0.866$, $H = 4$ s:

$$\omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{314.16\times0.08\times0.866}{8}} = 1.649\ \mathrm{s^{-1}},$$

Computed. The heavier the export, the softer the spring $P_{\max}\cos\delta_0$ and the slower the swing; at the static limit it stops. A slowing swing is the portrait's warning that centre and saddle are about to meet.

What is and is not claimed. Oscillations of one group of machines against another are called inter-area modes; the reading on small-signal stability is Kundur's chapter "Small-Signal Stability" and Machowski, Bialek and Bumby, ch. 5. Their typical frequencies in real grids were not checked against a source from here, so none is quoted; and nothing in this deck is a statement about oscillations measured on the Iberian or European system.

Equal areas

A fault makes the rotor accelerate; clearing it makes the rotor brake. It stays in step if the braking area can absorb the accelerating area — which is the first integral, read off the power-angle curve.

13 · equal areas

A fault, and the equal-area criterion

At $\delta_0$ a fault drops $P_e$ to zero and $P_m$ accelerates the rotor. At $\delta_c$ it is cleared: $P_e = P_{\max}\sin\delta > P_m$ returns, and the rotor brakes.

the criterion

The machine stays in step iff the accelerating area does not exceed the decelerating area available up to the saddle:

$$\begin{aligned}A_1 &= \int_{\delta_0}^{\delta_c}P_m\,\mathrm d\delta\\ &\le A_2 = \int_{\delta_c}^{\pi - \delta_0}(P_{\max}\sin\delta - P_m)\,\mathrm d\delta .\end{aligned}$$

Equality gives the critical clearing angle:

$$\cos\delta_c = \frac{P_m}{P_{\max}}(\pi - 2\delta_0) + \cos(\pi - \delta_0).$$

Computed, $\delta_0 = 30°$. The rotor gains energy $A_1$ while faulted, then spends it climbing the curve. Cleared at $79.56°$ it just reaches $150°$, the saddle, with nothing to spare.

Full proof of the formula

$A_1 = P_m(\delta_c - \delta_0)$. $A_2 = P_{\max}(\cos\delta_c - \cos\delta_{\max}) - P_m(\delta_{\max} - \delta_c)$ with $\delta_{\max} = \pi - \delta_0$. Setting $A_1 = A_2$, the $P_m\delta_c$ terms cancel: $P_m(\delta_{\max} - \delta_0) = P_{\max}(\cos\delta_c - \cos\delta_{\max})$, so $\cos\delta_c = (P_m/P_{\max})(\pi - 2\delta_0) + \cos(\pi - \delta_0)$. The criterion itself is proved on the next slide.

The fault. A short circuit on or near the tie — three phases to earth, say — holds the voltage at the fault near zero, and no real power crosses it: $P_e = 0$ is the textbook's worst case. Relays detect it and breakers open (named only; section 1). The textbook simplification used here is that after clearing the network is as it was before; in reality clearing may open the faulted line, leaving a weaker post-fault network with a lower $P_{\max}$, and the same construction then uses that curve.

Areas as energies. Divided by $\omega_s$, an area in the $P$–$\delta$ plane is energy (previous section). For Iberia at the critical clearing angle, $A_1 = 2000\ \mathrm{MW}\times0.865\ \mathrm{rad} = 1730$ MW·rad, which is $1730\times10^6/314.16 = 5.5$ MJ: the kinetic energy of the rotors' motion relative to the synchronously turning frame, $\tfrac12J(\omega_m - \omega_{sm})^2$, which the braking area must take back. It is not the rotors' whole gain. That is the unsold power times the fault's duration, $2000\ \mathrm{MW}\times1.05\ \mathrm s = 2.1$ GJ, and it is far larger because the rotors were already turning at synchronous speed: $\tfrac12J(\omega_{sm} + \Delta\omega)^2 - \tfrac12J\omega_{sm}^2 \approx J\omega_{sm}\Delta\omega$.

History. The criterion is standard (Bergen and Vittal, ch. 14; Kundur's chapter "Transient Stability"); who first stated it was not checked from here, so no name is attached.

13 · equal areas

Equal areas are the first integral

proof of the criterion

Faulted ($P_e = 0$): $\mathcal H_f = \frac H{\omega_s}\dot\delta^2 - P_m\delta$ is conserved, so at $\delta_c$, $\frac H{\omega_s}\dot\delta_c^2 = P_m(\delta_c - \delta_0) = A_1$.

Cleared: $\mathcal H = \frac H{\omega_s}\dot\delta^2 + U(\delta)$ is conserved, $U = -P_m\delta - P_{\max}\cos\delta$. The state returns iff $\mathcal H$ at clearing is at most the saddle's value $\mathcal H_s = U(\pi - \delta_0)$:

$$A_1 + U(\delta_c) \le U(\pi - \delta_0) \iff A_1 \le A_2 ,$$

since $U(\pi - \delta_0) - U(\delta_c) = \int_{\delta_c}^{\pi-\delta_0}U'\,\mathrm d\delta = A_2$. $\square$

So the criterion says: clear before the faulted orbit crosses the post-fault separatrix.

Computed, upper half-plane, $\dot\delta$ scaled as before. Faulted, the state climbs a parabola; at the critical angle it lands exactly on the separatrix and creeps into the saddle. Later, it lands outside.

Full proof

During the fault, the swing equation is $\tfrac{2H}{\omega_s}\ddot\delta = P_m$, with first integral $\mathcal H_f = \tfrac H{\omega_s}\dot\delta^2 - P_m\delta$ (the same multiplication by $\dot\delta$). It starts at rest relative to synchronous speed, $\dot\delta = 0$ at $\delta_0$, so $\tfrac H{\omega_s}\dot\delta^2 = P_m(\delta - \delta_0)$: a parabola in the phase plane, the figure's lower curve. At $\delta_c$ the kinetic term is $A_1$.

After clearing, the full $\mathcal H$ is conserved, with value $\mathcal H_c = A_1 + U(\delta_c)$, $U(\delta) = -P_m\delta - P_{\max}\cos\delta$. The orbit through $(\delta_c, \dot\delta_c)$ with $\dot\delta_c > 0$ moves right while $\tfrac H{\omega_s}\dot\delta^2 = \mathcal H_c - U(\delta) > 0$. Between $\delta_c$ and $\pi - \delta_0$, $U$ increases ($U' = P_{\max}\sin\delta - P_m > 0$ there). If $\mathcal H_c < U(\pi - \delta_0) = \mathcal H_s$, the kinetic term reaches zero at some $\delta^\dagger < \pi - \delta_0$, where $\ddot\delta = (\omega_s/2H)(P_m - P_{\max}\sin\delta^\dagger) < 0$: the rotor turns back, and the orbit is a closed level curve inside the separatrix. If $\mathcal H_c > \mathcal H_s$, the kinetic term stays positive through the saddle, beyond which $U$ falls for ever (on the unrolled washboard), and $\delta$ runs away. At equality the orbit is the separatrix itself.

The rearrangement. $\mathcal H_c \le \mathcal H_s \iff A_1 \le U(\pi - \delta_0) - U(\delta_c) = \int_{\delta_c}^{\pi - \delta_0}U'(\delta)\,\mathrm d\delta = \int_{\delta_c}^{\pi - \delta_0}(P_{\max}\sin\delta - P_m)\,\mathrm d\delta = A_2$. $\square$

What was assumed. The clearing angle is before the saddle, $\delta_c < \pi - \delta_0$ (otherwise there is no braking area at all); the post-fault curve is the pre-fault one; no damping. Each assumption is the textbook's, and Bergen and Vittal, ch. 14, and Kundur's "Transient Stability" relax them.

Every machine in a synchronous grid turns in step, so for slow events the whole of Continental Europe is one machine. Its swing equation turns a lost power station into a falling frequency, and reserves stop it.

13 · frequency

Every machine in step: one swing equation for a grid

Sum the swing equations of all $N$ machines, in watts. The network is lossless, so the electrical powers add up to the load:

$$\sum_i\frac{2H_iS_i}{\omega_s}\ddot\delta_i = \sum_iP_{m,i} - P_{\text{load}}.$$

For slow events the machines move together, $\dot\delta_i \approx 2\pi\Delta f$, the grid's frequency deviation. With $HS = \sum_iH_iS_i$:

the aggregate swing equation $$\frac{2HS}{f_0}\,\frac{\mathrm d\Delta f}{\mathrm dt} = -\Delta P + R(t) - D\,\Delta f$$

after losing $\Delta P$ of generation; $R$ the reserves' response; $D$ the load damping. Units: $\mathrm{J\cdot Hz^{-1}\cdot Hz\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm W$ ✓.

An imbalance anywhere changes every machine's speed: frequency is the grid's shared state, and inertia sets how fast it moves.

For seconds-long events the machines' relative swings (section 4) ride on a common motion. That common motion is the frequency, and its inertia is the sum of all of theirs.

Full proof

Each machine, in watts: $\tfrac{2H_iS_i}{\omega_s}\ddot\delta_i = P_{m,i} - P_{e,i}$. Sum over $i$. In a lossless network the power the machines put in equals what the loads take out (section 2: $\sum_kP_k = 0$), so $\sum_iP_{e,i} = P_{\text{load}}$. Define the centre-of-inertia angle $\bar\delta = \sum_iH_iS_i\delta_i/\sum_iH_iS_i$; then the left side is exactly $\tfrac{2HS}{\omega_s}\ddot{\bar\delta}$, with no approximation. The machines' relative swings — section 4's inter-area mode, for instance — average out of $\bar\delta$. The approximation is to call $\dot{\bar\delta}/2\pi$ "the" frequency deviation $\Delta f$, which is right once the relative swings are small.

Before the event everything balances. Losing a unit of $\Delta P$ removes it from $\sum P_m$; the reserves add $R(t)$; and loads whose power falls with frequency (motors, for instance) take $D\Delta f$ less when $\Delta f < 0$. With $\ddot{\bar\delta} = 2\pi\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt$ and $\omega_s = 2\pi f_0$, the left side becomes $\tfrac{2HS}{f_0}\tfrac{\mathrm d\Delta f}{\mathrm dt}$. (The lost unit's own inertia also leaves $HS$; at 1% of the total it is neglected.)

Reading. Machowski, Bialek and Bumby, ch. 9, "Frequency Stability and Control"; Kundur's chapter "Control of Active Power and Reactive Power". Both develop the governors and reserves that this section models in one line each.

The system. The regulation's figures are set per synchronous area, and these are CE's. The two-area model treats Iberia as part of that synchronous system, joined to the rest by its tie. Its size here, 300 GW online, is an assumption.

Worked: losing 3000 MW in 300 GW

worked example 5 — Continental Europe as one machine, and the reference incident

Assume $S = 300$ GW, $H = 5$ s ($HS = 1500$ GJ). Lose $\Delta P = 3000$ MW.

The first instant ($R = 0$, $\Delta f = 0$): the rate of change of frequency is

$$\frac{\mathrm d\Delta f}{\mathrm dt} = -\frac{f_0\Delta P}{2HS} = -0.050\ \mathrm{Hz/s}.$$

A ramp model: FCR rises linearly to its full 3000 MW at the allowed 30 s, $R = \Delta P\,t/T$, no load damping (assumed). Integrating,

$$\Delta f(t) = -\frac{f_0\Delta P}{2HS}\Big(t - \frac{t^2}{2T}\Big),$$ $$\Delta f_{\min} = -\frac{f_0\Delta P\,T}{4HS} = -0.75\ \mathrm{Hz}$$

at $t = T = 30$ s, as the reserve catches up: inside the 800 mHz.

Losing 1 GW: $-0.0167$ Hz/s at first, and a ramp-model dip of $0.25$ Hz.

A simple model's numbers, under an assumed delivery; the regulation's figures are what the system is run to, not derived from this.

Full proof of the dip

With $R = \Delta P\,t/T$ and $D = 0$: $\tfrac{2HS}{f_0}\tfrac{\mathrm d\Delta f}{\mathrm dt} = -\Delta P(1 - t/T)$ for $0 \le t \le T$. Integrate from $\Delta f(0) = 0$: $\Delta f(t) = -\tfrac{f_0\Delta P}{2HS}(t - t^2/2T)$. Its derivative vanishes only at $t = T$, where $\Delta f = -\tfrac{f_0\Delta P}{2HS}\cdot\tfrac T2$. Numbers: $f_0\Delta P/2HS = 50\times3\times10^9/(2\times5\times3\times10^{11}) = 0.05$ Hz/s; times $T/2 = 15$ s gives $0.75$ Hz. For 1 GW, a third of each. $\square$

Units. $f_0\Delta P/(HS)$: $\mathrm{Hz\cdot W/(s\cdot W)} = \mathrm{Hz/s}$ ✓; times $T$ in seconds, Hz ✓. Limits. $T \to 0$ (instant reserve): no dip ✓. $H \to \infty$: no dip ✓. $\Delta P \to 0$: none ✓.

What the ramp is, and is not. Two things that help are left out. Load damping: with 1% of load per hertz (assumed, on a load of 300 GW), the computed minimum is 0.39 Hz, at about 18 s — the problem sheet's numerical extension. And real reserves respond to the deviation itself, so they come out faster when the dip is deep, rather than along a fixed ramp. Against those two, the model is pessimistic. But the ramp itself is an assumption, not the slowest delivery the regulation allows. Art. 154(7) of the same regulation asks of CE's FCR that its activation "begin as soon as possible", that "at least 50 % of the full FCR capacity shall be delivered at the latest after 15 seconds", "100 %" after 30 s, and that it "rise at least linearly from 15 to 30 seconds". The ramp meets the last three with equality from 15 s on; before 15 s the figures alone would admit less. A reserve that gave nothing for 15 s and then followed those minimums would, in this model, take the frequency down 0.75 Hz by 15 s and 0.94 Hz by 30 s. So 0.75 Hz is the ramp model's dip, a consistency check, not a bound.

After 30 s, in this model, the reserve exactly matches the loss and the frequency would stay at its lowest point; the next slide says where it actually settles and why.

The rule is not derived here. That a 3000 MW incident, 800 mHz and 30 s fit together in the ramp model is a consistency check of the model against the rule, not an account of how the rule was set; the deck makes no claim about that.

Where it settles: droop, and the 200 mHz

FCR is delivered by droop: each reserve unit adds power in proportion to the frequency deficit, up to its full amount. Sized so that 3000 MW is out at 200 mHz,

$$R = K|\Delta f|,$$ $$K = \frac{3000\ \mathrm{MW}}{0.2\ \mathrm{Hz}} = 15\ \mathrm{GW/Hz}.$$

Steady state: $\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt = 0$ needs $R = \Delta P$, so $|\Delta f| = \Delta P/K = $ 0.2 Hz — exactly the regulation's maximum steady-state deviation. With load damping it is $\Delta P/(K + D) = 0.17$ Hz.

Droop stops the fall; it cannot bring the frequency back, since its output needs a deficit. That is the frequency restoration reserve's job, within the regulation's 15 minutes.

Computed. The reserve's characteristic against the lost 3000 MW: they meet at 200 mHz. The ramp of the last slide is an assumed path there; the droop says where it ends.

Full proof

In steady state the aggregate swing equation's left side is zero, so $0 = -\Delta P + R - D\Delta f$. With $R = K|\Delta f|$ and $\Delta f < 0$: $\Delta P = (K + D)|\Delta f|$. Without load damping, $|\Delta f| = 3000/15\,000 = 0.2$ Hz; with $D = 1\%$ of 300 GW per hertz $= 3000$ MW/Hz (assumed), $|\Delta f| = 3000/18\,000 = 0.167$ Hz. $\square$

The two models together. The ramp of worked example 5 and the droop here answer different questions with different simplifications: how deep, if the reserve arrives along the assumed ramp; and where the balance lies once it has arrived. The path between them — from the dip back up to the droop's 200 mHz — depends on the dynamics of governors, turbines and loads, which this unit does not model. Load damping is one thing that lifts the frequency off its lowest point: in the problem sheet's extension the frequency recovers once the reserve is out.

Restoration. A slower, centrally coordinated reserve shifts generation back until the frequency is nominal and the scheduled flows between areas are restored, within the 15 minutes of Annex III. Named, not modelled.

Droop in a machine. A governor with droop is a proportional controller on speed. Kundur's chapter "Control of Active Power and Reactive Power" develops it; nothing more is used here.
