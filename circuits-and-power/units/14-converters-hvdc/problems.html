Power electronics and HVDC: switching, and when the grid goes DC

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You prove the buck converter's averaged output \(V_o = DV_{\text{in}}\) from its switched equations. You compute the length at which a given AC cable's charging current reaches its thermal rating — 99 km for the 400 kV cable of capacitance 0.208 μF/km rated 1500 A — and the share of that rating INELFE's 64.5 km would spend on charging current as AC: 65%.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about four hours. P2, P3 and P8 are the checkpoint's. Conventions as in the deck: ideal switches (on: \(v = 0\); off: \(i = 0\)) and unit 03's ideal diodes; passive sign convention; a switching period \(T = 1/f\) with the switch on for \(DT\); inductor current \(i_L\), output voltage \(v_o\), load current \(I_o\); RMS phasors with \(e^{+i\omega t}\); three-phase quantities per phase, with 400 kV line-to-line meaning \(400/\sqrt3 = 230.9\) kV to earth (unit 11).

The running numbers, all assumed: the car charger as a buck converter, \(V_{\text{in}} = 12\) V, \(V_o = 5\) V, \(I_o = 2\) A, \(L = 10\ \mu\)H, \(C = 22\ \mu\)F, \(f = 500\) kHz. The 400 kV XLPE cable: \(a = 30\) mm, \(b = 57\) mm, \(\varepsilon_r = 2.4\), rated 1500 A. INELFE (Red Eléctrica de España, The Spain–France underground electrical interconnection, 2015; Prysmian Group, inauguration press release, 2015): 64.5 km, two 1000 MW links, each a pair of cables at \(\pm320\) kV. Constants: \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\) F/m; copper \(\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\)m at 20 °C (CRC Handbook). Every other device value is stated in its problem and is an assumption for the problem.

Switching, and the buck converter

P1 The ideal switch and switching loss routine

exercises: \(p = vi\) for an ideal switch, the linear model of a transition, loss proportional to frequency

  1. Show that an ideal switch and an ideal diode absorb no power at any instant.
  2. A switch turns on an inductive load current \(I\) clamped by a diode at voltage \(V\): the current rises linearly from 0 to \(I\) in \(t_r\) at full voltage, then the voltage falls linearly from \(V\) to 0 in \(t_f\) at full current. Find the energy lost, and show that it depends only on \(t_c = t_r + t_f\). Hence find the switching loss at frequency \(f\), with one turn-on and one turn-off per period.
  3. Evaluate it for the charger: 12 V, 2 A, \(t_c = 20\) ns, 500 kHz. What efficiency does switching loss alone allow for 10 W out?
  4. The switch also has an on-resistance of 50 mΩ (assumed), carrying 2 A while on. Find its conduction loss, and the switching frequency at which switching loss would equal it.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1–3 are each one product or one integral. Part 4 compares a loss that does not depend on \(f\) with one proportional to \(f\).

Rung 2

During the rise \(v = V\), \(i = It/t_r\); during the fall \(i = I\), \(v = V(1 - t/t_f)\). The conduction loss is \(I^2R_{\text{on}}\) for a fraction \(D\) of the time, with \(D = 5/12\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(E_r = \int_0^{t_r}V\cdot It/t_r\,\mathrm dt = \tfrac12VIt_r\).

Worked solution

Idea. Loss needs voltage and current at once. An ideal switch never has both; a real one has both only while changing state, so its loss is a fixed energy per transition, times the number of transitions per second.

1. On, \(v = 0\); off, \(i = 0\): in both, \(p = vi = 0\). The ideal diode's law is \(vi = 0\) with \(v \le 0\), \(i \ge 0\) (unit 03), so likewise.

2. Rise: \(\tfrac12VIt_r\). Fall: \(\int_0^{t_f}V(1 - t/t_f)I\,\mathrm dt = \tfrac12VIt_f\). Total \(\tfrac12VI(t_r + t_f) = \tfrac12VIt_c\), whatever the split. A turn-off reverses the sequence and costs the same, so two transitions per period give \(P_{\text{sw}} = VIt_cf\).

3. \(P_{\text{sw}} = 12\times2\times2\times10^{-8}\times5\times10^5 = 0.24\) W. Efficiency \(10/10.24 = 97.7\%\).

4. Conduction: \(D\,I^2R_{\text{on}} = (5/12)\times4\times0.05 = 0.0833\) W. Equal to \(VIt_cf\) at \(f = 0.0833/(12\times2\times2\times10^{-8}) = 174\) kHz. At 500 kHz switching loss is the larger; together they allow \(10/(10 + 0.24 + 0.083) = 96.9\%\).

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{V\,A\,s\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm W\) ✓. Limit \(t_c\to0\): no loss, the ideal switch ✓. Both losses scale as \(I\) or \(I^2\), so they fall at light load.

P2 Proof: the buck converter's averaged output medium

exercises: the switched equations, periodic steady state, volt-second and charge balance, the energy check — the checkpoint's proof

A buck converter: a switch from \(V_{\text{in}}\) to a node \(s\), on for \(DT\) of each period; a diode from ground to \(s\); an inductor \(L\) from \(s\) to the output; a capacitor \(C\) and a load \(R\) across the output.

  1. Write the inductor's equation in each switched state, assuming continuous conduction.
  2. Prove that in periodic steady state \(\langle v_o\rangle = DV_{\text{in}}\) exactly — without the small-ripple approximation.
  3. Prove that \(\langle i_L\rangle = I_o\), and that the source's mean current is \(DI_o\) (you may now use the small-ripple approximation). Check that power in equals power out.
  4. Check \(D = 0\) and \(D = 1\).
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 2 is a statement about averages over one period. Which quantity has an average you know to be zero?

Rung 2

The inductor's current is periodic. Integrate \(v_L = L\,\mathrm di_L/\mathrm dt\) over a period.

Rung 3

The voltage law round the loop through \(L\) and \(C\) holds in both states: \(v_s = v_L + v_o\). Average it.

Rung 4

First line: \(\int_0^Tv_L\,\mathrm dt = L\,[i_L(T) - i_L(0)] = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. In periodic steady state nothing accumulates in the energy stores over a period. For an inductor that means zero mean voltage; for a capacitor, zero mean current. The switch and diode fix the switch node's waveform, and the inductor's zero mean voltage hands its average to the output.

1. On: the switch connects \(s\) to \(V_{\text{in}}\) and the diode is reverse-biased by \(V_{\text{in}}\), so \(L\,\mathrm di_L/\mathrm dt = V_{\text{in}} - v_o\). Off: the inductor's current, positive by continuous conduction, can flow only through the diode, which therefore conducts with zero voltage, so \(v_s = 0\) and \(L\,\mathrm di_L/\mathrm dt = -v_o\). Both are \(v_L = v_s - v_o\).

2. Periodic steady state: \(i_L(T) = i_L(0)\). So \(\int_0^Tv_L\,\mathrm dt = L[i_L(T) - i_L(0)] = 0\), i.e. \(\langle v_L\rangle = 0\). Averaging \(v_s = v_L + v_o\): \(\langle v_o\rangle = \langle v_s\rangle = \frac1T\big(V_{\text{in}}DT + 0\big) = DV_{\text{in}}\). No approximation was made: only the inductor's law, periodicity, and the two states. \(\square\)

3. Likewise \(\int_0^Ti_C\,\mathrm dt = C[v_o(T) - v_o(0)] = 0\). The output node's current law, \(i_L = i_C + v_o/R\), averaged: \(\langle i_L\rangle = \langle v_o\rangle/R = I_o\). The source supplies \(i_L\) while the switch is on and nothing otherwise. With small ripple \(i_L\) rises linearly through its mean during the on-interval, so its mean there is \(I_o\), and \(\langle i_{\text{in}}\rangle = DI_o\). Power in: \(V_{\text{in}}DI_o = (DV_{\text{in}})I_o = V_oI_o\), power out ✓. The converter is a lossless "DC transformer" of ratio \(D\): voltages scale by \(D\), mean currents by \(1/D\).

4. \(D = 0\): the switch never closes, \(V_o = 0\) ✓. \(D = 1\): the switch never opens, \(V_o = V_{\text{in}}\) through an inductor, which is a short at DC ✓.

Check. Units: \(D\) is a number, so \(DV_{\text{in}}\) is a voltage ✓. For the charger, \(D = 5/12\), \(\langle i_{\text{in}}\rangle = 0.833\) A, and \(12\times0.833 = 10.0\) W \(= 5\times2\) ✓.

Where it would fail

If \(i_L\) reaches zero during the off-interval, the diode stops conducting and \(v_s\) is no longer 0 for the rest of the period: \(\langle v_s\rangle\) then depends on the load, and so does \(V_o\). That is discontinuous conduction (P3, part 4).

P3 Duty cycle and ripple routine

exercises: \(D\), \(\Delta I\), \(\Delta V = \Delta I/8fC\), the filter's resonance, the continuous-conduction boundary — the curriculum's problem

  1. For the charger, find \(D\), the inductor ripple \(\Delta I\) (peak to peak), the output ripple \(\Delta V\), and the filter's resonant frequency \(f_0\). By what factor does the filter divide the switching frequency's component, approximately?
  2. Derive \(\Delta V = \Delta I/(8fC)\) from the capacitor's charge.
  3. What inductance halves \(\Delta I\)? What happens to \(\Delta V\)?
  4. Below what load current does continuous conduction end?
  5. In a running car the 12 V supply may sit at 14 V (assumed). Find \(D\), \(\Delta I\) and \(\Delta V\) there.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every answer is a number from the small-ripple picture: \(i_L\) a triangle, \(i_C = i_L - I_o\) a triangle about zero.

Rung 2

\(\Delta I\) is the on-interval's slope times its length. The capacitor's voltage rises while \(i_C > 0\): find that interval and the charge it delivers. Continuous conduction needs the triangle's minimum, \(I_o - \Delta I/2\), to stay positive.

Rung 3

First line: \(\Delta I = (V_{\text{in}} - V_o)DT/L = 7\times(5/12)\times2\times10^{-6}/10^{-5}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The inductor's slopes are fixed by the two state voltages; the capacitor takes the ripple current and turns its charge into ripple voltage.

1. \(D = 5/12 = 0.4167\). \(\Delta I = 7\times0.8333\times10^{-6}/10^{-5} = 0.583\) A. \(\Delta V = 0.583/(8\times5\times10^5\times2.2\times10^{-5}) = 0.583/88 = 6.6\) mV. \(f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{2.2\times10^{-10}}) = 10.7\) kHz. The filter is second-order, so far above \(f_0\) it divides by about \((f/f_0)^2 = 46.6^2 = 2170\).

2. \(i_C\) is a triangle of peaks \(\pm\Delta I/2\); it is positive from halfway up one rise to halfway down the next fall, an interval of \(DT/2 + (1 - D)T/2 = T/2\). In it the charge delivered is the triangle's area, \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac T2\cdot\tfrac{\Delta I}2 = T\Delta I/8\). The voltage rises over exactly that interval from minimum to maximum: \(\Delta V = T\Delta I/(8C) = \Delta I/(8fC)\).

3. \(\Delta I\propto1/L\): \(L = 20\ \mu\)H gives 0.292 A. \(\Delta V\propto\Delta I\), so it halves too, to 3.3 mV.

4. \(I_o > \Delta I/2 = 0.29\) A.

5. \(D = 5/14 = 0.357\); \(\Delta I = 9\times0.357\times2\times10^{-6}/10^{-5} = 0.643\) A; \(\Delta V = 0.643/88 = 7.3\) mV. A higher input steepens the on-slope more than it shortens the on-time: \(\Delta I = V_oT(1 - V_o/V_{\text{in}})/L\) grows with \(V_{\text{in}}\).

Check. The fall, \(V_o(1 - D)T/L = 5\times(7/12)\times2\times10^{-6}/10^{-5} = 0.583\) A, equals the rise ✓ (volt-second balance). \(\Delta V/V_o = 0.13\%\): the small-ripple approximation is consistent ✓. Units of \(\Delta I/8fC\): \(\mathrm{A\,s/F} = \mathrm V\) ✓.

P4 Boost and buck–boost medium

exercises: volt-second balance on other circuits, charge balance, the energy check

Boost. An inductor \(L\) from \(V_{\text{in}}\) to a node \(s\); a switch from \(s\) to ground, on for \(DT\); a diode from \(s\) to the output, pointing towards it; \(C\) and the load across the output. Buck–boost. A switch from \(V_{\text{in}}\) to a node \(s\), on for \(DT\); an inductor from \(s\) to ground; a diode from the output to \(s\), pointing towards \(s\); \(C\) and the load across the output, whose voltage \(v_o\) is measured from ground.

  1. For each, write \(v_L\) in the two states (continuous conduction, small ripple) and use volt-second balance to show \(V_o = V_{\text{in}}/(1 - D)\) for the boost and \(V_o = -DV_{\text{in}}/(1 - D)\) for the buck–boost.
  2. For the boost, find the mean input current in terms of \(I_o\) and check power balance.
  3. Find \(D\) for a boost from 5 V to 12 V, and for a buck–boost from 12 V to \(-5\) V.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Same method as P2: two states, one balance. Only the loop that gives \(v_L\) changes.

Rung 2

Boost: switch on, \(L\) sits across \(V_{\text{in}}\); switch off, the diode conducts and \(L\) sits between \(V_{\text{in}}\) and \(v_o\). Buck–boost: switch on, \(L\) across \(V_{\text{in}}\); off, the diode connects \(L\) across the output.

Rung 3

First line for the boost: \(D\,V_{\text{in}} + (1 - D)(V_{\text{in}} - V_o) = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. In every converter in periodic steady state each inductor has zero mean voltage and each capacitor zero mean current.

1. Boost. On: \(v_L = V_{\text{in}}\) (the switch grounds \(s\)). Off: the inductor's current continues through the diode into the output, so \(v_s = V_o\) and \(v_L = V_{\text{in}} - V_o\). Balance: \(DV_{\text{in}} + (1 - D)(V_{\text{in}} - V_o) = 0\), so \(V_{\text{in}} = (1 - D)V_o\), \(V_o = V_{\text{in}}/(1 - D)\). Buck–boost. On: \(v_L = V_{\text{in}}\). Off: the inductor's current, flowing down through \(L\) to ground, must return through the diode from the output into \(s\), so \(v_s = v_o\) and \(v_L = V_o\). Balance: \(DV_{\text{in}} + (1 - D)V_o = 0\), \(V_o = -DV_{\text{in}}/(1 - D)\): negative, of magnitude less or more than \(V_{\text{in}}\) as \(D\) is below or above \(\tfrac12\).

2. In the boost the inductor is in series with the source at all times, so \(\langle i_{\text{in}}\rangle = \langle i_L\rangle\). The output receives \(i_L\) only while the switch is off; charge balance on \(C\) gives \((1 - D)\langle i_L\rangle = I_o\). So \(\langle i_{\text{in}}\rangle = I_o/(1 - D)\), and \(V_{\text{in}}I_o/(1 - D) = V_oI_o\) ✓.

3. Boost: \(1 - D = 5/12\), \(D = 0.583\). At 2 A out the input draws \(2/0.4167 = 4.8\) A: \(5\times4.8 = 24 = 12\times2\) W ✓. Buck–boost: \(D/(1 - D) = 5/12\), \(D = 5/17 = 0.294\).

Check. Limits: \(D = 0\) gives the boost \(V_o = V_{\text{in}}\) (a straight path through \(L\) and the diode) and the buck–boost 0 ✓. As \(D\to1\) both ratios diverge: in the ideal model the inductor is charged ever longer and discharged ever more briefly; real losses stop this. Erickson and Maksimović, ch. 6, "Converter Circuits", treats both converters.

P5 Harmonics of a PWM waveform medium

exercises: Fourier coefficients with symmetry (MR 19), placing a notch, Parseval, total harmonic distortion — the curriculum's problem

  1. Derive the Fourier series of the quasi-square wave: \(+V\) on \((\alpha, \pi - \alpha)\), \(-V\) on \((\pi + \alpha, 2\pi - \alpha)\), zero elsewhere.
  2. Find the notch \(\alpha\) that removes the 5th harmonic, and give \(b_1\), \(b_3\) and \(b_7\) there.
  3. The total harmonic distortion of a waveform is the RMS of everything but the fundamental, divided by the fundamental's RMS. Find it for the square wave (\(\alpha = 0\)) in closed form, using Parseval rather than summing the series term by term.
  4. Use the same calculation to evaluate \(\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}1/n^2\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The wave is odd and satisfies \(f(x + \pi) = -f(x)\): only odd sine terms survive. For part 3, Parseval gives the total mean square without any series.

Rung 2

\(b_n \propto \cos n\alpha\); a zero needs \(n\alpha = 90°\). The square wave's mean square is \(V^2\); its fundamental's is \(b_1^2/2\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(b_n = \frac2\pi\int_\alpha^{\pi-\alpha}V\sin nx\,\mathrm dx\) for odd \(n\).

Worked solution

Idea. Symmetry kills most coefficients; the rest are one integral. Parseval converts "the RMS of all the harmonics" into "the RMS of the waveform minus the fundamental's".

1. Odd, so \(a_n = 0\). Half-wave antisymmetric, so \(b_n = 0\) for even \(n\) and \(b_n = \frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f\sin nx\,\mathrm dx\) for odd \(n\). \(\frac{2V}\pi\int_\alpha^{\pi-\alpha}\sin nx\,\mathrm dx = \frac{2V}{n\pi}[\cos n\alpha - \cos n(\pi - \alpha)] = \frac{4V}{n\pi}\cos n\alpha\), since \(\cos n(\pi - \alpha) = -\cos n\alpha\) for odd \(n\).

2. \(5\alpha = 90°\): \(\alpha = 18°\). Then \(b_1 = (4/\pi)\cos18°\,V = 1.211V\), \(b_3 = (4/3\pi)\cos54°\,V = 0.249V\), \(b_7 = (4/7\pi)\cos126°\,V = -0.107V\).

3. Mean square of the square wave: \(V^2\). Fundamental amplitude \(4V/\pi\), mean square \(8V^2/\pi^2\). Parseval: the harmonics' mean square is \(V^2(1 - 8/\pi^2)\). THD \(= \sqrt{(1 - 8/\pi^2)/(8/\pi^2)} = \sqrt{\pi^2/8 - 1} = 0.483\), i.e. 48.3%.

4. Parseval in full: \(V^2 = \tfrac12\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}(4V/n\pi)^2 = (8V^2/\pi^2)\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}1/n^2\), so \(\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}1/n^2 = \pi^2/8 = 1.2337\).

Check. At \(\alpha = 0\) part 1 gives \(4V/n\pi\) ✓; at \(\alpha = 90°\), zero ✓. The first terms \(1 + 1/9 + 1/25 + 1/49 = 1.172\), approaching 1.234 ✓.

P6 Proof: triplen harmonics cancel line to line routine

exercises: delays as phase factors, \(\alpha = e^{i2\pi/3}\) (unit 11), zero sequence, the carrier ratio

Three inverter legs produce \(v_k(t) = g(t - kT/3)\), \(k = 0, 1, 2\), for one \(T\)-periodic waveform \(g\).

  1. Prove that no line-to-line voltage \(v_j - v_k\) contains a harmonic of order \(3, 6, 9, \ldots\), and that every other harmonic appears line to line with \(\sqrt3\) times its amplitude in one leg.
  2. Show that with a common triangular carrier at \(m_f\) times the output frequency, the hypothesis holds when \(m_f\) is a multiple of 3. Which of the 19th, 21st and 23rd harmonics then survive line to line, at \(m_f = 21\)?
  3. Into a balanced star load with an isolated star point, what voltage do the triplen components produce across each load impedance?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A delay by \(T/3\) multiplies the \(n\)th complex Fourier coefficient by a phase.

Rung 2

The phase is \(e^{-i2\pi n/3}\). For part 2, ask when the carrier delayed by \(T/3\) is the same function. For part 3, unit 11's zero sequence.

Rung 3

First line: \(v_k(t) = \sum_nc_ne^{in\omega(t - kT/3)} = \sum_nc_ne^{-i2\pi nk/3}e^{in\omega t}\).

Worked solution

Idea. A third-period delay is a rotation by \(2\pi n/3\) of the \(n\)th harmonic, which is no rotation at all when \(3 \mid n\).

1. From the first line, the \(n\)th coefficient of \(v_k\) is \(c_n\alpha^{-nk}\) with \(\alpha = e^{i2\pi/3}\). For \(n = 3j\), \(\alpha^{-3jk} = 1\): the same in every leg, so \(v_j - v_k\) has coefficient zero. For other \(n\), the coefficient of \(v_0 - v_1\) is \(c_n(1 - \alpha^{-n})\), and \(|1 - \alpha^{\mp1}| = |1 - e^{\mp i2\pi/3}| = \sqrt{(3/2)^2 + 3/4} = \sqrt3\); likewise for every pair. \(\square\)

2. The references are delays of one another by \(T/3\). The carrier has period \(T/m_f\); it equals its own delay by \(T/3\) iff \(T/3\) is a whole number of carrier periods, \(m_f/3\in\mathbb Z\). Then each leg's switched output is exactly \(g(t - kT/3)\). At \(m_f = 21\): the 21st is triplen and cancels; the 19th and 23rd are not multiples of 3 and survive, at \(\sqrt3\times0.220 = 0.381\) of \(V_{\text{dc}}/2\) (the deck's computed leg values).

3. The triplen components are equal in all three legs: a zero-sequence set (unit 11). A balanced star load with an isolated star point cannot carry zero-sequence current (the three currents must sum to zero, and equal currents summing to zero are zero), so the star point rises and falls with them and each load impedance sees none of them.

Check. The fundamental line to line is \(\sqrt3\times0.8 = 1.386\) of \(V_{\text{dc}}/2\) — unit 11's \(\sqrt3\) between line and phase voltages ✓; the deck's computation gives 1.3856.

P7 Power factor of a distorted current routine

exercises: orthogonality and Parseval (MR 19), distortion and displacement factors — unit 06's warning

  1. For a sinusoidal voltage of RMS \(V\) and a periodic current, prove \(P = VI_1\cos\varphi_1\) and \(P/(VI) = (I_1/I)\cos\varphi_1\).
  2. Find the power factor of a square-wave current in phase with the voltage.
  3. Find it for a quasi-square current with \(\alpha = 30°\), in phase.
  4. A current has distortion factor 0.9 and its fundamental lags by 20°. Find the power factor. Can a capacitor across the load raise it to 1?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The power is a mean of a product; the RMS is a mean of a square. Both are integrals of Fourier series over a period.

Rung 2

For the quasi-square wave, its RMS is \(V\sqrt{(\pi - 2\alpha)/\pi}\) (it is \(\pm V\) for that fraction of the time), and its fundamental's amplitude is P5's \(b_1\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(I_1/I = (4\hat I/\pi)/\sqrt2\ \big/\ \hat I\).

Worked solution

Idea. Only the current's component at the voltage's frequency can carry mean power; all of the current heats the wires.

1. \(P = \frac1T\int_0^T\sqrt2V\cos\omega t\,\big(I_0 + \sum_n\sqrt2I_n\cos(n\omega t - \varphi_n)\big)\mathrm dt\). Every term with \(n \ne 1\) (and the mean) integrates to zero by orthogonality; \(n = 1\) gives \(VI_1\cos\varphi_1\). Parseval: \(I^2 = I_0^2 + \sum I_n^2\). Divide. \(\square\)

2. \(I_1/I = 2\sqrt2/\pi = 0.900\); \(\cos\varphi_1 = 1\). Power factor 0.900.

3. RMS \(\hat I\sqrt{1 - 2\alpha/\pi} = \hat I\sqrt{2/3} = 0.816\hat I\). Fundamental RMS \(1.103\hat I/\sqrt2 = 0.780\hat I\). Ratio \(0.955\): better than the square wave, because the notch removed the third harmonic.

4. \(0.9\cos20° = 0.846\). A capacitor can cancel the fundamental's lagging part (unit 06), raising the displacement factor to 1, but it cannot touch the harmonics: the best it can do is 0.9. (In practice a capacitor also changes the harmonic currents, and can resonate with the supply's inductance; not treated.)

Check. For a sinusoid \(I_1 = I\) and the formula is unit 06's \(\cos\varphi\) ✓. For the square wave, \(\sqrt{1 - (I_1/I)^2}/(I_1/I)\) with \(I_1/I = 2\sqrt2/\pi\) is \(0.483\), P5's THD ✓.

Why the grid goes DC

P8 The critical length of an AC cable routine

exercises: the coaxial \(C'\), the charging current \(\omega C'V\) per km, the critical length, quadrature — the checkpoint's numbers

  1. Find \(C'\) for the 400 kV cable (\(a = 30\) mm, \(b = 57\) mm, \(\varepsilon_r = 2.4\)), and its charging current per kilometre at 50 Hz.
  2. Fed from one end and open at the other, at what length does the charging current alone reach the 1500 A rating?
  3. For INELFE's 64.5 km as such a cable: the charging current at the fed end, its share of the rating, the current left for power, and the reactive power three such cables make.
  4. With equal shunt reactors at both ends, what is the critical length, and what share of the rating does INELFE's length then spend at each end?
  5. Is the lumped estimate good at these lengths?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The charging current is a current per unit length times a length, a quarter-cycle ahead of the voltage. The voltage is the phase voltage to the earthed sheath.

Rung 2

\(C' = 2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r/\ln(b/a)\); \(V = 400/\sqrt3\) kV. The power current is in phase with \(V\), so the two add in quadrature. Reactors at both ends make the current zero in the middle. For part 5, compare \(\beta l\) with 1.

Rung 3

First line: \(\ln(57/30) = 0.6419\), so \(C' = 2\pi\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times2.4/0.6419 = 2.080\times10^{-10}\) F/m.

Worked solution

Idea. A cable is a coaxial capacitor along its whole length; on AC every kilometre draws its own charging current, and all of it passes through the copper at the fed end.

1. \(C' = 0.208\ \mu\)F/km. \(\omega C'V = 314.16\times2.080\times10^{-7}\times230\,940 = 15.09\) A/km.

2. \(I(x) = i\omega C'V(l - x)\), largest at \(x = 0\). \(l_c = 1500/15.09 = 99.4\) km.

3. \(15.09\times64.5 = 973\) A, \(973/1500 = 65\%\). Left for power: \(\sqrt{1500^2 - 973^2} = 1141\) A. Reactive power: \(3\times230.9\ \mathrm{kV}\times973\ \mathrm A = 674\) Mvar.

4. Each end supplies half: \(l = 2l_c = 199\) km. INELFE: 487 A at each end, 32%.

5. Wave speed \(c/\sqrt{2.4} = 1.94\times10^8\) m/s, \(\lambda = 3870\) km at 50 Hz. At 99.4 km \(\beta l = 0.161\) rad; unit 08's exact open-line current is larger by \(\tan\beta l/\beta l = 1.009\). Good to 1%.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{s^{-1}\,F\,km^{-1}\,V} = \mathrm{A/km}\) ✓. Using the line voltage, 400 kV, by mistake gives 26.1 A/km, 57 km, and an impossible 112% for INELFE — the inconsistency is the warning. Limit: \(l\to0\), no charging ✓; DC, \(\omega = 0\), none at any length ✓.

P9 Overhead against cable routine

exercises: the two-dimensional potential (MR 23), capacitance to the neutral, why the critical length matters for cables first

  1. Two long wires of radius \(r\), \(D \gg r\) apart, carry \(\pm\lambda\). Show that the capacitance per metre of one wire to the midplane is twice the capacitance per metre between the wires.
  2. For three wires at the corners of an equilateral triangle of side \(D\), with charges summing to zero, show that each wire's capacitance to the neutral is \(2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(D/r)\).
  3. For unit 07's 400 kV line (\(r = 0.2097\) m, \(D = 10\) m), find \(C'\), the charging current per kilometre, and the length at which it would reach 1500 A. Why is that length not meaningful?
  4. Compare with the cable of P8, and say which limit a long overhead line meets first.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is symmetry; part 2 is superposition of three line-charge potentials.

Rung 2

A line charge's potential is \(-(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln\rho\) plus a constant. At wire \(a\)'s surface, the other two are at distance \(D\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\varphi_a = \frac1{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\big[\lambda_a\ln\tfrac1r + (\lambda_b + \lambda_c)\ln\tfrac1D\big] + \text{const}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The neutral is where a balanced set of charges puts zero potential; capacitance to it is what one phase's voltage charges.

1. By antisymmetry the midplane is at the mean of the wires' potentials, so with \(V\) between the wires each is \(V/2\) from the plane. \(C'_{\text{plane}} = \lambda/(V/2) = 2\lambda/V = 2C'_{\text{loop}}\). With unit 08's \(C'_{\text{loop}} = \pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(D/r)\), \(C'_{\text{plane}} = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(D/r)\).

2. With \(\lambda_b + \lambda_c = -\lambda_a\), \(\varphi_a = (\lambda_a/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(D/r)\); the constant vanishes because the total charge is zero (far away the potential tends to zero). So \(C' = \lambda_a/\varphi_a = 2\pi\varepsilon_0/\ln(D/r)\). The earth is neglected.

3. \(\ln(10/0.2097) = 3.865\), \(C' = 14.4\) nF/km. \(\omega C'V = 314.16\times1.4395\times10^{-8}\times230\,940 = 1.04\) A/km. \(1500/1.044 = 1436\) km — close to a quarter wavelength in air, \(c/4f = 1499\) km, where unit 08 showed the lumped picture fails completely (the open-line current \(\propto\tan\beta l\) diverges).

4. The cable's \(C'\) is \(0.208/0.0144 = 14.4\) times larger, because its conductors are millimetres apart through a dielectric, not metres through air. Its critical length, 99 km, is short; the line's is meaningless. A long overhead line meets unit 13's reactance limit first: 1647 MW over 400 km at 400 kV.

Check. Over 400 km the line's lumped charging current is \(1.044\times400 = 418\) A, of the order of unit 08's single-phase 362 A lumped (385 A exact); the factor between them, \(2\times230.9/400 = 1.155\), is part 1's 2 times the phase-to-line voltage ratio ✓.

P10 DC against AC on INELFE's route medium

exercises: \(P = VI\) on a bipole, \(R = \rho l/A\), \(I^2R\) loss, three-phase apparent power, charging current's share — the curriculum's problem

One INELFE link carries 1000 MW on a pair of cables at \(\pm320\) kV. Assume 2500 mm² copper conductors at 20 °C.

  1. Find the current in each cable, the resistance of each, and the loss in the pair as a fraction of 1000 MW.
  2. The same 1562.5 A flows in each of three AC cables at 400 kV line-to-line. What apparent power do they carry, and per cable? Explain the ratio of DC to AC power per cable.
  3. Suppose those AC cables are rated 1562.5 A and have the capacitance of P8's cable. Over 64.5 km, how much real power can the three carry, and what is their loss as a fraction of it (same resistance, no skin effect)?
  4. One link's 1000 MW sent as three-phase AC at 400 kV through P8's cable: what current would the fed end carry? Against the 1500 A rating?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each part is one or two lines of arithmetic. The voltage between the DC cables is 640 kV; each AC cable is at 230.9 kV RMS to earth.

Rung 2

Part 3: the 1562.5 A rating is shared in quadrature with P8's 973 A of charging current. Part 4: the power current is \(10^9/(\sqrt3\times4\times10^5)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(I = 10^9/6.4\times10^5 = 1562.5\) A; \(R = 1.68\times10^{-8}\times64\,500/2.5\times10^{-3}\).

Worked solution

Idea. At the same current the copper loses the same; what differs is how much power each ampere carries, and how many amperes the cable spends on itself.

1. \(I = 1562.5\) A. \(R = 0.433\ \Omega\) (6.72 mΩ/km). Loss \(2\times1562.5^2\times0.433 = 2.12\) MW: 0.21%.

2. \(\sqrt3\times400\ \mathrm{kV}\times1562.5\ \mathrm A = 1083\) MVA, 361 MVA per cable, against 500 MW per DC cable. Ratio \(500/361 = 1.386 = 320/230.9\): DC power per cable is its voltage to earth times the current, AC's at most its RMS voltage to earth times the current. The AC cable's peak to earth, \(\sqrt2\times230.9 = 326.6\) kV, is nearly the DC cable's 320 kV.

3. Power current \(\sqrt{1562.5^2 - 973^2} = 1222\) A; real power \(3\times230.9\ \mathrm{kV}\times1222\ \mathrm A = 847\) MW. The full 1562.5 A flows only at the fed end: along the cable the power current is constant and the charging current falls linearly to zero at the far end, so \(|I(x)|^2 = I_P^2 + I_{\text{ch}}^2(1 - x/l)^2\), whose mean over the length is \(I_P^2 + I_{\text{ch}}^2/3 = 1222^2 + 973^2/3 = 1.810\times10^6\ \mathrm A^2\). Loss \(3\times1.810\times10^6\times0.433 = 2.35\) MW, 0.28% of 847 MW — against DC's 0.21% of 1000 MW. (Taking 1562.5 A all along would give an upper bound, 3.17 MW.) Skin effect would add to AC's.

4. \(10^9/(\sqrt3\times4\times10^5) = 1443\) A of power current; with 973 A of charging in quadrature, \(\sqrt{1443^2 + 973^2} = 1741\) A, above 1500 A. Fed equally from both ends, \(\sqrt{1443^2 + 487^2} = 1523\) A, still just above. With this assumed cable, one link would have needed more or larger cables as AC. That is a statement about the assumed cable, not the operator's reason; REE's own is that direct current "allows underground connections that run over long distances, such as this one of 64.5 kilometres".

Check. Energy: the DC link's receiving end gets \((640\,000 - 2\times1562.5\times0.433)\times1562.5 = 997.9\) MW; \(1000 - 997.9 = 2.1\) MW ✓. Units: \(\Omega\,\mathrm m\cdot\mathrm m/\mathrm m^2 = \Omega\) ✓. None of these numbers includes the converter stations, whose losses are not estimated.

P11 The break-even routine

exercises: a linear cost model, its crossover, and the critical length as a hard cap

Model AC transmission as costing \(a_{\text{AC}}\) per km, and DC as \(F + a_{\text{DC}}l\) with \(a_{\text{DC}} < a_{\text{AC}}\). All numbers below are assumed and illustrative.

  1. Find the break-even length \(l^*\), and the condition for DC to be cheaper.
  2. If conductors were the whole per-km cost, \(a_{\text{AC}} = \tfrac32a_{\text{DC}}\). With \(F = 100\) units and \(a_{\text{DC}} = 1\) unit/km, find \(l^*\).
  3. For a cable route, suppose compensation and the third cable make \(a_{\text{AC}} = 2.5a_{\text{DC}}\). Find \(l^*\). What else limits AC on a cable route, independently of cost?
  4. Explain, using P9, why the break-even is far longer for overhead lines.
Hints · 2 rungs
Rung 1

Two straight lines in the \((l, \text{cost})\) plane; one starts higher and rises more slowly.

Rung 2

First line: \(a_{\text{AC}}l^* = F + a_{\text{DC}}l^*\).

Worked solution

Idea. A fixed cost is repaid by a per-kilometre saving; the critical length is a wall the cost model does not see.

1. \(l^* = F/(a_{\text{AC}} - a_{\text{DC}})\); DC is cheaper for \(l > l^*\).

2. \(l^* = 100/0.5 = 200\) km, i.e. \(2F/a_{\text{DC}}\): the length at which the DC line itself costs twice the converters.

3. \(l^* = 100/1.5 = 66.7\) km. Independently, an uncompensated AC cable cannot exceed its critical length — 99 km for P8's — and with reactors at both ends 199 km; beyond that it needs intermediate compensation or cannot carry power at all, whatever it costs.

4. An overhead line's \(C'\) is fourteen times smaller, so charging costs little and imposes no cap at practical lengths; \(a_{\text{AC}} - a_{\text{DC}}\) is small and \(l^*\) long.

Check. Units: cost/(cost per km) = km ✓. Limit \(F\to0\): DC wins at any length ✓; \(a_{\text{AC}}\to a_{\text{DC}}\): \(l^*\to\infty\) ✓. Real break-even distances depend on project costs and are quoted in the HVDC literature; none is quoted here because none was checked.

P12 Inertia and inverters medium

exercises: unit 13's aggregate swing equation, synthetic inertia, energy behind it, the ramp model's dip

Use unit 13's model of Continental Europe: \(S = 300\) GW, \(H = 5\) s, \(f_0 = 50\) Hz, the 3000 MW reference incident, reserves ramping linearly to 3000 MW over 30 s, no load damping.

  1. Show that an inverter injecting \(-K\,\mathrm df/\mathrm dt\) with \(K = 2HS/f_0\) adds to the aggregate swing equation exactly what a machine of inertia \(H\) on rating \(S\) would. Evaluate \(K\) for \(H = 5\) s, \(S = 1\) GW.
  2. How much energy would that 1 GW machine release in a dip of 0.75 Hz? Where must the inverter find it?
  3. Half of \(S\) is replaced by grid-following inverters without synthetic inertia. Find the initial rate of change of frequency and the ramp model's dip.
  4. In this model, what fraction of \(S\) could be inertia-free before the dip exceeds 800 mHz? Comment.
  5. Say, without equations, what a grid-forming inverter supplies that a grid-following one does not.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything follows from \((2HS/f_0)\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt = \Delta P_m - \Delta P_e\) and unit 13's closed forms, which scale as \(1/H\).

Rung 2

Kinetic energy is \(HS\) at \(f_0\) and scales as \(f^2\). With a fraction \(x\) inertia-free, \(H_{\text{eff}} = 5(1 - x)\) s on the same \(S\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: initial rate \(f_0\Delta P/(2H_{\text{eff}}S) = 50\times3\times10^9/(2\times2.5\times3\times10^{11})\).

Worked solution

Idea. Inertia is stored kinetic energy released in proportion to the rate of change of frequency; an inverter can be told to do the same if it has energy to spend.

1. The machine's rotor contributes \((2HS/f_0)\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt\) to the left side, i.e. it delivers \(-(2HS/f_0)\,\mathrm df/\mathrm dt\) of extra electrical power. An inverter injecting \(-K\,\mathrm df/\mathrm dt\) with the same \(K\) adds the same term. \(K = 2\times5\times10^9/50 = 200\) MW per Hz/s.

2. Kinetic energy \(E = HS(f/f_0)^2\): \(5\times10^9\times[1 - (49.25/50)^2] = 149\) MJ, or about \(2HS\Delta f/f_0 = 150\) MJ to first order. From its DC side: an HVDC link from the other grid; a solar farm only if it was running below the available power, or has storage.

3. \(H_{\text{eff}} = 2.5\) s. Rate \(0.10\) Hz/s; dip \(f_0\Delta PT/(4H_{\text{eff}}S) = 50\times3\times10^9\times30/(4\times2.5\times3\times10^{11}) = 1.5\) Hz.

4. \(0.75/(1 - x) = 0.8\) gives \(x = 6.25\%\). This says the model is crude, not that a real grid behaves so: with unit 13's load damping the dip is about half, and real reserves are not a linear ramp — the regulation's minimum delivery would allow a deeper dip, 0.94 Hz at \(H = 5\) s in unit 13's model. The 0.75 Hz is a consistency check, not a bound. The model's lesson is the scaling, \(1/(1 - x)\).

5. A grid-forming inverter is a voltage behind a reactance with its own angle, so its power responds to the grid's angle at once, through the reactance, before any measurement — and its angle then moves by a programmed swing equation with chosen inertia and damping. A grid-following one injects a set current and responds only through measurements, if at all.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm{s\cdot W/Hz} = \mathrm W/(\mathrm{Hz/s})\) ✓. At \(x = 0\) part 3 returns unit 13's 0.050 Hz/s and 0.75 Hz ✓.
