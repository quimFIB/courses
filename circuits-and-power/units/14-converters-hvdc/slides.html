14 · the switch

Wasting seven twelfths

The obvious way to make 5 V from 12 V: drop the other 7 V across a series resistor. For the charger's 2 A, $R_s = 7/2 = 3.5\ \Omega$.

any series dropper

If the load current $I_o$ flows through an element with $V_{\text{in}} - V_o$ across it, that element absorbs $(V_{\text{in}} - V_o)I_o$, and the efficiency is at most $V_o/V_{\text{in}}$.

Proof. $p = vi$ for the element (unit 01); it delivers nothing, so all of it is heat. $\eta = V_oI_o/(V_{\text{in}}I_o)$. $\square$

in numbers

Wasted $7 \times 2 = \mathbf{14\ W}$ for $5\times2 = 10$ W delivered: $\eta = 5/12 = 41.7\%$, so 7/12 = 58% of the input is heat. In a thumb-sized box, 14 W is a hot box.

The divider that works, and what it costs. The phone is modelled, for this slide only, as the resistance that draws 2 A at 5 V.

Energy check. The source delivers $12\times2 = 24$ W; $14 + 10 = 24$ ✓. Its $p = vi$ is $-24$ W under the passive convention, since it delivers.

Why the theorem is general. It does not matter whether the dropper is a fixed resistor or something cleverer — a resistance that adjusts itself to hold $V_o$ at 5 V as the load changes, which is what a "linear regulator" does. Anything in series that carries the load current with $V_{\text{in}} - V_o$ across it turns $(V_{\text{in}} - V_o)I_o$ into heat. The only way out is to stop carrying current and holding voltage at the same time.

A second defect. A plain divider holds 5 V only at the load it was designed for: draw 1 A instead and the phone sees $12 - 3.5 = 8.5$ V. That is a regulation problem, not an efficiency one, and the converter of section 2 has a version of it (the duty cycle must be adjusted as the input changes); this deck does not treat regulation.

The model of the phone. A phone being charged is not a resistor; it draws whatever its own charging circuit asks. The 2.5 Ω is the resistance that draws the same current at the same voltage, and that is all this slide needs from it.

14 · the switch

The ideal switch: either $v = 0$ or $i = 0$

the ideal switch

A two-terminal element with two states, chosen from outside at any instant: on, $v = 0$ whatever $i$; off, $i = 0$ whatever $v$.

it converts without loss

At every instant $p = vi = 0$. Proof: one factor is zero in each state. $\square$

Unit 03's ideal diode is the same pair of half-axes with the choice made by the circuit: on when its current would be positive, off when its voltage would be negative. It too absorbs nothing.

A resistor's law is a line through the origin, and every point of it except the origin has $p > 0$. A switch lives on the axes, where $p = 0$.

The switch's law in the $(v, i)$ plane, passive marking: the two axes. The shaded quadrants are where a passive element dissipates; the switch never enters them.

What "chosen from outside" means. The switch is a time-varying element: its law at time $t$ is "$v = 0$" or "$i = 0$" according to a control signal. Unit 03's Tellegen theorem needs no assumption about elements, so it applies: in a network of sources, ideal switches, diodes, inductors, capacitors and a load, power is conserved at every instant, and the switches and diodes take none of it.

What a real switch is. In a converter the controlled switch is a transistor driven fully on or fully off. Its law is not exactly an axis — on, it has a small voltage; off, a small leakage current — and it takes time to move between states. The conduction loss ($I^2R_{\text{on}}$, say) is a resistor again and is not modelled here (problem P1 puts a number on it); the transition is the slide Switching loss. Erickson and Maksimović, ch. 4, "Switch Realization", for real switches.

Why the diode matters. An inductor's current cannot jump (unit 05: the state is the inductor current). A switch that opens in series with an inductor demands $L\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt\to\infty$ — unit 05's ignition coil did exactly that, on purpose, to make a spark. A converter must give the inductor's current another path at every instant the switch is off. The diode is that path, and it chooses itself.

Restriction to two quadrants. Real switches and diodes carry current one way and block voltage one way, so each occupies only half of each axis. The ideal switch drawn here is the idealisation of a switch placed so that its current and voltage keep one sign; the converters below are drawn that way.

Chop, then filter

Connect the 12 V to a node for a fraction $D$ of each period $T$, and short the node to 0 V for the rest. The node's voltage $v_s(t)$ is a rectangular wave, and its average is

$$\langle v_s\rangle = \frac1T\int_0^Tv_s\,\mathrm dt = DV_{\text{in}} .$$

In MR 19's language that average is the constant term of the Fourier series of $v_s$; every other term oscillates at $f = 1/T$ or a multiple of it.

the plan

Make $D = 5/12$, then pass $v_s$ through a filter that keeps the constant term and rejects everything at $f$ and above. Nothing in the path need dissipate: switches, and unit 02's $L$ and $C$, which store energy and return it.

The fraction $D$ is the duty cycle; $f$ the switching frequency.

The charger's switch node at $D = 5/12$ and $f = 500$ kHz. The dashed line is its average, the only part the phone should see.

The average, as a Fourier coefficient. MR 19's series for a $T$-periodic function has constant term $a_0 = \frac1T\int_0^Tv_s\,\mathrm dt$ in the convention $v_s = a_0 + \sum_{n\ge1}(a_n\cos n\omega t + b_n\sin n\omega t)$, $\omega = 2\pi/T$. For $v_s = V_{\text{in}}$ on $[0, DT)$ and $0$ on $[DT, T)$, $a_0 = DV_{\text{in}}$. The harmonics follow from the same integrals: $|c_n| = (2V_{\text{in}}/n\pi)|\sin n\pi D|$ for the amplitude at $nf$, so at $D = 5/12$ the first is $(24/\pi)\sin(75°) = 7.38$ V at 500 kHz. The filter of section 2 must cut that to millivolts.

Why a filter can be lossless. A network of ideal $L$ and $C$ has an impedance that is purely imaginary on the $i\omega$ axis (unit 05, Foster's reactance theorem), so by unit 06 it absorbs no mean power. Whatever it rejects is not burnt; it is stored for part of a cycle and returned. That is the whole difference from the divider.

How the node is shorted. In the buck converter of section 2, by a diode that conducts whenever the switch is off, because the inductor's current must flow somewhere. Section 2 makes this precise.

Switching loss: $\tfrac12 VIt_c$ per transition

A real switch takes time to change state. Model a turn-on with the load current $I$ held by an inductor and the switch voltage held at $V$ by a diode, as in section 2: first the current moves into the switch, at full voltage; then the voltage falls, at full current. Each linear, over $t_c$ in all.

switching loss $$E_{\text{sw}} = \tfrac12VIt_c\ \text{per transition},$$ $$P_{\text{sw}} = VIt_cf\ \text{for two per period.}$$

Proof: $p = vi$ rises linearly to $VI$ and falls linearly to $0$: a triangle of height $VI$ and base $t_c$. $\square$

Units: $\mathrm{V\,A\,s} = \mathrm J$; $\mathrm{J\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm W$ ✓.

At 12 V, 2 A, $t_c = 20$ ns, 500 kHz (all assumed): $12\times2\times20\times10^{-9}\times5\times10^5 = \mathbf{0.24\ W}$. Proportional to $f$: that is what bounds the switching frequency.

A turn-on in the linear model. The shaded triangle is the energy lost, $\tfrac12 VIt_c$; a turn-off is the same picture reversed.

Full proof. Let the current rise take $t_r$ and the voltage fall $t_f$, $t_c = t_r + t_f$. During the rise $v = V$ and $i = It/t_r$, so the energy is $\int_0^{t_r}VIt/t_r\,\mathrm dt = \tfrac12VIt_r$. During the fall $i = I$ and $v = V(1 - t/t_f)$, so the energy is $\tfrac12VIt_f$. The sum is $\tfrac12VIt_c$, whatever the split. A turn-off is the reverse sequence — voltage rises at full current, then current falls at full voltage — and costs the same. Two transitions per period at frequency $f$ give $P_{\text{sw}} = 2\times\tfrac12VIt_c\times f = VIt_cf$.

Why the voltage stays at $V$ while the current rises. Before turn-on the inductor's current $I$ flows in the diode, which holds the switch node at 0 V, so the switch has the full $V$ across it. As the switch takes current, the diode's share falls, but while the diode still carries any current it still conducts and the node stays put. Only once the switch carries all of $I$ can the diode turn off and the switch voltage fall. That is why the loss is a full triangle, not a smaller one, and why it is called a "hard" transition.

Energy check and efficiency. At 0.24 W for 10 W delivered, switching loss alone takes the efficiency to $10/10.24 = 97.7\%$. The currents and times are assumptions for illustration; the switch's own conduction loss and the inductor's resistance are not modelled. The current actually switched is the inductor current at the instant of switching, $I_o \pm \Delta I/2$ (section 2); using $I_o$ is the first approximation.

Further. Erickson and Maksimović, ch. 4, "Switch Realization", for real switches. The linear model here is a model: it says nothing about how a real device makes its transition, and its 20 ns is an assumption.

Why push the frequency up: the transformer shrinks as 1/f

A mains adapter must also step 230 V down, so it needs unit 07's transformer. Unit 07: each turn sees $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dt$. With $\Phi = \hat BA\cos\omega t$ in a core of area $A$,

$$V = \frac{\omega N\hat BA}{\sqrt2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = \frac{\sqrt2\,V}{2\pi fN\hat B}.$$

Units: $\mathrm{V}/(\mathrm{s^{-1}\,T}) = \mathrm{V\,s\,m^2/Wb} = \mathrm{m^2}$ ✓ ($\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{Wb/m^2}$, $\mathrm{Wb} = \mathrm{V\,s}$).

$\hat B$ is capped by saturation (unit 07). At fixed $V$, $N$ and $\hat B$, the core's area falls as $1/f$: 100 kHz against 50 Hz is a factor of 2000 (the frequency is assumed).

So the adapter rectifies the mains to DC, chops it at a high frequency, transforms the chopped wave in a small core, and rectifies and filters again.

The adapter as blocks (the circuit inside each is not this deck's subject), and the two core cross-sections drawn to scale in area: side ratio $\sqrt{2000} = 44.7$.

Unit 07's formula, in the form used. Unit 07 wrote $v_1 = N_1\,\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dt$ for a winding of $N_1$ turns round a core carrying flux $\Phi$. With $\Phi(t) = \hat BA\cos\omega t$ — the flux density uniform over the core's cross-section $A$ — the voltage is $v = -\omega N\hat BA\sin\omega t$, of peak $\omega N\hat BA$ and RMS $\omega N\hat BA/\sqrt2$. Solving for $A$ gives the box. The peak flux density is limited by the core's saturation (unit 07, Iron, in one slide), so it cannot be raised to make up for a lower frequency.

The chopped wave is not a sinusoid. If a square wave of amplitude $\hat V$ is applied, the flux ramps linearly for half a period: $N\cdot2\hat BA = \hat V\cdot T/2$, so $A = \hat V/(4fN\hat B)$. The constant differs from the sinusoidal $\sqrt2/2\pi$ (per RMS volt), the dependence $A\propto1/f$ does not. That dependence is the whole argument; the factor 2000 is $10^5/50$.

What the factor is not. Area is not size: the windings, the insulation between them and the need to shed heat do not shrink as $1/f$, and the switching loss of the previous slide grows as $f$. Unit 07's eddy-current loss per unit volume, $\sigma\omega^2\hat B^2d^2/24$, grows as $f^2$ at fixed $\hat B$, so a core laminated for 50 Hz would not do at 100 kHz; the core material changes as well as its size. These are qualitative statements; how real adapters balance them is a design question this deck leaves alone, and it does not name any particular adapter's circuit.

Why the first rectifier. The 50 Hz mains cannot be fed to a 100 kHz transformer directly; it is first turned into DC, about the mains' peak, $230\sqrt2 = 325$ V, held up by a capacitor. Section 4 shows what that rectifier does to the current the adapter draws.

14 · buck

A switch, a diode, and unit 05's LC pair

the buck converter

A controlled switch from $V_{\text{in}}$ to a node $s$, on for $DT$ of each period $T = 1/f$; a diode from ground to $s$; an inductor $L$ from $s$ to the output; a capacitor $C$ across the load.

Switch on ($0 \le t < DT$): $v_s = V_{\text{in}}$, and the diode, reverse-biased, blocks.

Switch off ($DT \le t < T$): the inductor's current cannot stop (unit 05), so it drives the diode on, its freewheeling diode: $v_s = 0$.

$$\text{on: } L\frac{\mathrm di_L}{\mathrm dt} = V_{\text{in}} - v_o, \qquad \text{off: } L\frac{\mathrm di_L}{\mathrm dt} = -v_o .$$

Both are $v_L = v_s - v_o$, by the voltage law round the loop through $L$ and $C$.

The buck converter. The switch and diode make the node $s$ either $V_{\text{in}}$ or 0; the $LC$ pair is section 1's filter. Every element is marked by the passive convention.

Why the diode turns itself on. Unit 03's ideal diode conducts with $v = 0$ when its current would be positive. At the instant the switch opens, the inductor carries $i_L > 0$ to the right. The only path left for it is up through the diode from ground into $s$, so the diode's current is $i_L > 0$: it conducts, and $v_s = 0$. While the switch is on, $v_s = V_{\text{in}}$ puts the diode's cathode above its anode, $v = -V_{\text{in}} < 0$: it blocks. Neither element absorbs power in either state.

The loop equation. Going round from ground through the diode (or the switch and source) to $s$, through $L$ to the output and down through $C$: $v_s = v_L + v_o$. With $v_L = L\,\mathrm di_L/\mathrm dt$ (unit 02), the two states give the box. The output node obeys the current law: $i_L = C\,\mathrm dv_o/\mathrm dt + v_o/R$.

A proviso. The off-state equation needs $i_L > 0$ throughout the off-interval, so that the diode keeps conducting. That is continuous conduction, checked for the charger on Continuous conduction, and two relatives. If $i_L$ reaches zero the diode stops and a third state appears.

Symbols. The diode is the IEC triangle and bar; current flows in the direction the triangle points. The switch is drawn as a plain lever, standing for a transistor driven on and off; its control signal is not drawn.

Volt-second balance: $V_o = DV_{\text{in}}$

periodic steady state

Every state variable — here $i_L$ and $v_o$ — returns to its value after one period: $i_L(t + T) = i_L(t)$, $v_o(t + T) = v_o(t)$.

theorem · the averaged output

In periodic steady state with continuous conduction, $\langle v_o\rangle = DV_{\text{in}}$.

proof · volt-second balance

$\int_0^Tv_L\,\mathrm dt = L\,[i_L(T) - i_L(0)] = 0$. And $v_L = v_s - v_o$, so $\langle v_o\rangle = \langle v_s\rangle = DV_{\text{in}}$. $\square$

With the small-ripple approximation $v_o \approx V_o$ within a period, $v_L$ is $V_{\text{in}} - V_o$ then $-V_o$, and the balance reads $D(V_{\text{in}} - V_o) - (1 - D)V_o = 0$: the same answer, and the two shaded areas are equal.

The charger in periodic steady state. Equal areas above and below in $v_L$ are the same statement as $i_L$ returning to where it started.

Full proof

Integrate the inductor's law $v_L = L\,\mathrm di_L/\mathrm dt$ over one period: $\int_0^Tv_L\,\mathrm dt = L\,(i_L(T) - i_L(0))$, which vanishes in periodic steady state. Divide by $T$: $\langle v_L\rangle = 0$. The loop equation $v_s = v_L + v_o$ holds at every instant, in both states, so averaging it gives $\langle v_o\rangle = \langle v_s\rangle$. With continuous conduction, $v_s = V_{\text{in}}$ for $DT$ and $0$ for $(1 - D)T$, so $\langle v_s\rangle = DV_{\text{in}}$. $\square$

Notice what was not used. Neither $C$ nor $R$ nor the small-ripple approximation entered: the average is exact, given an ideal switch and diode and continuous conduction. The small-ripple approximation is what makes $v_L$ piecewise constant and $i_L$ piecewise linear — the figure — and it is needed for the ripple, next.

The charge-balance twin. The same argument on the capacitor: $\int_0^Ti_C\,\mathrm dt = C\,[v_o(T) - v_o(0)] = 0$, so $\langle i_C\rangle = 0$ — capacitor charge balance. The output node's current law $i_L = i_C + v_o/R$ then gives $\langle i_L\rangle = \langle v_o\rangle/R = I_o$: the inductor's mean current is the load current. Erickson and Maksimović, ch. 2, "Principles of Steady State Converter Analysis", develops this steady-state method, small-ripple approximation included, for converters in general.

Why the steady state is reached. Averaged over a period, the converter is a source $DV_{\text{in}}$ driving unit 05's series $L$ into a parallel $RC$: a second-order linear circuit with positive $R$, whose natural responses decay (unit 05, poles in the left half-plane). That the switched circuit's own transients decay too is assumed here, not proved; it is the behaviour of the averaged model, and of the real converter.

Units and limits. $DV_{\text{in}}$ is a volt ✓. $D = 1$: switch always on, $V_o = V_{\text{in}}$ ✓. $D = 0$: always off, $V_o = 0$ ✓.

Worked: the charger's duty cycle, and where the power goes

worked example 1 · averaged output and the energy check

Duty cycle. $D = V_o/V_{\text{in}} = 5/12 = \mathbf{0.4167}$: on for $0.833\ \mu$s of every $2\ \mu$s.

Input current. The source supplies current only while the switch is on, and then it supplies $i_L$. Over the on-interval $i_L$ rises linearly through its mean $I_o$, so

$$\langle i_{\text{in}}\rangle = \frac1T\int_0^{DT}i_L\,\mathrm dt = DI_o = 0.833\ \mathrm A.$$

Energy. $V_{\text{in}}\langle i_{\text{in}}\rangle = 12\times0.833 = 10.0$ W $= V_oI_o$ ✓. Nothing is lost.

Read it as a two-port. $V_o = DV_{\text{in}}$ and $\langle i_{\text{in}}\rangle = DI_o$: unit 07's ideal transformer with ratio $1 : D$ — but working at DC, which no transformer can.

The input current is the inductor current, gated by the switch. Scale 60 px per ampere from zero.

The integral, written out. On $[0, DT]$, with the small-ripple approximation, $i_L(t) = I_o - \Delta I/2 + \Delta I\,t/(DT)$. Its integral over the interval is $DT\,(I_o - \Delta I/2) + \Delta I\,DT/2 = DT\,I_o$. Dividing by $T$: $DI_o$. Put another way: $i_L$ rises linearly from its minimum to its maximum while the switch is on and falls linearly back while it is off, so its mean over either interval is the midpoint of the two, which is its mean over the whole period, $\langle i_L\rangle = I_o$ by charge balance. For the charger: $0.41667\times2 = 0.8333$ A; $12\times0.8333 = 10.000$ W.

Energy, as Tellegen says it must. With ideal switch and diode the network's only dissipating element is the load, and the stores $L$ and $C$ return to their energies each period. So the mean power in equals the mean power out — exactly, not approximately. The calculation above is that statement checked.

The transformer reading. Unit 07's ideal transformer has $v_2 = v_1/n$ and $i_2 = -ni_1$ (in its convention), and passes power unchanged. Here voltages scale by $D$ and mean currents by $1/D$ in the opposite direction. Unit 07's transformer works only for changing flux: at DC, $v = N\,\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm dt = 0$. The buck converter is a "DC transformer" that steps down only, with a ratio set by timing rather than turns.

What the source sees. Pulses of about 2 A at 500 kHz. In a car that is drawn from the 12 V wiring; a capacitor at the converter's input would supply the pulses locally, another $LC$ problem not treated here.

Ripple: the inductor's triangle and the capacitor's charge

Inductor. During the on-interval the slope is $(V_{\text{in}} - V_o)/L$, for a time $DT$:

$$\Delta I = \frac{(V_{\text{in}} - V_o)DT}{L}\quad\text{peak to peak.}$$

Units: $\mathrm{V\,s/H} = \mathrm{V\,s}/(\mathrm{V\,s\,A^{-1}}) = \mathrm A$ ✓.

Capacitor. The load takes $I_o$; the capacitor takes the rest, $i_C = i_L - I_o$, a triangle of peak $\Delta I/2$. It is positive for half a period, and in that half it delivers the charge $q = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac T2\cdot\tfrac{\Delta I}2$. So $v_o$ rises by

output ripple $$\Delta V = \frac qC = \frac{\Delta I}{8fC}$$

Units: $\mathrm A/(\mathrm{s^{-1}\,F}) = \mathrm{A\,s/F} = \mathrm{C/F} = \mathrm V$ ✓.

The capacitor current and the output ripple it makes. The shaded triangle is the charge $q$; the voltage rises exactly while $i_C > 0$, so its peak-to-peak swing is $q/C$.

Full proof of the box. In the small-ripple approximation $i_L$ is a triangle of peak-to-peak $\Delta I$ about its mean $I_o$, so $i_C = i_L - I_o$ is a triangle about zero with peaks $\pm\Delta I/2$. It crosses zero halfway up each rise and halfway down each fall, and between these crossings — half of the rising interval plus half of the falling one, $DT/2 + (1 - D)T/2 = T/2$ — it is positive, a triangle of base $T/2$ and height $\Delta I/2$, area $T\Delta I/8$. The capacitor voltage rises over exactly that interval (since $C\,\mathrm dv_o/\mathrm dt = i_C$), from its minimum to its maximum, so the peak-to-peak ripple is $q/C = \Delta I/(8fC)$. $\square$

An assumption hidden in "the load takes $I_o$". The load current is $v_o/R$, which ripples by $\Delta V/R$ too. For the charger that is $6.6\ \mathrm{mV}/2.5\ \Omega = 2.7$ mA against $\Delta I = 583$ mA: neglected, consistently with the small-ripple approximation. The same statement in impedances: at 500 kHz the capacitor's $1/\omega C = 1/(2\pi\times5\times10^5\times22\times10^{-6}) = 14.5$ mΩ, against $R = 2.5\ \Omega$, so the ripple current goes into $C$.

The off-interval gives the same $\Delta I$. The fall is $V_o(1 - D)T/L$. Equal to the rise exactly when $V_o = DV_{\text{in}}$ — volt-second balance again, as a check on the arithmetic.

Why the LC pair passes the average and rejects the switching

From the switch node to the output, $L$ in series and $C \parallel R$ across: unit 05's impedances give

$$H(i\omega) = \frac{v_o}{v_s} = \frac{1}{1 - \omega^2LC + i\omega L/R}.$$

At DC, $H = 1$: the average passes untouched. Far above $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, $|H| \approx (\omega_0/\omega)^2$: a second-order low-pass, falling a hundredfold per decade.

the charger at 500 kHz

Full proof of $H$

The capacitor and load in parallel have impedance $Z_p = R/(1 + i\omega RC)$ (unit 05: admittances add). The inductor's $i\omega L$ is in series, so the divider gives $H = Z_p/(i\omega L + Z_p) = R/(R + i\omega L(1 + i\omega RC)) = 1/(1 + i\omega L/R - \omega^2LC)$. Units: $\omega L/R$ and $\omega^2LC$ are dimensionless ✓. Limits: $\omega\to0$ gives 1 (an inductor is a short and a capacitor an open circuit at DC, unit 05); $R\to\infty$ gives the unloaded $1/(1 - \omega^2/\omega_0^2)$, unit 05's lossless resonance.

Why it applies to a switched circuit. The switch and diode make $v_s(t)$, a definite periodic waveform, whatever the rest of the circuit does — in continuous conduction. From $s$ onwards the circuit is linear and time-invariant, so each Fourier component of $v_s$ (MR 19) passes through $H$ at its own frequency, by unit 05's phasor method applied term by term. The DC term $DV_{\text{in}}$ comes out as $DV_{\text{in}}$, which is the averaged-output theorem again, now from the other side.

Continuous conduction, and two relatives

continuous conduction

$i_L > 0$ throughout the period, so the diode conducts for the whole off-interval. It holds while $I_o - \Delta I/2 > 0$.

$\Delta I$ does not depend on the load, so for the charger continuous conduction holds down to $I_o = \Delta I/2 = \mathbf{0.29\ A}$. Below that the current reaches zero, the diode stops, and a third state — both off — changes the analysis (Erickson and Maksimović, ch. 5, "The Discontinuous Conduction Mode").

The same method, other circuits. Rearranging the same three elements gives the boost converter, $V_o = V_{\text{in}}/(1 - D)$, which steps up, and the buck–boost converter, $V_o = -DV_{\text{in}}/(1 - D)$. Both by volt-second balance: problem P4.

Two loads with the same $\Delta I$ (scale 61.7 px per ampere from zero), and a lighter one, dashed, in which the current rests at zero.

The boundary. The minimum of $i_L$ is $I_o - \Delta I/2$ in the small-ripple picture, so continuous conduction needs $I_o > \Delta I/2 = (V_{\text{in}} - V_o)DT/(2L)$. For the charger, $0.5833/2 = 0.2917$ A: a phone drawing less than about 0.29 A at 5 V (1.46 W) puts this model into discontinuous conduction. Either a larger $L$ or a higher $f$ lowers the boundary.

Why "$\Delta I$ does not depend on the load". The slopes are set by $V_{\text{in}} - V_o$ and $V_o$ across $L$, and those voltages are fixed by $D$; $I_o$ only shifts the triangle up or down. That is a consequence of continuous conduction and the small-ripple approximation; it fails exactly when the triangle hits zero.

Discontinuous conduction, named only. In the third state both switch and diode are off, $i_L = 0$ and $v_L = 0$, so $v_s = v_o$. Volt-second balance still holds but over three intervals whose lengths depend on the load, and $V_o$ is no longer $DV_{\text{in}}$ at fixed $D$. Erickson and Maksimović give it a chapter; this course does not need it.

The relatives. The boost puts $L$ at the input, the switch across to ground after it and the diode to the output; the buck–boost puts the switch at the input, $L$ to ground and the diode reversed to a negative output. Their circuits are described in P4, with the derivations; Erickson and Maksimović, ch. 6, "Converter Circuits", for the family. Both ratios exceed 1 for $D$ near 1, so they step up; the price is a ripple current and stresses this deck does not compute.

The dashed trace. Drawn with the on-time cut to a quarter of the period and the output held at 5 V, so the current rises at $7/L$ and falls at $5/L$, taking 7/5 as long to fall as to rise, and then rests at zero. It shows the shape of the third state; the load that goes with it is not computed. (Kept at the original on-time and started from zero, the triangle would end exactly at the end of the period: that is the boundary case again.)

14 · PWM

The half-bridge: a leg that switches between two rails

the half-bridge

Two switches in series across a DC source $V_{\text{dc}}$, split by two equal capacitors at a midpoint O. Exactly one switch is on at a time, so the leg's midpoint A sits at $+V_{\text{dc}}/2$ or $-V_{\text{dc}}/2$ relative to O.

If the upper switch is on for a fraction $d$ of a short period, the local average of $v_{AO}$ is

$$\langle v_{AO}\rangle = d\,\tfrac{V_{\text{dc}}}2 - (1 - d)\tfrac{V_{\text{dc}}}2 = (2d - 1)\tfrac{V_{\text{dc}}}2 .$$
pulse-width modulation (PWM)

Vary $d$ slowly: $d(t) = \tfrac12(1 + m\sin\omega t)$ makes the local average $m\tfrac{V_{\text{dc}}}2\sin\omega t$ — a sinusoid, from DC. $m \in [0, 1]$ is the modulation index.

A circuit that makes AC from DC this way is an inverter.

One leg. Drawn with the upper switch on: A is at $+V_{\text{dc}}/2$ relative to O. The capacitors hold O halfway between the rails.

Why "exactly one". Both on would short the source through the two switches; both off would leave a load current with nowhere to go, the inductor problem of section 2. Each switch here must carry current in either direction when on, because an AC load's current reverses: in practice a transistor with a diode across it. The ideal switch of section 1 does that by definition.

The local average. Over a short interval $\tau$ in which the upper switch is on for $d\tau$, $\frac1\tau\int v_{AO} = d(V_{\text{dc}}/2) + (1 - d)(-V_{\text{dc}}/2)$. "Slowly" means $d$ hardly changes over one switching period: the switching frequency is far above $\omega/2\pi$. The next slides measure exactly what is left over when it is not infinitely far.

Power, both ways. When the load absorbs power the DC source delivers it; when the load returns energy (an inductive load in part of each cycle, unit 06) it flows back into the capacitors and the source. The same leg is a rectifier when power flows from AC to DC. That is what lets one converter station send power either way.

The midpoint. Holding O exactly at half the DC voltage takes the capacitors' charge balance over a cycle, as in section 2. Here it is assumed.

One pulse per half-cycle: the quasi-square wave

The crudest inverter: $+V$ for most of each half-cycle, with a notch of half-width $\alpha$ at each zero crossing. This quasi-square wave is odd and half-wave antisymmetric, so (MR 19) only sine terms of odd order survive:

its Fourier coefficients $$b_n = \frac{4V}{n\pi}\cos n\alpha\quad (n\ \text{odd});$$

$b_n = 0$ for even $n$.

Units: $V$ times a number ✓. Limit $\alpha\to0$: the square wave, $4V/n\pi$ ✓; $\alpha\to\pi/2$: no pulse, $0$ ✓.

Proof. $b_n = \frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f\sin nx\,\mathrm dx = \frac{2V}{\pi}\int_\alpha^{\pi-\alpha}\sin nx\,\mathrm dx = \frac{2V}{n\pi}\big(\cos n\alpha - \cos n(\pi - \alpha)\big)$, and $\cos n(\pi - \alpha) = -\cos n\alpha$ for odd $n$. $\square$

One free number, $\alpha$, and a cosine in every coefficient: a notch can be placed to kill one harmonic.

The quasi-square wave at $\alpha = 30°$ (solid) and its fundamental (dashed), $b_1 = (4/\pi)\cos30° = 1.103\,V$.

Full proof

Take the period $2\pi$ in $x = \omega t$. The waveform is $f = V$ on $(\alpha, \pi - \alpha)$, $-V$ on $(\pi + \alpha, 2\pi - \alpha)$, and $0$ elsewhere. It is odd, $f(-x) = -f(x)$, so every $a_n$ vanishes (MR 19, Sine, cosine, complex: one system per boundary condition). It also satisfies $f(x + \pi) = -f(x)$; then $b_n = \frac1\pi\int_0^{2\pi}f\sin nx = \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi f\sin nx\,(1 - (-1)^n)$, which is zero for even $n$ and $\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi f\sin nx$ for odd $n$. The integral: $\int_\alpha^{\pi-\alpha}\sin nx\,\mathrm dx = [\cos n\alpha - \cos n(\pi - \alpha)]/n$, and for odd $n$, $\cos(n\pi - n\alpha) = \cos n\pi\cos n\alpha = -\cos n\alpha$. So $b_n = (2V/n\pi)\cdot2\cos n\alpha$. $\square$

Convergence. The waveform is piecewise constant with jumps, so the series converges to it at every point of continuity and to the mid-value at a jump (MR 19, Dirichlet), and in mean square by Parseval. The coefficients decay as $1/n$, as any function with a jump must (MR 19, smoothness against decay).

Why this waveform is "one pulse". Each half-cycle has a single pulse of width $\pi - 2\alpha$; the switches change state at the output frequency itself. A half-bridge has only two levels, $\pm V_{\text{dc}}/2$, so alone it makes the square wave ($\alpha = 0$) but not the notches; two legs with the load between their midpoints make the zero level by putting both midpoints on the same rail. Here it is the cleanest example of how timing shapes a spectrum.

Sine–triangle PWM: switching at the crossings

Compare a reference $m\sin\omega t$ with a triangular carrier sweeping $[-1, 1]$ at $m_f$ times the output frequency. The upper switch is on while the reference is above the carrier.

the local average follows the reference

If the reference is nearly constant, $r$, over one carrier period, the upper switch is on for the fraction $d = (1 + r)/2$ of it, so $\langle v_{AO}\rangle = r\,V_{\text{dc}}/2$.

Proof. The carrier rises linearly from $-1$ to $1$ and back; it is below $r$ for the fraction $(r + 1)/2$ of each ramp. Then the half-bridge's $(2d - 1) = r$. $\square$

The figure: $m = 0.8$, $m_f = 21$. The leg output is two-valued; its local average is the bold sinusoid.

Computed: the switching instants are the crossings of the two curves above. Where the reference is near $+0.8$ the output is high nine-tenths of the time.

The proof, with the time. On a rising ramp of duration $\tau/2$ the carrier is $-1 + 4t/\tau$, below $r$ for $t < (1 + r)\tau/4$: half of the ramp's $\tau/2$ times $(1 + r)$. The falling ramp is the mirror image. So the fraction is $(1 + r)/2$, as stated, and the average is $(2d - 1)V_{\text{dc}}/2 = rV_{\text{dc}}/2$. With $r = m\sin\omega t$, the local average is $m(V_{\text{dc}}/2)\sin\omega t$: the half-bridge's PWM of the first slide, now with the switching instants named.

How "nearly constant" is quantified. Over one carrier period the reference changes by at most $m\omega\tau = 2\pi m/m_f$, about 0.24 at $m = 0.8$, $m_f = 21$: not small. The approximation is not what makes the result true; the next slide's spectrum, computed exactly from the switched waveform, shows the fundamental is $m$ to the computation's precision regardless.

Why $m \le 1$. The reference must stay within the carrier's range, or some carrier periods have no crossing and the output sticks at one rail. Beyond $m = 1$ the local average can no longer follow the reference, since $d = (1 + r)/2$ would leave $[0, 1]$; not treated.

Why 21. Chosen for the figure: odd, so the waveform keeps the half-wave symmetry of the previous slides; a multiple of 3, which matters for three legs (Three legs: the three-phase inverter).

Three legs: the three-phase inverter

Three half-bridge legs on one DC source, driven by references $m\sin(\omega t - 2\pi k/3)$, $k = 0, 1, 2$: their averages are unit 11's balanced set.

triplen harmonics cancel line to line

If each leg's voltage is the same waveform delayed by a third of a period, $v_k(t) = g(t - kT/3)$, then harmonics of order $n = 3, 6, 9, \ldots$ are absent from every line-to-line voltage $v_j - v_k$.

Proof. The $n$th harmonic of $v_k$ is that of $g$ times $e^{-i2\pi nk/3}$, which is $1$ for $n$ a multiple of 3: identical in every leg, so it cancels in any difference. $\square$

With a common carrier and $m_f = 21$ — a multiple of 3 — the delay of $T/3$ is exactly seven carrier periods, so the hypothesis holds and even the 21st cancels between lines (computed: 0.000; the 19th and 23rd become 0.381 of $V_{\text{dc}}/2$, $\sqrt3$ times a leg's).

Three legs on one DC bus feeding a star-connected load. Drawn at one instant of the switching pattern; each leg has exactly one switch on.

Full proof

Write $g$'s Fourier series with complex coefficients, $g(t) = \sum_nc_ne^{in\omega t}$ (MR 19). Then $v_k(t) = g(t - kT/3) = \sum_nc_ne^{-i2\pi nk/3}e^{in\omega t}$, since $\omega T/3 = 2\pi/3$. For $n = 3j$ the factor is $e^{-i2\pi jk} = 1$, so the $n$th harmonic is $c_ne^{in\omega t}$ in every leg, and $v_j - v_k$ has none of it. For other $n$, $|1 - e^{-i2\pi n/3}| = \sqrt3$: line-to-line components are $\sqrt3$ times a leg's — unit 11's $\sqrt3$, harmonic by harmonic. $\square$

When the hypothesis holds. The references are delays of one another by $T/3$. The carrier must also be: with one carrier shared by all three legs, the carrier delayed by $T/3$ is itself only if $T/3$ is a whole number of carrier periods, i.e. $m_f$ is a multiple of 3. At $m_f = 21$ it is, and the computation agrees: line-to-line fundamental $1.3856 = \sqrt3\times0.8$; 3rd, 21st, 39th, 45th, 63rd all 0.0000; 19th and 23rd 0.3808; 41st and 43rd 0.5445.

The star point. Into a balanced star load (unit 11), the triplen components, identical in all three legs, raise and lower the load's star point $n$ together with the legs and drive no current, since nothing returns to O. That is unit 11's zero sequence: harmonics of order $3j$ form a zero-sequence set. Had the star point been joined to O by a fourth wire, they would drive a current in it at three (or six, nine, …) times the output frequency — unit 11's "a current at three times the supply frequency in each phase is in step in all three, so it is zero sequence and returns through the neutral; unit 14 meets such currents", here.

INELFE's converters: a voltage that chooses its phase

A voltage-source converter makes, on its AC side, a voltage whose magnitude $\mathcal E$ and phase $\delta$ its controller chooses — PWM's reference, scaled up. Connected to a grid $V\angle0$ through a reactance $X$, it is unit 13's two-bus system, per phase:

$$P = \frac{\mathcal EV\sin\delta}{X},\qquad Q = \frac{\mathcal E^2 - \mathcal EV\cos\delta}{X}.$$

So the converter sets $P$ with $\delta$ and $Q$ with $\mathcal E$: it can send real power either way and make or absorb reactive power, independently, at each end.

INELFE, as REE describes it

Proof. $P = \langle vi\rangle$. Products of sinusoids of different frequencies average to zero (MR 19, orthogonality), so only $i$'s fundamental survives: unit 06's mean $VI_1\cos\varphi_1$. And $I^2 = \sum_nI_n^2$ by Parseval. $\square$

Full proof

Write $v = \sqrt2V\cos\omega t$ and $i = I_0 + \sum_{n\ge1}\sqrt2I_n\cos(n\omega t - \varphi_n)$ (MR 19's series in amplitude–phase form, with $I_n$ the RMS of the $n$th harmonic and $I_0$ the mean). A rectifier draws current in peaks

The mains adapter's first stage: four diodes in a bridge and a capacitor. The bridge connects the capacitor to the mains the right way up in either half-cycle, so $v_C$ charges towards the mains' peak, $230\sqrt2 = 325$ V.

When does current flow? A diode conducts only when it would carry forward current. Between peaks the rest of the adapter draws from $C$, and $v_C$ sags. The diodes stay off until $|v|$ climbs back above $v_C$ — near the next peak — and recharge $C$ in a short burst.

so

The current is a train of short pulses near the voltage maxima, alternating in sign: periodic, odd-symmetric, and far from a sinusoid. Unit 06's "the currents drawn by rectifiers" are these.

The bridge rectifier and its capacitor. The rest of the adapter — the switch and transformer of section 1 — is drawn as the resistor it looks like on average.

How the bridge works. In the half-cycle where the top of the source is positive, current can flow from it through the upper-right diode to the $+$ node, through $C$ and the load, and back from the $-$ node through the lower-left diode to the source's bottom. In the other half-cycle the other two diodes do the same, so the capacitor always charges with $+$ on the right. Each diode is unit 03's ideal diode. Whether a given pair conducts is settled by the inequality $|v| > v_C$, as the slide says, and by the direction it would carry current, which a pair can do only while $|v|$ is rising past $v_C$ or held at it.

Why "short". While the diodes conduct, $v_C$ follows $|v|$, and the current is $C\,\mathrm d|v|/\mathrm dt$ plus the load's. After the peak $|v|$ starts to fall; the moment it falls faster than the capacitor would discharge into the load on its own, the current would have to reverse, the diodes block, and the capacitor carries on alone. So conduction runs from the instant $|v|$ catches up with the sagging $v_C$ to just after the peak: a small fraction of each half-cycle when $C$ is large enough to keep the sag small.

What is derived and what is not. The shape — pulses near the peaks — follows from the diode law and the capacitor alone, as above. Its exact width depends on the mains' own impedance, which the next slide's model sets to zero; nothing is claimed here about any particular adapter.

Symbol. The circle with a tilde is an AC source. The small hop where the $-$ wire passes the source's lower lead is a crossing without a connection.

The rest of VI is not one reactive power

With a sinusoidal $V$, Parseval splits the square of the apparent power exactly:

$$(VI)^2 = P^2 + Q_1^2 + D_{\text h}^2,$$

with $P = VI_1\cos\varphi_1$, $Q_1 = VI_1\sin\varphi_1$ — unit 06's $Q$, for the fundamental alone — and $D_{\text h}^2 = V^2\sum_{n\ne1}I_n^2$ (h for harmonics; not the duty cycle). $D_{\text h}$ comes from current at frequencies where the voltage has nothing: it is not stored energy sloshing at 50 Hz, and the power triangle no longer closes.

unit 06's warning, made concrete

IEEE Std 1459-2010 sets out definitions for sinusoidal, nonsinusoidal, balanced and unbalanced conditions; this course keeps $Q$ for sinusoids and uses $P$, $VI$ and the factors above.

Full proof

Why "several inequivalent definitions". For sinusoids, $Q$ has three readings that agree: the amplitude of the oscillating part of $p(t)$ in quadrature with $v$, the energy sloshing in the load's fields times $2\omega$ (unit 06, What $Q$ is: stored energy, sloshing), and $\sqrt{(VI)^2 - P^2}$. Which split is "right" depends on what it is for — billing, compensation, measurement — and that is a matter of definition, not of physics.

The standard. IEEE Std 1459-2010, IEEE Standard Definitions for the Measurement of Electric Power Quantities Under Sinusoidal, Nonsinusoidal, Balanced, or Unbalanced Conditions; IEEE lists it as inactive-reserved since 2021, with a 2025 revision. Its title and status were checked; its specific definitions were not, so none is quoted here, and nothing in this deck depends on them.

What direct current removes, and what it costs

In the steady state, with $V$ and $I$ constant, each in one line:

  • No charging current: $i = C\,\mathrm dv/\mathrm dt = 0$. The whole rating carries power, at any length.
  • No reactive power: there is no phase; $p = VI$ at every instant.
  • No skin effect: at 50 Hz the current crowds towards a conductor's surface and its AC resistance exceeds its DC one (branch 10, optional); at DC it fills the conductor, and $R = \rho l/A$ exactly (unit 00).
  • Two conductors, not three.
  • Power set by the converters (section 3), not by a phase difference (unit 13).

The break-even, as a model

A model, with its assumptions in view. AC costs $a_{\text{AC}}$ per kilometre. DC costs a fixed $F$ for the two converter stations and $a_{\text{DC}} < a_{\text{AC}}$ per kilometre — two conductors against three, no compensation:

$$C_{\text{AC}} = a_{\text{AC}}l,\qquad C_{\text{DC}} = F + a_{\text{DC}}l,$$ $$\text{equal at}\quad l^* = \frac{F}{a_{\text{AC}} - a_{\text{DC}}}.$$

Units: cost over cost per km = km ✓.

If conductors were the whole per-km cost, $a_{\text{AC}} = \tfrac32a_{\text{DC}}$ and $l^* = 2F/a_{\text{DC}}$: DC wins once its line costs twice its converters.

cables against lines

For cables, $a_{\text{AC}}$ also carries compensation, and the critical length caps AC outright (99 km here, 199 km with reactors at both ends): the break-even is short. For overhead lines charging costs little (a fourteenth), so it is long.

The model with one assumed set of numbers ($F = 100$ units, $a_{\text{DC}} = 1$ unit/km, $a_{\text{AC}} = 1.5$): $l^* = 200$ km. The numbers are illustrative only.

What is derived and what is not. The crossover formula is algebra. The ordering — break-even much shorter for cables than for overhead lines — follows from the critical-length numbers: a cable's $C'$ is fourteen times a line's, so its charging grows fourteen times as fast and it hits its rating at a hundred kilometres rather than at a length where the lumped model has failed. Real break-even distances depend on costs of converters, cables, towers, land and losses, which vary by project and time. Published break-even figures exist in the HVDC literature; none was checked here, so none is quoted.

Why $\tfrac32$. Three conductors against two at the same current per conductor. That is a deliberate simplification: it ignores the insulation, the towers, the trench, the compensation and the land, any of which may dominate.

The losses belong in it too. A full model adds the capitalised cost of losses per kilometre to each $a$, and the converters' losses to $F$. The DC line's lower loss per unit power (previous slide) lowers $a_{\text{DC}}$ further; the converters' loss raises $F$. The form of $l^*$ survives.

Unit 07's "beyond some length direct current wins (unit 14)". This is that length, as a formula, with the reason it is short under the sea or the ground and long through the air.

14 · inertia

What the rotor gave for free

Unit 13's swing equation, per unit on a machine's rating, and summed over a synchronous area of rating $S$:

$$\frac{2H}{\omega_s}\ddot\delta = P_m - P_e,$$ $$\frac{2HS}{f_0}\frac{\mathrm d\Delta f}{\mathrm dt} = \Delta P_m - \Delta P_e .$$

Units: $\mathrm{s\cdot W/Hz\cdot Hz/s} = \mathrm W$ ✓ ($H$ in seconds: stored kinetic energy over rating).

When generation is lost, $\Delta P_e - \Delta P_m > 0$ at once, and the rotors slow: their kinetic energy flows into the grid at the instant of the loss, with no measurement and no controller. That is what sets unit 13's initial rate, $f_0\Delta P/(2HS) = 0.050$ Hz/s for the reference incident.

an inverter has no rotor

Its output is whatever its controller sets. Unless told otherwise, it contributes nothing to $H$.

Unit 13's swing equation drawn as a loop. A grid-following inverter removes the shaded block from its share of the grid; a grid-forming one puts a programmed copy back.

From unit 13. Unit 13 (Newton for the rotor: the swing equation) derives the swing equation from Newton's law for rotation, $J\dot\omega_m = T_m - T_e$, multiplied by $\omega_m$ and written with $H$, the kinetic energy at synchronous speed divided by the rating. The aggregate form: with all machines in step, $\dot\delta = 2\pi\Delta f$ for each, and summing $(2H_kS_k/\omega_s)\cdot2\pi\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt$ over machines gives $(2HS/f_0)\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt$ with $HS = \sum H_kS_k$ — unit 13's Every machine in step: one swing equation for a grid, whose right side, $-\Delta P + R(t) - D\,\Delta f$, is this slide's $\Delta P_m - \Delta P_e$ written out. The numbers are unit 13's assumptions: $S = 300$ GW, $H = 5$ s (1500 GJ stored), and the reference incident of 3000 MW from Commission Regulation (EU) 2017/1485, Art. 153.

Why "for free". The kinetic energy is released by physics: a synchronous machine's electrical power depends on its rotor angle relative to the grid's, and when the grid's angle starts to fall behind, every machine's $P_e$ rises in the same instant (unit 13's $P = \mathcal EV\sin\delta/X$). Only after that do governors and reserves act, over seconds.

What changes with inverters. A converter connected through its reactance has no rotor angle forced on it by physics; it has a controlled one. Section 3's last slide: the converter chooses $\mathcal E$ and $\delta$. Whether it behaves like a machine is a property of its control law, and Grid-following, and synthetic inertia and Grid-forming: a voltage with an angle of its own are the two families of law.

The running example. A solar farm reaches the grid only through inverters; so does an HVDC link like INELFE at each end. From either grid's side, a DC link is a converter whose power is set by control. How INELFE's converters are controlled is not stated in the sources used here and is not claimed.

14 · inertia

Grid-following, and synthetic inertia

grid-following control

The inverter measures the grid voltage's phase with a phase-locked loop and injects a set current in step with it: a current source that follows. Its power is $P_{\text{set}}$, whatever the frequency does.

To help, its controller can add a term proportional to the measured rate of change of frequency: synthetic inertia,

$$P = P_{\text{set}} - K\frac{\mathrm df}{\mathrm dt},\qquad K = \frac{2HS}{f_0}$$

mimics a machine of inertia $H$ on rating $S$. For $H = 5$ s, $S = 1$ GW: $K = 2\times5\times10^9/50 = \mathbf{200\ MW\ per\ Hz/s}$. At the reference incident's 0.050 Hz/s, that is 10 MW.

Units: $\mathrm{s\cdot W/Hz} = \mathrm{W/(Hz/s)}$ ✓.

Grid-following, as blocks. Everything it does starts with a measurement of the grid; the synthetic-inertia term needs the frequency's derivative, measured.

Full proof of $K = 2HS/f_0$

A machine of inertia $H$ on rating $S$ in a grid whose frequency changes at $\mathrm df/\mathrm dt$ delivers, from its rotor, the power that its swing equation releases: from $(2HS/f_0)\,\mathrm d\Delta f/\mathrm dt = P_m - P_e$ with $P_m$ unchanged, its extra electrical output is $\Delta P_e = -(2HS/f_0)\,\mathrm df/\mathrm dt$. An inverter that injects $-K\,\mathrm df/\mathrm dt$ with $K = 2HS/f_0$ adds the same term to the aggregate equation. $\square$ $2\times5\times10^9/50 = 2\times10^8$ W per Hz/s; times 0.05 Hz/s, $10^7$ W.

Energy has to come from somewhere. Over a dip of $\Delta f$ a rotor gives up $\Delta(\tfrac12J\omega^2) \approx 2HS\,\Delta f/f_0$: for $H = 5$ s, $S = 1$ GW and unit 13's ramp-model dip of 0.75 Hz, $2\times5\times10^9\times0.75/50 = 150$ MJ. An inverter emulating it must find that energy on its DC side. An HVDC link can draw it from the grid at its other end; a solar farm only if it was running below what the sun allowed, or has storage. These follow from energy conservation alone; how real plants do it is not this deck's subject.

Why measured matters. The rotor's response needs no measurement. The synthetic term needs $\mathrm df/\mathrm dt$, estimated from a measured, noisy voltage, so it arrives after a delay and with filtering. That difference is qualitative here; its consequences are control theory, which this course does not do.

Phase-locked loop, named only: a feedback circuit or algorithm that adjusts an internal oscillator until its phase matches a measured waveform's, so it reports that phase and its frequency.

Grid-forming: a voltage with an angle of its own

grid-forming control

The inverter is a voltage source $\mathcal E\angle\delta$ behind its reactance $X$ (section 3), and its controller moves $\delta$ by a swing equation of its own choosing — a virtual synchronous machine:

$$\dot\delta = \omega - \omega_s,$$ $$\frac{2H_v}{\omega_s}\dot\omega = P_{\text{set}} - P_e - D_v\frac{\omega - \omega_s}{\omega_s}.$$

Per unit on the inverter's rating, as unit 13's. Units: $\mathrm{s\cdot s^{-2}/s^{-1}}$, a pure number; $D_v$ a number ✓.

The equations, unpacked. The controller keeps an internal angle $\delta$ and speed $\omega$. It computes the power actually flowing, $P_e$, and integrates the swing equation forward — exactly unit 13's equation, but with $H_v$ and $D_v$ chosen in software and $P_{\text{set}}$ playing the mechanical power. Its inertia then shapes how $\delta$ follows.

Both questions, answered

The charger stays cool because it never holds voltage and carries current at once. A divider would burn 14 W to deliver 10. A switch and unit 05's $LC$ pair make the average, $DV_{\text{in}} = 5$ V, exactly; the filter, resonant 47 times below the switching frequency, leaves 6.6 mV of ripple. INELFE is DC because an underground AC cable is a long capacitor. On this route an AC cable would spend 65% of its rating on charging current and make 674 Mvar; DC spends none, has no reactive power, no skin effect, two conductors to a link, and a power flow set by its converters. Its cables lose 2.12 MW per 1000 MW link. The operator's reason, in its own words: DC "allows underground connections that run over long distances".

The two answers, each as one comparison. Both rest on stated assumptions: the charger's circuit and switching times, and the AC cable's dimensions.

The thread from unit 01. Unit 01 found that energy reaches a load through the insulation, as a Poynting flux, and that the copper only guides it. In an AC cable the insulation is also the capacitor's dielectric, refilled a hundred times a second along its whole length, and the current that refills it must flow through the copper. DC lets the insulation do only its first job.
