15 · the pad

A charging pad, from nothing

A phone that charges "wirelessly" lies on a pad. Under the pad's surface is a flat coil, driven with alternating current; behind the phone's back cover is another. The pad's changing field links the phone's coil and induces an EMF there — unit 07's mutual inductance — which a rectifier in the phone turns into charging current.

inductive power transfer · Qi

Power passed between coils by their mutual inductance alone. Qi is the Wireless Power Consortium's standard for phones. Its Introduction (v1.3, 2021) states (cited):

  • "The operating frequency typically is in the range of 87 to 205 kHz" (§4.2);
  • Baseline Power Profile ≤ 5 W, Extended up to 15 W (§4.1);
  • a transmitter coil 50 mm across, a receiver coil 40 mm across (§4.3);
  • coupling is highest with the coils aligned, close, and "externally shielded by ferrite" (§4.4).

Schematic and exploded, not to scale. Each coil is a flat spiral, seen here cut across; the ferrite behind each one is the WPC's "externally shielded by ferrite". Only the two diameters and the shielding are the WPC's.

What each part does. The pad's driver makes an alternating current in the transmitter coil. Its field threads the receiver coil, and Faraday's law induces an EMF there. A rectifier (a set of diodes, not studied here) turns the induced alternating current into direct current, and the phone's charging circuit feeds the battery. In this unit's language: two coils, coupled by $M$, with a source on one port and a load on the other — unit 07's two-port.

Ferrite is a magnetic material; the WPC's §4.4 names it as the external shield of the coils and says coupling is highest when they are so shielded. Its properties are not used in this unit: it is named here and not modelled, and the model coils of the next slide sit in air.

The source, and what it does not say. Wireless Power Consortium, Qi Specification: Introduction, version 1.3 (January 2021), the consortium's own public document. Its §4.2 says the operating frequency "typically is in the range of 87 to 205 kHz", that the system "typically has a resonance near the lower end of the operating frequency range", and that the transmitter "can—but does not have to—use the operating frequency to control the amount of power". §4.1 gives the Baseline Power Profile (up to 5 W) and the Extended Power Profile (up to 15 W). §4.3 bases power transfer on "a single coil in the Power Transmitter that has an outer diameter of 50 mm ... and a coil in the Power Receiver that has an outer diameter of 40 mm". §4.4 says coupling is highest when the coils are aligned, "the distance between the coils is small (less than the diameter of the coils)" and "the coils are externally shielded by ferrite". Nothing about the rectifier, the communication protocol or the control loop is used in this unit.

"Wireless." No wire crosses between pad and phone, and — unlike every circuit since unit 00 — no conductor either. Section 4 shows where the energy goes instead.

15 · the pad

The model: two loops inside one boundary

The coils (assumed): $N = 10$ turns concentrated on a circle of radius $a = 20$ mm, winding radius $b = 0.5$ mm, coaxial, gap $d$. Self-inductance from the thin-loop formula (approximate, assumed):

$$L = \mu_0aN^2\Big(\ln\frac{8a}{b} - 2\Big) = 9.47\ \mu\mathrm H .$$

At 140 kHz: $\omega L = 8.331\ \Omega$; with $Q = 100$, $R = \omega L/Q = 0.0833\ \Omega$; a series capacitor $C = 1/\omega^2L = 136.5$ nF resonates it. Units: $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\cdot m} = \mathrm H$ ✓.

lumped, by five decades

$\lambda = c/f = 2141$ m, so the 40 mm coil is $\ell/\lambda = 1.9\times10^{-5}$ of a wavelength: unit 02's (H3) holds, as its When is a circuit small? found for a pad at 100 kHz.

But not two elements. Changing flux threads the gap, so unit 02's (H1) puts both coils inside one boundary: pad and phone together are a single two-port element.

The model: two loops of $N$ turns, coaxial and aligned. Both port currents enter the + terminals, unit 07's passive marking; with both coils wound the same way round the axis, $M > 0$.

The inductance formula is assumed. $L \approx \mu_0aN^2(\ln(8a/b) - 2)$ for $N$ turns concentrated on a circle of radius $a$, wound from conductor of radius $b \ll a$, is a standard approximate result that has not been checked against a named source for this deck, so it is labelled an assumption. Its constant is $-2$ when the current flows on the conductor's surface and $-7/4$ when it is spread uniformly across the cross-section; with $-7/4$ the same model gives $10.10\ \mu$H and every $k$ in this deck would be 6% smaller. The model is not good to that level anyway — the turns are not really concentrated on one circle — so $-2$ and $9.47\ \mu$H are used throughout. Nothing qualitative depends on the choice.

The numbers. $\mu_0aN^2 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\times0.02\times100 = 2.513\times10^{-6}$ H; $\ln(8a/b) = \ln 320 = 5.768$; so $L = 2.513\times10^{-6}\times3.768 = 9.471\ \mu$H. $\omega = 2\pi\times140\,000 = 8.796\times10^5$ s⁻¹; $\omega L = 8.331\ \Omega$; $R = 0.08331\ \Omega$; $C = 1/(\omega^2L) = 1.365\times10^{-7}$ F. $Q = 100$ is an assumption about the winding, not a consequence of it: section 8 shows what keeps a real coil's $R$ that low at 140 kHz.

The wavelength. $\lambda = 2.998\times10^8/1.4\times10^5 = 2141$ m, and $0.04/2141 = 1.87\times10^{-5}$. Unit 02's table put a 5 cm pad at 100 kHz at $1.67\times10^{-5}$; the same conclusion. The phase of the field changes by $2\pi\ell/\lambda \approx 1.2\times10^{-4}$ rad across the coils: retardation is nothing here.

One boundary. Unit 02's (H1) asks that changing magnetic flux stay inside elements. The flux of the pad's coil threads the air between the coils and the phone's coil itself, so no boundary can be drawn round the pad alone that keeps (H1) true outside it. Draw one round both, and it holds: this is what unit 02's closing notes meant by "a pair of coils that must be drawn inside one boundary", and it is unit 07's coupled pair exactly.

Full proof

The efficiency. The load takes $R_L|I_2|^2$, and $|I_2|^2 = \omega^2M^2|I_1|^2/|Z_2|^2$. The driver delivers $\operatorname{Re}(V_sI_1^*) = \operatorname{Re}Z_{\text{in}}|I_1|^2$, with $\operatorname{Re}Z_{\text{in}} = R_1 + \omega^2M^2(R_2 + R_L)/|Z_2|^2$. The ratio, with $|Z_2|^2$ cleared, is the formula on the slide. Energy check: the denominator times $|I_1|^2/|Z_2|^2$ is $R_1|I_1|^2 + (R_2 + R_L)|I_2|^2$, the three resistors' heat, which is all the real power there is ✓.

The optimum. Write $x = R_L$, $X_2 = \omega L_2$, $W = \omega^2M^2$. Then $$\frac1\eta = \frac{R_1x^2 + (2R_1R_2 + W)x + R_1(R_2^2 + X_2^2) + WR_2}{Wx} = \frac{R_1x + C/x + 2R_1R_2 + W}{W},$$ with $C = R_1(R_2^2 + X_2^2) + WR_2$. By AM–GM, $R_1x + C/x \ge 2\sqrt{R_1C}$, with equality iff $x = \sqrt{C/R_1}$ — the formula for $R_L^*$. This is the step of unit 06's When only the resistance can change, and for weak coupling ($WR_2/R_1 \ll X_2^2$) the same kind of answer: $R_L^* \approx |R_2 + i\omega L_2|$, the magnitude of coil 2's own impedance.

The closed form. At the optimum, $1/\eta^* = (2\sqrt{R_1C} + 2R_1R_2 + W)/W$. Divide top and bottom by $R_1R_2$: $W/R_1R_2 = U^2$, and $\sqrt{R_1C}/(R_1R_2) = \sqrt{R_1^2(R_2^2 + X_2^2) + WR_1R_2}/(R_1R_2) = \sqrt{1 + Q_2^2 + U^2}$. So $1/\eta^* = (U^2 + 2 + 2\sqrt{1 + U^2 + Q_2^2})/U^2$. $\square$

The numbers. At 5 mm: $\omega M = 3.3213\ \Omega$, so $R_L^* = \sqrt{0.00694 + 69.405 + 11.032} = 8.969\ \Omega$; $U = 39.87$, $Q_2 = 100$, $\eta^* = 1589.4/(1589.4 + 2 + 2\times107.66) = 0.880$. At 20 mm, $U = 10.43$ and $\eta^* = 108.86/(108.86 + 2 + 201.10) = 0.349$. When $Q_2 \gg U$ the root is about $Q_2$, and with $U = kQ$ for equal coils $\eta^* \approx k^2Q/(k^2Q + 2)$: 0.888, 0.353, 0.043 — one factor of $Q$ where the tuned pair will have two.

15 · untuned

What tuning buys

Put a capacitor in series with each coil, $C_k = 1/(\omega^2L_k)$ — the coil is then tuned — so that $i\omega L_k + 1/(i\omega C_k) = 0$ at the drive frequency.

tuning, coil by coil

(i) Tuning the secondary sets $X_2 = 0$ in the efficiency: $Q_2^2$ leaves the root, and

$$\eta^* = \frac{U^2}{U^2 + 2 + 2\sqrt{1 + U^2}} .$$

(ii) Tuning the primary does not change $\eta$ at all. It makes $\operatorname{Im}Z_{\text{in}} = 0$: the driver carries no reactive power.

proof of (ii)

$\eta = R_L|I_2|^2/(\operatorname{Re}Z_{\text{in}}|I_1|^2)$, and $I_2/I_1 = -i\omega M/Z_2$ and $\operatorname{Re}Z_{\text{in}} = R_1 + \operatorname{Re}(\omega^2M^2/Z_2)$ involve circuit 2 only. $C_1$ changes $\operatorname{Im}Z_{\text{in}}$ alone. $\square$

The tuned pair. Each capacitor cancels its own coil's reactance at 140 kHz; for the pad, $C = 136.5$ nF each. Tune the secondary for efficiency, the primary for the driver.

Proof of (i). The previous slide's derivation never used that $X_2 = \omega L_2$; it used only that circuit 2's loop impedance is $R_2 + R_L + iX_2$ with $X_2$ fixed while $R_L$ varies. With $C_2$ in the loop, $X_2 = \omega L_2 - 1/(\omega C_2)$, which is zero at the drive frequency, so $Q_2 = X_2/R_2 = 0$ in the formula. The optimum load becomes $R_L^* = \sqrt{R_2^2 + \omega^2M^2R_2/R_1} = R_2\sqrt{1 + U^2}$ — section 5's result, reached from here.

What (i) buys, in numbers. At 20 mm: $\eta^*$ goes from 0.349 to 0.826; at 40 mm from 0.043 to 0.519. Section 5 tabulates it. Tuning the secondary removes exactly the reactance that a resistive load cannot cancel — the $X_s^2$ of unit 06's When only the resistance can change, here coil 2's own.

What (ii) buys. With $C_1$ resonating $L_1$, $Z_{\text{in}} = R_1 + \omega^2M^2/Z_2$; if circuit 2 is tuned too, $Z_2$ is real and so is $Z_{\text{in}}$. The driver's 64 var per watt at 20 mm becomes 0. Nothing about the efficiency changes — the copper losses depend on $|I_1|$ and $|I_2|$, which a given power fixes — but the driver no longer carries current for reactive power, which in a real driver would heat its switches and wiring (unit 06). So: tune the secondary for efficiency, the primary for the driver.

Tuned means at one frequency. Each capacitor cancels its coil only at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{L_kC_k}$ (unit 05's Foster theorem: a reactance rises with frequency). The WPC notes that Qi systems typically resonate "near the lower end of the operating frequency range" and may control power by moving the frequency (§4.2, cited); this unit's model works at resonance exactly.

The inductance matrix is the mass matrix

Take the capacitor charges $q_1$, $q_2$ as coordinates, $i_k = \dot q_k$. With no source, no load and no loss, the voltage law round each tuned loop is

$$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}L_1 & M\\ M & L_2\end{pmatrix}}_{\mathcal L}\ddot q + \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1/C_1 & 0\\ 0 & 1/C_2\end{pmatrix}}_{K}q = 0 :$$

MR 07's equation of motion, with unit 02's inductance matrix $\mathcal L$ in the place of the mass matrix: a mass matrix that is not diagonal. Its positive-definiteness is unit 02's $M^2 < L_1L_2$.

the two modes, identical coils $$\omega_\mp = \frac{\omega_0}{\sqrt{1 \pm k}},\qquad v = (1, 1),\ (1, -1).$$

At 5 mm: 118.4 and 180.5 kHz; at 20 mm, 133.2 and 147.9; at 40 mm, 137.95 and 142.15. $k$ is a pure number ✓; $k \to 0$: both → $\omega_0$ ✓.

The equations. Loop 1, in the direction of $i_1$: the coil's voltage $L_1\ddot q_1 + M\ddot q_2$ (unit 07's equations in the time domain, losses set aside for now) plus the capacitor's $q_1/C_1$ sums to zero; likewise loop 2. In matrix form, $\mathcal L\ddot q + Kq = 0$.

MR 07's two quadratic forms. The magnetic energy is $\tfrac12\dot q^{\!\top}\mathcal L\dot q = \tfrac12L_1i_1^2 + Mi_1i_2 + \tfrac12L_2i_2^2$ (unit 02's The inductance matrix is positive semi-definite), and the capacitors' energy is $\tfrac12q^{\!\top}Kq$. Full proof of the frequencies

MR 07's The generalised eigenvalue problem: look for $q = v\cos\omega t$; then $Kv = \omega^2\mathcal Lv$. For identical coils, $\mathcal L = \begin{pmatrix}L & M\\ M & L\end{pmatrix}$ and $K = (1/C)I$. Try $v = (1, 1)$: $Kv = (1/C)(1, 1)$, $\mathcal Lv = (L + M)(1, 1)$, so $\omega^2 = 1/(C(L + M)) = \omega_0^2/(1 + k)$ with $\omega_0^2 = 1/LC$ and $k = M/L$. Try $v = (1, -1)$: $\mathcal Lv = (L - M)(1, -1)$, so $\omega^2 = \omega_0^2/(1 - k)$. Two eigenvectors for a $2\times2$ pencil: these are all the modes. The shapes are MR 07's problem P4's two equal masses, for the same reason — the swap symmetry $q_1 \leftrightarrow q_2$.

The figure of merit U

the figure of merit $$U = k\sqrt{Q_1Q_2} = \frac{\omega M}{\sqrt{R_1R_2}} .$$

Units: $k$ and each $Q$ are pure numbers; $\omega M$ and $\sqrt{R_1R_2}$ are both ohms ✓.

proof of the equality

$k\sqrt{Q_1Q_2} = \dfrac{M}{\sqrt{L_1L_2}}\sqrt{\dfrac{\omega L_1}{R_1}\cdot\dfrac{\omega L_2}{R_2}} = \dfrac{\omega M}{\sqrt{R_1R_2}}$, using unit 05's $Q = \omega_0L/R$. $\square$

What it measures. Energy crosses at a rate set by the mode splitting, about $k\omega_0/2$; each coil loses its amplitude at $\omega_0/2Q$. Their ratio is $kQ = U$: large $U$ means the energy crosses many times before it is lost.

For the pad: $U = 39.9$ at 5 mm, 10.4 at 20 mm, 3.0 at 40 mm.

Schematic. The two rates that decide the link: how fast energy sloshes across, and how fast each coil bleeds it into heat. Section 5 proves that the efficiency depends on their ratio alone.

The two rates. The beat envelope of the previous slide is $\cos(\Delta\omega t/2)$: amplitude moves across at the angular rate $\Delta\omega/2 \approx k\omega_0/2$. A free oscillator's amplitude decays as $e^{-\gamma t/2}$ with $\gamma = \omega_0/Q$ (MR 06; unit 05's $\gamma = R/L$), so each coil's amplitude decay rate is $\omega_0/2Q$. For equal coils the ratio is $(k\omega_0/2)/(\omega_0/2Q) = kQ = U$; for unequal coils, the geometric mean of the two loss rates gives $k\sqrt{Q_1Q_2}$. Section 5 meets the same ratio as Kurs and colleagues' $\kappa/\Gamma$.

Why two $Q$'s, not one. Writing $U = kQ$ is right only for identical coils. The checkpoint's failure modes include "U defined as $kQ$ with no account of two different $Q$'s": a phone's coil and a pad's coil are not the same, and the efficiency depends on the geometric mean of their two $Q$'s.

The numbers. At 5 mm, $\omega M = 3.3213\ \Omega$ and $R = 0.08331\ \Omega$: $U = 39.87$; equivalently $kQ = 0.3987\times100$. At 20 mm, $0.10434\times100 = 10.43$; at 40 mm, $0.02996\times100 = 3.00$.

What $U$ leaves out. The load. $U$ is a property of the two coils and their geometry; the next slide adds the load, and section 5 finds the load that makes the most of a given $U$.

Circuit 2's voltage law, $0 = i\omega MI_1 + Z_2I_2$ (with $Z_2$ everything in loop 2), times $I_2^*$; real parts:

$$\operatorname{Re}Z_2\,|I_2|^2 = \operatorname{Re}\big(-i\omega MI_1I_2^*\big)$$ $$= \omega M\operatorname{Im}\big(I_1I_2^*\big).$$

Units: $\mathrm{s^{-1}\cdot H\cdot A^2} = \Omega\,\mathrm A^2 = \mathrm W$ ✓.

In time. Coil 1 induces $-M\,\mathrm di_1/\mathrm dt$ round loop 2, which delivers $p(t) = -Mi_2\,\mathrm di_1/\mathrm dt$. At $\theta = 90°$ it is $2\omega M|I_1||I_2|\sin^2\omega t \ge 0$: it never reverses — unlike unit 06's The flux runs backwards.

The power coil 1's induced voltage delivers to circuit 2, over two cycles, at three phase differences. Only at 90° does it flow one way all the time.

Full proof

From the phasors. Loop 2 contains coil 2 and whatever is connected to it — tuned or not, loaded or not — with total impedance $Z_2$, and is driven only through $M$: $Z_2I_2 + i\omega MI_1 = 0$. Multiply by $I_2^*$: $Z_2|I_2|^2 = -i\omega MI_1I_2^*$. The real part of the left is the mean power dissipated in loop 2 (unit 06: a passive impedance absorbs $\operatorname{Re}Z|I|^2$). On the right, $\operatorname{Re}(-iz) = \operatorname{Im}z$. With $I_1 = |I_1|e^{i\alpha}$ and $I_2 = |I_2|e^{i(\alpha - \theta)}$, $I_1I_2^* = |I_1||I_2|e^{i\theta}$, so $\operatorname{Im}(I_1I_2^*) = |I_1||I_2|\sin\theta$. $\square$

From the waveforms. Take $i_1 = \sqrt2|I_1|\cos\omega t$ and $i_2 = \sqrt2|I_2|\cos(\omega t - \theta)$. The EMF that coil 1 induces round loop 2 is $-M\,\mathrm di_1/\mathrm dt = \sqrt2\omega M|I_1|\sin\omega t$, and it delivers $p = \sqrt2\omega M|I_1|\sin\omega t\cdot\sqrt2|I_2|\cos(\omega t - \theta)$. With $2\sin A\cos B = \sin(A + B) + \sin(A - B)$: $p = \omega M|I_1||I_2|\big(\sin(2\omega t - \theta) + \sin\theta\big)$, whose mean is $\omega M|I_1||I_2|\sin\theta$ ✓. At $\theta = 90°$, $\cos(\omega t - 90°) = \sin\omega t$ and $p = 2\omega M|I_1||I_2|\sin^2\omega t$, never negative. At $\theta = 0$, $p = \omega M|I_1||I_2|\sin2\omega t$: a pure slosh with mean zero.

Where the mean goes. Loop 2 turns it into heat in $R_2$ and $R_L$; nothing is stored on average. So the formula is the power that crosses from circuit 1 to circuit 2, and it holds whatever is in either loop.

Against unit 06. There, a reactive load made the energy flow backwards for $\varphi/\pi$ of every cycle. Here, at quadrature, the induced EMF and the current it drives are in phase for the whole cycle, and the energy only goes forward.

A closed surface between the coils

The surface. $\Sigma$: the mid-plane between the coils, closed by a hemisphere at infinity on the phone's side. Inside it: all of circuit 2 — coil 2, $C_2$, $R_L$. Outside: all of circuit 1.

Split the fields by their sources (superposition, MR 24): $\vec E = \vec E_1 + \vec E_2$, $\vec H = \vec H_1 + \vec H_2$, circuit 1's charges and currents making the first, circuit 2's the second. Then the mean Poynting vector (RMS phasors)

$$\bar{\vec S} = \operatorname{Re}(\vec E\times\vec H^*)$$

has two self terms, $\vec E_1\times\vec H_1^*$ and $\vec E_2\times\vec H_2^*$, and a cross term, $\vec E_1\times\vec H_2^* + \vec E_2\times\vec H_1^*$.

assumptions, stated once

Quasi-static: $\nabla\times\vec H_k = \vec J_k$ — radiation neglected (branch 16). Magnetic coupling only: each capacitor's field stays inside it, so outside, $\vec E_k = -i\omega\vec A_k$.

Schematic. $V$ (shaded) holds all of circuit 2; $\Sigma$ is its boundary, the mid-plane plus a hemisphere far away. The power circuit 2 absorbs must come in through $\Sigma$.

Why this surface. Unit 01's theorem was about a surface crossed by two leads. No lead crosses here: $\Sigma$ separates the two circuits completely. Whatever circuit 2 dissipates must enter $V$ as a Poynting flux, by Poynting's theorem itself (MR 24), since nothing else can carry energy across $\Sigma$. The question is which part of the field carries it.

RMS phasors and the mean. With $\vec e(t) = \operatorname{Re}(\sqrt2\vec Ee^{i\omega t})$ and $\vec h$ likewise, the time average of $\vec e\times\vec h$ is $\operatorname{Re}(\vec E\times\vec H^*)$: the vector version of unit 06's $\langle vi\rangle = \operatorname{Re}(VI^*)$, by the same computation component by component. $\vec H = \vec B/\mu_0$ in air.

The split. Maxwell's equations are linear (MR 24), so the fields made by circuit 1's sources alone and by circuit 2's alone add to the total, and each pair $(\vec E_k, \vec H_k)$ satisfies the equations with its own sources $\vec J_k$. The Poynting vector, being quadratic, splits into the four products on the slide.

The assumptions. Quasi-static: the displacement current is dropped from Ampère's law, as unit 01's H2 dropped it on its surface; unit 02's (H3) holds by five decades, and branch 16 shows what radiation the pad really makes. Magnetic coupling only: the charges on each capacitor's plates make a field that stays essentially between the plates; elsewhere the electric field is the induced one, $-\partial_t\vec A$, which in phasors is $-i\omega\vec A$ (MR 24's potentials, Coulomb gauge). The small charges on the windings themselves are neglected — the same "changing flux stays inside, charge stays inside" bookkeeping as unit 02's hypotheses, applied to each circuit separately.

Neither coil's own field carries mean power

the self terms

Through the mid-plane, $\operatorname{Re}\int(\vec E_1\times\vec H_1^*)\cdot\hat z\,\mathrm dA = 0$, and the same for coil 2. In fact both vanish point by point on the plane.

proof, pointwise

On the plane, coil $k$'s fields are $E_\varphi = -i\omega A_\varphi I_k$ and $H_\rho = B_\rho I_k/\mu_0$, with $A_\varphi$, $B_\rho$ real (per ampere). Then $E_\varphi H_\rho^* = -i\omega A_\varphi B_\rho|I_k|^2/\mu_0$ is imaginary, and $\bar S_z = -\operatorname{Re}(E_\varphi H_\rho^*) = 0$. $\square$

The global reason. In the half-space away from coil 1, $\vec J_1 = 0$, and

$$\nabla\cdot(\vec E_1\times\vec H_1^*) = -i\omega\mu_0|\vec H_1|^2 ,$$

purely imaginary: coil 1's own field only stores energy there, and returns it. Likewise coil 2's on the other side.

Schematic of the phases at one point on the plane, tuned, $I_1$ real and $I_2 = -i|I_2|$. One coil's $E$ and $H$ are a quarter-cycle apart: they store and return. Coil 1's $E$ with coil 2's $H$ are in step (up to sign): they carry.

Full proof of the global statement

An identity. For any two field pairs, $\nabla\cdot(\vec E_a\times\vec H_b^*) = \vec H_b^*\cdot(\nabla\times\vec E_a) - \vec E_a\cdot(\nabla\times\vec H_b^*)$ (the product rule MR 24 uses in Poynting's theorem: energy, locally). With Faraday $\nabla\times\vec E_a = -i\omega\mu_0\vec H_a$ and quasi-static Ampère $\nabla\times\vec H_b = \vec J_b$: $$\nabla\cdot(\vec E_a\times\vec H_b^*) = -i\omega\mu_0\,\vec H_a\cdot\vec H_b^* - \vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b^* .$$

Coil 1's self term. Take $a = b = 1$ over $V$, the half-space above the plane, where $\vec J_1 = 0$. The divergence theorem gives the outward flux of $\vec E_1\times\vec H_1^*$ through $\Sigma$ as $-i\omega\mu_0\int_V|\vec H_1|^2$, purely imaginary, so its real part — the mean power — is zero. The hemisphere contributes nothing (next slide), so the plane alone carries zero mean flux of coil 1's own field.

Coil 2's self term. The same argument on the other side: over the lower half-space, where $\vec J_2 = 0$, coil 2's self term has purely imaginary total flux, so its mean flux through the plane is zero too. (Over $V$, where coil 2 lives, the same identity would say that its own field's mean flux out of $V$ is what it radiates — zero in the quasi-static model.)

Pointwise, on the plane. By symmetry about the axis, a coaxial loop's vector potential is azimuthal and its magnetic field has no azimuthal part; on the plane, the vertical Poynting component needs $E_\varphi$ and $H_\rho$: $(\vec E\times\vec H^*)_z = E_\rho H_\varphi^* - E_\varphi H_\rho^*$, and $E_\rho = 0$ by "magnetic coupling only". For one coil, $A_\varphi$ and $B_\rho$ at a given point are real numbers times the same current phasor, so the product carries the factor $-i$ and no real part. The global statement is then trivially true; the global proof shows it does not depend on the coils' shape.

The figure's phases. $E_1 = -i\omega A_{1}I_1$ points along $-i$. On the plane $B_{2\rho}$ is negative (coil 2 is above, its field lines spread downward and outward), and $I_2 = -i|I_2|$, so $H_2 \propto (-)(-i)$ points along $+i$: collinear with $E_1$, opposite in sign, and $-\operatorname{Re}(E_\varphi H_\rho^*)$ is positive — upward, towards the phone.

Set-up. Mean power into $V$ through the plane is $\operatorname{Re}\int_{\text{plane}}(\vec E\times\vec H^*)\cdot\hat z\,\mathrm dA$ (upward is into $V$). The self terms contribute nothing (previous slide); the hemisphere contributes nothing: in the quasi-static model each coil's $\vec H$ falls as $r^{-3}$ (a dipole field, MR 23) and $\vec E = -i\omega\vec A$ as $r^{-2}$, so $|\vec E||\vec H|$ times the hemisphere's area $\sim r^2$ falls as $r^{-3}$.

The cross term. Let $\vec X = \vec E_1\times\vec H_2^* + \vec E_2\times\vec H_1^*$. The divergence theorem over $V$, whose outward normal on the plane is $-\hat z$: $\int_{\text{plane}}\vec X\cdot\hat z\,\mathrm dA = -\int_V\nabla\cdot\vec X\,\mathrm dV$. The identity of the previous slide's notes gives $\nabla\cdot(\vec E_1\times\vec H_2^*) = -i\omega\mu_0\vec H_1\cdot\vec H_2^* - \vec E_1\cdot\vec J_2^*$ and $\nabla\cdot(\vec E_2\times\vec H_1^*) = -i\omega\mu_0\vec H_2\cdot\vec H_1^*$ ($\vec J_1 = 0$ in $V$). Since $\vec H_1\cdot\vec H_2^* + \vec H_2\cdot\vec H_1^* = 2\operatorname{Re}(\vec H_1\cdot\vec H_2^*)$, the first part is $-2i\omega\mu_0$ times a real number: imaginary. So $\operatorname{Re}\int_{\text{plane}}\vec X\cdot\hat z = \operatorname{Re}\int_V\vec E_1\cdot\vec J_2^*$.

The thin coil. For a thin winding, $\vec J_2\,\mathrm dV = I_2\,\mathrm d\vec l_2$ along it, so $\int_V\vec E_1\cdot\vec J_2^* = I_2^*\oint\vec E_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2$. Coil 1's field along coil 2 is $\vec E_1 = -i\omega\vec A_1$ (magnetic coupling only; a gradient part would integrate to zero round the closed loop anyway). By Stokes, $\oint\vec A_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2$ is the flux of $\vec B_1$ through coil 2, which is $MI_1$ — exactly unit 02's proof of Neumann's formula. So the integral is $-i\omega MI_1I_2^*$, and $\operatorname{Re}(-i\omega MI_1I_2^*) = \omega M\operatorname{Im}(I_1I_2^*)$. $\square$

Two derivations, one number. The circuit gave $\operatorname{Re}Z_2|I_2|^2$ for the power circuit 2 absorbs; the field gives the work coil 1's induced field does on coil 2's current. They agree because circuit 2's voltage law is that same line integral.

A numerical identity. On the plane the integrated cross term for unit currents in quadrature, $(2\omega/\mu_0)\int A_\varphi B_\rho\,2\pi\rho\,\mathrm d\rho$ with both factors from one coil (the other's are mirror images), must equal $\omega M$. Computed for the model at 5, 20 and 40 mm, the two agree to better than $10^{-9}$ — a check on the figure that follows.

section 5

The efficiency, and the one number

At resonance the efficiency depends on two numbers only: $U$, which the coils fix, and the load ratio, which the designer picks. The best load gives $\eta_{\max} = U^2/(1 + \sqrt{1 + U^2})^2$. This is the checkpoint.

15 · the one number

Two divisions: η depends on U and the load alone

At resonance the input is a resistance, $R_1 + R_{\text{refl}}$. Write $a = R_L/R_2$ and $\omega^2M^2 = U^2R_1R_2$, so $R_{\text{refl}} = U^2R_1/(1 + a)$. The power divides twice:

circuit 2's share of the input: $\dfrac{R_{\text{refl}}}{R_1 + R_{\text{refl}}} = \dfrac{U^2}{1 + a + U^2}$;

the load's share of circuit 2's: $\dfrac{R_L}{R_2 + R_L} = \dfrac{a}{1 + a}$.

the tuned pair's efficiency $$\eta(a) = \frac{aU^2}{(1 + a)(1 + a + U^2)} .$$

Dimensionless ✓. Limits: $a \to 0$ (shorted load) and $a \to \infty$ (open) both give $\eta \to 0$ ✓; $U = 0$ gives 0 ✓.

The tuned pair at resonance, unfolded. The driver sees $R_1$ in series with the reflected resistance; circuit 2 sees the induced voltage driving $R_2$ in series with $R_L$. Each resistor's share of a loop's power is its share of the loop's resistance.

Full proof

Circuit 2's share. In loop 1 the current $I_1$ flows through $R_1$ and, as far as the driver can tell, through $R_{\text{refl}}$. The real power into loop 1 is $(R_1 + R_{\text{refl}})|I_1|^2$; of it, $R_1|I_1|^2$ heats coil 1, and the rest, $R_{\text{refl}}|I_1|^2$, is what crosses — check: $R_{\text{refl}}|I_1|^2 = \frac{\omega^2M^2}{R_2 + R_L}|I_1|^2 = (R_2 + R_L)|I_2|^2$ using $|I_2| = \omega M|I_1|/(R_2 + R_L)$ ✓, which is section 4's power across.

In $U$. $R_{\text{refl}}/R_1 = \omega^2M^2/(R_1(R_2 + R_L)) = U^2R_2/(R_2 + R_L) = U^2/(1 + a)$, so the share is $\frac{U^2/(1 + a)}{1 + U^2/(1 + a)} = \frac{U^2}{1 + a + U^2}$. The load's share of loop 2 is $R_L/(R_2 + R_L) = a/(1 + a)$. The product is $\eta(a)$. $\square$

What it says. Every quantity of the coils — $L$, $M$, $R$, $\omega$ — has dropped out except through $U$. Two links with the same $U$ and the same $a$ have the same efficiency, whatever their size, frequency or power. That is why $U$ is called the figure of merit.

The figure is not a circuit anyone builds: the left loop is unit 07's reflected impedance made visible, and the right loop's source is the voltage that coil 1 induces round loop 2. It is drawn to show where the two fractions come from.

The maximum-efficiency theorem

theorem

With $s = \sqrt{1 + U^2}$, $\eta(a)$ is largest at $a^* = s$, the maximum-efficiency load $R_L = R_2\sqrt{1 + U^2}$, where

$$\eta_{\max} = \frac{U^2}{\big(1 + \sqrt{1 + U^2}\big)^2} = \frac{s - 1}{s + 1} .$$

Dimensionless ✓. $U \to 0$: $\eta \to 0$; $U \to \infty$: $\eta \to 1$ ✓.

proof, by AM–GM

$(1 + a)(1 + a + U^2) = a^2 + (2 + U^2)a + s^2$, so

$$\frac1\eta = \frac{a + s^2/a + 2}{U^2} + 1 \ \ge\ \frac{(1 + s)^2}{U^2},$$

using $a + s^2/a \ge 2s$ (AM–GM, unit 06's tool; unit 04's completed square in another form), equal iff $a = s$, and $U^2 + 2s + 2 = (1 + s)^2$. Since $U^2 = (s - 1)(s + 1)$, $\eta_{\max} = (s - 1)/(s + 1)$. $\square$

Computed for the pad at 5 mm, $U = 39.87$. The best load is $a^* = 39.9$, $R_L = 3.32\ \Omega$. The conjugate match of section 6, $a = 1 + U^2 = 1590$, sits far down the right flank.

Full proof

The expansion. $(1 + a)(1 + a + U^2) = (1 + a)^2 + U^2(1 + a) = a^2 + 2a + 1 + U^2 + U^2a = a^2 + (2 + U^2)a + s^2$ with $s^2 = 1 + U^2$. Dividing by $aU^2$: $1/\eta = (a + 2 + U^2 + s^2/a)/U^2 = (a + s^2/a + 2)/U^2 + 1$.

The bound. For $a > 0$, $a + s^2/a - 2s = (\sqrt a - s/\sqrt a)^2 \ge 0$, with equality iff $a = s$. So $1/\eta \ge (2s + 2)/U^2 + 1 = (2s + 2 + U^2)/U^2$, and $2s + 2 + U^2 = 2s + 1 + s^2 = (1 + s)^2$. Hence $\eta \le U^2/(1 + s)^2$ for every load, with equality only at $a = s$. Finally $U^2 = s^2 - 1 = (s - 1)(s + 1)$, so $U^2/(1 + s)^2 = (s - 1)/(s + 1)$. $\square$

Against section 2. The untuned closed form with $Q_2 = 0$ was $U^2/(U^2 + 2 + 2\sqrt{1 + U^2}) = U^2/(1 + s)^2$ — the same expression, as it must be: section 2's derivation with $X_2 = 0$ is this one.

Calculus, as a check. $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm da}\big(a + s^2/a\big) = 1 - s^2/a^2$, zero at $a = s$, negative below, positive above.

The figure's numbers. $U = 39.867$, $s = 39.879$: $\eta(s) = 0.9511$. At $a = 1 + U^2 = 1590.4$: $\eta = 0.4994$. The curve is broad on a logarithmic axis: $\eta \ge 0.9$ for $a$ from about 9.6 to about 165 — the design does not need the load exactly right.

Unit 04 and unit 06 each found that the matched load wastes half the power. Here the load that draws the most power from a given drive is not the most efficient one, and the gap between them is enormous.

15 · two design points

Seen from the load: a source of resistance R₂(1 + U²)

From $R_L$'s terminals, with the driver as an ideal $V_s$ and both loops tuned, the link is a Thévenin source (unit 04):

$$V_{\text{th}} = \frac{-i\omega M}{R_1}V_s,$$ $$R_{\text{th}} = R_2 + \frac{\omega^2M^2}{R_1} = R_2(1 + U^2) .$$
the conjugate match, here

Unit 06's theorem: the most power from a given $V_s$ goes to $R_L = R_2(1 + U^2)$, i.e. $a = 1 + U^2$, where

$$\eta = \frac{U^2}{2(2 + U^2)} < \tfrac12 .$$

Dimensionless ✓; $U \to \infty$ gives unit 04's ½ ✓.

Schematic. Everything to the left of the load's terminals, at resonance, as unit 04's Thévenin equivalent. Its resistance is coil 2's own plus coil 1's reflected back through the coupling.

unit 04's caveat, with numbers

The equivalent's own efficiency at the match is $\tfrac12$, but the real link's is less: $R_{\text{th}}$ stands for the losses in two coils.

Full proof

Open-circuit voltage. With the load removed, $I_2 = 0$, so $I_1 = V_s/R_1$ (loop 1 tuned) and the voltage coil 1 induces round loop 2, which now appears across the open terminals (loop 2's own reactances cancel, and no current flows in $R_2$), is $-i\omega MI_1$ in the direction that drives $I_2$: $V_{\text{th}} = -i\omega MV_s/R_1$, of magnitude $\omega M|V_s|/R_1$.

Resistance. Set $V_s = 0$ (a short, unit 04's rule for an ideal voltage source) and look in from the load: coil 2's loop with $R_2$, its tuned reactances cancelling, and coil 1's loop reflected through the coupling — unit 07's reflected impedance turned round — giving $\omega^2M^2/R_1$. So $R_{\text{th}} = R_2 + \omega^2M^2/R_1 = R_2(1 + U^2)$. All quantities are at the drive frequency; at resonance the equivalent is purely resistive.

How k falls with distance

Unit 02's Neumann formula, for two coaxial loops, reduces to one integral. Far apart, its leading term vanishes and the next falls as the cube of the distance — which is why millimetres are easy and metres need very high $Q$.

15 · how k falls

k from Neumann, exactly

Two coaxial loops of radius $a$, $N$ turns each, a distance $d$ apart. A point of loop 1 at angle $\varphi_1$, of loop 2 at $\varphi_2$; put $u = \varphi_1 - \varphi_2$:

$$\mathrm d\vec l_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2 = a^2\cos u\,\mathrm d\varphi_1\mathrm d\varphi_2,$$ $$|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|^2 = 2a^2(1 - \cos u) + d^2 .$$

Unit 02's double integral depends on $u$ alone, so one integration gives $2\pi$:

mutual inductance of two coaxial loops $$M = \frac{\mu_0N^2a^2}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\cos u\,\mathrm du}{\sqrt{2a^2(1 - \cos u) + d^2}} .$$

Units: $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\cdot m^2/m} = \mathrm H$ ✓. Limit $d \to \infty$: $M \to 0$ ✓.

Section 5's table is this integral, evaluated numerically. (It has a classical closed form in elliptic integrals, not used here.)

Neumann's formula pairs every element of one loop with every element of the other. For coaxial loops the pair's contribution depends only on the angle between them, $u = \varphi_1 - \varphi_2$.

Full proof

Coordinates. Put loop 1 in the plane $z = 0$ and loop 2 in $z = d$, both centred on the $z$-axis: $\vec r_1 = a(\cos\varphi_1, \sin\varphi_1, 0)$, $\vec r_2 = a(\cos\varphi_2, \sin\varphi_2, d/a)$. Then $\mathrm d\vec l_k = a(-\sin\varphi_k, \cos\varphi_k, 0)\,\mathrm d\varphi_k$, and $\mathrm d\vec l_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2 = a^2(\sin\varphi_1\sin\varphi_2 + \cos\varphi_1\cos\varphi_2)\,\mathrm d\varphi_1\mathrm d\varphi_2 = a^2\cos u\,\mathrm d\varphi_1\mathrm d\varphi_2$. The squared distance is $a^2[(\cos\varphi_1 - \cos\varphi_2)^2 + (\sin\varphi_1 - \sin\varphi_2)^2] + d^2 = 2a^2(1 - \cos u) + d^2$.

The reduction. Unit 02's Mutual inductance: Neumann's formula for single loops is $\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint\oint\frac{\mathrm d\vec l_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2}{|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|}$. With $N$ turns on each, concentrated on one circle, every turn of one links every turn of the other: a factor $N^2$. The integrand depends on $\varphi_1 - \varphi_2$ only and is $2\pi$-periodic, so for each $\varphi_2$ the $\varphi_1$-integral is the same $\int_0^{2\pi}(\cdot)\,\mathrm du$, and the $\varphi_2$-integral then gives $2\pi$: $M = \frac{\mu_0N^2}{4\pi}\cdot2\pi\cdot a^2\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\cos u\,\mathrm du}{\sqrt{2a^2(1 - \cos u) + d^2}}$, the formula on the slide.

The closed form. The integral can be written with complete elliptic integrals of the first and second kinds; that form is classical but was not checked against a named source for this deck, so it is only mentioned. Evaluated both ways for the table's gaps, the two agree to about $10^{-14}$ relative.

Why the pair can be written with one $N^2$. The model concentrates each coil's turns on one circle. A real flat spiral has turns at different radii; its $M$ is the sum of Neumann integrals over every pair of turns, which is how coil-design programs compute it. The model trades that realism for one integral.

Far apart: the leading term vanishes, and M falls as 1/d³

For $d \gg a$, expand the root in $x = 2a^2(1 - \cos u)/d^2$: $(1 + x)^{-1/2} = 1 - \tfrac x2 + \tfrac38x^2 - \dots$ Against $\cos u$:

  • the zeroth term gives $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos u\,\mathrm du = 0$ — unit 02's $\oint\mathrm d\vec l = 0$;
  • the first gives $\int(1 - \cos u)\cos u\,\mathrm du = -\pi$, which survives.
the far-field mutual inductance $$M \approx \frac{\mu_0\pi N^2a^4}{2d^3}\Big(1 - \frac{3a^2}{d^2} + \dots\Big)$$

Units: $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\cdot m^4/m^3} = \mathrm H$ ✓.

So $k \propto (a/d)^3$ once $d \gg a$: Kurs and colleagues' "$D^{-3}$ dependence characteristic of dipole–dipole coupling" (cited). Unit 02's P11 promised the rate; here it is.

Schematic. Far away, coil 1's field is a dipole's; on the axis it has fallen to $\mu_0NIa^2/2d^3$ and is nearly uniform across coil 2, which links $N\pi a^2$ times it.

Full proof

The expansion. $\big(2a^2(1 - \cos u) + d^2\big)^{-1/2} = d^{-1}(1 + x)^{-1/2}$ with $x = 2a^2(1 - \cos u)/d^2 \le 4a^2/d^2$, small for $d \gg a$. With $(1 + x)^{-1/2} = 1 - x/2 + 3x^2/8 - 5x^3/16 + \dots$ and the integrals $\int_0^{2\pi}\cos u = 0$, $\int(1 - \cos u)\cos u = -\pi$, $\int(1 - \cos u)^2\cos u = -2\pi$, $\int(1 - \cos u)^3\cos u = -15\pi/4$: $$\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\cos u\,\mathrm du}{\sqrt{\cdot}} = \frac1d\Big[\frac{\pi a^2}{d^2} - \frac{3\pi a^4}{d^4} + \frac{75\pi a^6}{8d^6} - \dots\Big].$$ Multiply by $\mu_0N^2a^2/2$: $M = \frac{\mu_0\pi N^2a^4}{2d^3}\big(1 - 3a^2/d^2 + \tfrac{75}8a^4/d^4 - \dots\big)$. $\square$

Unit 02's observation. Its problem P11 noticed that the leading term of Neumann's integral for distant loops is $\frac1D\oint\oint\mathrm d\vec l_1\cdot\mathrm d\vec l_2$, which vanishes because each $\oint\mathrm d\vec l = 0$, "and $M$ falls faster than $1/D$ — unit 15 finds the rate". The rate is $D^{-3}$: in the general expansion of $1/|\vec r_1 - \vec r_2|$ about the centres' distance, the $1/D$ term vanishes by $\oint\mathrm d\vec l_1 = 0$, and so does the $1/D^2$ term, each of whose pieces carries one loop's $\oint\mathrm d\vec l$ as a factor; the coaxial expansion here has no even powers to begin with.

A second derivation, from Biot–Savart. MR 23: the field on the axis of a loop of $N$ turns carrying $I$, at distance $d$ from its plane, is $B = \mu_0NIa^2/\big(2(a^2 + d^2)^{3/2}\big)$. For $d \gg a$ it is $\mu_0NIa^2/2d^3$ and nearly uniform over a coaxial loop of radius $a$, whose $N$ turns then link $N\pi a^2B = \mu_0\pi N^2a^4I/2d^3$: the same $M$. It is the field of a magnetic dipole of moment $NI\pi a^2$, which is why Kurs and colleagues call the coupling dipole–dipole.

scale invariance of k

Scale every length — $a$, $b$, $d$ — by $\lambda$. Then $M$ and $L$ both scale by $\lambda$, and $k$ is unchanged: it depends only on the shape, $d/a$ and $a/b$.

proof

In $M$, $a^2$ gives $\lambda^2$ and the root $\lambda$; in $L$, $a$ gives $\lambda$ and $\ln(8a/b)$ nothing. $\square$

A scrap of foil: eddy currents in circles

A thin aluminium disc, radius $R$, thickness $t$, in a uniform axial field $\hat B\cos\omega t$ — a foreign object on the pad. Faraday's law on a circle of radius $r$ (MR 24), in amplitude:

$$2\pi r\hat E = \pi r^2\omega\hat B\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat E = \frac{\omega\hat Br}2 .$$

The eddy current runs in circles: unit 07's laminated sheet, with a circle for a line. Mean loss density $\sigma\hat E^2/2$, over the disc:

thin-disc loss $$P = \frac{\sigma\omega^2\hat B^2\pi tR^4}{16}$$

Units: $\mathrm{S\,m^{-1}\cdot s^{-2}\cdot T^2\cdot m\cdot m^4} = \mathrm{A\,V^{-1}\,m^{-1}\cdot V^2\,m^{-2}\cdot m^3} = \mathrm W$ ✓.

Note the $R^4$: a disc twice as wide loses 16 times as much.

Schematic. The changing flux through each circle drives current round it; the EMF grows as $r^2$ and the path as $r$, so $\hat E \propto r$ and the loss is concentrated at the rim. The circulation's sense opposes the change of flux (Lenz).

Full proof

The field. By symmetry about the axis, the induced field is azimuthal and depends on $r$ only. Faraday's law round the circle of radius $r$: $\oint\vec E\cdot\mathrm d\vec l = -\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int\vec B\cdot\mathrm d\vec A$, i.e. $2\pi rE(t) = -\pi r^2\dot B$, so $E = -\tfrac12r\dot B$ — unit 07's $E = -x\dot B$ for a sheet, with $\tfrac12r$ for $x$ because the area grows as $r^2$ and the path as $r$. With $B = \hat B\cos\omega t$, the amplitude is $\hat E = \omega\hat Br/2$.

The loss. Ohm's law, $J = \sigma E$; mean power density $\sigma\langle E^2\rangle = \sigma\hat E^2/2 = \sigma\omega^2\hat B^2r^2/8$. Integrate over the disc, volume element $2\pi rt\,\mathrm dr$: $P = \frac{\sigma\omega^2\hat B^2}{8}\cdot2\pi t\int_0^Rr^3\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\sigma\omega^2\hat B^2}{8}\cdot2\pi t\cdot\frac{R^4}4 = \frac{\sigma\omega^2\hat B^2\pi tR^4}{16}$. $\square$

The assumption hidden in "uniform field". The eddy currents make a field of their own, opposing the applied one. The formula ignores it. The next slide finds when that is allowed — and it is a stronger condition than unit 07's $d \ll \delta$.

When the formula holds: tR ≪ 2δ²

The eddy current is a sheet current $K = \sigma t\hat E = \sigma t\omega\hat Br/2$ (A/m). Each ring of it adds $\mu_0K\,\mathrm dr/2r$ at the centre:

$$B_{\text{reaction}} = \int_0^R\frac{\mu_0K}{2r}\mathrm dr = \frac{\mu_0\sigma t\omega\hat BR}4 = \hat B\,\frac{tR}{2\delta^2},$$

using $\mu_0\sigma\omega = 2/\delta^2$. The reaction field is small only if

the thin-disc condition $$tR \ll 2\delta^2 ,$$

stronger than $t \ll \delta$ by the factor $R/\delta$. In aluminium at 140 kHz, $\delta = 0.219$ mm. Units: m² against m² ✓.

The scraps. 20 μm × 10 mm: $tR/2\delta^2 = $ 2.09 — outside the formula's range. 10 μm × 5 mm: 0.52, and the formula is 13% high.

Computed: a 10 mm disc in a uniform 1 mT field (peak), the disc cut into 200 rings coupled by unit 02's Neumann formula. The scrap's assumed 20 μm sits near the worst case.

Full proof

The sheet current. Current density $\sigma\hat E$ through thickness $t$ is a current per unit length $K = \sigma t\hat E$ along each circle. The rings between $r$ and $r + \mathrm dr$ carry $K\,\mathrm dr$, and a circular current $I$ of radius $r$ makes $\mu_0I/2r$ at its centre (MR 23). Summing: $\int_0^R\mu_0\sigma t\omega\hat B\,\mathrm dr/4 = \mu_0\sigma t\omega\hat BR/4$. With $\delta^2 = 2/(\omega\mu_0\sigma)$, $\mu_0\sigma\omega = 2/\delta^2$ and the reaction is $\hat B\cdot tR/(2\delta^2)$. The formula assumed the reaction negligible, so it needs $tR/2\delta^2 \ll 1$. Since a scrap's radius is much larger than $\delta$, this is far stricter than $t \ll \delta$, which is what unit 07's laminations needed.

The numbers. $\delta_{\text{Al}} = \sqrt{2\times2.65\times10^{-8}/(8.796\times10^5\times4\pi\times10^{-7})} = 0.2190$ mm, $2\delta^2 = 9.59\times10^{-8}$ m². For 20 μm × 10 mm, $tR = 2\times10^{-7}$ m², ratio 2.09; the formula gives 0.287 W at 0.5 mT and 1.147 W at 1 mT; the computed loss at 1 mT is 0.402 W, 0.35 of the formula. For 10 μm × 5 mm, $tR = 5\times10^{-8}$ m², ratio 0.52; formula 0.0358 W, computed 0.0317 W at 1 mT.

How the curve was computed. The disc is cut into 200 concentric rings, each a resistor $\rho\cdot2\pi r/(t\,\Delta r)$ with uniform current through the thickness. Each ring's self-inductance uses the thin-loop formula with the rectangle's geometric mean distance, and each pair's mutual inductance is Neumann's integral for coaxial circles in one plane. The applied flux through each ring drives it; the coupled equations are solved and the losses summed. Doubling the rings changes the answer by about 0.1%. The model's own assumption — uniform current through the thickness — weakens as $t$ approaches $\delta$, so the curve is dashed beyond $\delta/4$.

Reading the curve. Thin foil: the formula, loss $\propto t$. Thicker: the eddy currents shield the interior and the loss falls. The peak, about 0.41 W near 15 μm, is a result of this model; the scrap's assumed kitchen-foil thickness, 20 μm, sits close to it — thin foil of kitchen thickness is near the worst case, as a result of the model, not a measurement.

How the pad can notice

The foil is a third coupled circuit, and unit 07's reflected impedance carries it back to the primary. Computed for the pad coil alone, the 20 μm scrap 2.5 mm above it:

$$\Delta Z = 0.087 - 0.111i\ \Omega .$$

The pad's $Q$ falls from 100 to 48, its inductance by 1.3%, its resonance rises 0.67%. The smaller scrap: $Q$ 100 → 93.

With a phone present, the scrap's loss is power the pad sends that the phone does not report receiving.

foreign object detection (WPC, §4.6, cited)

The field "can induce eddy currents in electrically-conductive materials ... The eddy currents cause those materials to heat up." The transmitter "cannot by itself distinguish between foreign objects and friendly metals that are insufficiently shielded", and "the specification does not define a single method for foreign object detection (FOD)".

Computed: the pad's coil, tuned to 140 kHz and driven at fixed voltage, bare and with the scrap above it. The resonance broadens, sinks and shifts: three things a transmitter could measure.

The reflected impedance. Each ring $j$ of the foil couples to the pad coil with mutual inductance $m_j$ (Neumann, coaxial circles 2.5 mm apart) and to the other rings through the ring model's matrix $Z_r = R + i\omega L_r$. Unit 07's argument with a vector of secondary currents gives $\Delta Z = \omega^2m^{\!\top}Z_r^{-1}m$: $0.0872 - 0.1112i\ \Omega$. The coil's resistance becomes $0.0833 + 0.0872 = 0.1705\ \Omega$ and its reactance $8.331 - 0.111 = 8.220\ \Omega$; $Q = 8.220/0.1705 = 48.2$. The inductance falls by $0.111/8.331 = 1.33\%$, so the resonance rises by $1/\sqrt{1 - 0.0133} - 1 = 0.67\%$. The 10 μm × 5 mm scrap: $\Delta Z = 0.0062 - 0.0022i\ \Omega$, $Q = 93.1$.

The figure. A series RLC driven at fixed voltage: $|I|^2 \propto 1/|Z|^2$, normalised to the bare peak. The loaded peak is lower by $(0.0833/0.1705)^2 = 0.239$, twice as wide, and moved up by 0.67%.

What the WPC says, and what it does not (§4.6 of the Introduction, cited). Foreign objects such as "coins, keys, paper clips" heat up; the transmitter and/or receiver "have to detect the presence of such foreign objects and take appropriate action to prevent the objects from heating up to unacceptably high temperatures"; the transmitter "cannot by itself distinguish between foreign objects and friendly metals that are insufficiently shielded" — friendly metals being, in its words, "metallic parts that are used in the construction of Power Transmitter and Power Receiver Products", usually shielded from the field; the specification "does not define a single method for foreign object detection (FOD)"; and compliance testing "verifies that a Power Transmitter does not heat up a set of reference foreign objects in a set of reference scenarios". This deck shows that a scrap changes quantities a transmitter could measure — $Q$, resonance, and unaccounted power — and does not claim which a given product uses.

The question, answered. How does 5 W cross 5 mm of air with nothing crossing? Two tuned coils with $U = 39.9$ hand energy back and forth forty times faster than they lose it; at the load $R_L = 3.32\ \Omega$ the driver supplies 5.257 W, coil 1 loses 0.132 W, 5.125 W crosses the gap as a Poynting flux carried by the product of the two coils' fields, coil 2 loses 0.125 W, and the phone receives 5 W.
