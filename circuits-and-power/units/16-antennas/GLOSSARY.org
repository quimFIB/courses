The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: unit 15's phone, which its pad charges with 5 W across 5 mm
at 95.1%, now 1 km from a base station. All assumed: 2.4 GHz
(\(\lambda = 12.49\) cm), the base station 20 W at 17 dBi, the phone's antenna
a half-wave dipole (\(G = 1.64\), \(73\ \Omega\)), the phone transmitting
0.2 W. The phone receives \(1.62\times10^{-7}\) W (\(-37.9\) dBm), a factor
\(3.1\times10^7\) short of 5 W. Contrast case: 1 m of 1 mm copper wire at
1 MHz, \(R_{\mathrm{rad}} = 8.78\) mΩ.

* Radiation from a current: the radiation resistance

- antenna :: A conductor shaped so that a current driven into it at a pair of
  terminals radiates, or so that a passing wave drives a current at its
  terminals. /Slide: A current element./
- current element :: A model: a straight segment of length \(l\ll\lambda\)
  carrying the same current \(i(t)\) along its whole length, with charges
  \(\pm q\), \(\mathrm dq/\mathrm dt = i\), at its ends. Its dipole moment
  changes as \(\dot{\vec p} = l\,i\,\hat z\).
- coherent sum (coherently) :: Adding the contributions \(q_j\vec a_j\) of many
  charges to \(\ddot{\vec p}\) before squaring: \(P = \mu_0|\sum_jq_j\vec
  a_j|^2/6\pi c\). For \(N\) charges in step it is \(N^2\) times one charge's
  power, not \(N\) times. /Slide: Add the fields, then square./
- radiation resistance :: The resistance \(R_{\mathrm{rad}}\) defined by
  \(\bar P = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^2\) for the mean radiated power,
  with \(\hat I\) the peak current at the feed (\(= R_{\mathrm{rad}}|I|^2\)
  with RMS phasors). For a current element, \(\tfrac{2\pi}3\eta_0(l/\lambda)^2
  = 789.0(l/\lambda)^2\ \Omega\); a quarter of that for the bare wire's
  triangular current. /Slide: Worked: the radiation resistance from Larmor./

* The fields of a current element: near and far

- near field :: The region \(kr\ll1\), i.e. \(r\ll\lambda/2\pi\), where a
  small source's fields are close to their quasi-static forms and fall as
  \(1/r^3\) (electric) and \(1/r^2\) (magnetic, for a current element). Unit
  15's pad works here. /Slide: Where near becomes far: r = λ/2π./
- far field :: The region \(kr\gg1\), where the fields are transverse, fall as
  \(1/r\), satisfy \(E = \eta_0H\), and carry energy to infinity.
- directivity :: \(D(\theta,\varphi) = 4\pi\mathcal U/P_{\mathrm{rad}}\), with
  \(\mathcal U\) the far-field power per unit solid angle; "the directivity" is
  its maximum. \(\oint D\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi\). A current element has
  \(D = 1.5\). /Slide: Directivity: where the power goes./
- isotropic radiator :: A reference that radiates equally in every direction,
  \(D = 1\); not a device.
- radiation efficiency :: \(e_{\mathrm{rad}} = P_{\mathrm{rad}}/P_{\mathrm{in}}
  = R_{\mathrm{rad}}/(R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}})\): the fraction of
  the power accepted at the terminals that is radiated.
  /Slide: Gain, efficiency, and decibels./
- gain :: \(G = e_{\mathrm{rad}}D = 4\pi\mathcal U/P_{\mathrm{in}}\). For a
  lossless antenna \(\oint G\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi\).
- decibel :: A power ratio \(x\) expressed as \(10\log_{10}x\) dB, so that
  products of ratios become sums.
- dBi :: A gain in decibels relative to an isotropic radiator: \(D = 1.5\) is
  1.76 dBi, 1.64 is 2.15 dBi.
- dBm :: A power in decibels relative to 1 mW: 1 W is 30 dBm, 20 W is 43.0 dBm,
  \(9.88\times10^{-11}\) W is \(-70.1\) dBm.

* A short antenna: mostly reactive, and when it is mostly lossy

- loss resistance :: The part \(R_{\mathrm{loss}}\) of an antenna's input
  resistance that heats its conductors. For a wire of radius \(a\gg\delta\)
  with a uniform current, \(\rho l/2\pi a\delta\); a third of that for a
  triangular current. /Slide: The antenna at its terminals./
- half-wave dipole :: A centre-fed wire half a wavelength long. With the
  standard assumed sinusoidal current, \(R_{\mathrm{in}}\approx73\ \Omega\) and
  \(D = 1.64\) (Balanis §4.6, cited; 73.08 Ω and 1.641 by numerical
  integration). 6.25 cm at 2.4 GHz. /Slide: The half-wave dipole./

* Reciprocity

- Lorentz reciprocity theorem :: In linear media with symmetric \(\varepsilon\)
  and \(\mu\), \(\int\vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b\,\mathrm dV = \int\vec E_b\cdot\vec
  J_a\,\mathrm dV\) for any two source distributions at one frequency; for two
  antennas, \(z_{12} = z_{21}\). Cited (Balanis §3.8; Pozar §1.9), proved here
  for two current elements. /Slide: The Lorentz reciprocity theorem, cited./

* The receiving antenna: a Thévenin source, its aperture, and the lost half

- effective length :: The length \(l_e\) with \(|V_{\mathrm{oc}}| = |\vec
  E\cdot\hat l|\,l_e\) for an incident field \(\vec E\): \(l\) for a current
  element, \(l/2\) for a short bare wire. Equal to \(\dot p/\hat I\).
  /Slide: The open-circuit voltage: effective length./
- effective aperture :: \(A_e = P_L/S\): the power delivered to a matched load
  divided by the incident intensity, for matched polarisation. \(3\lambda^2/8\pi\)
  for any short dipole (18.6 cm² at 2.4 GHz); in general \(G\lambda^2/4\pi\).
  /Slide: Worked: the collecting area of a short dipole./
- brightness :: Power per unit area, solid angle and bandwidth. Black-body
  radiation at temperature \(T\) has \(2k_BT/\lambda^2\) in the
  Rayleigh–Jeans regime, half of it in each polarisation.
  /Slide: The same constant from thermodynamics: a sketch./
- re-radiated power :: The power a receiving antenna's own current radiates
  back out. For a matched lossless current element it equals the power
  delivered to the load: the "lost half" in its Thévenin resistance.
  /Slide: Where the lost half goes./
- scattering cross-section :: The re-radiated power divided by the incident
  intensity. For a tuned, shorted, lossless current element, \(4A_e =
  3\lambda^2/2\pi = 0.477\lambda^2\). /Slide: Open, matched, shorted — and the limits./

* Friis: how much arrives

- Friis transmission equation :: \(P_r/P_t = G_tG_r(\lambda/4\pi d)^2 =
  A_tA_r/(\lambda d)^2\), for free space, far field, matched loads and
  polarisation, gains including efficiency. /Slide: The Friis transmission equation./
- path gain :: \((\lambda/4\pi d)^2\), the fraction passed between two
  isotropic antennas: \(-100.1\) dB at 2.4 GHz and 1 km. It measures spreading,
  not absorption.
- effective isotropic radiated power :: \(P_tG_t\): the power an isotropic
  antenna would need to give the same intensity in the main direction; 1002 W
  for the base station. /Slide: Worked: 0.1 nW, and the phone's link./

* Why beaming power is hard

- Fresnel region :: The region closer to an aperture than its Fraunhofer
  distance \(2w^2/\lambda\) (but outside its near field), where its field is
  not yet the far-field pattern. A pair with \(w_tw_r\gtrsim\lambda d\) — a
  receiver as large as the beam's spot — lies in the larger aperture's Fresnel
  region, and Friis does not apply.
  /Slide: When Friis fails./
- Fraunhofer distance :: \(d_F = 2w^2/\lambda\) for an aperture of width \(w\):
  beyond it the path difference across the aperture is below \(\lambda/16\) and
  the receiver is in the far field. At \(d_F\), equal circular apertures get at
  most \(\pi^2/64 = 15\%\) by Friis.
  /Slide: The far-field distance, and Friis's ceiling./
- rectenna :: A rectifying antenna: an array of receiving antennas, each
  feeding a rectifier, turning microwave power into direct current. The first
  was conceived at Raytheon in 1963 and built by R. George of Purdue (Brown
  1980). /Slide: The rectenna./
