Antennas: radiation resistance, reciprocity, and why beaming power is hard

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You derive the short dipole's radiation resistance from Larmor, \(\tfrac{2\pi}3\eta_0(l/\lambda)^2\approx790(l/\lambda)^2\ \Omega\) for a uniform current of peak amplitude \(\hat I\), and show by Friis that 1 W at 2.4 GHz between isotropic antennas 1 km apart delivers about 0.1 nW (\(9.9\times10^{-11}\) W).

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about three and a half hours. P1 and P10 are the checkpoint's; P5, P6, P7 and P9 are proofs. Conventions as in the deck: time dependence \(e^{+i\omega t}\), so an outgoing wave is \(e^{i(\omega t - kr)}/r\) with \(k = \omega/c = 2\pi/\lambda\) (MR 24 and Jackson write \(e^{-i\omega t}\): replace \(i\) by \(-i\)); the radiation resistance is defined with the peak feed current, \(\bar P = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^2\), and field amplitudes are peak, so the complex Poynting vector is \(\tfrac12\vec E\times\vec H^*\); every port is marked passively, its current entering at \(+\).

Constants: \(\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\) H/m, \(\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\) F/m, so \(c = 2.998\times10^8\) m/s and \(\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} = \mu_0c = 376.73\ \Omega\); copper \(\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\)m; \(k_B = 1.380649\times10^{-23}\) J/K and \(h = 6.62607015\times10^{-34}\) J s (CODATA); \(GM_E = 3.986004418\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\), \(R_E = 6378.137\) km and the Earth's angular velocity \(\omega_E = 7.292115\times10^{-5}\) rad/s (WGS 84), so the sidereal day is \(2\pi/\omega_E = 86\,164.1\) s. The skin depth \(\delta = \sqrt{2\rho/\omega\mu_0}\) is stated from branch 10. Every device value — frequencies, powers, gains, wire sizes, coil \(Q\)s, apertures — is an assumption for the problem.

Radiation, and the short antenna

P1 Radiation resistance from Larmor routine

exercises: the coherent sum, \(\ddot{\vec p} = l\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt\), cycle averages, \(\eta_0 = \mu_0c\) — the checkpoint's first part

A current element of length \(l\ll\lambda\) along \(\hat z\) carries the uniform current \(i(t) = \hat I\cos\omega t\). Start from MR 24's Larmor formula in the form \(P = \mu_0|\ddot{\vec p}|^2/6\pi c\).

  1. Show that \(\ddot{\vec p} = l\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt\,\hat z\), and explain why the Larmor formula is applied to the total \(\ddot{\vec p}\) of the conduction electrons rather than electron by electron. If \(N\) electrons move in step, by what factor would the electron-by-electron sum understate the power?
  2. Find the mean radiated power and show that \(R_{\mathrm{rad}} = \mu_0\omega^2l^2/6\pi c = \tfrac{2\pi}3\eta_0(l/\lambda)^2\). Check its units.
  3. Evaluate the coefficient. Why do many books write \(80\pi^2\), and by how much does it differ?
  4. Evaluate \(R_{\mathrm{rad}}\) for 10 cm at 100 MHz, 1 m at 1 MHz, and 1 cm at 2.4 GHz.
  5. A bare centre-fed wire carries \(i(z) = \hat I(1 - 2|z|/l)\cos\omega t\). Show that its radiation resistance, referred to the feed current, is a quarter of the element's.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every part follows from one quantity, \(\dot{\vec p}\). For the element, think of the charges \(\pm q\) that continuity piles up at its ends.

Rung 2

\(\vec p = q\,l\,\hat z\) and \(\dot q = i\). The radiation field is linear in \(\ddot{\vec p}\), the power quadratic. For part 5, \(\dot p = \int i(z)\,\mathrm dz\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\mathrm di/\mathrm dt = -\omega\hat I\sin\omega t\), so \(P(t) = \mu_0l^2\omega^2\hat I^2\sin^2\omega t/6\pi c\).

Worked solution

Idea. The element is a changing dipole; Larmor gives its power; averaging and comparing with \(\tfrac12R\hat I^2\) defines \(R\).

1. Continuity at the top end: \(\mathrm dq/\mathrm dt = i\), and \(-q\) at the bottom. So \(\vec p = ql\hat z\), \(\dot{\vec p} = li\hat z\), \(\ddot{\vec p} = l\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt\,\hat z\) (equivalently MR 24's \(\int\vec J\,\mathrm d^3y = \dot{\vec p}\)). MR 24's radiation field is linear in \(\ddot{\vec p} = \sum_jq_j\vec a_j\), so the fields of all the charges add before the power is formed: \(P = \mu_0|\sum q_j\vec a_j|^2/6\pi c\). For \(N\) charges with equal \(q\vec a\) this is \(N^2\) times one charge's power; summing powers would give \(N\) times. The understatement is the factor \(N\) — for the 10 cm wire at 100 MHz, of order the \(3.5\times10^{20}\) electrons in its skin.

2. \(P(t) = \frac{\mu_0l^2\omega^2\hat I^2}{6\pi c}\sin^2\omega t\), mean \(\frac{\mu_0l^2\omega^2\hat I^2}{12\pi c}\). Setting this equal to \(\tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^2\): \(R_{\mathrm{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0\omega^2l^2}{6\pi c}\). With \(\omega = 2\pi c/\lambda\): \(\frac{\mu_0\cdot4\pi^2c^2l^2}{6\pi c\lambda^2} = \frac{2\pi}3\mu_0c\frac{l^2}{\lambda^2}\), and \(\mu_0c = \eta_0\). Units: \(\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\cdot s^{-2}\cdot m^2/(m\,s^{-1})} = \mathrm{H\,s^{-1}} = \Omega\).

3. \(\frac{2\pi}3\times376.73 = 789.03\ \Omega\). Books round \(\eta_0\) to \(120\pi = 376.99\ \Omega\), giving \(\frac{2\pi}3\cdot120\pi = 80\pi^2 = 789.57\ \Omega\): 0.07% higher. Both round to 790.

4. \(l/\lambda = 0.1/2.998 = 0.03336\): \(R = 789.03\times1.113\times10^{-3} = 0.878\ \Omega\) (0.879 Ω with \(80\pi^2\)). \(1/299.8 = 0.003336\): 8.78 mΩ. \(0.01/0.1249 = 0.0801\): 5.06 Ω.

5. \(\dot p = \int_{-l/2}^{l/2}\hat I(1 - 2|z|/l)\,\mathrm dz\cos\omega t = \hat I\,(l/2)\cos\omega t\): half the element's, for the same feed current. Power goes as \(\ddot p^2\), so a quarter, and \(R = \tfrac\pi6\eta_0(l/\lambda)^2 = 197.3(l/\lambda)^2\ \Omega\) (\(20\pi^2 = 197.4\) with \(120\pi\)).

Check. Energy: MR 24's oscillating dipole radiates \(\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4/12\pi c\); here \(p_0 = l\hat I/\omega\), giving \(\mu_0l^2\hat I^2\omega^2/12\pi c\), part 2's mean ✓. Limit: \(\omega\to0\) gives \(R\to0\) — a steady current does not radiate ✓.

P2 A short antenna's budget medium

exercises: skin-depth loss, a capacitance estimate, unit 05's \(Q\) and bandwidth, unit 08's \(\Gamma\) — "mostly reactive, and once tuned, mostly lossy"

Two copper wire dipoles of radius \(a = 1\) mm, centre-fed, modelled with a uniform current: 10 cm at 100 MHz, and 1 m at 1 MHz.

  1. Find \(\delta\), \(R_{\mathrm{loss}} = \rho l/2\pi a\delta\) and the radiation efficiency of the wire alone. Why do both have the same \(R_{\mathrm{loss}}\)? Repeat the efficiency for the triangular current, showing first that its loss resistance is a third.
  2. Estimate the capacitance of the two arms, \(C\approx\pi\varepsilon_0l/[2\ln(l/2a)]\), from the potential of a line charge, and hence \(X_a\) and \(Q_a = |X_a|/R_{\mathrm{rad}}\).
  3. The reactance is cancelled by a series coil of quality factor \(Q_L\) (assume 100, then 200). Show that the efficiency is \(R_{\mathrm{rad}}/(R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}} + |X_a|/Q_L)\to Q_L/Q_a\) for \(Q_a\gg Q_L\), and evaluate it.
  4. Find the half-power bandwidth of the tuned 10 cm dipole with a lossless coil and with the \(Q_L = 100\) coil.
  5. Compare the 1 m wire's \(R_{\mathrm{rad}}\) with unit 05's 1 MHz coil, \(R = 10\ \Omega\).
  6. A half-wave dipole (take \(73\ \Omega\)) and the bare 10 cm dipole are each connected to a \(50\ \Omega\) line. Find \(\Gamma\) and the fraction of the forward power delivered, first showing from unit 08's phasor waves \(V(z) = V_+e^{-i\beta z} + V_-e^{i\beta z}\) that \(\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)\) holds for a complex load and that the fraction delivered is \(1 - |\Gamma|^2\). Optional, if you took branch 09: what quarter-wave section matches the half-wave dipole?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a table of resistances, two efficiencies per case, and a statement about which of "reactive" and "lossy" holds when.

Rung 2

A uniform line charge \(q'\) on a segment of length \(h\) has potential about \((q'/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(h/a)\) at its middle; two arms are two capacitors in series. The coil's resistance is \(\omega L/Q_L\) with \(\omega L = |X_a|\). Unit 05: bandwidth \(f_0/Q\) with \(Q = |X|/R_{\mathrm{total}}\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\omega\mu_0 = 2\pi\times10^8\times4\pi\times10^{-7} = 789.6\), so \(\delta = \sqrt{2\times1.68\times10^{-8}/789.6} = 6.52\ \mu\mathrm m\).

Worked solution

Idea. The antenna is \(R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}} + iX_a\). The wire's own loss is small or not depending on the case; the reactance is always huge, and the coil that cancels it brings a resistance \(|X_a|/Q_L\) that swamps \(R_{\mathrm{rad}}\).

1. \(\delta = 6.52\ \mu\)m at 100 MHz and \(65.2\ \mu\)m at 1 MHz (\(\delta\propto f^{-1/2}\)). \(R_{\mathrm{loss}} = 1.68\times10^{-8}\times0.1/(2\pi\times10^{-3}\times6.52\times10^{-6}) = 0.0410\ \Omega\), and for 1 m the same, because \(l\) and \(\delta\) both grew tenfold. \(e = 0.878/(0.878 + 0.041) = 95.5\%\) and \(0.00878/(0.00878 + 0.0410) = 17.6\%\). Triangular: the loss is \(\tfrac12R'\hat I^2\int_{-l/2}^{l/2}(1 - 2|z|/l)^2\mathrm dz = \tfrac12R'\hat I^2\,l/3\), so \(R_{\mathrm{loss}}\) is a third, while \(R_{\mathrm{rad}}\) is a quarter (P1): \(e = 0.2195/(0.2195 + 0.01366) = 94.1\%\) and \(0.002195/0.01586 = 13.8\%\).

2. An arm of length \(h = l/2\) with charge \(q'h\) has potential about \(\frac{q'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\frac{\mathrm dz}{\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}}\approx\frac{q'}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\ln\frac ha\), so \(C_{\mathrm{arm}}\approx2\pi\varepsilon_0h/\ln(h/a)\); in series, \(C\approx\pi\varepsilon_0h/\ln(h/a) = \pi\varepsilon_0l/[2\ln(l/2a)]\). 10 cm: \(\ln50 = 3.912\), \(C = 0.356\) pF, \(X_a = -1/\omega C = -4.48\) kΩ, \(Q_a = 4477/0.878 = 5100\). 1 m: \(\ln500 = 6.215\), \(C = 2.24\) pF, \(X_a = -71.1\) kΩ, \(Q_a = 8.1\times10^6\). An estimate: factors of order one are dropped.

3. At resonance the loop is three resistances in series and only \(R_{\mathrm{rad}}\)'s share is radiated: \(e = 1/(1 + R_{\mathrm{loss}}/R_{\mathrm{rad}} + Q_a/Q_L)\to Q_L/Q_a\). 10 cm: \(|X_a|/Q_L = 44.8\ \Omega\), \(e = 0.878/45.7 = 1.9\%\); at \(Q_L = 200\), 3.8%. 1 m: \(711\ \Omega\), \(e = 1.2\times10^{-5}\); at 200, \(2.5\times10^{-5}\).

4. Lossless coil: \(Q = 4477/0.919 = 4870\), bandwidth \(10^8/4870 = 20.5\) kHz. With \(Q_L = 100\): \(Q = 4477/45.7 = 98\), bandwidth 1.02 MHz. Fifty times the band for a fiftieth of the efficiency: the loss that wastes the power widens the band. (Approximate: the antenna's reactance is treated as a fixed capacitor near resonance.)

5. \(10/0.00878 = 1139\): unit 05's coil alone would dissipate over a thousand times what the wire radiates or collects.

6. At the load, \(V = V_+'(1 + \Gamma)\) and \(I = V_+'(1 - \Gamma)/Z_0\) with \(V_+' = V_+e^{-i\beta l}\) and \(\Gamma\) the ratio of backward to forward wave there; \(V = Z_LI\) gives \(\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)\), complex or not. The power in is \(\mathrm{Re}(VI^*) = (|V_+|^2/Z_0)\mathrm{Re}[(1 + \Gamma)(1 - \Gamma^*)] = (|V_+|^2/Z_0)(1 - |\Gamma|^2)\), because \(\Gamma - \Gamma^*\) is imaginary; the forward wave alone brings \(|V_+|^2/Z_0\). Then \(\Gamma = 23/123 = 0.187\); delivered \(1 - 0.187^2 = 96.5\%\). Short dipole, \(Z_L = 0.919 - 4477i\ \Omega\): \(1 - |\Gamma|^2 = 4\cdot50\cdot0.919/(50.92^2 + 4477^2) = 9.2\times10^{-6}\), so \(|\Gamma| = 0.999995\): without a matching network almost everything is reflected. Optional: \(Z_1 = \sqrt{73\times50} = 60.4\ \Omega\).

Check. Units of \(C\): \(\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}\,m}\) ✓. Limit: \(Q_L\to\infty\) returns the wire's own 95.5% ✓. Energy: \(e\le1\) in every row ✓. The honest summary: always mostly reactive; lossy on its own only at 1 m and 1 MHz; mostly lossy in both cases once tuned with a real coil.

P3 Directivity and gain routine

exercises: \(D = 4\pi\mathcal U/P_{\mathrm{rad}}\), the solid-angle integral, gain with efficiency, decibels

  1. Show that a current element has \(D(\theta) = \tfrac32\sin^2\theta\) and \(D = 1.5\), or 1.76 dBi.
  2. Prove \(\oint G\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi e_{\mathrm{rad}}\), so \(4\pi\) for a lossless antenna.
  3. Find the gains, in dBi, of the two wires of P2 (wire alone, uniform current).
  4. The half-wave dipole's \(D = 1.64\) and \(R_{\mathrm{in}}\approx73\ \Omega\) are cited (Balanis §4.6). Express \(D\) in dBi. Optional numerical check, which the solution does not depend on: with the sinusoidal current, \(D = 4/\mathrm{Cin}(2\pi)\) and \(R = (\eta_0/4\pi)\mathrm{Cin}(2\pi)\), \(\mathrm{Cin}(x) = \int_0^x(1 - \cos t)/t\,\mathrm dt\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Directivity is a normalised angular distribution: you need the shape of \(\mathcal U\) and its integral over the sphere.

Rung 2

MR 24: \(\mathcal U\propto\sin^2\theta\). \(\oint\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\Omega = 2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\). For part 2 use \(G = e_{\mathrm{rad}}D\) and the definition of \(D\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,\mathrm d\theta = \int_{-1}^1(1 - u^2)\,\mathrm du = \tfrac43\).

Worked solution

Idea. \(D\) redistributes a fixed total; \(G\) multiplies by the fraction actually radiated.

1. \(\mathcal U = \mathcal U_0\sin^2\theta\), \(P_{\mathrm{rad}} = \mathcal U_0\cdot2\pi\cdot\tfrac43 = \tfrac{8\pi}3\mathcal U_0\), so \(D = 4\pi\sin^2\theta/(8\pi/3) = \tfrac32\sin^2\theta\), maximum 1.5; \(10\log_{10}1.5 = 1.76\) dBi.

2. \(\oint G\,\mathrm d\Omega = e_{\mathrm{rad}}\oint D\,\mathrm d\Omega = e_{\mathrm{rad}}\frac{4\pi}{P_{\mathrm{rad}}}\oint\mathcal U\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi e_{\mathrm{rad}}\). \(\square\)

3. \(G = 0.955\times1.5 = 1.43\), or 1.56 dBi; \(G = 0.176\times1.5 = 0.265\), or \(-5.77\) dBi. A gain below 1 is no paradox: the antenna turns most of its input into heat.

4. \(10\log_{10}1.64 = 2.15\) dBi. Numerically \(\mathrm{Cin}(2\pi) = 2.4377\), so \(D = 1.641\) and \(R = 376.73\times2.4377/4\pi = 73.08\ \Omega\) (73.13 Ω with \(120\pi\)).

Check. Dimensionless ✓. Limit: an isotropic \(\mathcal U\) gives \(D = 1\) ✓; the half-wave dipole's pattern is narrower than \(\sin^2\theta\), so its \(D\) exceeds 1.5 ✓.

Near and far

P4 Near and far routine

exercises: the exact potential of a point element, a curl in spherical coordinates, \(\lambda/2\pi\), the \(d^{-6}\) and \(d^{-2}\) laws

  1. From \(\vec A = (\mu_0\hat Il/4\pi)(e^{-ikr}/r)\hat z\), derive \(B_\varphi = (\mu_0\hat Il\sin\theta/4\pi)(ik/r + 1/r^2)e^{-ikr}\). Identify the two terms with MR 23 and MR 24.
  2. Where are the two terms equal? Evaluate that radius at 140 kHz, 9.9 MHz and 2.4 GHz, and \(kr\) for unit 15's pad at 5 mm and for the base station at 1 km.
  3. Unit 15's tuned pad: \(U = 0.55\) at 80 mm and 0.078 at 160 mm, with \(\eta_{\max} = U^2/(1 + \sqrt{1 + U^2})^2\). Compare the fall in \(\eta_{\max}\) over this doubling with \(d^{-6}\), and with the far field's \(d^{-2}\).
  4. Does the pad radiate? A loop of \(N\) turns and radius \(a\) has \(R_{\mathrm{rad}} = \tfrac\pi6\eta_0N^2(ka)^4\) (Balanis §5.2, cited; the deck's notes to Near against far derive it from the current element). Evaluate it for \(N = 10\), \(a = 20\) mm at 140 kHz, against the coil's \(0.0833\ \Omega\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is one curl of a radial function times a fixed vector; the rest is arithmetic with \(\lambda = c/f\).

Rung 2

\(\nabla\times(f(r)\hat z) = f'(r)\,\hat r\times\hat z\), and \(\hat r\times\hat z = -\sin\theta\,\hat\varphi\). The ratio of the two terms is \(kr\).

Rung 3

First line: \(f = Ce^{-ikr}/r\) gives \(f' = -C(ik/r + 1/r^2)e^{-ikr}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The point element's potential is exact; its curl splits into a static part and a radiating part whose ratio is \(kr\).

1. \(B_\varphi = -f'\sin\theta = C\sin\theta(ik/r + 1/r^2)e^{-ikr}\), \(C = \mu_0\hat Il/4\pi\). For \(kr\ll1\): \(\mu_0\hat Il\sin\theta/4\pi r^2\), MR 23's Biot–Savart field of the element. For \(kr\gg1\): \(ik\mu_0\hat Il\sin\theta/4\pi r\), MR 24's \(\mu_0\ddot{\vec p}\times\hat r/4\pi cr\) with \(\ddot p = i\omega\hat Il\).

2. Equal at \(kr = 1\), \(r = \lambda/2\pi\): 341 m, 4.82 m, 1.99 cm. \(kr = 2\pi\times1.4\times10^5\times0.005/c = 1.5\times10^{-5}\) for the pad; \(50.3\times1000 = 5.0\times10^4\) for the base station.

3. \(\eta_{\max}(0.55) = 0.0660\), \(\eta_{\max}(0.078) = 0.00152\): a factor 44. The \(d^{-6}\) law would give 64, the far field's \(d^{-2}\) only 4. The difference from 64 comes from \(U\) falling by \(0.55/0.078 = 7.1\) rather than 8 (the coils, of radius 20 mm, are not yet far apart at 80 mm) and from \(U = 0.55\) not being \(\ll1\) (\(U^2/4 = 0.076\), 15% off).

4. \(k = 2.934\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(ka = 5.87\times10^{-5}\), \((ka)^4 = 1.19\times10^{-17}\); \(R_{\mathrm{rad}} = 197.3\times100\times1.19\times10^{-17} = 2.3\times10^{-13}\ \Omega\), a fraction \(2.8\times10^{-12}\) of the copper's. The pad is not an antenna.

Check. Units of \(B\): \(\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,A\,m\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm T\) ✓. Limit: \(\omega\to0\) leaves only Biot–Savart ✓.

P5 Proof: the complex power of a current element medium

exercises: Ampère–Maxwell in phasor form, the complex Poynting vector, reading reactive power — "mostly reactive", derived

  1. From P4's \(B_\varphi\) and \(\vec E = (c^2/i\omega)\nabla\times\vec B\) outside the source, derive \(E_\theta = \frac{i\eta_0k\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi r}\big[1 + \frac1{ikr} - \frac1{(kr)^2}\big]e^{-ikr}\). Check that its \(1/r^3\) term is MR 23's static dipole field with \(p = \hat Il/i\omega\).
  2. Prove \(\oint\tfrac12\vec E\times\vec H^*\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = P_{\mathrm{rad}}[1 - i/(kr)^3]\) over a sphere of radius \(r\), with \(P_{\mathrm{rad}} = \eta_0k^2l^2\hat I^2/12\pi\).
  3. Explain why the real part must be independent of \(r\), and what the sign of the imaginary part means.
  4. Evaluate \(1/(kr)^3\) for the 10 cm dipole at 100 MHz on a sphere of radius 5 cm.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 2 needs only \(E_\theta\) and \(H_\varphi\): the radial flux is \(\tfrac12E_\theta H_\varphi^*\). The phases \(e^{\mp ikr}\) cancel.

Rung 2

\((\nabla\times\vec B)_\theta = -\frac1r\partial_r(rB_\varphi)\). With \(x = kr\), \(H_\varphi\propto i(1 + 1/ix)\). Use \(1/(ix) = -i/x\).

Rung 3

First line: \(rB_\varphi\propto(ik + 1/r)e^{-ikr}\), so \(\partial_r(rB_\varphi)\propto(k^2 - ik/r - 1/r^2)e^{-ikr}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The flux of the complex Poynting vector through a sphere is radiated power plus \(i\times\)(reactive power of the field outside).

1. \(E_\theta = \frac c{ik}\cdot\big(-\frac1r\big)\frac{\mu_0\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi}(k^2 - \frac{ik}r - \frac1{r^2})e^{-ikr} = \frac{i\eta_0k\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi r}\big[1 - \frac i{kr} - \frac1{(kr)^2}\big]e^{-ikr}\), using \(c\mu_0 = \eta_0\) and \(-1/(ik) = i/k\); and \(-i/(kr) = 1/(ikr)\). The \(1/r^3\) term: \(\frac{i\eta_0k\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi r}\cdot\frac{-1}{k^2r^2} = \frac{\eta_0\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi ikr^3}\), and \(\eta_0/k = 1/(\omega\varepsilon_0)\), so this is \(\frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}\) with \(p = \hat Il/(i\omega)\) — the \(\theta\)-component of MR 23's dipole field. Continuity: \(q = \hat I/(i\omega)\), the phasor form of \(\dot q = i\) ✓.

2. \(H_\varphi = \frac{ik\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi r}(1 + \frac1{ikr})e^{-ikr}\). Then \(\tfrac12E_\theta H_\varphi^* = \frac{\eta_0k^2\hat I^2l^2\sin^2\theta}{32\pi^2r^2}\,(1 - \frac ix - \frac1{x^2})(1 + \frac ix)\), and the bracket is \(1 + \frac ix - \frac ix + \frac1{x^2} - \frac1{x^2} - \frac i{x^3} = 1 - \frac i{x^3}\). Integrate with \(r^2\mathrm d\Omega\) and \(\oint\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\Omega = 8\pi/3\): \(\frac{\eta_0k^2l^2\hat I^2}{12\pi}(1 - \frac i{(kr)^3})\). \(\square\) And \(\frac{\eta_0k^2l^2}{12\pi} = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{\mu_0\omega^2l^2}{6\pi c} = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\) ✓.

3. Between two spheres there are no sources and no loss, so by Poynting's theorem the mean power through each is the same. The imaginary part is \(2\omega(\bar W_m - \bar W_e)\) for the field outside the sphere (unit 06's reactive power); negative means more electric than magnetic energy: capacitive, as the end charges suggest.

4. \(k = 2.096\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(kr = 0.1048\), \((kr)^{-3} = 869\): the field outside the antenna's own sphere holds 869 times the radiated power as reactive power. That is "mostly reactive", from the fields alone.

Check. Units: \(\Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-2}\,A^2\,m^2} = \mathrm W\) ✓. Limit \(kr\to\infty\): the imaginary part vanishes, a pure outgoing wave ✓.

Reciprocity

P6 Proof: reciprocity for two current elements medium

exercises: the induced port voltage, the field kernel as a symmetric matrix, where a proof uses its hypothesis

  1. Show that a field \(\vec E\) arriving at a short element \(l\hat l\) produces, at its port (passive marking), the voltage \(-l\hat l\cdot\vec E\).
  2. Show that the field of element 1 can be written \(\vec E_1(\vec r) = \frac{I_1l_1}{4\pi i\omega\varepsilon_0}\mathsf G(\vec r - \vec r_1)\hat l_1\) with \(\mathsf G = k^2g\,\mathsf 1 + \nabla\nabla g\), \(g = e^{-ikr}/r\).
  3. Prove \(z_{12} = z_{21}\).
  4. Suppose a medium changed the kernel to \(\mathsf G + \mathsf K\) with \(\mathsf K\) antisymmetric (\(\mathsf K^{\mathsf T} = -\mathsf K\)) and even in \(\vec r\). Show that then \(z_{12} - z_{21}\) is generally non-zero. Which step of part 3 failed?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a statement about a bilinear form \(\hat l_2\cdot\mathsf M\hat l_1\): it is symmetric in the two vectors exactly when \(\mathsf M\) is a symmetric matrix.

Rung 2

Part 1: the work the field does on the element's current, \(\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\), is power the port delivers. Part 2: \(\nabla\times\nabla\times(g\hat l) = \nabla(\hat l\cdot\nabla g) - \hat l\nabla^2g\) and \(\nabla^2g = -k^2g\) away from the origin.

Rung 3

First line of part 3: \(z_{21} = -\frac{l_1l_2}{4\pi i\omega\varepsilon_0}\hat l_2\cdot\mathsf G(\vec r_2 - \vec r_1)\hat l_1\).

Worked solution

Idea. Each coupling is the incident field projected on the other element; the field kernel is a Hessian plus a multiple of the identity, hence symmetric.

1. The field does work on the element's current at the rate \(i\,l\,\hat l\cdot\vec E\) (\(\vec E\) uniform over the short element). That energy enters the circuit at the port, which therefore absorbs \(-i\,l\,\hat l\cdot\vec E\); under the passive convention \(vi\) is power absorbed, so \(v = -l\hat l\cdot\vec E\). It does not depend on \(i\), so it holds with the port open.

2. \(\vec A_1 = \frac{\mu_0I_1l_1}{4\pi}g\hat l_1\); \(\vec E = \frac{c^2}{i\omega}\nabla\times\nabla\times\vec A\) and \(\nabla\times\nabla\times(g\hat l) = (\hat l\cdot\nabla)\nabla g + k^2g\hat l = \mathsf G\hat l\); \(c^2\mu_0 = 1/\varepsilon_0\).

3. \(z_{21} = -l_2\hat l_2\cdot\vec E_1(\vec r_2)/I_1 = -\frac{l_1l_2}{4\pi i\omega\varepsilon_0}\hat l_2\cdot\mathsf G(\vec r_2 - \vec r_1)\hat l_1\), and \(z_{12}\) the same with 1 and 2 exchanged. \(\nabla\nabla g\) is the Hessian \(\partial_i\partial_jg\), symmetric because partial derivatives commute; for radial \(g\) it is \(g''\hat r\hat r^{\mathsf T} + (g'/r)(\mathsf 1 - \hat r\hat r^{\mathsf T})\), even in \(\vec r\). So \(\hat l_1\cdot\mathsf G(\vec r_1 - \vec r_2)\hat l_2 = \hat l_2\cdot\mathsf G^{\mathsf T}(\vec r_2 - \vec r_1)\hat l_1 = \hat l_2\cdot\mathsf G(\vec r_2 - \vec r_1)\hat l_1\): \(z_{12} = z_{21}\). \(\square\)

4. With \(\mathsf K\) added, \(z_{12} - z_{21}\propto\hat l_1\cdot\mathsf K\hat l_2 - \hat l_2\cdot\mathsf K\hat l_1 = 2\,\hat l_1\cdot\mathsf K\hat l_2\), which is non-zero for suitable orientations whenever \(\mathsf K\ne0\) (e.g. \(\mathsf K_{xy} = -\mathsf K_{yx} = \kappa\), \(\hat l_1 = \hat x\), \(\hat l_2 = \hat y\)). The failed step is \(\mathsf G^{\mathsf T} = \mathsf G\) — the analogue of unit 03's symmetric \(R_e\hat\imath_e\tilde\imath_e\), which a dependent source breaks.

Check. Units: \(\mathrm m^2\cdot\mathrm m^{-3}/(\mathrm s^{-1}\,\mathrm F\,\mathrm m^{-1}) = \Omega\) ✓. Limit \(\omega\to0\): \(\mathsf G\to\nabla\nabla(1/r)\), the electrostatic coupling of two dipoles, symmetric as MR 23's dipole–dipole energy is ✓.

P7 Proof: the Lorentz reciprocity theorem hard

exercises: vector identities with Maxwell's curl equations, the surface term at infinity, recovering a special case

Two sets of sources \(\vec J_a\), \(\vec J_b\) at one frequency, in a medium with scalar \(\varepsilon\), \(\mu\), make fields \((\vec E_a, \vec H_a)\) and \((\vec E_b, \vec H_b)\).

  1. From \(\nabla\times\vec E = -i\omega\mu\vec H\) and \(\nabla\times\vec H = \vec J + i\omega\varepsilon\vec E\), derive \(\nabla\cdot(\vec E_a\times\vec H_b - \vec E_b\times\vec H_a) = \vec E_b\cdot\vec J_a - \vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b\).
  2. Show that the surface integral of the left side over a sphere of radius \(R\to\infty\) in free space vanishes, using that both far fields are transverse with \(\vec H = \hat r\times\vec E/\eta_0\).
  3. Conclude \(\int\vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b = \int\vec E_b\cdot\vec J_a\), and recover P6's \(z_{12} = z_{21}\) for two current elements.
  4. Where did the proof use that \(\varepsilon\) and \(\mu\) are scalars (or symmetric matrices)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is an identity that holds point by point; part 3 is the divergence theorem applied to it.

Rung 2

\(\nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec B) = \vec B\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) - \vec A\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B)\). For part 2, \(\vec E\times(\hat r\times\vec F) = \hat r(\vec E\cdot\vec F) - \vec F(\vec E\cdot\hat r)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\nabla\cdot(\vec E_a\times\vec H_b) = \vec H_b\cdot(-i\omega\mu\vec H_a) - \vec E_a\cdot(\vec J_b + i\omega\varepsilon\vec E_b)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The cross terms between the two solutions cancel exactly when the medium's response is symmetric; what remains is a divergence whose integral is a surface term, and the far fields kill it.

1. As in the first line, and \(\nabla\cdot(\vec E_b\times\vec H_a) = \vec H_a\cdot(-i\omega\mu\vec H_b) - \vec E_b\cdot(\vec J_a + i\omega\varepsilon\vec E_a)\). Subtract: the terms \(-i\omega\mu\vec H_a\cdot\vec H_b\) and \(-i\omega\varepsilon\vec E_a\cdot\vec E_b\) appear in both and cancel, leaving \(\vec E_b\cdot\vec J_a - \vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b\). \(\square\)

2. On the sphere, \(\hat r\cdot(\vec E_a\times\vec H_b) = \frac1{\eta_0}\hat r\cdot(\vec E_a\times(\hat r\times\vec E_b)) = \frac1{\eta_0}\hat r\cdot[\hat r(\vec E_a\cdot\vec E_b) - \vec E_b(\vec E_a\cdot\hat r)] = \frac1{\eta_0}\vec E_a\cdot\vec E_b\), since \(\vec E_a\cdot\hat r = 0\). The same with \(a\), \(b\) exchanged gives the same, so the integrand cancels at order \(1/R^2\); corrections are \(O(1/R^3)\) against area \(4\pi R^2\), so the integral tends to 0.

3. Integrate part 1 over all space: \(0 = \int(\vec E_b\cdot\vec J_a - \vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b)\,\mathrm dV\). \(\square\) For element \(b\) fed with \(I_b\), \(\int\vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b = I_bl_b\hat l_b\cdot\vec E_a(\vec r_b) = -I_bz_{ba}I_a\) by P6 part 1, and likewise \(\int\vec E_b\cdot\vec J_a = -I_az_{ab}I_b\). So \(z_{ab} = z_{ba}\).

4. In the cancellation: \(\vec H_b\cdot\mu\vec H_a = \vec H_a\cdot\mu\vec H_b\) and \(\vec E_a\cdot\varepsilon\vec E_b = \vec E_b\cdot\varepsilon\vec E_a\), true for scalars or symmetric matrices and false otherwise.

Check. Units: each term of part 1 is \(\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}\cdot A\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{W\,m^{-3}}\) ✓. The special case agrees with P6, which used no integral theorem at all ✓.

P8 The receiving element medium

exercises: Thévenin from a two-port, effective length, conjugate matching in peak amplitudes, effective aperture

  1. From \(V_2 = z_{21}I_1 + z_{22}I_2\), show that a receiving antenna is a Thévenin source, and identify its internal impedance.
  2. A plane wave of peak field \(\hat E\) along a lossless current element. Find the power into a conjugate-matched load, and show \(A_e = 3\lambda^2/8\pi\) whatever \(l\) is.
  3. Repeat for the bare wire's triangular current, and show that \(A_e\) is unchanged.
  4. Evaluate \(A_e\) at 2.4 GHz, and compare with the half-wave dipole's \(1.64\lambda^2/4\pi\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

\(A_e\) is a ratio of a delivered power to an intensity; both scale as \(\hat E^2\).

Rung 2

\(S = \hat E^2/2\eta_0\); \(\hat V_{\mathrm{oc}} = \hat El_e\) with \(l_e = l\) or \(l/2\); matched, \(P_L = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2/8R_{\mathrm{rad}}\).

Rung 3

First line: \(A_e = \frac{\hat E^2l^2/8R_{\mathrm{rad}}}{\hat E^2/2\eta_0} = \frac{\eta_0l^2}{4R_{\mathrm{rad}}}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The voltage collected and the internal resistance it must pass through both scale with the antenna's \(\dot p\) per ampere, so their ratio — the area — does not.

1. With \(I_1\) fixed by the transmitter, \(V_2 = V_{\mathrm{oc}} + z_{22}I_2\) with \(V_{\mathrm{oc}} = z_{21}I_1\): an EMF in series with \(z_{22}\), unit 04's Thévenin form. \(z_{22}\) is antenna 2's own input impedance, \(R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}} + iX_a\).

2. \(\hat V_{\mathrm{oc}} = \hat El\). Matched, the loop impedance is \(2R_{\mathrm{rad}}\), the peak current \(\hat El/2R_{\mathrm{rad}}\), and \(P_L = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}(\hat El/2R_{\mathrm{rad}})^2 = \hat E^2l^2/8R_{\mathrm{rad}}\). \(A_e = \eta_0l^2/4R_{\mathrm{rad}} = \eta_0l^2\lambda^2/(4\cdot\tfrac{2\pi}3\eta_0l^2) = 3\lambda^2/8\pi\).

3. \(l_e = l/2\) and \(R_{\mathrm{rad}}\) is a quarter: \(A_e = \eta_0(l/2)^2/(4R_{\mathrm{rad}}/4) = \eta_0l^2/4R_{\mathrm{rad}}\), the same.

4. \(3\times0.12491^2/8\pi = 1.86\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2} = 18.6\ \mathrm{cm^2}\); the half-wave dipole's \(1.64\times0.015603/4\pi = 20.4\ \mathrm{cm^2}\).

Check. Units \(\mathrm m^2\) ✓. \(G\lambda^2/4\pi\) with \(G = 1.5\) is \(3\lambda^2/8\pi\) ✓. Limit: a lossy element (\(R_{\mathrm{loss}}\gt0\)) delivers less, \(A_e\) times \(e_{\mathrm{rad}}\), consistent with \(G = e_{\mathrm{rad}}D\) ✓.

P9 Proof: where the lost half goes, and \(A_e/G\) medium

exercises: re-radiation from the receiving current, scattering cross-section, the limits of the Thévenin picture, reciprocity to \(A_e = G\lambda^2/4\pi\)

  1. A lossless receiving current element with load \(Z_L\). Using section 1's \(P = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^2\), find the re-radiated power for (a) the conjugate match, (b) an open circuit, (c) a tuned short, \(Z_L = -iX_a\). Express (c) as a cross-section in units of \(\lambda^2\).
  2. Collin (2003) warns that the power dissipated in a Thévenin equivalent is not in general the scattered power. Why does part 1's model escape the warning, and what kind of antenna would not?
  3. Prove \(G_aA_b = G_bA_a\) for two matched antennas in each other's far field, from \(z_{ab} = z_{ba}\); then fix the constant with P8.
  4. Check with two parallel 10 cm current elements at 100 MHz, side by side 10 m apart: compute \(|z_{21}|\) from P6 and compare \(|z_{21}|^2/4R_{\mathrm{rad}}^2\) with \(G^2(\lambda/4\pi d)^2\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

In this model the antenna's current is \(\hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/(Z_a + Z_L)\), and every current element carrying a current radiates. For part 3, write the received fraction two ways: from the definitions of \(G\) and \(A\), and from the two-port.

Rung 2

Two-port, weak coupling: \(a\) accepts \(\tfrac12R_a\hat I_a^2\); \(b\) delivers \(|z_{ba}|^2\hat I_a^2/8R_b\). For part 4, side by side means \(\hat l\cdot\hat r = 0\), so only \(k^2g + g'/r\) survives.

Rung 3

First line of part 1(a): \(\hat I = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/2R_{\mathrm{rad}}\), so \(P_{\mathrm{re}} = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2/4R_{\mathrm{rad}}^2 = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2/8R_{\mathrm{rad}}\).

Worked solution

Idea. In the one-mode model the scattering is entirely the load-dependent current's radiation; reciprocity makes the two-way fractions equal.

1. (a) \(P_{\mathrm{re}} = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2/8R_{\mathrm{rad}} = P_L\): the "lost half" is re-radiated. (b) \(\hat I = 0\): nothing absorbed, nothing re-radiated. (c) Loop impedance \(R_{\mathrm{rad}}\), \(\hat I = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/R_{\mathrm{rad}}\), \(P_{\mathrm{re}} = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2/2R_{\mathrm{rad}} = 4P_L^{\mathrm{match}}\); cross-section \(4A_e = 3\lambda^2/2\pi = 0.477\lambda^2\).

2. The model's only current is the port current: open-circuited, it carries nothing and scatters nothing, so all of its scattering depends on the load and is accounted for. Kahn and Kurss (1965) call antennas that become "invisible" when open-circuited minimum-scattering, and show that for them, terminated in matched receivers, scattered and absorbed power are equal. A structure that scatters with its port open — a large reflector, say — has currents the equivalent circuit knows nothing about; for it the Thévenin dissipation is not the scattered power, which is Collin's point.

3. Definitions: \(P_b/P_a = G_aA_b/4\pi d^2\). Two-port: \(P_b/P_a = |z_{ba}|^2/4R_aR_b\). The other way, \(G_bA_a/4\pi d^2 = |z_{ab}|^2/4R_bR_a\). With \(z_{ab} = z_{ba}\), \(G_aA_b = G_bA_a\), so \(A/G\) is a universal constant; P8's element gives \((3\lambda^2/8\pi)/1.5 = \lambda^2/4\pi\). \(\square\)

4. \(z_{21} = -\frac{l^2}{4\pi i\omega\varepsilon_0}\big(\frac{k^2}r - \frac{ik}{r^2} - \frac1{r^3}\big)e^{-ikr}\). At \(kr = 20.96\): \(z_{21} = 0.0523 - 0.0347i\ \Omega\), \(|z_{21}| = 0.0628\ \Omega\) (the far-field form \(\eta_0kl^2/4\pi r\) gives 0.0628). \(|z_{21}|^2/4R_{\mathrm{rad}}^2 = 0.003939/(4\times0.7707) = 1.278\times10^{-3}\), against \(1.5^2(2.998/4\pi\times10)^2 = 1.281\times10^{-3}\): agreement to 0.2%, the near-field terms' share at \(kr = 21\).

Check. Energy in 1(a): the wave loses \(P_L + P_{\mathrm{re}} = 2P_L\), both accounted for ✓. In the far-field limit the part 4 ratio is exactly \(\frac{(\eta_0kl^2/4\pi r)^2}{4(\eta_0k^2l^2/6\pi)^2} = \frac9{16(kr)^2} = 1.5^2\big(\frac\lambda{4\pi r}\big)^2\) ✓.

P10 Friis routine

exercises: gain and aperture combined, decibels, reciprocity in numbers — the checkpoint's second part

  1. Derive \(P_r/P_t = G_tG_r(\lambda/4\pi d)^2\), and its aperture form. State its conditions.
  2. 1 W at 2.4 GHz between isotropic antennas 1 km apart: find \(P_r\) in watts and dBm, and the path gain in dB.
  3. The running example (all assumed): a base station of 20 W at 17 dBi, 1 km from a phone modelled as a half-wave dipole (\(G = 1.64\)). Find the effective isotropic radiated power, the intensity and RMS field at the phone, and the received power in W and dBm; then the uplink at 0.2 W.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The intensity at the receiver comes from the transmitter's gain; what is collected comes from the receiver's area.

Rung 2

\(S = G_tP_t/4\pi d^2\), \(P_r = SA_r\), \(A_r = G_r\lambda^2/4\pi\). In dB, multiply by adding.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\lambda = 2.998\times10^8/2.4\times10^9 = 0.1249\) m, so \(\lambda/4\pi d = 9.94\times10^{-6}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Spread, then collect: the power is not absorbed on the way, it is spread over \(4\pi d^2\), and the receiver catches \(A_r\) of it.

1. \(S = G_tP_t/4\pi d^2\); \(P_r = SA_r = G_tP_tG_r\lambda^2/(4\pi d)^2\). With \(G = 4\pi A/\lambda^2\) for both, \(P_r/P_t = A_tA_r/(\lambda d)^2\). Conditions: free space, far field, matched loads and polarisation, gains including efficiency.

2. \(P_r = (9.940\times10^{-6})^2 = 9.88\times10^{-11}\) W, about 0.1 nW; \(-100.05\) dB below 1 W, i.e. \(-70.1\) dBm.

3. \(G_t = 10^{1.7} = 50.1\); EIRP \(= 1002\) W. \(S = 1002/4\pi\times10^6 = 7.98\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{W/m^2}\), \(E_{\mathrm{rms}} = \sqrt{S\eta_0} = 0.173\) V/m. \(P_r = 1002\times1.64\times9.88\times10^{-11} = 1.62\times10^{-7}\) W \(= -37.9\) dBm (\(43.0 + 17.0 - 100.05 + 2.15\)). Uplink: \(0.2\times50.1\times1.64\times9.88\times10^{-11} = 1.62\times10^{-9}\) W, \(-57.9\) dBm: the same fraction, \(8.1\times10^{-9}\), as reciprocity requires.

Check. Dimensionless ratio ✓; \(P_r\ll P_t\) ✓. Far field: the base station's aperture, \(0.062\ \mathrm{m^2}\), gives \(2w^2/\lambda\) of about a metre, far below 1 km ✓.

P11 The thermodynamic argument hard

exercises: mode counting, equipartition, brightness, Nyquist's matched-line argument, the second law as a constraint on \(A_e\)

A lossless antenna in a black enclosure at temperature \(T\) is joined by a matched lossless line to a resistor at \(T\) outside.

  1. Count the field's modes in a box of volume \(V\) with frequency in \(\mathrm d\nu\), two polarisations, and show the energy density is \(u_\nu = 8\pi\nu^2k_BT/c^3\) if each mode holds \(k_BT\). Show the brightness is \(2k_BT/\lambda^2\).
  2. Nyquist (1928) showed that a resistor sends \(k_BT\,\mathrm d\nu\) into a matched line. Write the balance of power along the line, and show \(\oint A_e\,\mathrm d\Omega = \lambda^2\).
  3. Using \(A_e\propto G\) (P9) and \(\oint G\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi\), conclude \(A_e = G\lambda^2/4\pi\).
  4. Check \(h\nu/k_BT\) at 2.4 GHz and 300 K. Which steps were cited rather than proved?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two powers must be equal in every band: what the resistor sends the antenna to radiate, and what the antenna collects from the enclosure.

Rung 2

Modes: \(V/(2\pi)^3\) per unit \(\vec k\)-volume, shell \(4\pi k^2\mathrm dk\), \(k = 2\pi\nu/c\). Isotropic radiation of density \(u\) has brightness \(uc/4\pi\). The antenna takes one polarisation: \(k_BT/\lambda^2\) per unit area, solid angle and bandwidth.

Rung 3

First line: \(N = 2\cdot\frac V{(2\pi)^3}4\pi k^2\,\mathrm dk = \frac{8\pi V\nu^2\,\mathrm d\nu}{c^3}\).

Worked solution

Idea. In equilibrium nothing may flow net along the line in any band; that fixes the antenna's total collecting solid-angle integral independently of its shape.

1. \(u_\nu = Nk_BT/(V\mathrm d\nu) = 8\pi\nu^2k_BT/c^3\). Brightness \(B_\nu = u_\nu c/4\pi = 2\nu^2k_BT/c^2 = 2k_BT/\lambda^2\).

2. From solid angle \(\mathrm d\Omega\) the antenna delivers \(\frac{k_BT}{\lambda^2}A_e\,\mathrm d\Omega\,\mathrm d\nu\) into the line; in all, \(\frac{k_BT\,\mathrm d\nu}{\lambda^2}\oint A_e\,\mathrm d\Omega\). The resistor sends \(k_BT\,\mathrm d\nu\) the other way, all of which the matched antenna radiates. A net flow would warm one body and cool the other at equal temperatures with no work done, which the second law forbids; Nyquist's argument with a lossless filter shows it must hold band by band. So \(\oint A_e\,\mathrm d\Omega = \lambda^2\).

3. \(A_e = \kappa G\) with one constant \(\kappa\) (P9); \(\kappa\oint G\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi\kappa = \lambda^2\), so \(\kappa = \lambda^2/4\pi\).

4. \(h\nu/k_BT = 6.626\times10^{-34}\times2.4\times10^9/(1.381\times10^{-23}\times300) = 3.84\times10^{-4}\ll1\): classical. Cited: equipartition (\(k_BT\) per mode) and Nyquist's \(k_BT\,\mathrm d\nu\); Rayleigh–Jeans is equipartition plus the mode count derived in part 1. The step "\(A_e\propto G\) with one constant" is P9's, from reciprocity.

Check. Units: \(k_BT\,\mathrm d\nu\) is W; \(\frac{k_BT}{\lambda^2}\,\mathrm m^2\,\mathrm d\nu\) is W ✓. The short dipole: \(\oint\frac{3\lambda^2}{8\pi}\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\Omega = \frac{3\lambda^2}{8\pi}\cdot\frac{8\pi}3 = \lambda^2\) ✓.

P12 Beaming power medium

exercises: Friis against its own validity, Kepler's third law, \(\lambda d\) as the scale of beamed power, the Fraunhofer distance

  1. By what factor does the base station of P10 fall short of the 5 W that unit 15's pad delivers? At what distance would Friis give 5 W, and why is Friis not valid there?
  2. Find the geostationary distance above the equator from Kepler's third law with the preamble's constants.
  3. At an assumed 2.45 GHz, find \(\lambda d\), and the diameter of equal circular apertures whose Friis ratio is 1.
  4. An assumed 1 km transmitter and 10 km receiver: find the Friis ratio and the spot size \(\lambda d/w_t\). Friis gives more than 1: is energy conservation violated?
  5. Show that at the Fraunhofer distance \(2w^2/\lambda\), equal circular apertures of diameter \(w\) have Friis ratio \(\pi^2/64\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Friis in aperture form, \(A_tA_r/(\lambda d)^2\), cannot exceed 1; ask what hypothesis must fail before it would.

Rung 2

Friis scales as \(d^{-2}\) at fixed gains. Kepler: \(r^3 = GM_ET^2/4\pi^2\), with \(T\) the Earth's rotation period. A beam from width \(w_t\) spreads by about \(\lambda/w_t\).

Rung 3

First line: \(5/1.62\times10^{-7} = 3.1\times10^7\), and \(d_5 = 1000\ \mathrm m\times\sqrt{1.62\times10^{-7}/5}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Collecting most of a beam needs apertures whose product is of order \(\lambda d\); Friis's \(d^{-2}\) law describes only receivers much smaller than the spot.

1. \(3.1\times10^7\). \(d_5 = 1000\times1.80\times10^{-4} = 0.180\) m. There \(\sqrt{\lambda d} = 0.150\) m, smaller than the base station's own aperture (\(0.062\ \mathrm{m^2}\), about 25 cm across): the phone is not in its far field, and Friis does not apply.

2. A geostationary satellite turns with the Earth, period \(T = 2\pi/\omega_E\), so \(r^3 = GM_ET^2/4\pi^2 = GM_E/\omega_E^2\): \(r = (3.986004418\times10^{14}/(7.292115\times10^{-5})^2)^{1/3} = 42\,164\) km; \(d = 42\,164 - 6378 = 35\,786\) km.

3. \(\lambda = 0.1224\) m, \(\lambda d = 4.38\times10^6\ \mathrm{m^2}\). \(A = \lambda d\): \(w = 2\sqrt{\lambda d/\pi} = 2.36\) km.

4. \(A_t = 7.85\times10^5\), \(A_r = 7.85\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^2}\); ratio \(6.17\times10^{13}/1.92\times10^{13} = 3.2\). Spot \(\approx4.38\times10^6/1000 = 4.4\) km. No violation: Friis assumed a receiver small against the spot. A 10 km receiver covers the 4.4 km spot; it can collect most of the beam, never more than all of it. The formula has left its domain.

5. \(\lambda d_F = 2w^2\); \(A^2/(\lambda d_F)^2 = (\pi w^2/4)^2/4w^4 = \pi^2/64 = 0.154\).

Check. Units: \(\lambda d\) is \(\mathrm m^2\), an area ✓. Scaling: at fixed \(d\) the required diameters go as \(\sqrt{\lambda}\propto f^{-1/2}\) ✓. Limit: \(d\to\infty\) at fixed apertures returns Friis's small ratios ✓.
