Antennas: radiation resistance, reciprocity, and why beaming power is hard

A changing current radiates, and to the circuit that feeds it the loss looks like a resistor whose heat leaves as a wave. Reciprocity makes the same conductor a source when a wave arrives, with a collecting area fixed by its gain; and the Friis equation says how little arrives far away.

16 · overview

Two conventions, said once

time dependence $e^{+i\omega t}$

As everywhere in this course. An outgoing spherical wave is then

$$\frac{e^{i(\omega t - kr)}}{r},\qquad k = \frac\omega c = \frac{2\pi}\lambda .$$

MR 24 writes waves as $e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec x - \omega t)}$, and Jackson uses $e^{-i\omega t}$ throughout: to translate any of their formulas, replace $i$ by $-i$ (unit 08 said the same for lines).

$k$ is a wavenumber here

Unit 15's coupling coefficient was also called $k$. In this unit $k = \omega/c$ always; unit 15's coupling enters only through $M$ and $U$.

radiation resistance uses the peak current

With $i(t) = \hat I\cos\omega t$ at the feed, the mean radiated power is written

$$\bar P = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^{\,2} = R_{\mathrm{rad}}|I|^2,$$

the second form with unit 05's RMS phasor, $|I| = \hat I/\sqrt2$. The same $R$ serves both, so the course's RMS convention is not broken; antenna books state the first form.

Field amplitudes are peak in this unit ($\hat E$, and the complex Poynting vector $\tfrac12\vec E\times\vec H^*$), as in the antenna literature. With RMS amplitudes the $\tfrac12$ goes.

And $\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} = \mu_0c = 376.73\ \Omega$: unit 02 wrote $\mu_0c$, unit 08 $\eta_0$.

Why the conventions differ. Power engineers quote RMS values because $P = |V||I|\cos\varphi$ then needs no $\tfrac12$; antenna engineers quote peak currents because the radiation resistance was first defined that way, and every book on antennas states $\bar P = \tfrac12R\hat I^2$. Both are the same resistance. Where this unit writes a phasor $I$ without a hat it is RMS, as in unit 05; where it writes $\hat I$ or $\hat E$ it is a peak amplitude.

Translating $e^{-i\omega t}$. A field $\mathrm{Re}(Fe^{-i\omega t})$ is the same physical field as $\mathrm{Re}(F^*e^{+i\omega t})$, so a formula written for $e^{-i\omega t}$ becomes this course's by conjugating every complex coefficient, which for formulas built from real quantities and $i$ means replacing $i$ by $-i$. An impedance $R - iX$ in Jackson's convention is $R + iX$ here.

The numbers. $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ H/m and $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}$ F/m (AUTHORING.md) give $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = 2.998\times10^8$ m/s and $\eta_0 = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0} = 376.73\ \Omega$; and $\mu_0c = \mu_0/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0} = \sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0}$ exactly.

section 1

Radiation from a current: the radiation resistance

A short wire carrying a changing current is a changing dipole moment. MR 24's Larmor formula, applied to the whole moment, gives the power it radiates — and averaged over a cycle that power is a resistor's.

16 · radiation

A current element

an antenna

A conductor shaped so that a current driven into it at a pair of terminals radiates — or, used the other way, so that a passing wave drives a current at its terminals.

a current element (a model)

A straight segment of length $l \ll \lambda$ along $\hat z$, carrying the same current $i(t)$ along its whole length.

Continuity (unit 00): current arriving at the top end has nowhere to go, so charge piles up there, $\mathrm dq/\mathrm dt = i$, and $-q$ at the bottom. The element is a dipole $\vec p = q\,l\,\hat z$ whose moment changes as

$$\dot{\vec p} = l\,i(t)\,\hat z,\qquad \ddot{\vec p} = l\,\frac{\mathrm di}{\mathrm dt}\,\hat z .$$

Units: $\mathrm{m\cdot A} = \mathrm{C\,m\,s^{-1}}$ ✓. The same as MR 24's $\int\vec J\,\mathrm d^3y = \dot{\vec p}$.

The element, with plates at its ends to hold the charge a uniform current delivers. The port is marked passively: the current enters at $+$.

A model, said so. A thin wire with nothing at its ends cannot carry the same current all the way to its tips: at a bare tip the current must vanish, because there is nowhere for it to go. To carry a uniform current, the element needs somewhere for the charge to accumulate, such as the end plates drawn here. The element is a model, and the uniform current is its defining assumption; the triangular current of a bare wire is treated on 789 or 790, and three numbers. No claim is made about any device built this way.

Why "short". $l\ll\lambda$ is MR 24's condition $d\ll cT$ for the dipole approximation, with $d = l$ and $cT = \lambda$: the retardation across the element is negligible, so every part of it radiates in phase, and the current has time to be the same all along it.

The two routes to $\dot{\vec p}$ agree. Directly: $\vec p = q(t)\,l\,\hat z$ for charges $\pm q$ a distance $l$ apart, so $\dot{\vec p} = l\,\dot q\,\hat z = l\,i\,\hat z$. From MR 24 (Far from a small source): $\int\vec J\,\mathrm d^3y = \dot{\vec p}$, and for a thin wire carrying $i$ along $\hat z$ the volume integral of $\vec J$ is $i$ times the length, $l\,i\,\hat z$.

The term "antenna". The definition is functional: any conductor carrying a changing current radiates something, as unit 02's estimate showed for the kettle's cable; an antenna is one shaped so that it does so usefully.

Add the fields, then square

MR 24's radiation field is linear in $\ddot{\vec p}$, and $\ddot{\vec p} = \sum_jq_j\vec a_j$ over every moving charge. Fields add; powers do not.

sum coherently

The power radiated by many charges whose motion is small against $\lambda$ is

$$P = \frac{\mu_0}{6\pi c}\Big|\sum_jq_j\vec a_j\Big|^2,\quad\text{not}\quad \sum_j\frac{q_j^2|\vec a_j|^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}.$$

For $N$ electrons moving together the first is $N^2$ times one electron's power, the second $N$ times.

The lattice ions do not move and add nothing. Unit 00's drifting electrons have tiny accelerations, and their number makes up for it: whatever their distribution in the wire, $\sum_jq_j\vec a_j = l\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt\,\hat z$.

Four charges' contributions $q\vec a$ to $\ddot{\vec p}$, added as vectors. In a wire they are in step, and the power goes as the square of the count.

Why the sum comes first. MR 24's radiation field, $\vec B = \mu_0\ddot{\vec p}\times\hat r/(4\pi cr)$, is linear in the sources, so the field of many charges is the field of their total $\ddot{\vec p}$, provided all of them sit within a region small against $\lambda$ (the dipole approximation's condition). The power is quadratic in the field. So $P\propto|\sum_jq_j\vec a_j|^2 = \sum_jq_j^2a_j^2 + \sum_{j\ne k}q_jq_k\vec a_j\cdot\vec a_k$, and the cross terms are what the incoherent sum forgets. When the charges move in step, every cross term is as large as a diagonal one, and there are $N(N-1)$ of them. When their phases are random, the cross terms average to zero and the incoherent sum is right on average: that is the right panel, and the reason light from a hot filament adds by powers.

The count, for scale. The 10 cm dipole at 100 MHz carries its current in a skin about $6.5\ \mu\mathrm m$ deep (stated from branch 10, section 3); the conduction electrons in that skin number about $n\cdot2\pi a\delta\cdot l = 8.47\times10^{28}\times2\pi\times10^{-3}\times6.52\times10^{-6}\times0.1 = 3.5\times10^{20}$. The incoherent sum would understate the power by that factor. The total $\sum_jq_j\vec a_j$ does not depend on the count at all: it is $l\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$, the derivative of the total current times the length, however the current is spread over the cross-section.

The ions. They carry charge too, but in unit 00's model they are fixed, so $\vec a = \vec 0$ and they contribute nothing to $\ddot{\vec p}$. Their static charge cancels the electrons' in $\vec p$ itself, which is why only the charges $\pm q$ at the ends remain.

16 · radiation

A current radiates as the square of its rate of change

Put $\ddot{\vec p} = l\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt\,\hat z$ into MR 24's Larmor formula, $P = \mu_0|\ddot{\vec p}|^2/6\pi c$:

radiation from a current element $$P(t) = \frac{\mu_0\,l^2}{6\pi c}\Big(\frac{\mathrm di}{\mathrm dt}\Big)^2,$$

with $\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$ at the retarded time, and a $\sin^2\theta$ pattern about the element.

Units: $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,m^2\,s\,m^{-1}\,A^2\,s^{-2}} = \mathrm{H\,A^2\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm W$ ✓.

A steady current radiates nothing; a current that changes fast radiates a lot. This is what unit 08 named: the near end of its nanosecond switch "is then a small antenna", and lightning's "electromagnetic pulse".

no number for either

Unit 08's near end is about 1 m across, while $c\times1\ \mathrm{ns} = 0.3$ m: MR 24's condition $d\ll cT$ fails. The formula gives at best an order of magnitude there.

A current switched on linearly in a time $\tau$: it radiates only while it changes, at a power $\propto(I/\tau)^2$. Halve $\tau$ and the power quadruples, the energy radiated doubles.

The energy in a switch-on. For a linear ramp from 0 to $I$ in time $\tau$, $\mathrm di/\mathrm dt = I/\tau$ during the ramp, so the radiated energy is $\frac{\mu_0l^2}{6\pi c}\frac{I^2}{\tau^2}\tau = \frac{\mu_0l^2I^2}{6\pi c\,\tau}$: it grows without limit as $\tau\to0$. A real switch is not instantaneous, and once $c\tau$ is not large against the size of the circuit the formula itself fails, because the dipole approximation assumed the whole source radiates in phase.

Unit 08's pointers, landed. Unit 08 (What the model claims, and what it does not) said that when the switch closes in a nanosecond, "the near end is then a small antenna, and some energy leaves it as radiation (branch 16), which this model leaves out"; and (Where the energy goes, and what carries it) that a lightning stroke loses energy as "an electromagnetic pulse — radiation, branch 16", which "is what any rapidly changing current radiates (branch 16 computes radiation from a current; here it is only named)". This slide is that computation. For both cases it gives no number, for the reason in the box: the near end of unit 08's puzzle is a metre across, the stroke's channel kilometres long, and both change faster than light crosses them. A number would need the full retarded potentials of MR 24 over the actual current distribution, which this unit does not attempt.

Retarded time. MR 24's radiation field at distance $r$ carries $\ddot{\vec p}(t - r/c)$, so the pulse arrives at $r$ a time $r/c$ after the switch moves.

Worked: the radiation resistance from Larmor

worked example 1 · a resistor whose heat leaves as a wave

1. Drive the element with $i = \hat I\cos\omega t$. Then $\mathrm di/\mathrm dt = -\omega\hat I\sin\omega t$, and the previous slide gives $P(t) = \dfrac{\mu_0l^2\omega^2\hat I^2}{6\pi c}\sin^2\omega t$.

2. Average over a cycle ($\overline{\sin^2} = \tfrac12$): $\bar P = \dfrac{\mu_0l^2\omega^2\hat I^2}{12\pi c}$.

3. Define the radiation resistance by $\bar P = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^2$, as a resistor carrying $\hat I\cos\omega t$ would dissipate. With $\omega = 2\pi c/\lambda$:

$$\boxed{R_{\mathrm{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0\omega^2l^2}{6\pi c} = \frac{2\pi}3\,\eta_0\Big(\frac l\lambda\Big)^2 = 789.0\Big(\frac l\lambda\Big)^2\ \Omega}$$

4. The 10 cm dipole at 100 MHz: $l/\lambda = 0.03336$, $R_{\mathrm{rad}} = 789.0\times0.001113 = 0.878\ \Omega$.

Units: $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,s^{-2}\,m^2\,s\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{H\,s^{-1}} = \Omega$ ✓. Energy: $\bar P$ is MR 24's $\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4/12\pi c$ with $p_0 = l\hat I/\omega$ ✓.

To the source, the element is a resistor $R_{\mathrm{rad}}$ (IEC rectangle; the American symbol is a zigzag), marked passively. What it "dissipates" is the radiated power.

Unit 02's sentence, landed. Unit 02 (notes to What each hypothesis neglects) estimated the radiation of a short current element "of length $\ell$ carrying $i = \hat\imath\cos\omega t$": "the radiated power is $\mu_0\ddot p^2/6\pi c$, and its mean is $\tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat\imath^2$ with $R_{\mathrm{rad}} = \mu_0\omega^2\ell^2/6\pi c = \tfrac{2\pi}3\mu_0c(\ell/\lambda)^2 \approx 789(\ell/\lambda)^2\ \Omega$ … Unit 16 derives this as the radiation resistance." Steps 1–3 are that derivation, in unit 02's notation with $\ell\to l$, $\hat\imath\to\hat I$ and $\mu_0c = \eta_0$. What unit 02 took on trust, and this slide supplies, is the previous two slides: that the Larmor formula applies to the current's total $\ddot{\vec p}$, and that the element's $\ddot{\vec p}$ is $l\,\mathrm di/\mathrm dt$.

Step 3 in full. $\frac{\mu_0\omega^2l^2}{6\pi c} = \frac{\mu_0(2\pi c)^2l^2}{6\pi c\lambda^2} = \frac{4\pi^2\mu_0c}{6\pi}\frac{l^2}{\lambda^2} = \frac{2\pi}3\mu_0c\,\frac{l^2}{\lambda^2}$, and $\frac{2\pi}3\times376.73 = 789.03\ \Omega$.

The energy check, in full. MR 24 (notes to The Larmor formula) gives the oscillating dipole $\vec p = p_0\cos\omega t\,\hat z$ a mean radiated power $\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4/12\pi c$, obtained by integrating the Poynting vector over a large sphere. Here $\dot{\vec p} = l\hat I\cos\omega t\,\hat z$, so $\vec p = (l\hat I/\omega)\sin\omega t\,\hat z$ plus a constant, and $p_0 = l\hat I/\omega$. Then $\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4/12\pi c = \mu_0l^2\hat I^2\omega^2/12\pi c$, step 2 ✓. So the power the source delivers to $R_{\mathrm{rad}}$ is exactly the power that crosses a large sphere: Poynting's theorem for the region between the element and the sphere, which stores no net energy over a cycle.

Step 4. $\lambda = 2.998\times10^8/10^8 = 2.998$ m; $l/\lambda = 0.1/2.998 = 0.03336$; $(l/\lambda)^2 = 1.1127\times10^{-3}$; $789.03\times1.1127\times10^{-3} = 0.8779\ \Omega$.

Where the energy comes from. The source does work against a component of the field at the element that is in phase with the current — the field the element's own radiation makes at itself. Section 2 computes those fields and finds the in-phase part.

section 2

The fields of a current element: near and far

For a point element the retarded potential is exact. Its curl has a $1/r^2$ part — Biot–Savart — and a $1/r$ part — radiation — and they are equal at $r = \lambda/2\pi$. The reactive power stored inside that radius is what makes a short antenna mostly reactive.

16 · near and far

The exact potential of a point element

Shrink the element to a point: $\vec J = \hat I\,l\,\hat z\,\delta^3(\vec x)\,e^{i\omega t}$. MR 24's retarded potential then has no approximation in it — the source is a point, so there is no retardation across it to drop:

$$\vec A = \frac{\mu_0\hat Il}{4\pi}\,\frac{e^{-ikr}}r\,\hat z\qquad(\times\,e^{i\omega t}).$$

$\vec B$ in one line. $\nabla\times(f(r)\hat z) = f'(r)\,\hat r\times\hat z = -f'(r)\sin\theta\,\hat\varphi$, so

the magnetic field $$B_\varphi = \frac{\mu_0\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi}\Big(\frac{ik}r + \frac1{r^2}\Big)e^{-ikr}$$

Units: $\mathrm{H\,m^{-1}\,A\,m\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm{Wb\,m^{-2}} = \mathrm T$ ✓.

The $1/r^2$ term is MR 23's Biot–Savart field of a current element; the $ik/r$ term is MR 24's radiation field.

Spherical coordinates about the element. $\vec B$ is along $\hat\varphi$, round the axis; $\vec E$, next slide, lies in the plane of $\hat r$ and $\hat\theta$.

The retarded potential, in phasor form. MR 24 (Retarded potentials) gives $\vec A(\vec x, t) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec J(\vec y, t - |\vec x - \vec y|/c)}{|\vec x - \vec y|}\,\mathrm d^3y$. With $\vec J(\vec y, t) = \hat Il\hat z\,\delta^3(\vec y)e^{i\omega t}$, the integral picks out $\vec y = 0$: $\vec A = \frac{\mu_0\hat Il}{4\pi r}\hat z\,e^{i\omega(t - r/c)} = \frac{\mu_0\hat Il}{4\pi}\frac{e^{-ikr}}r\hat z\,e^{i\omega t}$. The dipole approximation of MR 24 (Far from a small source) replaced $|\vec x - \vec y|$ by $r$ and dropped a relative error $d/cT$; for a point source $d = 0$ and both replacements are exact. The physical element has $l\ll\lambda$, so the point is a model of it with error of order $l/\lambda$ in the phases and $l/r$ in the distances.

The curl. For a scalar $f(r)$, $\nabla\times(f\hat z) = \nabla f\times\hat z = f'(r)\hat r\times\hat z$. In spherical coordinates $\hat z = \cos\theta\,\hat r - \sin\theta\,\hat\theta$, so $\hat r\times\hat z = -\sin\theta\,\hat r\times\hat\theta = -\sin\theta\,\hat\varphi$. With $f = Ce^{-ikr}/r$, $f' = C\big(-\frac{ik}r - \frac1{r^2}\big)e^{-ikr}$, so $B_\varphi = -f'\sin\theta = C\sin\theta\big(\frac{ik}r + \frac1{r^2}\big)e^{-ikr}$ with $C = \mu_0\hat Il/4\pi$.

The two limits. For $kr\ll1$, $e^{-ikr}\approx1$ and $B_\varphi\approx\mu_0\hat Il\sin\theta/(4\pi r^2)$: MR 23's Biot–Savart law for a current element $\hat Il\hat z$, at every instant (quasi-static). For $kr\gg1$, $B_\varphi\approx ik\,\mu_0\hat Il\sin\theta\,e^{-ikr}/(4\pi r)$; MR 24's $\vec B = \mu_0\ddot{\vec p}\times\hat r/(4\pi cr)$ with $\ddot{\vec p} = i\omega\hat Il\hat z$ gives $\frac{\mu_0 i\omega\hat Il}{4\pi cr}\hat z\times\hat r = \frac{i k\mu_0\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi r}\hat\varphi$ ✓, since $\hat z\times\hat r = \sin\theta\,\hat\varphi$.

The electric field, and two checks

Outside the source, Ampère–Maxwell in phasor form is $\nabla\times\vec B = i\omega\vec E/c^2$, so $\vec E = (c^2/i\omega)\nabla\times\vec B$. Its $\theta$-component (notes):

the electric field $$E_\theta = \frac{i\eta_0k\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi r}\Big[1 + \frac1{ikr} - \frac1{(kr)^2}\Big]e^{-ikr}$$ $$E_r = \frac{\eta_0\hat Il\cos\theta}{2\pi r^2}\Big[1 + \frac1{ikr}\Big]e^{-ikr}$$

Units: $\Omega\,\mathrm{m^{-1}\,A\,m\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm{V/m}$ ✓.

Far ($kr\gg1$): $E_\theta = \eta_0H_\varphi$, transverse — MR 24's $\vec E = c\vec B\times\hat r$ ✓. Near ($kr\ll1$): the $1/r^3$ terms are MR 23's static dipole field with $p = \hat Il/(i\omega)$, that is $q = \hat I/(i\omega)$: continuity ✓.

Inside $r = \lambda/2\pi$ the electric field is close to the static dipole's (field lines from $+q$ to $-q$, sketched); far outside it is a transverse outgoing wave. Not to scale.

Full proof of $E_\theta$ and $E_r$

Write $B_\varphi = \frac{\mu_0\hat Il}{4\pi}\sin\theta\,g(r)$ with $g = (ik/r + 1/r^2)e^{-ikr}$. For a field with only a $\varphi$-component, $(\nabla\times\vec B)_r = \frac1{r\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta B_\varphi)$ and $(\nabla\times\vec B)_\theta = -\frac1r\partial_r(rB_\varphi)$. So $(\nabla\times\vec B)_r = \frac{\mu_0\hat Il}{4\pi}\frac{2\cos\theta}{r}g$ and $(\nabla\times\vec B)_\theta = -\frac{\mu_0\hat Il}{4\pi}\frac{\sin\theta}{r}(rg)'$. Now $rg = (ik + 1/r)e^{-ikr}$, and $(rg)' = \big(-\frac1{r^2} + k^2 - \frac{ik}{r}\big)e^{-ikr}$. Multiply by $c^2/(i\omega) = c/(ik)$ and use $\mu_0c = \eta_0$: $E_\theta = -\frac{\eta_0\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi r}\cdot\frac1{ik}\big(k^2 - \frac{ik}r - \frac1{r^2}\big)e^{-ikr} = \frac{i\eta_0k\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi r}\big(1 + \frac1{ikr} - \frac1{(kr)^2}\big)e^{-ikr}$, using $-\frac{k^2}{ik} = ik$, $\frac{ik/r}{ik} = \frac1r$ and $\frac{1/r^2}{ik} = \frac{-i}{kr^2}$ — collect: $ik\big[1 + \frac1{ikr} - \frac1{k^2r^2}\big]$ ✓. And $E_r = \frac{c}{ik}\frac{\mu_0\hat Il}{4\pi}\frac{2\cos\theta}r\big(\frac{ik}r + \frac1{r^2}\big)e^{-ikr} = \frac{\eta_0\hat Il\cos\theta}{2\pi r^2}\big(1 + \frac1{ikr}\big)e^{-ikr}$. Jackson §9.2 gives the same fields for an oscillating electric dipole (cited; Jackson writes $e^{-i\omega t}$, so his $i$ is this course's $-i$).

The near check, in full. The $1/r^3$ terms are $E_\theta\to\frac{\eta_0\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi ikr^3}$ and $E_r\to\frac{\eta_0\hat Il\cos\theta}{2\pi ikr^3}$. With $\eta_0/k = \mu_0c/(\omega/c) = 1/(\varepsilon_0\omega)$, these are $\frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}(2\cos\theta\,\hat r + \sin\theta\,\hat\theta)$ with $p = \hat Il/(i\omega)$: MR 23's field of a static dipole $p\hat z$. And $p = ql$ with $q = \hat I/(i\omega)$ is the phasor form of $\mathrm dq/\mathrm dt = i$: continuity, as on A current element.

The far check. For $kr\gg1$, $E_\theta\to i\eta_0k\hat Il\sin\theta e^{-ikr}/(4\pi r)$ and $H_\varphi = B_\varphi/\mu_0\to ik\hat Il\sin\theta e^{-ikr}/(4\pi r)$, so $E_\theta = \eta_0H_\varphi$, and $E_r = O(1/r^2)$ is negligible: a transverse field with $|\vec E| = c|\vec B|$, as MR 24's radiation field.

The sketch in the figure is qualitative: the near-zone field lines are those of the static dipole, and how they detach into closed loops further out is not drawn.

Mostly reactive, derived

Integrate the complex Poynting vector $\tfrac12\vec E\times\vec H^*$ (peak amplitudes) over a sphere of radius $r$ round the element:

complex power through a sphere $$\oint\tfrac12\vec E\times\vec H^*\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = P_{\mathrm{rad}}\Big[1 - \frac i{(kr)^3}\Big],$$ $$P_{\mathrm{rad}} = \frac{\eta_0k^2l^2\hat I^2}{12\pi} = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^2 .$$
  • The real part does not depend on $r$: no energy is lost between two spheres in empty space ✓.
  • The imaginary part is unit 06's reactive power, negative: capacitive. It is the reactive power of the field outside the sphere, and it grows as $1/(kr)^3$ as the sphere shrinks onto the antenna.

For the 10 cm dipole at 100 MHz, a sphere of radius $l/2 = 5$ cm has $kr = 0.105$: the field outside it holds 869 times the radiated power as reactive power.

Computed. The radiated power is the same through every sphere; the reactive power outside the sphere overtakes it inside $kr = 1$ and rises as the cube.

Full proof

The radial component of $\tfrac12\vec E\times\vec H^*$ is $\tfrac12E_\theta H_\varphi^*$ ($\hat\theta\times\hat\varphi = \hat r$; $E_r$ gives no radial flux). With $H_\varphi = \frac{ik\hat Il\sin\theta}{4\pi r}\big(1 + \frac1{ikr}\big)e^{-ikr}$ and $E_\theta$ from the previous slide, the phases $e^{\mp ikr}$ cancel and, with $x = kr$, $E_\theta H_\varphi^* = \frac{\eta_0k^2\hat I^2l^2\sin^2\theta}{16\pi^2r^2}\cdot i\Big(1 + \frac1{ix} - \frac1{x^2}\Big)\cdot\overline{i\Big(1 + \frac1{ix}\Big)}$. Since $1/(ix) = -i/x$, the last two factors are $i(1 - \frac ix - \frac1{x^2})\cdot(-i)(1 + \frac ix) = (1 - \frac ix - \frac1{x^2})(1 + \frac ix) = 1 + \frac ix - \frac ix + \frac1{x^2} - \frac1{x^2} - \frac i{x^3} = 1 - \frac i{x^3}$. Integrate over the sphere with $\mathrm dA = r^2\mathrm d\Omega$ and $\int\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\Omega = 8\pi/3$ (MR 24): $\tfrac12\cdot\frac{\eta_0k^2\hat I^2l^2}{16\pi^2}\cdot\frac{8\pi}3\big(1 - \frac i{x^3}\big) = \frac{\eta_0k^2l^2\hat I^2}{12\pi}\big(1 - \frac i{(kr)^3}\big)$. $\square$

The real part is section 1's. $\frac{\eta_0k^2l^2}{12\pi} = \frac{\mu_0c\,\omega^2l^2}{12\pi c^2} = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{\mu_0\omega^2l^2}{6\pi c} = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}$ ✓. The constancy in $r$ is Poynting's theorem (MR 24) for the shell between two spheres: in a lossless region with no sources, the mean power in equals the mean power out.

The imaginary part. Poynting's theorem in complex form gives, for the region outside the sphere, $\oint\tfrac12\vec E\times\vec H^*\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = P_{\mathrm{rad}} + 2i\omega(\bar W_m - \bar W_e)$, with $\bar W_m$, $\bar W_e$ the mean magnetic and electric energies stored outside the sphere — unit 06's $Q = 2\omega(\bar W_m - \bar W_e)$, with fields in place of components. A negative imaginary part means more electric than magnetic energy: the near field of a short element is mostly the electric field of its end charges, as the previous slide's $1/r^3$ term said. That is capacitive reactance, and section 3 finds it at the terminals. (Each of $\bar W_e$ and $\bar W_m$ alone diverges if the sphere includes the far zone out to infinity; their difference does not, and only the difference enters.)

The number. $k = 2\pi\times10^8/2.998\times10^8 = 2.0958\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, $kr = 0.10479$ at 5 cm, $(kr)^{-3} = 869.0$. Unit 06 (A source with reactance) said "branch 16 shows that a short antenna is mostly reactive": this is the field-side reason. Problem P5 derives the result in full.

The general question. L. J. Chu, "Physical limitations of omni-directional antennas", J. Appl. Phys. 19, 1163–1175 (1948), computed the least $Q$ an antenna of given size can have, and found that it rises steeply once the antenna is small against the wavelength. Its bound is not quoted here; this slide's $1/(kr)^3$ is derived for the current element alone and needs no citation.

Near against far: d⁻⁶ against d⁻²

Near (unit 15). The quasi-static fields fall as $1/r^3$, so $M$, and with it $U$, fall as $d^{-3}$. At $U\ll1$, $\eta_{\max} = U^2/(1 + \sqrt{1 + U^2})^2\approx U^2/4$: $d^{-6}$.

Far (this unit). $|\vec E|$, $|\vec B|\propto1/r$, so the intensity and the received power fall as $d^{-2}$.

doubling the distance

costs a factor 64 near, a factor 4 far. Unit 15's pad from 80 to 160 mm: $U$ from 0.55 to 0.078, $\eta_{\max}$ from 0.066 to 0.0015, a factor 44 — not yet 64, because 0.55 is not $\ll1$ and the coils are not yet far apart.

Does the pad radiate? Its coil's radiation resistance (Balanis, §5.2, cited, and derived from the current element in the notes; problem P4) is $2.3\times10^{-13}\ \Omega$, against its $0.0833\ \Omega$ of copper: the charger is not an antenna.

The two laws, each normalised to 1 at its own reference distance. They describe different machines; where they cross for any real pair of devices is not claimed.

The near law. Unit 15 found $M\propto d^{-3}$ once $d$ is large against the coils (Far apart: the leading term vanishes, and M falls as 1/d³), so $U = \omega M/\sqrt{R_1R_2}\propto d^{-3}$. For $U\ll1$, $\sqrt{1 + U^2}\approx1 + U^2/2$ and $\eta_{\max} = U^2/(2 + U^2/2)^2\approx U^2/4$. So the efficiency falls as $d^{-6}$. With unit 15's numbers (The distance table: tuned against untuned, and $\eta_{\max}$ from The maximum-efficiency theorem): $U = 0.55$ at 80 mm gives $\eta_{\max} = 0.0660$ (against $U^2/4 = 0.0756$: the approximation is still 15% off there); $U = 0.078$ at 160 mm gives $0.00152$ (against $0.00152$). The ratio of the efficiencies is $0.0660/0.00152 = 44$. Over the same doubling $U$ fell by $0.55/0.078 = 7.1$, not 8, because at 80 mm the coils (radius 20 mm) are not yet far enough apart for the $d^{-3}$ law to be exact (unit 15's problem P8 finds the leading term 4.7% high even at 160 mm). Asymptotically both factors approach $8$ and $64$.

The far law. Section 6 derives it: $P_r\propto1/d^2$ for fixed antennas, because the radiated power spreads over a sphere of area $4\pi d^2$ and is not lost on the way.

Why the comparison is not a contest. The near-field law is an efficiency of a link designed around its coupling; the far-field law is the fraction collected from a wave that went everywhere. Each curve is normalised at its own reference distance, so the figure compares slopes only. The part's thesis is in the slopes: within the near field, distance is ruinous; beyond it, distance is merely expensive.

The pad's radiation. A small loop of $N$ turns and radius $a$ has $R_{\mathrm{rad}} = \frac{\pi}{6}\eta_0N^2(ka)^4$ (Balanis, §5.2, "Small Circular Loop", cited; with $\eta_0\approx120\pi$ it is $20\pi^2N^2(C/\lambda)^4$, $C = 2\pi a$). It also follows from this unit's element. Far away, keep the phase across the loop as on When Friis fails: $\vec A\approx\frac{\mu_0e^{-ikr}}{4\pi r}N\hat I\oint e^{ik\hat r\cdot\vec y}\,\mathrm d\vec y \approx\frac{\mu_0e^{-ikr}}{4\pi r}N\hat I\,ik\oint(\hat r\cdot\vec y)\,\mathrm d\vec y$, since $\oint\mathrm d\vec y = \vec 0$. For a plane loop of area $S$ and normal $\hat n$, $\oint(\hat r\cdot\vec y)\,\mathrm d\vec y = S\,\hat n\times\hat r$ (check it with $\hat r = \hat x$ on a loop in the $xy$-plane: $\oint x\,\mathrm d\vec y = S\hat y$). So $|\vec A| = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\,kN\hat IS\sin\theta$, with $\theta$ from $\hat n$: the far field of a current element with $\hat Il$ replaced by $kN\hat IS$, and the same $\sin^2\theta$ pattern. The radiated power, and so $R_{\mathrm{rad}}$, follow by that substitution in worked example 1: $\frac{2\pi}3\eta_0\big(kN\pi a^2/\lambda\big)^2 = \frac{2\pi}3\eta_0N^2\pi^2a^4\frac{k^4}{4\pi^2} = \frac\pi6\eta_0N^2(ka)^4$, which is the cited formula. For unit 15's coil, $N = 10$, $a = 20$ mm at 140 kHz: $ka = 5.87\times10^{-5}$, $(ka)^4 = 1.19\times10^{-17}$, and $R_{\mathrm{rad}} = 197.3\times100\times1.19\times10^{-17} = 2.34\times10^{-13}\ \Omega$, a fraction $2.8\times10^{-12}$ of the coil's $0.0833\ \Omega$. Unit 15 (The phone on the pad, closed) left to this branch why a coil 4 cm across at $\lambda = 2.1$ km radiates almost nothing; this is the number.

Directivity: where the power goes

directivity

With $\mathcal U(\theta,\varphi)$ the far-field power per unit solid angle and $P_{\mathrm{rad}} = \oint\mathcal U\,\mathrm d\Omega$,

$$D(\theta,\varphi) = \frac{4\pi\,\mathcal U(\theta,\varphi)}{P_{\mathrm{rad}}} .$$

An isotropic radiator, a reference that radiates equally in every direction, has $D = 1$. "The directivity" means the maximum.

The current element. $\mathcal U\propto\sin^2\theta$ (MR 24), and $\oint\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\Omega = 8\pi/3$, so

$$D(\theta) = \frac{4\pi\sin^2\theta}{8\pi/3} = \tfrac32\sin^2\theta,\qquad D = 1.5 .$$

And $\oint D\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi$ for any antenna, by the definition: directivity only redistributes the power.

Computed. The doughnut sends 1.5 times the isotropic intensity sideways and none along its axis; both shapes carry the same total power.

$\mathcal U$ for $\mathcal U$. The power per unit solid angle is written $\mathcal U$ to keep it apart from unit 15's figure of merit $U$; Balanis writes $U$ for it (Balanis, ch. 2, "Fundamental Parameters and Figures-of-Merit of Antennas", for directivity, gain, radiation efficiency and effective area; cited for the definitions, which are standard). In the far field, $\mathcal U = r^2|\bar{\vec S}|$ with $\bar{\vec S}$ the mean Poynting vector, which is independent of $r$ there.

The current element in full. MR 24 (The Larmor formula) gives the angular distribution $\propto\sin^2\theta$ about $\ddot{\vec p}$, which for the element is along $\hat z$. Write $\mathcal U = \mathcal U_0\sin^2\theta$; then $P_{\mathrm{rad}} = \mathcal U_0\cdot8\pi/3$ and $D = 4\pi\mathcal U_0 \sin^2\theta/(\mathcal U_0\,8\pi/3) = \tfrac32\sin^2\theta$. The integral: $\oint\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\Omega = 2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,\mathrm d\theta = 2\pi\cdot\tfrac43$. The triangular current has the same pattern (its $\ddot{\vec p}$ is along $\hat z$ too), so the same $D = 1.5$.

$\oint D\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi$, the one line. $\oint D\,\mathrm d\Omega = \frac{4\pi}{P_{\mathrm{rad}}}\oint\mathcal U\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi$. Section 5 needs this for gain, through the next slide.

What "isotropic" is. A reference, used only as a yardstick: $D = 1$ in every direction. No antenna in this unit is claimed to be one; section 6 uses $G = 1$ to state a path's loss without any particular antenna.

Gain, efficiency, and decibels

radiation efficiency and gain

Of the power $P_{\mathrm{in}}$ accepted at the terminals, a fraction $e_{\mathrm{rad}} = P_{\mathrm{rad}}/P_{\mathrm{in}}$ is radiated; the rest heats the conductor. For a series model with loss resistance $R_{\mathrm{loss}}$,

$$e_{\mathrm{rad}} = \frac{R_{\mathrm{rad}}}{R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}}},$$ $$G(\theta,\varphi) = e_{\mathrm{rad}}D(\theta,\varphi).$$

So $G = 4\pi\,\mathcal U/P_{\mathrm{in}}$, and for a lossless antenna $\oint G\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi$.

decibels

A power ratio $x$ is $10\log_{10}x$ dB. dBi: a gain in dB against isotropic, $D = 1.5$ is 1.76 dBi. dBm: a power in dB against 1 mW, so 1 W is 30 dBm and 20 W is 43.0 dBm.

A logarithmic ruler, to scale: eleven decades of power on one line, which is why link budgets are kept in decibels.

Gain and the definitions. Balanis, ch. 2 (cited), defines the gain with the power accepted by the antenna, so that $G = e_{\mathrm{rad}}D$ with $e_{\mathrm{rad}}$ the conduction and dielectric efficiency; losses from a mismatch between the antenna and its feed are accounted separately. This unit follows that: mismatch is handled by unit 08's $\Gamma$ (Feeding it: matching, optional), and gain includes only the antenna's own loss.

The lossless integral, one line. $\oint G\,\mathrm d\Omega = e_{\mathrm{rad}}\oint D\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi e_{\mathrm{rad}}$, which is $4\pi$ when $e_{\mathrm{rad}} = 1$. A lossy antenna has $\oint G\,\mathrm d\Omega \lt 4\pi$: some of what it accepts is heat.

The decibel numbers. $10\log_{10}1.5 = 1.761$; $10\log_{10}(20/0.001) = 43.01$; $10\log_{10}1.64 = 2.15$; $10\log_{10}50.12 = 17.0$. The ruler's marks: $-37.9$ dBm is $1.62\times10^{-7}$ W and $-70.05$ dBm is $9.88\times10^{-11}$ W, section 6's two answers. A ratio in dB adds where the ratios multiply, which is why section 6 can add a transmitter's dBm, two antennas' dBi and a path's dB.

Field quantities. A voltage ratio $x$ is $20\log_{10}x$ dB, since power goes as its square; this unit uses only power ratios.

section 3

A short antenna: mostly reactive, and when it is mostly lossy

At its terminals an antenna is an impedance: radiation resistance, loss resistance and a reactance. For a short one the reactance dwarfs the rest; the wire alone may still be efficient, but tuning the reactance out with a real coil is what makes it lossy.

16 · short antennas

The antenna at its terminals

Seen from its feed, a transmitting antenna is a one-port: unit 05's impedance,

$$Z_a = R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}} + iX_a,\qquad \mathrm{Re}\,Z_a \ge 0\ ✓.$$

Loss. At these frequencies the current flows in a skin of depth $\delta = \sqrt{2\rho/\omega\mu_0}$ (stated from branch 10, as unit 07's problems did). A wire of radius $a\gg\delta$ then has the loss resistance

$$R_{\mathrm{loss}} = \frac{\rho\,l}{2\pi a\,\delta}\quad\text{(uniform current)},$$

a third of that for the bare wire's triangular current (problem P2).

Units: $\Omega\,\mathrm m\cdot\mathrm m/(\mathrm m\cdot\mathrm m) = \Omega$ ✓. Copper, $\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm m$: $\delta = 6.52\ \mu\mathrm m$ at 100 MHz, $65.2\ \mu\mathrm m$ at 1 MHz.

The antenna (dashed) as a series $R_{\mathrm{rad}}$, $R_{\mathrm{loss}}$ and reactance, marked passively. For a short antenna the reactance is capacitive (next slide). Only $R_{\mathrm{rad}}$'s power leaves as radiation.

Why an impedance. The antenna, with the space around it, is linear; at one frequency the ratio of the feed voltage phasor to the feed current phasor is a complex number, $Z_a$. Its real part is the mean power taken per unit $|I|^2$: the radiated power (section 1) plus the conductor's heat. Its imaginary part is section 2's reactive power, $Q = X_a|I|^2$, negative and large for a short antenna. $\mathrm{Re}\,Z_a\ge0$ because the antenna is passive: it never returns more mean power than it takes (unit 05's positive-real impedance).

The skin depth, stated. Branch 10 proves $\delta = \sqrt{2/\omega\mu_0\sigma} = \sqrt{2\rho/\omega\mu_0}$; this unit uses it as a stated result, exactly as unit 07's problem sheet did. A learner who skipped branch 10 needs only the picture: at high frequency the current keeps to a layer of thickness $\delta$ at the conductor's surface. Numbers: at 100 MHz, $\omega\mu_0 = 2\pi\times10^8\times4\pi\times10^{-7} = 789.6$, $\delta = \sqrt{2\times1.68\times10^{-8}/789.6} = 6.52\ \mu\mathrm m$; at 1 MHz, ten times larger, $65.2\ \mu\mathrm m$.

$R_{\mathrm{loss}}$. For $a\gg\delta$ the current is spread over a ring of circumference $2\pi a$ and effective thickness $\delta$, so the resistance per metre is $\rho/(2\pi a\delta)$ (branch 10's result, used as stated). With a uniform current $\hat I$ the loss is $\tfrac12(\rho l/2\pi a\delta)\hat I^2$. With the triangular current $\hat I(1 - 2|z|/l)$ the loss is $\tfrac12(\rho/2\pi a\delta)\hat I^2\int(1 - 2|z|/l)^2\mathrm dz = \tfrac12(\rho/2\pi a\delta)\hat I^2\,l/3$: referred to the feed current, a third.

16 · short antennas

The reactance: two arms as a capacitor

A short antenna's near field is mostly the electric field of its end charges (section 2), so at its terminals it looks like a capacitor. An estimate, with factors of order one dropped:

  • Each arm, of length $h = l/2$ and radius $a$, carries a charge spread along it. A uniform line charge $q'$ on a thin segment has potential about $(q'/2\pi\varepsilon_0)\ln(h/a)$ at its middle (MR 23's logarithmic potential), so an arm's capacitance is $C_{\mathrm{arm}}\approx2\pi\varepsilon_0h/\ln(h/a)$.
  • The two arms in series:
$$C\approx\frac{\pi\varepsilon_0l}{2\ln(l/2a)},\qquad X_a\approx-\frac1{\omega C}.$$

10 cm at 100 MHz: $C\approx0.356$ pF, $X_a\approx-4.5$ kΩ. 1 m at 1 MHz: $2.24$ pF, $-71$ kΩ.

Units: $\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}\,m} = \mathrm F$ ✓. $X_a\propto1/\omega$: unit 09's frequency-dependent antenna impedance, in one case.

The arms hold opposite charges, and the field between them stores the reactive energy of section 2. Seen from the feed, two arm capacitances in series.

The estimate, in full. A thin segment of length $h$ with uniform line charge $q'$ has, at its middle and on its surface, the potential $\frac{q'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\frac{\mathrm dz}{\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}}\approx\frac{q'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,2\ln(h/a)$ for $h\gg a$ — MR 23's logarithmic potential of a line charge, cut off at the segment's ends. So its capacitance to a distant reference is about $q'h/\varphi = 2\pi\varepsilon_0h/\ln(h/a)$. The two arms carry $\pm q$ and their difference of potential is twice one arm's: two such capacitances in series, $C\approx\pi\varepsilon_0h/\ln(h/a)$ with $h = l/2$, which is the boxed form with $\ln(l/2a)$. The neglected pieces — the charge is not uniform along a real arm, each arm's charge changes the other's potential, the logarithm's argument is uncertain by a factor of order one — make this an estimate, good to tens of per cent for thin wires. Problem P2 asks for it. Antenna books give refined formulas; none is quoted here, because none could be checked against its source.

Two antennas are one two-port

Take two antennas, each with a port. Maxwell's equations are linear, and so are the conductors, so the port voltages are linear in the port currents (phasors at one frequency):

$$V_1 = z_{11}I_1 + z_{12}I_2,\qquad V_2 = z_{21}I_1 + z_{22}I_2 .$$

This is unit 03's two-port, with the field between the antennas inside the box.

  • $z_{11}$ is antenna 1's own input impedance, $Z_a$ of section 3, when antenna 2 is open-circuited.
  • $z_{21}$ is the voltage antenna 2 develops, open-circuited, per ampere fed into antenna 1: the coupling.

Unit 03 proved $z_{12} = z_{21}$ when every element law is symmetric. The question is whether the field is.

The two-port. Each port is marked passively, its current entering at $+$; nothing inside the dashed box is a lumped component.

Why a two-port exists. Each antenna is fed at a small gap across which a voltage is defined, as for unit 02's lumped elements: the gap is small, and outside it the conductor carries the current. Everything else — the conductors, the space, any other linear objects nearby — is inside the box. Linearity holds because Maxwell's equations are linear in the fields and sources, and Ohm's law in the conductors is linear (unit 00). So with both ports driven, the response is the sum of the responses to each, and the voltages are linear in the currents. Unit 03 (Reciprocity, from Tellegen) defined $z_{21}$ exactly so: the voltage at port 2 per ampere into port 1, with port 2 open.

What unit 03's proof does not cover. Its proof used Tellegen's theorem on a graph whose every edge obeys a symmetric law. A field is not a graph of lumped elements, so the proof does not apply as it stands, and unit 03 said as much: "For fields — an antenna transmitting and receiving — the corresponding statement is the Lorentz reciprocity theorem, which branch 16 cites" (Where reciprocity fails, notes). The next two slides prove the field's version for two current elements and cite the general theorem.

16 · reciprocity

Reciprocity for two current elements

theorem · two current elements

For two current elements, $l_1\hat l_1$ at $\vec r_1$ and $l_2\hat l_2$ at $\vec r_2$, each carrying a uniform current: $z_{12} = z_{21}$.

proof

A field $\vec E$ does work on element 2's current at the rate $I_2l_2\hat l_2\cdot\vec E$, power the port delivers; under the passive convention, $V_2 = -l_2\hat l_2\cdot\vec E$. So $z_{21} = -l_2\hat l_2\cdot\vec E_1(\vec r_2)/I_1$.

Section 2's fields: $\vec E_1(\vec r) = \dfrac{I_1l_1}{4\pi i\omega\varepsilon_0}\,\mathsf G(\vec r - \vec r_1)\,\hat l_1$ with $\mathsf G = k^2g\,\mathsf 1 + \nabla\nabla g$, $g = e^{-ikr}/r$. Hence

$$z_{21} = -\frac{l_1l_2}{4\pi i\omega\varepsilon_0}\,\hat l_2\cdot\mathsf G(\vec r_2 - \vec r_1)\,\hat l_1 .$$

$\mathsf G$ is a symmetric matrix, and even in $\vec r$. Exchanging 1 and 2 changes nothing: $z_{12} = z_{21}$. $\square$

Two elements in any positions and orientations, near or far. The coupling depends on them only through $\hat l_2\cdot\mathsf G(\vec r)\hat l_1$. Units of $z_{21}$: $\mathrm{m^2\,m^{-3}}/(\mathrm{s^{-1}\,F\,m^{-1}}) = \mathrm{s/F} = \Omega$ ✓.

Full proof

The port voltage. Let element 2 carry a current $i_2$ and sit in a field $\vec E$ made by other sources. The rate at which that field does work on element 2's charges is $\int\vec J_2\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV = i_2l_2\hat l_2\cdot\vec E$ (thin, short, uniform current; $\vec E$ is uniform over it). That energy leaves the field and enters the circuit at the port: it is power the port delivers. Under the passive convention the port absorbs $v_2i_2$, so $v_2i_2 = -i_2l_2\hat l_2\cdot\vec E + (\text{the element's own terms})$. The own terms are $z_{22}$'s; the external part is $v_2 = -l_2\hat l_2\cdot\vec E$, at every instant and so for phasors. With $\vec E = \vec E_1$, the field of element 1 alone, this is $z_{21}I_1$. The voltage does not depend on $I_2$, so it is the same when port 2 is open, as $z_{21}$'s definition requires. It is the familiar statement that the open-circuit voltage is minus the line integral of the incident field along the element.

The field of element 1. On The exact potential of a point element, $\vec A = \frac{\mu_0I_1l_1}{4\pi}g(r)\hat l_1$ with $g = e^{-ikr}/r$, $r = |\vec r - \vec r_1|$. Outside the source $\vec E = \frac{c^2}{i\omega}\nabla\times\nabla\times\vec A$, and $\nabla\times\nabla\times(g\hat l) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot(g\hat l)) - \hat l\,\nabla^2g = (\hat l\cdot\nabla)\nabla g + k^2g\hat l$, using $\nabla^2g = -k^2g$ for $r\ne0$ (the Helmholtz equation, which $e^{-ikr}/r$ solves away from the origin). So $\vec E_1 = \frac{c^2\mu_0I_1l_1}{4\pi i\omega}\big(k^2g\,\mathsf 1 + \nabla\nabla g\big)\hat l_1$, and $c^2\mu_0 = 1/\varepsilon_0$.

Symmetry. $\nabla\nabla g$ is the Hessian matrix of $g$, $\partial_i\partial_jg$, which is symmetric because mixed partial derivatives commute. For a radial $g(r)$ it is $g''\hat r\hat r^{\mathsf T} + \frac{g'}r(\mathsf 1 - \hat r\hat r^{\mathsf T})$: the two pieces $a(r)\hat l_1 + b(r)(\hat l_1\cdot\hat r)\hat r$ of section 2's field. It depends on $\hat r$ only through $\hat r\hat r^{\mathsf T}$, so $\mathsf G(-\vec r) = \mathsf G(\vec r)$. Now $z_{12} = -\frac{l_1l_2}{4\pi i\omega\varepsilon_0}\hat l_1\cdot\mathsf G(\vec r_1 - \vec r_2)\hat l_2 = -\frac{l_1l_2}{4\pi i\omega\varepsilon_0}\hat l_2\cdot\mathsf G(\vec r_2 - \vec r_1)\hat l_1 = z_{21}$, using $\mathsf G^{\mathsf T} = \mathsf G$ and evenness. $\square$

The one step that used the physics. The field kernel $\mathsf G$ is a symmetric matrix — the analogue of unit 03's symmetric $R_e\hat\imath_e\tilde\imath_e$. A medium whose response is not symmetric would break exactly this step (problem P6).

The Lorentz reciprocity theorem, cited

the Lorentz reciprocity theorem · cited

In linear media whose $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ are symmetric matrices, any two source distributions $\vec J_a$, $\vec J_b$ at one frequency, with fields $\vec E_a$, $\vec E_b$, satisfy

$$\int\vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b\,\mathrm dV = \int\vec E_b\cdot\vec J_a\,\mathrm dV .$$

Why (notes): Maxwell's equations give $\nabla\cdot(\vec E_a\times\vec H_b - \vec E_b\times\vec H_a) = \vec E_b\cdot\vec J_a - \vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b$; integrate over all space, and the surface at infinity contributes nothing because both far fields are transverse with $\vec H = \hat r\times\vec E/\eta_0$.

For two antennas, $\int\vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b = -I_b(z_{ba}I_a)$ and the theorem is $z_{ab} = z_{ba}$; the previous slide is the case of two elements. Unit 03's sentence is landed.

Where it fails: a medium whose $\varepsilon$ or $\mu$ is not a symmetric matrix.

The theorem's setting: two sets of sources at one frequency, in all space. Only the far fields reach the sphere at infinity, and there their contributions cancel.

The derivation, sketched (problem P7 does it in full)

At one frequency, with $e^{+i\omega t}$, Maxwell's curl equations with sources are $\nabla\times\vec E = -i\omega\mu\vec H$ and $\nabla\times\vec H = \vec J + i\omega\varepsilon\vec E$, for scalar $\varepsilon$, $\mu$ here (the symmetric-matrix case goes the same way). Use $\nabla\cdot(\vec A\times\vec B) = \vec B\cdot(\nabla\times\vec A) - \vec A\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B)$: $\nabla\cdot(\vec E_a\times\vec H_b) = \vec H_b\cdot(-i\omega\mu\vec H_a) - \vec E_a\cdot(\vec J_b + i\omega\varepsilon\vec E_b)$ and $\nabla\cdot(\vec E_b\times\vec H_a) = \vec H_a\cdot(-i\omega\mu\vec H_b) - \vec E_b\cdot(\vec J_a + i\omega\varepsilon\vec E_a)$. Subtract: the $\mu\vec H_a\cdot\vec H_b$ and $\varepsilon\vec E_a\cdot\vec E_b$ terms cancel — this is where symmetry of $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ is used — leaving $\nabla\cdot(\vec E_a\times\vec H_b - \vec E_b\times\vec H_a) = \vec E_b\cdot\vec J_a - \vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b$.

The surface at infinity. On a sphere of radius $R\to\infty$ in free space, both far fields are transverse with $\vec H = \hat r\times\vec E/\eta_0$ (MR 24's $\vec E = c\vec B\times\hat r$). Then $\hat r\cdot(\vec E_a\times\vec H_b) = \frac1{\eta_0}\hat r\cdot(\vec E_a\times(\hat r\times\vec E_b)) = \frac1{\eta_0}\vec E_a\cdot\vec E_b$ (since $\vec E_a\times(\hat r\times\vec E_b) = \hat r(\vec E_a\cdot\vec E_b) - \vec E_b(\vec E_a\cdot\hat r)$ and $\vec E_a\cdot\hat r = 0$), and the same with $a$, $b$ exchanged. The integrand cancels identically at order $1/R^2$, and the corrections are $O(1/R^3)$ against an area $4\pi R^2$. So the volume integral of the right side vanishes: $\int\vec E_b\cdot\vec J_a = \int\vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b$.

To the two-port. If $\vec J_b$ is antenna $b$'s current when fed with $I_b$, the previous slide's argument gives $\int\vec E_a\cdot\vec J_b\,\mathrm dV = -I_bV_b^{(a)}$, where $V_b^{(a)} = z_{ba}I_a$ is the open-circuit voltage that $a$'s field produces at $b$'s port. So $-I_bz_{ba}I_a = -I_az_{ab}I_b$. For general antennas this step assumes the current's shape on each antenna is fixed by its own port (one mode), as for the elements; in general the theorem is applied to the antennas' terminals directly.

Sources. Balanis, §3.8, "Reciprocity and Reaction Theorems", treats the theorem for antennas; D. M. Pozar, Microwave Engineering (4th ed., Wiley, 2012), §1.9, "Some Useful Theorems", gives it for fields. Both cited for the general statement; the derivation above is the standard one, written out.

Where it fails. The cancellation needed $\vec H_b\cdot\mu\vec H_a = \vec H_a\cdot\mu\vec H_b$, true for scalar $\mu$ or a symmetric matrix and false otherwise; likewise for $\varepsilon$. No particular material is named here.

What reciprocity buys: one path, both ways

The same pattern. Put antenna 2 far away in direction $(\theta,\varphi)$ and move it round. Transmitting from 1, $|z_{21}|$ traces 1's transmitting pattern; receiving at 1, $|z_{12}|$ traces its receiving pattern. They are equal: an antenna receives best from the directions it transmits best into.

The same loss. With both ports matched, the fraction of power that crosses (section 5) depends on the pair only through $|z_{21}|^2 = |z_{12}|^2$: the path loses the same fraction both ways at one frequency.

the running example

The base station and the phone, at the assumed single frequency of 2.4 GHz: the downlink and the uplink see the same path gain, $-100.1$ dB plus the two antennas' gains (section 6).

No claim is made that real networks use one frequency both ways.

At one frequency the path is symmetric: each direction delivers the same fraction of the power sent, $8.1\times10^{-9}$ here (section 6's numbers).

The pattern, in full. Fix antenna 1 at the origin and put a short element 2 at distance $d$ in direction $\hat r$, oriented along the far field's polarisation there. The open-circuit voltage: effective length

From Reciprocity for two current elements: a field $\vec E$ from distant sources, arriving at a short element, produces the open-circuit voltage

$$V_{\mathrm{oc}} = -\vec E\cdot\hat l\,l\qquad\text{(uniform current)}.$$

For the bare wire's triangular current the same argument weights $\vec E$ by the current's shape, and gives $-\vec E\cdot\hat l\,l/2$.

effective length

The length $l_e$ with $|V_{\mathrm{oc}}| = |\vec E\cdot\hat l|\,l_e$: $l$ for the element with end plates, $l/2$ for the bare short wire. It is $\dot p/\hat I$ — the same weighting that set $R_{\mathrm{rad}}$.

A wave whose $\vec E$ is perpendicular to the element induces nothing: the element receives one polarisation.

A plane wave polarised along the element. The open-circuit voltage is the incident field's component along the element times the effective length.

The weighting, in full. The proof of reciprocity for two elements computed the rate at which an external field does work on the element's current: $\int\vec J\cdot\vec E\,\mathrm dV$. For a current of fixed shape $i(z) = I\,s(z)$ along the element, with $s = 1$ at the feed, this is $I\,\hat l\cdot\vec E\int s(z)\,\mathrm dz$ when $\vec E$ is uniform over the element (short against $\lambda$). So $V_{\mathrm{oc}} = -\hat l\cdot\vec E\int s\,\mathrm dz$, and $l_e = \int s\,\mathrm dz$: $l$ for $s = 1$, $l/2$ for the triangle. The same integral gave $\dot p = I\int s\,\mathrm dz$ in section 1. That the same shape function serves for transmitting and receiving is reciprocity again.

The sign depends on which terminal is called $+$; only $|V_{\mathrm{oc}}|$ enters the power.

Polarisation. If the incident $\vec E$ makes an angle $\psi$ with the element, $|V_{\mathrm{oc}}|$ carries $\cos\psi$ and the received power $\cos^2\psi$. Everything below assumes the wave's polarisation matches the antenna's, as section 6's Friis equation will state among its conditions.

Aperture over gain: one constant for every antenna

Step 1 · reciprocity. Antennas $a$, $b$, $d$ apart, matched, far field. By the definitions, each way:

$$\frac{P_b}{P_a} = \frac{G_aA_b}{4\pi d^2},\qquad \frac{P_a}{P_b} = \frac{G_bA_a}{4\pi d^2}.$$

From the two-port, each ratio is $\dfrac{|z_{ab}|^2}{4R_aR_b}$, the same since $z_{ab} = z_{ba}$. So $G_aA_b = G_bA_a$:

$$\frac{A_a}{G_a} = \frac{A_b}{G_b}\quad\text{for every pair, in every direction.}$$

Step 2 · the constant. The short dipole has $A_e = 3\lambda^2/8\pi$ and $G = 1.5$: the ratio is $\lambda^2/4\pi$.

effective aperture and gain $$A_e(\theta,\varphi) = \frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi}\,G(\theta,\varphi)$$

Units: $\mathrm m^2$ ✓. The phone's dipole: 20.4 cm².

The same pair used both ways. Reciprocity makes the two fractions equal, so the ratio $A/G$ cannot depend on which antenna it belongs to.

Full proof of step 1

By the definitions. Antenna $a$ accepts $P_a$ and radiates with gain $G_a$ towards $b$, so at distance $d$ the intensity is $S = G_aP_a/(4\pi d^2)$ (gain is $4\pi\mathcal U/P_{\mathrm{in}}$, and $\mathcal U = Sd^2$ in the far field). Antenna $b$, matched, delivers $SA_b$ to its load, by the definition of $A_b$. So $P_b/P_a = G_aA_b/(4\pi d^2)$, and symmetrically.

By the two-port. Drive $a$ with peak current $\hat I_a$. In the weak-coupling limit (the previous slides) $a$ accepts $P_a = \tfrac12R_a\hat I_a^2$ with $R_a = \mathrm{Re}\,z_{aa}$, and $b$ is a Thévenin source $z_{ba}\hat I_a$ behind $z_{bb}$, delivering $|z_{ba}|^2\hat I_a^2/(8R_b)$ into its matched load. The ratio is $|z_{ba}|^2/(4R_aR_b)$. The other way round it is $|z_{ab}|^2/(4R_bR_a)$. With $z_{ab} = z_{ba}$ the two are equal. Hence $G_aA_b = G_bA_a$, i.e. $A_a/G_a = A_b/G_b$. Since $b$ may be placed in any direction from $a$, the statement holds direction by direction.

Conditions. Matched loads, matched polarisation, free space, far field; gains include each antenna's efficiency, as $P_a$ is the power accepted and $R_a$ includes loss. These are the conditions of section 6's Friis equation, which is step 1 read forwards.

Step 2. For any antenna $b$, $A_b/G_b = A_{\mathrm{dip}}/G_{\mathrm{dip}} = (3\lambda^2/8\pi)/1.5 = \lambda^2/4\pi$. The constant is fixed by any one antenna whose aperture and gain are both known, and the short dipole is the one this unit computed from scratch. The numbers: $\lambda^2/4\pi = 12.4\ \mathrm{cm^2}$ at 2.4 GHz; times 1.64, 20.4 cm²; times the base station's 50.1, $0.0622\ \mathrm{m^2}$.

Sources. The relation $A_e = G\lambda^2/4\pi$ is in Balanis, ch. 2 (cited); the proof here is the reciprocity argument written out for this unit's two-port.

The same constant from thermodynamics: a sketch

A lossless matched antenna in a black enclosure at temperature $T$, joined by a matched lossless line (unit 08) to a resistor at $T$ outside.

  • Out. The resistor sends $k_BT\,\mathrm d\nu$ down the line (Nyquist 1928, cited) and the antenna radiates it.
  • In. The enclosure's radiation has brightness $2k_BT/\lambda^2$ per unit bandwidth, area and solid angle (Rayleigh–Jeans); the antenna takes one polarisation, $k_BT/\lambda^2$, so it delivers $k_BT\,\mathrm d\nu\cdot\frac1{\lambda^2}\oint A_e\,\mathrm d\Omega$.
  • Balance, by the second law, in every band:
$$\oint A_e\,\mathrm d\Omega = \lambda^2 .$$

With $A_e\propto G$ (previous slide) and $\oint G\,\mathrm d\Omega = 4\pi$ (lossless): $A_e = G\lambda^2/4\pi$ — with no particular antenna.

Valid while $h\nu\ll k_BT$: $3.8\times10^{-4}$ at 2.4 GHz and 300 K.

Everything at one temperature. Whatever the antenna's pattern, the power it collects from the enclosure must equal the power the resistor sends it.

A sketch, labelled. Two inputs are cited, not proved. Equipartition: in thermal equilibrium each oscillator mode holds a mean energy $k_BT$ — a result of classical statistical mechanics, not derived in this course or in MR 00–24 (unit 00 uses its kinetic form, citing Ashcroft & Mermin, ch. 1), and invoked here exactly as Nyquist invokes "the equipartition law". Nyquist's result, $k_BT\,\mathrm d\nu$ from a resistor into a matched line (below). The Rayleigh–Jeans brightness is not a third input: the notes below derive it from equipartition and the mode count. The rest is filled in by problem P11. No attribution of this antenna version of the argument is made.

Nyquist's half. H. Nyquist, "Thermal agitation of electric charge in conductors", Phys. Rev. 32, 110–113 (1928): two resistors $R$ at temperature $T$ joined by a lossless line of characteristic impedance $R$ — unit 08's matched line, which absorbs everything sent into it. Shorting the line's ends traps its modes, which number "$2l\,\mathrm d\nu/v$" in a band $\mathrm d\nu$ for a line of length $l$ and speed $v$, each with "an energy equal to $kT$ on the average, on the basis of the equipartition law". Half of that energy travels each way, and it crosses in time $l/v$, so "the average power, transferred from each conductor to the line within the frequency interval $\mathrm d\nu$ … is $kT\,\mathrm d\nu$", which is his $E^2\,\mathrm d\nu = 4RkT\,\mathrm d\nu$ (his $k$ is Boltzmann's constant, $k_B$ here, not a wavenumber). Nyquist also notes that a lossless resonant network between them shows the balance must hold within any frequency band, not only in total.

The enclosure's half. MR 24 (Every mode is an oscillator): each spatial Fourier mode of the field is an oscillator. In a box of volume $V$ the modes with frequency in $\mathrm d\nu$ number $V\cdot8\pi\nu^2\mathrm d\nu/c^3$, counting two polarisations (the volume of a shell in $\vec k$-space, $4\pi k^2\mathrm dk$, times $V/(2\pi)^3$, times 2). Each has mean energy $k_BT$ (equipartition, cited above), so the energy density is $u_\nu = 8\pi\nu^2k_BT/c^3$. Isotropic radiation of energy density $u$ crosses unit area, per unit solid angle, at $uc/4\pi$: the brightness $B_\nu = 2\nu^2k_BT/c^2 = 2k_BT/\lambda^2$. One polarisation carries half.

The balance. From a solid angle $\mathrm d\Omega$, the antenna collects $\frac{k_BT}{\lambda^2}\mathrm d\nu\,A_e(\theta,\varphi)\,\mathrm d\Omega$ into its matched line. Integrated, $\frac{k_BT\,\mathrm d\nu}{\lambda^2}\oint A_e\,\mathrm d\Omega$. In equilibrium the line carries equal power each way in every band, or the resistor would warm and the enclosure cool, with no work done: the second law forbids it. So $\oint A_e\,\mathrm d\Omega = \lambda^2$. Write $A_e = \kappa G$ (the previous slide); then $\kappa\cdot4\pi = \lambda^2$.

Validity. $h\nu/k_BT = 6.626\times10^{-34}\times2.4\times10^9/(1.381\times10^{-23}\times300) = 3.84\times10^{-4}$, with $h = 6.62607015\times10^{-34}$ J s and $k_B = 1.380649\times10^{-23}$ J/K (CODATA): far inside the classical regime where each mode holds $k_BT$.

Where the lost half goes

The matched receiving element carries a current $\hat I = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/(2R_{\mathrm{rad}})$. A current element carrying a current radiates (section 1):

$$P_{\mathrm{re}} = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^2 = \frac{\hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2}{8R_{\mathrm{rad}}} = P_L .$$
the lost half, for a lossless element

The power "dissipated" in the Thévenin $R_{\mathrm{rad}}$ is exactly the power the antenna sends back out as a wave: re-radiated power. With $R_{\mathrm{loss}} \gt 0$, part of the internal power is real heat.

The wave loses $P_L + P_{\mathrm{re}} = 2P_L$ in all (Poynting's theorem round the antenna): the incident and re-radiated fields interfere to remove it from the forward direction.

The matched, lossless element: half of what it takes from the wave goes to the load; the other half, the power in its own $R_{\mathrm{rad}}$, leaves again as an outgoing wave.

Unit 04's question, answered in this model. Unit 04 (Where matching is right: a radio receiver): "What the 'lost' half in $R_a$ is physically is branch 16's question." For the lossless current element at the conjugate match, the current is $\hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/(Z_a + Z_a^*) = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/(2R_{\mathrm{rad}})$; the element is then a current element carrying that current, and by section 1 it radiates $\tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^2 = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2/(8R_{\mathrm{rad}})$, the same as the load's $P_L$. That radiation is the re-radiated or scattered power.

Energy, said carefully. Poynting's theorem on a large sphere round the element: the total field is the incident plane wave plus the element's own radiated field. The mean power flowing inward through the sphere equals the power absorbed inside, $P_L$. The outward flux of the scattered field alone is $P_{\mathrm{re}}$. The cross term between incident and scattered fields therefore carries $P_L + P_{\mathrm{re}}$ inward: the wave behind the element has lost that much, removed by interference in the forward direction. The figure of $2P_L$ is stated for this model only; computing the interference term in detail is not done here.

With loss. The internal resistance is $R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}}$. At the match the current is $\hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/2(R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}})$, the load takes $\tfrac12(R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}})\hat I^2$, and of the equal internal power a fraction $R_{\mathrm{rad}}/(R_{\mathrm{rad}} + R_{\mathrm{loss}})$ is re-radiated and the rest heats the wire.

Open, matched, shorted — and the limits

In the same model, with $\hat I = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/(Z_a + Z_L)$ and $P_{\mathrm{re}} = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat I^2$ (lossless):

  • Open, $Z_L = \infty$: $\hat I = 0$; nothing absorbed, nothing re-radiated.
  • Matched, $Z_L = Z_a^*$: $P_L = P_{\mathrm{re}}$.
  • Tuned and shorted, $Z_L = -iX_a$: $\hat I = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/R_{\mathrm{rad}}$, $P_L = 0$, and $P_{\mathrm{re}} = 4P_L^{\mathrm{matched}}$: a scattering cross-section $4A_e = 3\lambda^2/2\pi = 0.477\lambda^2$. A resonant short wire scatters strongly.
limits of the Thévenin picture

This used a one-mode model: the port current is the only current, and nothing on the structure scatters independently of the load. For antennas in general, equating the power in the Thévenin impedance with the scattered power is not correct (Collin 2003, cited).

Three loads on the same lossless element, in this one-mode model. Only the match delivers anything; the short re-radiates four times the matched power.

The shorted case, in full. With $Z_L = -iX_a$ the loop impedance is $R_{\mathrm{rad}} + iX_a - iX_a = R_{\mathrm{rad}}$, so $\hat I = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}/R_{\mathrm{rad}}$, twice the matched current, and $P_{\mathrm{re}} = \tfrac12R_{\mathrm{rad}}\hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2/R_{\mathrm{rad}}^2 = \hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2/(2R_{\mathrm{rad}}) = 4\cdot\hat V_{\mathrm{oc}}^2/(8R_{\mathrm{rad}})$. Divided by the incident intensity, this is a cross-section $4A_e = 4\cdot3\lambda^2/8\pi = 3\lambda^2/2\pi = 0.4775\lambda^2$. "Tuned" matters: an untuned short element has $|Z_a|\gg R_{\mathrm{rad}}$, its current is tiny, and it scatters weakly.

The Friis transmission equation

A transmitter accepting $P_t$ with gain $G_t$ produces, at distance $d$, the intensity $S = P_tG_t/4\pi d^2$. A matched receiver collects $SA_r$, with $A_r = G_r\lambda^2/4\pi$:

the Friis transmission equation $$\frac{P_r}{P_t} = G_tG_r\Big(\frac\lambda{4\pi d}\Big)^2 = \frac{A_tA_r}{\lambda^2d^2}$$

Units: dimensionless, both forms ✓.

Conditions: free space; each antenna in the other's far field; matched loads; matched polarisation; $G$ including each antenna's efficiency.

$(\lambda/4\pi d)^2$ is the path gain between isotropic antennas: not a loss in the medium, which absorbs nothing, but the spreading of the power over $4\pi d^2$ against an isotropic antenna's $\lambda^2/4\pi$.

Energy: $P_r\le P_t$ is not built in. Section 7 shows that is exactly where Friis stops being true.

The transmitter's power spreads over the sphere, concentrated towards the receiver by $G_t$; the receiver takes the part that falls on its effective area.

The derivation, step by step. In the far field the transmitter's power per unit solid angle towards the receiver is $\mathcal U = G_tP_t/4\pi$ (the definition of gain with $P_t$ the accepted power). At distance $d$ a solid angle $\mathrm d\Omega$ covers an area $d^2\mathrm d\Omega$, so the intensity is $S = \mathcal U/d^2 = G_tP_t/4\pi d^2$. The matched receiver delivers $SA_r$ by the definition of $A_r$. With $A_r = G_r\lambda^2/4\pi$: $P_r/P_t = G_tG_r\lambda^2/(4\pi d)^2$. Writing $G_t = 4\pi A_t/\lambda^2$ as well gives the second form, $P_r/P_t = A_tA_r/(\lambda d)^2$.

Source. H. T. Friis, "A note on a simple transmission formula", Proc. IRE 34(5), 254–256 (1946), cited for the formula. The derivation above stands on this unit's results and does not depend on the paper. The equation is also in Balanis, §2.17, "Friis Transmission Equation and Radar Range Equation", and in Pozar, ch. 14, "Introduction to Microwave Systems" (both cited).

The conditions, one by one. Free space: nothing between or near the antennas reflects or absorbs. Far field: the intensity falls as $1/d^2$ and the receiver sees a plane wave across its aperture; section 7 makes this quantitative. Matched loads: $A_r$ was defined at the conjugate match. Polarisation: otherwise a factor $\cos^2\psi$. Gain with efficiency: $P_t$ is the power accepted, $P_r$ the power delivered.

Real links receive less than this: reflections from the ground and from buildings, absorption by bodies and obstacles, and the atmosphere are named here as reasons, with no figures.

When Friis fails

The aperture form $P_r/P_t = A_tA_r/(\lambda d)^2$ exceeds 1 once $\sqrt{A_tA_r} \gt \lambda d$. That is impossible, so a hypothesis — the far field — has failed before it.

Why, by spot size. The far field of a source of width $w_t$ is, in each direction, the Fourier transform of its current distribution (notes, from MR 24's retarded potential). By MR 20's uncertainty relation, a source of width $w_t$ spreads into angles of order

$$\Delta\theta\sim\frac\lambda{w_t},\qquad\text{a spot of width}\ \sim\frac{\lambda d}{w_t}\ \text{at}\ d .$$
  • A receiver much smaller than the spot takes the fraction Friis gives.
  • A receiver as large as the spot, $w_tw_r\gtrsim\lambda d$, collects most of the beam, and the $d^{-2}$ law no longer describes it. Then $d\lesssim w_{\max}^2/\lambda$: the pair lies inside the larger aperture's Fresnel region (next slide).

Not to scale. A beam from an aperture of width $w_t$ cannot be much narrower than $\lambda/w_t$ in angle. A receiver covering the spot (shaded) gathers most of the power; a small one inside it gathers the Friis fraction.

The Fourier transform, from MR 24. In the retarded vector potential, keep the phase that the dipole approximation dropped: far from a source of size $w$, $|\vec x - \vec y|\approx r - \hat r\cdot\vec y$, and the retarded time shifts by $\hat r\cdot\vec y/c$. At one frequency the potential becomes $\vec A\approx\frac{\mu_0e^{-ikr}}{4\pi r}\int\vec J(\vec y)\,e^{ik\hat r\cdot\vec y}\,\mathrm d^3y$: the source's Fourier transform at the wave-vector $k\hat r$. (This needs $r\gg w^2/\lambda$ as well as $r\gg w$, so that the next term of the expansion, of order $w^2/r$, is small against $\lambda$: the Fraunhofer condition of the next slide.) For an aperture of width $w$ along $x$, the far field in a direction at angle $\theta$ from the axis samples the transform at spatial frequency $k\sin\theta$.

The spread. MR 20 (The uncertainty relation): a function and its Fourier transform cannot both be narrow; the product of their widths is bounded below by a constant of order one. An aperture field of width $w$ has a transform of width of order $1/w$ in $k\sin\theta$; the constant is fixed by one example. A uniform current across $|x|\le w/2$ has the transform $\int_{-w/2}^{w/2}e^{iqx}\,\mathrm dx = 2\sin(qw/2)/q$, whose first zeros are at $q = \pm2\pi/w$. With $q = k\sin\theta$ that is $\sin\theta = \lambda/w$: the main beam reaches out to an angle of about $\lambda/w$ on each side, which is the slide's $\Delta\theta\sim\lambda/w_t$. Other illuminations, and other ways of measuring width, change the constant by a factor of order one, not the scaling. This is the same width–bandwidth reciprocity. Balanis, ch. 12, "Aperture Antennas", treats apertures properly, with their fields rather than a current sheet (cited for further reading); only the order of magnitude is used here, and nothing on these slides depends on the constant.

The receiver. Friis treats the receiver as a point collecting $SA_r$: true when the intensity is uniform across it and falls as $1/d^2$, i.e. when the receiver is much smaller than the spot. When $w_r\gtrsim\lambda d/w_t$ the receiver covers the beam, and the fraction collected approaches 1 rather than growing without bound. The receiver is then too wide to see the transmitter as a point: since $w_{\max}^2\ge w_tw_r\gtrsim\lambda d$, the distance is inside the larger aperture's Fraunhofer distance $2w^2/\lambda$ (next slide), in its Fresnel region, where its field has not yet settled into its far-field pattern. By reciprocity it does not matter which of the two is the transmitter.

The far-field distance, and Friis's ceiling

Seen from a point at distance $d$ on the axis, the edge of an aperture of width $w$ is further away than its centre by

$$\sqrt{d^2 + (w/2)^2} - d\approx\frac{w^2}{8d}.$$

Keep it below $\lambda/16$ (phase within $22.5°$):

the Fraunhofer distance $$d_F = \frac{2w^2}\lambda$$

Beyond it: the far field (Balanis §2.2, cited, who writes $D$ for $w$).

Friis's ceiling. Equal circular apertures at $d_F$, $A = \pi w^2/4$: $A^2/(\lambda d_F)^2 = (\pi w^2/4)^2/(2w^2)^2 = \pi^2/64 = 0.154$. Inside its own region of validity, Friis never predicts more than about 15% for equal apertures.

The path from the aperture's edge is longer than from its centre. Far enough away the difference is a small fraction of a wavelength, and the aperture radiates as one source.

The expansion. $\sqrt{d^2 + (w/2)^2} = d\sqrt{1 + w^2/4d^2}\approx d(1 + w^2/8d^2)$, so the excess is $w^2/8d$. Setting $w^2/8d = \lambda/16$ gives $d = 2w^2/\lambda$. The phase error is then $2\pi/16 = 22.5°$. The choice of $\lambda/16$ is a convention. Balanis treats the field regions in §2.2 and, for wire antennas, in §4.4, and states the far-field distance as $2D^2/\lambda$ with $D$ the largest dimension of the antenna; this unit writes $w$ to keep $D$ for directivity. A stricter criterion gives a larger distance and a smaller ceiling.

The ceiling, in full. Equal circular apertures of diameter $w$ have $A = \pi w^2/4$ each. At $d = 2w^2/\lambda$, $\lambda d = 2w^2$, and $A^2/(\lambda d)^2 = \pi^2w^4/16\div4w^4 = \pi^2/64 = 0.1542$. Further away the ratio is smaller, as $d^{-2}$. So any pair of equal apertures for which Friis is valid by this criterion receives at most about 15% by Friis. Collecting most of a transmitter's power means working inside the Fraunhofer distance, where Friis is not the right tool.

Unequal apertures. For a small receiver and a large transmitter the relevant condition is the transmitter's $2w_t^2/\lambda$; the previous slide's spot argument says the same thing in terms of the receiver's size.

Worked: beaming from geostationary orbit

worked example 5 · orbit to ground

1. The distance. A circular orbit with the sidereal day as its period, by Kepler's third law: $r^3 = GM_ET^2/4\pi^2$, $r = 42\,164$ km, and above the equator $d = r - R_E = 35\,786$ km.

2. The scale. At an assumed 2.45 GHz, $\lambda = 0.1224$ m and $\lambda d = 4.38\times10^6\ \mathrm{m^2}$.

3. Equal apertures reach Friis's ratio of 1 at $A = \lambda d$: circles 2.36 km across, each.

4. Unequal. An assumed 1 km transmitter and 10 km receiver: $A_tA_r/(\lambda d)^2 = (7.85\times10^5)(7.85\times10^7)/(4.38\times10^6)^2 = 3.2$.

5. Is energy conservation violated? No: Friis does not apply. The spot, $\lambda d/w_t\sim4.4$ km, fits in the 10 km receiver (the pair is in its Fresnel region), which can collect most of it — not 320%.

Not to scale. The spot from a 1 km transmitter is about 4.4 km across; a 10 km receiver covers it, so the pair is in its Fresnel region, where Friis's 3.2 means nothing.

Step 1. Three World Geodetic System 1984 values, as published by the US National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency: $GM_E = 3.986004418\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$, the equatorial radius $R_E = 6378.137$ km, and the Earth's angular velocity $\omega_E = 7.292115\times10^{-5}$ rad/s. A geostationary orbit turns with the Earth relative to the stars, so its period is $T = 2\pi/\omega_E = 86\,164.1$ s, the sidereal day. Then $r^3 = GM_ET^2/4\pi^2 = GM_E/\omega_E^2 = 7.496\times10^{22}\ \mathrm{m^3}$, $r = 4.2164\times10^7$ m, and $d = 42\,164.2 - 6378.1 = 35\,786$ km, the distance to the point directly beneath. Kepler's third law for a circular orbit is MR 08's (Third law: the constant, not just the power).

Steps 2–4. $\lambda = 2.998\times10^8/2.45\times10^9 = 0.12237$ m; $\lambda d = 0.12237\times3.5786\times10^7 = 4.379\times10^6\ \mathrm{m^2}$. Equal circles of area $\lambda d$: diameter $2\sqrt{\lambda d/\pi} = 2361$ m. For $w_t = 1$ km and $w_r = 10$ km, $A_t = 7.854\times10^5\ \mathrm{m^2}$, $A_r = 7.854\times10^7\ \mathrm{m^2}$, and $A_tA_r/(\lambda d)^2 = 6.169\times10^{13}/1.9175\times10^{13} = 3.22$.

Step 5. Friis assumed the receiver small against the spot and in the far field. Here the transmitter's Fraunhofer distance is $2w_t^2/\lambda = 2\times10^6/0.1224 = 1.6\times10^7$ m: less than $d$, so the transmitter's own far field does begin before the ground. But the receiver, 10 km across, is wider than the 4.4 km spot, and a receiver cannot collect more than the beam brings; Friis's point-receiver assumption is what fails. Seen from the other end, the 10 km aperture's own Fraunhofer distance is $2\times10^8/0.1224 = 1.6\times10^9$ m, far beyond $d$: the pair is in that aperture's Fresnel region, and by reciprocity that is the same failure. The spot width is an order of magnitude, with a constant of order one.

The lesson, and the scaling. Collecting most of the power over a distance $d$ needs apertures whose product is of order $\lambda d$: kilometres, at microwave frequencies from geostationary orbit. At fixed $d$, $\lambda d\propto1/f$, so the required diameters shrink as $1/\sqrt f$.

The rectenna

a rectenna

A rectifying antenna: an array of receiving antennas, each feeding a rectifier that turns its share of the microwave power into direct current. Each element is section 5's receiving antenna, with a rectifier as its load.

History (Brown 1980, cited):

  • the first rectenna was conceived at Raytheon in 1963 and built and tested by R. George of Purdue University: "28 half-wave dipoles spaced one-half wave-length apart, each dipole terminated in a bridge-type rectifier", with a reflecting sheet a quarter-wavelength behind;
  • a microwave-powered helicopter flew on 1 July 1964, carrying a special "string type" rectenna of rectifier diodes; a photograph of a demonstration in October 1964 shows it "in flight 18.28 meters above a transmitting antenna";
  • at Marshall Space Flight Center in September 1970, at 2450 MHz, the "ratio of DC power out of rectenna to the rf power into the horn was 40.8%. Overall DC-to-DC efficiency was 26.5%."

Schematic, three elements of many: half-wave dipoles, each with a bridge rectifier (diamond), DC outputs joined, a reflecting sheet behind. Not George's layout in detail.

Source. W. C. Brown, "The history of the development of the rectenna", in Solar Power Satellite Microwave Transmission and Reception, NASA Johnson Space Center, 1980 (SPS Microwave Systems Workshop, JSC, 15–18 January 1980; NASA NTRS 19810008041). The quotations are Brown's: the first rectenna is in his figure 2 ("Conceived at Raytheon Company in 1963, it was built and tested by R. George of Purdue University"); the flight of 1 July 1964 is in his text, made "with this string type rectenna" — the special array of his figure 4, built for the helicopter, not George's dipole array — and the text adds that an Air Force contract followed that included a ten-hour continuous flight; the 18.28 m photograph's caption dates that demonstration October 1964. The Marshall Space Flight Center test is captioned "September 1970" (a later sentence in the scan's text layer reads 1979; the page image reads 1970).

Reading the 26.5%. It is DC into the transmitter to DC out of the rectenna, over a short ground test: it includes the conversion to microwaves, the beam between horn and rectenna, and the rectification. It is not a figure for any proposed system, and none is given here.

Why an array. Each half-wave dipole collects from about $1.64\lambda^2/4\pi$ (section 5); to collect from a large beam the rectenna tiles the area with many of them, each with its own rectifier, so that no single rectifier must handle the whole power and the array's elements need not be combined at microwave frequency. The reflecting sheet sends back towards the dipoles what passes between them; why a quarter-wavelength spacing suits is not derived here.

The symbol. The diamond with a diode mark stands for a bridge rectifier. Diodes and rectifiers are not studied in this course; the rectenna's rectifier is treated here only as the receiving antenna's load.
