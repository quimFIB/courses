The premise

Four commitments shape this curriculum. The first three are about what gets taught and in which order; the fourth is about whether the graph actually closes.

The physics leads and the mathematics follows, and there is no unit called “vector calculus”. You asked to start directly with mechanics, and unit 00 does: Newton's second law read as a differential equation and reduced to quadrature in the three cases where that works. None of vector calculus is assumed anywhere, because none of it is needed until unit 04, where the work integral demands it. Every tool then arrives under pressure with a problem already waiting for it — linear ODE theory with the damped driven oscillator, Frobenius and Bessel with Kepler's equation, Legendre with the Earth's oblateness, the calculus of variations with the brachistochrone, Fourier and Sturm–Liouville with the plucked string and the drum, complex analysis with causality, tensors with the inertia tensor, and differential geometry with the failure of flat spacetime to hold gravity. The tensor unit is called Rigid bodies and the PDE unit is called the wave equation. This costs nothing in rigour and buys the one thing that makes self-study of mathematical physics survivable: at every moment you know why you are learning this.

You have the maturity, so nothing is drilled, everything load-bearing is proved, and nothing is mimed. The course will not re-explain a limit, a linear map or an inner product. It will prove Picard–Lindelöf in unit 01, because determinism is a theorem and not a slogan; prove that the Euler–Lagrange equations are coordinate-invariant in unit 12, because that single fact is the seed of general covariance; prove Cauchy's tetrahedron theorem in unit 22, the one place in classical physics where you prove rather than assume that a physical quantity is bilinear; prove Noether in unit 13 so that unit 36's Killing vectors are recognisably the same theorem; and prove the Levi-Civita connection unique in unit 32 so that the Christoffel symbols are derived rather than posited. Mathematics carries the definition and physics carries the motivation, always in that order: a tensor is a multilinear map, and the transformation law is a theorem about its components under a change of basis. Every claim is either proved on the slide, proved in the notes, or labelled cited with where the proof lives — there is no fourth option, and where the proof is a research monograph (KAM, completeness of Sturm–Liouville eigenfunctions, Lovelock, the singularity theorems) the statement and its hypotheses are given exactly and the proof is cited rather than gestured at.

The three ways a mathematically strong beginner fails at physics are designed against explicitly. They are: no dimensional sense, no feel for approximation, and no picture of a system you cannot solve. So unit 00 proves the Buckingham π theorem and makes you predict the pendulum's period up to a function of one dimensionless group before you touch an integral; unit 21 is a full spine unit on asymptotics — optimal truncation, secular terms, boundary layers, Poincaré–Lindstedt — and it is the unit that sets up Mercury's precession as a secular term you deliberately do not remove; and unit 02 is a spine unit on phase portraits, Lyapunov functions and bifurcations, which feeds almost nothing downstream and stays on the spine anyway, because it is where a mathematician first sees a mechanical system rather than solving one. The reinforcement is numerical: almost every checkpoint ends in a quantity the world can refute. 31.8 h for the Foucault pendulum at the Panthéon, 98.6° for a sun-synchronous orbit at 800 km, 343 m/s for sound in air at 20 °C against Newton's wrong 290, 2.46 × 10−15 for Pound–Rebka, 42.98″ per century for Mercury. Dimensions and at least one limit are checked on every boxed result, and a derivation with the right structure and the wrong number has not finished.

It is a dependency graph, it is nearly a chain, and the express route is genuinely closed. Unlike the sister optimization course there are no three independent trunks here; each tool is load-bearing for the next piece of physics, and saying so is more honest than faking parallelism. What independence exists sits in six branch units — 15, 17, 34, 38, 39, 40 — and in one real fork: the analytic-and-field line (18–25) and the geometric line (16, then 26–33) both descend from unit 12 and rejoin at unit 28, which needs 27 from one side and 25 from the other. The graph was then built so that a short path to general relativity is a subgraph rather than a set of apologies: unit 26 derives the Lorentz group from homogeneity, isotropy and one invariant speed, with Maxwell (unit 25) earning that speed's value rather than defining it, and unit 35 gives two derivations of the field equations, the Bianchi–Lovelock–Newtonian-limit route needing only curvature and the equivalence principle. The express route is therefore 24 units with every needs satisfied on the route, and what it costs is named rather than hidden.

What a unit is

Every unit is the same four things. There is no lab and no code: the assessment is that the checkpoint is either true of you or it is not. A unit is done when the checkpoint is true — not when you have read the chapter.

Theory

A deck of 25–45 slides, speaker notes on every content slide, run with ./mr slides NN. Mathematics carries the definition, physics carries the motivation, and every claim is proved on the slide, proved in the notes, or labelled cited. Nothing is used before a unit in its needs has introduced it.

Worked examples

One running example per unit, introduced on the overview slide, returned to in every section and closed at the end, plus three or four fully worked examples carrying every step. Dimensions and at least one known limit are checked on every boxed result.

Problems and solutions

8–14 problems, at least one a proof rather than a computation: ./mr problems NN. Every problem has a full worked solution (./mr solutions NN) and a hint ladder that goes rung by rung from “what kind of object is the answer” to the first line (./mr hints NN). You cannot ask a question, so no step is “clearly”.

Checkpoint

One falsifiable claim about what you can now do, phrased so that failing it is unambiguous; usually it ends in a number the world can refute. It is the last slide and the head of the problem sheet, and ./mr done NN records it. If you cannot make it true, the unit is not finished — and you know precisely what is missing.

How long. Each unit's title slide carries its own estimate: 2–3.5 h of slides and 4–7 h of problems, so 6–10.5 h a unit. Summed over all 41 units that is 109 h of slides and 224 h of problems — 333 hours, plus 20–25 h for the capstone. By part: A 79.5 h, B 54 h, C 67.5 h, D 37.5 h, E 37.5 h, F 57 h. Take it as a scale, not a schedule; there are no deadlines in it. The CLI is standard-library Python, so there is no virtualenv to recreate and nothing to install — ./mr slides 00 works on a clone.

Who this assumes you are

Everything below is used hard and never re-taught. Everything not on this list is taught here, including all of the physics. To find out in an afternoon whether it holds for you, work the readiness sheet (./mr ready): a dozen problems on exactly this background, with hints and worked solutions, and a note on where to fill each gap. The part that decides is not proof but computational calculus — the worked solutions are long chains of integrals and series carried to a checked number.

Assumed

  • Mathematical maturity. You read and write proofs, you are comfortable with abstraction, quantifiers, ε–δ arguments and “suppose not”, and you habitually ask which hypothesis a theorem actually needs. Assumed everywhere; it is the reason the course can move this fast.
  • Linear algebra, properly. Vector spaces, bases, linear maps, dual spaces, determinants, eigenvalues, inner products, the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices, Jordan normal form (stated, used in 06), quadratic forms and their signature, Sylvester's law of inertia. Units 07, 16, 26 and 31 cash this in hard.
  • Single-variable calculus and real analysis. Limits, continuity, uniform convergence, differentiation, the Riemann integral, integration by parts and substitution, Taylor with remainder, power series and radius of convergence.
  • Point-set topology of metric spaces. Open sets, compactness, completeness and the Banach fixed-point theorem — used in the first proof of the course (01) and again in unit 04's inverse function theorem.
  • Elementary group theory. Groups, homomorphisms, group actions. Used lightly for SO(3) in 16, one-parameter symmetry groups in 13 and the Lorentz group in 26. No representation theory assumed or taught.
  • A computer algebra system or numerical environment you are comfortable in. Nothing is built in this course, but units 09, 17, 19, 21, 37 and 39 have problems whose last step is arithmetic you should not do by hand.

Not assumed — all taught here

  • Any physics whatsoever. “Start directly with mechanics” means no pre-physics warm-up, not that anything may be assumed.
  • Dimensional analysis — unit 00, proved as a rank argument rather than absorbed by osmosis.
  • Multivariable and vector calculus — units 03, 04, 05.
  • ODE solution methods and qualitative ODE theory — units 00, 01, 02, 06, 09.
  • Fourier analysis — units 19 and 20.
  • Complex analysis — unit 20, as the tool that makes the Fourier transform usable.
  • Partial differential equations — unit 18, arriving as the continuum limit of a chain of oscillators.
  • Asymptotics and perturbation theory — unit 21, on the spine.
  • The calculus of variations — unit 11, conjugate points included.
  • Tensors — unit 16, as multilinear maps.
  • Differential forms — unit 34, as the unification and not as the entry point.
  • Differential geometry — units 31, 32, 33.

Part A — the equation of motion, and the calculus it forces

Newton's second law is a differential equation, and eleven units are one long answer to “which one, and how do you solve it?”

The mathematics arrives in five waves, each pulled in by a problem that stalls without it: dimensional analysis and quadrature for one-dimensional motion, qualitative theory for the equations with no formula, multivariable calculus for motion in space, line and flux integrals for work and gravity, and linear ODE theory plus series solutions for oscillators and orbits. It ends with the Kepler problem solved completely — shape and timetable — and the inclination of a real satellite predicted to within a degree. Eleven units, 79.5 h.

00

Newton's second law is an ODE — and dimensions tell you the answer's shape before you solve it

spine

needs nothing

Theory
The three laws and what each actually asserts — the first is the definition of an inertial frame, not a special case of the second, and the force law is constitutive input rather than theorem. One-dimensional motion under constant gravity, linear drag and quadratic drag; terminal velocity. Energy as a first integral, with turning points, bounded and unbounded motion and equilibria read straight off V.
Tools
Second-order ODEs reducible to quadrature in exactly three cases: F(t), integrate twice; F(v), separable then inverted; F(x), multiply by ẋ and integrate, which is where energy comes from. Separation of variables, implicit solutions, inverting t(x) and recognising when you cannot; improper integrals at a turning point and when the singularity is integrable. The Buckingham π theorem, stated and proved as a rank argument on the dimension matrix; similarity and scaling solutions. Elliptic integrals of the first kind named, not developed.
Problems
The slides do the plane pendulum twice on one slide: dimensionally, where the only groups available give T = √(l/g)·f(θ₀) with no mass dependence in two lines; then exactly, reducing to T = 4√(l/g)·K(sin(θ₀/2)) and finding the amplitude at which the small-angle formula is 1 per cent wrong (about 23°). The calculus determined f and nothing else. The sheet drills all three quadrature cases with the trap in each, then carries dimensional arguments to a number: Taylor's blast wave, R(t) = C(Et²/ρ)1/5, read off the declassified Trinity photographs and recovering a yield near 21 kilotons — a classified number obtained from dimensions and a photograph — and the period of V = k|x|n as a Beta function, with the unique n for which period is amplitude-independent.
Checkpoint. Given any force of the form F(t), F(v) or F(x) you first write the answer's dimensional form and name the dimensionless group the calculus is left to determine, then reduce to quadrature and produce the explicit solution or the period integral — and you can classify which of the three forms a problem is in before starting it.
01

Determinism, and the one hypothesis it rests on

spine

needs 00

Theory
What “Newton's laws determine the future” means as a mathematical claim, and the two ways it fails. Norton's dome: a mass on a dome shaped so that it can spontaneously start moving after an arbitrary delay, with no force applied and no hypothesis of the second law violated. Finite-time blow-up: an inverse-cube attractor reaching the origin in finite time, and what that does to global determinism.
Tools
The phase-space reformulation: any mẍ = F(x, ẋ, t) is a first-order system on ℝ2n. Picard–Lindelöf proved via the Banach fixed-point theorem on C⁰ with a weighted sup norm, with the Lipschitz condition doing all the work. Gronwall's inequality and continuous dependence on initial data. Peano: existence without uniqueness. Maximal intervals of existence and blow-up.
Problems
The slides build Norton's dome in full: h(r) = −(2/3g)r3/2 gives r̈ = √r; exhibit the one-parameter family r(t) = (t−T)⁴/144 together with r ≡ 0, verify every member solves the equation with identical initial data, and locate precisely which hypothesis fails — Lipschitz continuity at r = 0. The sheet verifies the hypotheses where you care and breaks them where you do not: prove the pendulum globally deterministic from local Lipschitzness on bounded sets; show ẋ = x² escapes in finite time and mẍ = −k/x³ reaches the origin in finite time, then decide whether either contradicts the theorem.
Checkpoint. You state and prove Picard–Lindelöf, and exhibit Norton's dome's one-parameter solution family together with the exact hypothesis that fails — without appealing to physical intuition at any step.
02

Phase portraits: what you know when there is no formula

spine

needs 01

Theory
The honest position, stated early because everything later depends on accepting it: almost no equation of motion has a closed-form solution, and that is not a gap in the literature. What you get instead is the qualitative picture — how many equilibria, of what type, with which basins, and whether anything periodic exists. The pendulum's separatrix on the cylinder. Damped systems as gradient-like flows and conservative systems as flows preserving a function, which is the first hint of the symplectic structure of unit 14.
Tools
Autonomous systems as vector fields; flows and the one-parameter group property. Equilibria, linearisation and the classification of planar linear systems by trace and determinant. Hartman–Grobman stated, with the sharp fact that it says nothing about centres. Lyapunov's direct method with LaSalle's invariance principle for the degenerate case. Limit cycles; Poincaré–Bendixson, stated with its essential reliance on the plane; the index of a vector field around a closed curve; the saddle-node, transcritical, pitchfork and Hopf bifurcations with normal forms.
Problems
The running example is the damped pendulum θ̈ + γθ̇ + sin θ = 0: the full cylinder portrait, the alternating saddles and spirals, then V = ½θ̇² + (1 − cos θ) and LaSalle proving asymptotic stability of the down position — a proof linearisation alone cannot give — and finally γ = 0, where the spiral becomes a centre, which is exactly the case Hartman–Grobman excludes. The sheet is about the traps: for ẋ = −y − x³, ẏ = x − y³ show linearisation predicts a centre while the origin is asymptotically stable and give the Lyapunov function that proves it; prove van der Pol has a limit cycle by constructing an explicit trapping region, without computing the cycle.
Checkpoint. Given a planar nonlinear system you locate and classify every equilibrium, decide whether linearisation is conclusive at each, construct a Lyapunov function where it is not, and either prove or rule out a periodic orbit using Poincaré–Bendixson together with the index theorem.
03

Where things are: curves, frames, and the frame that lies to you

spine

needs 00

Theory
Motion in three dimensions: projectile motion with drag, uniform circular motion and why the acceleration points inward. Rotating reference frames, with the centrifugal, Coriolis and Euler terms exposed as artefacts of differentiating in a frame that is itself turning. The Foucault pendulum, the eastward deflection of falling bodies, and why cyclones rotate.
Tools
Partial derivatives; the total derivative of a map ℝn → ℝm as the linear map best approximating it, and the multivariable chain rule stated once as composition of linear maps rather than as a page of subscripts. Clairaut's theorem with its counterexample. Arc length and the Frenet–Serret frame, with a = v̇T + (v²/ρ)N. Time-dependent orthogonal matrices; antisymmetry of Ω = RTṘ in 𝔰𝔬(3); the identification of 𝔰𝔬(3) with ℝ³ by the cross product; the transport theorem.
Problems
The slides derive the full rotating-frame equation by differentiating xin = R(t)xrot twice, then apply it to a pendulum at latitude λ to get a precession rate ω sin λ — 11.33° per hour at the Panthéon's 48.85° N, so a full turn in 31.8 h, against the observed roughly 32 h. The sheet applies the transport theorem until it stops being surprising: the eastward Coriolis deflection of a mass dropped 100 m at the equator (about 2.2 cm, with the sign right), and circular motion from scratch, showing ‖v‖ constant ⟺ a·v = 0 and deriving the Frenet decomposition from it.
Checkpoint. Starting only from xinertial = R(t)xrotating with R ∈ SO(3) you derive the Coriolis and centrifugal terms, identify ω from RTṘ, and predict the Foucault period at a stated latitude to within an hour.
04

Work, and the arrival of vector calculus

spine

needs 03

Theory
Work as a line integral and the work–energy theorem. Conservative forces and potential energy; energy conservation as a first integral in three dimensions. Why friction and magnetism both fall outside this frame, for opposite reasons. Potential landscapes, with equilibrium and stability read off the Hessian.
Tools
Line integrals of vector fields; the gradient and the fundamental theorem of line integrals — with the distinction planted quietly and once: the derivative is a covector and the gradient is what you get after using an inner product to convert it, which is index-raising in unit 26 and nothing else. Green's theorem and Stokes' theorem in ℝ³, proved for a rectangle with the patching argument sketched. Curl as the local obstruction to being a gradient; the Poincaré lemma on a simply connected domain. Taylor in ℝn, the Hessian and nondegenerate critical points; the inverse and implicit function theorems by contraction mapping; Lagrange multipliers derived from the implicit function theorem rather than asserted.
Problems
The slides show the inverse-square field conservative by constructing V = −GMm/r along a radial path, confirm path-independence on a deliberately awkward path, and then take the punctured-plane vortex F = (−y, x)/(x²+y²): curl zero everywhere it is defined, loop integral 2π, and a precise statement of what is being measured — the topology of the domain, not the field. The question is left open until unit 34. The sheet: five fields, three conservative and two not, each answered with V explicitly or a loop with nonzero circulation; and the monkey saddle V = x³ − 3xy², whose Hessian vanishes identically at its only critical point, so the classification has to come from the flow rather than from the second derivatives.
Checkpoint. Given F on an open set U you decide whether it is conservative and defend the answer by producing V explicitly or exhibiting a loop with nonzero circulation — including at least one case where the answer depends on the shape of U rather than on F.
05

Flux, Gauss, and gravity stops being a force between pairs

spine

needs 04

Theory
Universal gravitation and the shell theorem, which cost Newton years. The gravitational field as an object in its own right, with the field equation div g = −4πGρ, equivalently ∇²Φ = 4πGρ. Tidal forces as the variation of the field across a body, and the tidal tensor ∂i∂jΦ, trace-free in vacuum. Why the Moon raises two bulges and not one.
Tools
Multiple integrals and Fubini; change of variables and the Jacobian; spherical and cylindrical coordinates with their coordinate singularities identified. Surface integrals and flux; the divergence theorem, proved for a box with patching sketched. The Laplacian and harmonic functions; the mean-value property and the maximum principle. Solid angle, and why 1/r² is the unique power in three dimensions for which flux is radius-independent.
Problems
The slides prove the shell theorem twice — by brute integration over the sphere with the law of cosines, where the punchline hides in an algebraic cancellation, then by Gauss's flux law and symmetry in three lines — and compute Φ inside a uniform sphere, showing a body dropped down a tunnel through the Earth executes simple harmonic motion with period 84.3 min, wherever the tunnel's ends are. The sheet computes the tidal tensor of a point mass, shows the trace vanishes and the eigenvalues are (−2, 1, 1)GM/r³, and reads the two bulges off the eigenvectors — this object is the Newtonian limit of the Riemann tensor and unit 33 will say so — plus a non-symmetric distribution for which Gauss's law is true and useless, with a precise statement of what symmetry buys.
Checkpoint. You prove the shell theorem by Gauss's law in under half a page, and compute the tidal tensor of a point mass, showing it is trace-free and reading its two bulges off its eigenvectors.
06

The damped driven oscillator, which is most of linear physics

spine

needs 01 02

Theory
Small oscillations about a stable equilibrium as the universal local model: Taylor-expand any V at a minimum and the leading term is ½kx². Damping regimes and the quality factor Q; sinusoidal driving, resonance, the phase lag sweeping through it, the half-power width. The impulse response, and the claim that a linear system's response to everything is its response to one thing. Causality as a constraint on the response function — stated here, cashed at unit 20.
Tools
ℂ and the complex exponential as computational infrastructure — algebra, not yet analysis. Linear ODEs with constant coefficients: characteristic polynomial, the solution space as a two-dimensional vector space (Picard–Lindelöf from unit 01, not folklore), the Wronskian and Abel's identity, superposition, variation of parameters, resonance as the failure of the particular-solution ansatz. The Green's function of L = d²/dt² + γ d/dt + ω₀², built by matching a jump in the derivative across t = t′. Convolution and Duhamel's principle.
Problems
The slides solve ẍ + γẋ + ω₀²x = F₀ cos ωt completely — amplitude, phase lag sweeping 0 to π, peak position, full width at half power γ — then build G(t) from its defining property and recover the identical steady state by convolution: the same answer twice by unrelated machinery. The sheet: a square-pulse drive solved by convolution and separately by matching piecewise solutions at the pulse edges, agreeing at the corners; the amplitude response peaking at √(ω₀² − γ²/2) while the velocity response peaks exactly at ω₀, and which one a physicist means; and critical damping as a genuine Jordan-block degeneracy, the fastest return without overshoot.
Checkpoint. You construct G(t − t′) from its defining property, solve an arbitrary drive by convolution, verify against a directly computed piecewise solution, and can say why the solution space is two-dimensional by citing unit 01.
07

Many oscillators, and the spectral theorem earning its keep

spine

needs 06 04

Theory
Two masses and three springs; beats as interference between two normal modes. Normal modes and normal coordinates: the change of basis in which a coupled system becomes n uncoupled oscillators. N masses in a line with fixed ends; zero modes and the symmetries responsible for them; why a system with n coordinates has exactly n modes.
Tools
Linear systems ẋ = Ax; the matrix exponential; the general solution from eigendecomposition, with Jordan form for the degenerate case. The generalised eigenvalue problem Kv = ω²Mv with M symmetric positive definite, reduced by M1/2 to a symmetric problem — hence real ω² and M-orthogonal modes, the spectral theorem doing physical work, and the reason the naive “diagonalise both” is false in general. Simultaneous diagonalisation of two quadratic forms when one is definite, with the counterexample when neither is. The Rayleigh quotient and the variational characterisation of the lowest frequency.
Problems
The slides take N equal masses and N+1 equal springs, try vj = sin(jkπ/(N+1)) and get exactly ωk = 2√(k/m) sin(kπ/2(N+1)); plotted at N = 20 the curve bends away from the straight line, and that bending is dispersion — the straight part is the sound wave unit 18 recovers in the continuum limit, and the correction −(ck)(ka)²/24 is a rare chance to see a continuum theory's own error bar. The sheet: beats in the two-mass system; verifying viTMvj = 0 on a chain with one heavy mass while Euclidean orthogonality fails; and bounding the lowest mode of a five-mass chain by a trial vector.
Checkpoint. For any (M, K) with M symmetric positive definite you produce the normal frequencies and mode shapes, verify orthogonality in the M-inner product rather than the Euclidean one, identify every zero mode with the symmetry responsible, and can explain why complex frequencies cannot occur.
08

The central-force problem, and the two conservation laws that solve it

spine

needs 04 06

Theory
Torque and angular momentum; conservation for central forces and the immediate consequence that motion is planar. The two-body problem reduced to one body of reduced mass μ. The effective potential Veff = V(r) + L²/2μr², from whose graph the entire qualitative theory of orbits can be read — bound, unbound, circular, and the centrifugal barrier. Kepler's three laws derived rather than quoted. The Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector, and the fact that it is extra: most central forces have nothing like it.
Tools
The cross product and its identities. Conserved quantities as first integrals lowering the order of an ODE system, six to two here. Quadrature for r(t) and θ(r). The Binet substitution u = 1/r, turning the orbit into u″ + u = −(μ/L²u²)F(1/u) — linear for the inverse-square law, and the single most useful change of variable in the course, since unit 37 adds exactly one term to it and gets general relativity. Bertrand's theorem cited; its computational half is a problem.
Problems
The slides run Kepler end to end: from F = −k/r² to r = p/(1 + e cos θ) with p and e in terms of E and L, the classification by e, and T² = 4π²a³/GM from the areal velocity; then A = p × L − μkr̂, verified conserved by direct differentiation, with the orbit equation read off A·r in one line. The sheet: which power laws admit stable circular orbits (n > −3) and which close (only n = −2 and n = 1, from ωθ√(3+n)); escape speed and the hyperbolic deflection angle, arriving at Rutherford's formula.
Checkpoint. From V(r) = −k/r alone you derive r(θ) = p/(1 + e cos θ), express e and p in terms of E and L, prove Kepler's second and third laws, and verify the Runge–Lenz vector is conserved by explicit differentiation.
09

Where is the planet at time t? Kepler's equation and the birth of Bessel functions

spine

needs 08

Theory
The orbit's shape was easy; its timetable is not. The eccentric anomaly and Kepler's equation E − e sin E = M: transcendental, with no closed-form inverse — the problem that occupied celestial mechanics for two centuries and that every ephemeris still solves.
Tools
Solution by series in a parameter; the Fourier–Bessel expansion E = M + 2∑Jn(ne) sin(nM)/n, which is where Bessel functions were actually born — its sine coefficients computed directly from the orthogonality of the sines, which is all this unit borrows, since unit 19 is where that becomes a completeness theorem. Bessel's equation; the Frobenius method at a regular singular point, the indicial equation, the two roots and the logarithmic second solution when they differ by an integer. Recurrence relations, the generating function, Bessel's integral representation, and the asymptotic √(2/πx) cos(x − nπ/2 − π/4). Lagrange inversion as the general mechanism. The Laplace limit e = 0.6627434…, asserted here and proved in unit 20 — a debt deliberately incurred.
Problems
The slides derive the Bessel series for E(M) by computing the Fourier coefficients of E − M and recognising Bessel's integral representation, then locate Mars (e = 0.0934) at a stated epoch to five significant figures with three terms, cross-checked against Newton's method on the transcendental equation with the iterations each route costs counted. The sheet is Frobenius practice and honest convergence: Bessel's equation of order zero by series with its logarithmic second solution, the method detecting the obstruction on its own; and Halley's comet at e = 0.967, past the Laplace limit, where the series fails numerically and you locate empirically where it begins to diverge.
Checkpoint. You derive the Bessel-function solution of Kepler's equation from its Fourier coefficients and use it to place a planet to five digits — and you can state exactly where it diverges while owing unit 20 the reason.
10

Real planets are not points: multipoles, Legendre, and secular perturbation

spine

needs 05 08

Theory
The potential of a non-spherical body. The Earth's oblateness and its J₂ = 1.0826 × 10−3, the largest non-spherical term in the solar system by effect, and its consequence: a satellite's orbital plane precesses, which is exploited rather than fought. Tides revisited as the ℓ = 2 term, and why the Moon is tidally locked.
Tools
The generating function (1 − 2xt + t²)−1/2 defining the Legendre polynomials; Legendre's equation and its polynomial solutions; Rodrigues' formula; orthogonality on [−1, 1]. The multipole expansion of 1/|r − r′| with monopole, dipole and quadrupole terms and their coordinate-independent meanings. First-order perturbation theory for ODEs: osculating orbital elements, the method of averaging, and the distinction between secular and periodic terms — the first appearance of the idea unit 21 develops properly and unit 37 spends.
Problems
The slides expand Φ for an axially symmetric body, identify J₂ with the quadrupole moment, derive the nodal precession rate, and then solve Ω̇ = 360°/year for a circular orbit at 800 km altitude to get i = 98.6° — the actual inclination of every sun-synchronous satellite ever flown. The sheet: J₂ for an oblate spheroid of given flattening, checked against the Earth's f = 1/298.257; and the apsidal precession the same term produces, with the critical inclination 63.4° at which it vanishes — which is why Molniya orbits use exactly that.
Checkpoint. From J₂ alone you predict the sun-synchronous inclination at a stated altitude to within a degree of the published value, and you can say which Legendre term produces each of the two precessions.

Part B — the action, and coordinates ceasing to matter

Nothing here says anything Newton did not, and that is exactly the point

It is a change of language whose entire value is what it makes obvious. Extremising an integral gives the equations of motion; those equations are then invariant under any change of coordinates, which is the idea that ends the course as general covariance. Symmetry becomes conservation by one theorem. And the part quietly builds the two objects relativity needs — an action principle, and a real definition of a tensor, arriving with the inertia tensor because that is the first place nine numbers per basis will not do. Unit 21's Poincaré–Lindstedt method in its intended use: the secular term is the whole answer, giving 6πGM/c²a(1−e²) per orbit. The null case with an impact parameter giving 4GM/c²b, and the explicit comparison with the Newtonian-corpuscle calculation, which gives exactly half — so the factor of two is the discriminating prediction, and it comes from the spatial part of the metric.
Problems
The slides run Mercury end to end with a number: metric to orbit equation to perturbative solution to the answer, using a = 5.79 × 10¹⁰ m, e = 0.2056 and 415.2 orbits per century, arriving at 42.98″ per century against the observed 42.98 ± 0.04. Then light deflection both ways — 0.8756″ as a Newtonian corpuscle, 1.7512″ in general relativity — identifying precisely which metric component supplies the missing half. The sheet: find the innermost stable circular orbit at six gravitational radii from the effective potential and compute the binding energy there, 5.7 per cent of rest mass, which is the efficiency of a thin accretion disc, and compare with the observed radiative efficiency of accreting black holes; and compute the round-trip radar delay for a signal grazing the Sun, evaluate it for the 2002 superior conjunction geometry, and compare with the published Cassini measurement including its stated uncertainty.
Checkpoint. From scratch, with no formula looked up, you compute Mercury's perihelion advance to within 1″ per century of 43 and the solar light deflection as 1.75″, show the latter is exactly twice the Newtonian value and name the metric component responsible — and you can say why unit 21 forbade you from removing the secular term.

Gravitational waves

branch

needs 35 21 24 20

Theory
Linearised general relativity. Gauge freedom as infinitesimal diffeomorphism — structurally the same redundancy as unit 24's electromagnetic gauge, and worth meeting twice for exactly that reason. Transverse-traceless gauge; two polarisations; the effect on a ring of free test masses. The quadrupole formula, and the structural reason there is no dipole radiation where electromagnetism has one: mass conservation kills the monopole and momentum conservation kills the dipole, so gravity's lowest radiating multipole is two orders up, which is why the waves are so weak. The Hulse–Taylor binary's orbital decay matching prediction to 0.2 per cent over forty years. LIGO's GW150914: strain 10−21, three solar masses radiated in 0.2 s. The detector is a Michelson interferometer, so the same experiment that found no ether found gravitational waves.
Tools
Perturbation to first order; the trace-reversed perturbation and its sourced wave equation; the retarded solution using exactly the Green's function unit 24 computed by unit 20's contour methods. The transverse-traceless projector and an honest degree-of-freedom count: ten components, minus four gauge, minus four residual, equals two. Multipole expansion of the source in the slow-motion limit; unit 21's two-timing for the adiabatic inspiral. Why the energy carried by a wave is not a local tensor, the Isaacson average, and what that says about the phrase “energy of the gravitational field”.
Problems
The slides compute the inspiral chirp: the quadrupole moment of a circular binary, the radiated power, the balance against orbital energy giving dr/dt, the coalescence time, and ḟ ∝ f11/3 with the chirp mass as the only combination that appears — evaluated for PSR B1913+16 to give an orbital period derivative of −2.40 × 10−12 against the measured −2.423 × 10−12. The sheet: start from ten components and reduce to two by fixing the gauge twice, explicit about the residual freedom at each stage; compute the power radiated by a steel bar spun as fast as its tensile strength allows and compare with LIGO's sensitivity, which is why nobody built a laboratory source; and estimate the strain from GW150914's parameters against 10−21, computing how far a 4 km arm actually moves — about 10−18 m.
Checkpoint. You derive the linearised wave equation, reduce ten components to two by explicit gauge fixing, exhibit both polarisations' effect on a ring of test masses, and reproduce the Hulse–Taylor orbital decay rate to within 5 per cent of the measured value.

Cosmology

Theory
The cosmological principle as an assumption, with the evidence for it (CMB isotropy to 10−5, galaxy surveys) stated separately from the assumption itself, and the honest note that it concerns large scales only. FLRW geometry and the three spatial curvatures. The Friedmann equations read as an energy equation with an effective potential — a direct callback to unit 08, and the cheapest honest way to understand what a cosmological constant does. Matter, radiation and Λ eras and their scalings. Cosmological redshift as the stretching of wavelengths, and why it is not a Doppler shift. The horizon and flatness problems, with inflation presented with its motivation and its unresolved status.
Tools
Maximally symmetric spaces and the uniqueness of the FLRW form. Computing the Einstein tensor for FLRW — substantial, entirely mechanical, and a good test of whether unit 33's machinery is fluent. Comoving and proper coordinates; conformal time. Checkpoint. You derive the Friedmann equations from FLRW plus Einstein without consulting a reference, solve for the scale factor in the matter-, radiation- and Λ-dominated eras, and compute the age of the universe for the Planck parameters to within 0.3 Gyr of 13.8.

Capstone — one orbit, six descriptions, and an error budget that must close

The sister course attacked one problem with four traditions and refereed them. This does the same to the two-body problem — the only system that appears in every part of this curriculum, and one with a century of published measurements to be wrong against. Estimated 20–25 h.

The brief

Describe a gravitationally bound two-body system six ways, in increasing order of what the description costs. (1) Newtonian (00, 08): closed ellipses, Kepler's laws, exact — and what that predicts for Mercury's perihelion and for Hulse–Taylor's period. (2) Analytical (11–14, and 15 if you took it): the same orbits from the action, every conserved quantity produced from a symmetry including Runge–Lenz, and the whole problem in action–angle variables so that “precession” becomes the statement that two frequencies fail to be equal. (3) Perturbative (10, 21): the roughly 531″ per century caused by the other planets, computed by osculating elements and averaging, not looked up — the step usually skipped, and the one that makes the classical test honest. (4) Special-relativistic (27): relativistic kinematics with a 1/r potential, which precesses and gives roughly one sixth of the residual; state precisely why the answer is wrong. (5) Schwarzschild geodesics (36, 37): the full answer, and the factor of two in the deflection traced to a named metric component. (6) Radiative (38, if taken): quadrupole luminosity, orbital decay, inspiral time.

Then confront the data. Assemble the arcsecond budget for Mercury and defend every line — 5025.6 from the equinoxes, about 531.5 from Venus, Jupiter and the rest, about 0.03 from solar oblateness, 42.98 from general relativity, against 5600 observed. Do the same for light. Do the same for Hulse–Taylor's period derivative against (−2.423 ± 0.001) × 10−12. Integrate the Schwarzschild geodesics numerically as a cross-check on the perturbative answers, and report where the perturbation series stops being trustworthy.

The deliverable is a document in which each contribution is derived from stated principles, each approximation is named with its order, each number carries an uncertainty on both sides of the comparison, and the special-relativistic factor of six is confronted rather than skipped. Every number is computed by you; reference values appear only for comparison. If the error budget does not close to within the observational uncertainty the capstone is not finished — and you will know precisely which term you got wrong. That failing-loudly property is this course's substitute for the sister course's solver benchmark: it is the assessment, not a decoration on it.

If forty-one is too many

There is a short path, and unlike the two drafts this curriculum was synthesised from it is genuinely closed: every unit on the route has all of its needs on the route. Nothing is bought with an apology in a footnote.

The express route — 24 units, about 193 h

00 → 01 → 02 → 03 → 04 → 05 → 06 → 08 → 10 → 11 → 12 → 13 → 14 → 16 → 21 → 26 → 27 → 30 → 31 → 32 → 33 → 35 → 36 → 37

It reaches the Einstein field equations, Schwarzschild, and Mercury's 43 arcseconds in about 193 hours against the full course's 333.

What the route costs, named rather than hidden. Unit 26 derives the Lorentz group from homogeneity, isotropy and one invariant speed — which is honest mathematics — but the value of that speed is taken on credit, because unit 25 is what forces it out of Maxwell. You will believe relativity rather than having been cornered into it. Unit 35 gives you route two only: the field equations are shown to be the unique divergence-free second-order symmetric tensor by Bianchi and Lovelock, with the coupling fixed by the Newtonian limit — correct, complete, and silent on where the right-hand side comes from, which is what unit 29 answers. The stress tensor is restricted to dust, defined directly in unit 27's four-vector language; that is enough for 36 and 37 and not enough for 39.

What else you lose: the entire wave, Fourier, Sturm–Liouville, complex-analysis and electromagnetic apparatus (07, 09, 18, 19, 20, 22, 23, 24, 25, 28, 29) — and with it the historical reason anyone believed relativity, Maxwell as the canonical tensor field, Cauchy's proof that stress is bilinear, and any route into 34, 38 or 39.

A second abbreviation, for a learner who wants the mathematics more than the physics: 00, 01, 02, 04, 06, 11, 18, 19, 20, 21, 31, 32, 33 — thirteen units and about 109.5 h, ending at Riemannian geometry, which is a respectable graduate methods course in its own right with no physics prerequisite beyond unit 00. One honest caveat, since the claim matters: this one is a reading order rather than a closed subgraph. Taken literally it leaves nine needs off the list — 04 wants 03, 18 wants 07 and 12, 19 wants 09 and 10, 21 wants 08, 31 wants 16 and 30, and 33 wants 05 — so either add those or accept that the corresponding motivating physics arrives on trust.

Reading and tools

Chosen so that most units have one chapter to read rather than four books to triangulate between. Every entry says which units it is for; nothing here is required, and the slides are self-contained.

Mechanics, and where the problems come from

  • Landau & Lifshitz, Mechanics — 170 pages deriving all of classical mechanics from symmetry with zero padding, terse to the point of rudeness, and exactly right for a reader with maturity and no patience for drill. The primary text for Parts A and B.
  • Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics — the same material as geometry, and the book that makes units 14, 15 and 17 feel inevitable. Read it alongside Landau, not instead.
  • Spivak, Physics for Mathematicians: Mechanics I — written for precisely this learner and unusually honest about where the physics literature is sloppy; slow, and worth it for units 12–14.
  • Goldstein, Poole & Safko — consulted when Landau is too compressed, particularly for rigid bodies.
  • Morin, Introduction to Classical Mechanics — every problem has a full worked solution, which is this course's format exactly, so it is the model as well as the supply.
  • Lim, Problems and Solutions on Mechanics and its electromagnetism and relativity companions — thousands of solved problems, the right raw material for a course whose every problem carries a solution.

The analytic toolkit, and electromagnetism

  • Teschl, Ordinary Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems (free from the author) — units 01, 02, 06 and 17, with Picard–Lindelöf at the right level of proof.
  • Strogatz, Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos — unit 02's pictures, and the best-written book on this list.
  • Hubbard & Hubbard, Vector Calculus, Linear Algebra, and Differential Forms — units 04, 05 and 34 in one volume, rigorous without austerity; Spivak's Calculus on Manifolds is the 140-page alternative and the proof of Stokes' theorem to own.
  • Gelfand & Fomin, Calculus of Variations — unit 11: short, Dover-cheap, and including the conjugate-point theory most books omit.
  • Strauss, Partial Differential Equations — unit 18, the cleanest treatment of classification and well-posedness at this level; Evans chapters 2–4 when you want more.
  • Stein & Shakarchi, Fourier Analysis and Complex Analysis — units 19 and 20, two volumes of one series, which keeps notation consistent across the seam where the course needs both at once.
  • Bender & Orszag, Advanced Mathematical Methods for Scientists and Engineers — unit 21, the only book that takes divergent series seriously and the source of several of its problems.
  • Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics — units 23 and 24, the standard for good reason; Zangwill when Griffiths is too brief, Jackson consulted and never read.
  • Purcell & Morin, Electricity and Magnetism — the source of unit 28's wire-and-charge argument in its original form; Schwartz, Principles of Electrodynamics (Dover) derives magnetism from electrostatics plus a boost, which is unit 28's worked example written as a whole book.

Relativity, geometry, and Part F

  • Taylor & Wheeler, Spacetime Physics (free PDF from the authors) — unit 26's geometric framing and the source of “proper time is arc length”.
  • Rindler, Relativity: Special, General and Cosmological — the group-theoretic derivation done carefully, and the best account of Born rigidity for unit 27.
  • Lee, Introduction to Smooth Manifolds (2nd ed., chapters 1–17 and 19) — unit 31's reference, and the book that makes “a tensor is a multilinear map” stick; Introduction to Riemannian Manifolds for unit 32, with the Levi-Civita uniqueness proof done properly, do Carmo the shorter alternative.
  • O'Neill, Semi-Riemannian Geometry with Applications to Relativity — the one geometry book written in indefinite signature from the start, and therefore the one that never has to apologise later; the ideal bridge from unit 33 into Part F.
  • Frankel, The Geometry of Physics — spans units 04, 16, 31, 32 and 34 in one volume and connects them to the physics as it goes.
  • Carroll, Spacetime and Geometry (lecture-note version free) — the main text for Part F, best on physical motivation and on getting to Schwarzschild quickly, and matching this course's (−,+,+,+) conventions.
  • Wald, General Relativity — the rigorous account and the obvious second book for someone who writes proofs; chapters 1–6 track units 31–37 almost exactly, and 9–12 are where units 36 and 40 get their precision.
  • Misner, Thorne & Wheeler, Gravitation — consulted rather than read, but unit 33's geodesic-deviation-versus-tidal-tensor comparison is theirs and worth reading in the original.
  • Hartle, Gravity — physics-first, useful as a second pass over unit 37's tests. Zee, Einstein Gravity in a Nutshell — unit 35's Einstein–Hilbert variation, done more readably than anyone. Poisson, A Relativist's Toolkit — unit 35's boundary term and unit 40's horizons and surface gravity.
  • Maggiore, Gravitational Waves, Volume 1 — unit 38. Weinberg, Gravitation and Cosmology — unit 39, and worth an afternoon as the dissenting view that deliberately downplays geometry.
  • Lightman, Press, Price & Teukolsky, Problem Book in Relativity and Gravitation — 475 problems with complete worked solutions. For units 26–40 this is not a supplement; it is the problem sets.

What this proposal still needs from you

Eight open questions, two of which — the format of the solutions, and how much electromagnetism — should be settled before unit 00 is built rather than after. None of the rest blocks starting.

Open

  • How much electromagnetism, and this is the biggest sizing decision left. Three spine units before relativity (23, 24, 25) plus two after (28, 29), 37.5 h in all, on four grounds: it is the only place vector calculus reaches its final form, since Stokes' theorem does no real work until Ampère's law; it makes the invariance of c a conclusion rather than a decree; Fμν is the canonical tensor field that makes unit 33's index work routine; and its stress–energy tensor is a standard source. Electrostatics and magnetostatics are merged into one unit because unit 19 already gave you Legendre, Bessel and Green's functions. The alternative is one unit that states Maxwell and moves on, saving about 30 h and turning relativity into a postulate you accepted. Your call changes 23–25, 28, 29 and 38.
  • Should differential forms (34) be spine rather than branch? One draft made forms the first treatment of vector calculus, at unit 05. That was rejected: teaching flux as a 2-form to someone who has never computed a flux produces a learner who can prove d² = 0 and cannot guess which way a field points. But the reverse case is real — Maxwell as dF = 0, gauge freedom as exactness, the solenoid as a de Rham class, and Cartan making every curvature computation about four times shorter. If you take it, take it immediately after unit 33 rather than at the end, and accept a translation tax on every textbook page.
  • Derived or postulated field equations? Unit 35 offers both routes and unit 29 is spine so that the full course does the derivation. Route two is complete, forced, and silent about where Tμν comes from; route one costs unit 29 and answers exactly the question a mathematician asks first. Both are kept, and 35 needs only route two's prerequisites — which is what lets the express route be genuinely closed. If you never intend to take the express route, 29 could become a formal prerequisite of 35 and the unit would be cleaner.
  • Signature convention. (−,+,+,+) throughout, matching Carroll, Wald, MTW, O'Neill and Weinberg; the particle-physics literature uses (+,−,−,−), and unit 27's Mandelstam invariants are conventionally written there. Whichever you choose must be chosen once and printed on every slide: a sign error at unit 32 propagates silently to unit 37 and surfaces as a wrong number for Mercury, which is the worst possible place to discover it.
  • Does any of this get formalised? You write Lean and Rocq, and four things here are tractable: Picard–Lindelöf (01, mathlib already has uniqueness), the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations (11), the uniqueness of the Levi-Civita connection (32), and the Riemann tensor's symmetries and component count (33). Formalising any of them would give this course what the sister course's solver comparisons gave it — an external referee that does not care how convincing the argument felt. It is also a substantial detour. Worth deciding before unit 01 is built.
  • Where the worked solutions live. Every problem needs a full solution plus a hint ladder, which is a large amount of LaTeX-heavy prose. The sister course settled on one Org file per unit with #+startup: content, after discovering that HTML <details> folds only in a browser. The same shape would work, but heavy mathematics renders badly in Org's LaTeX fragments. The alternatives are a compiled PDF per unit (typesets best, folds worst) and a second reveal.js deck revealed one rung at a time (native hint ladder, worst to write). Settle this before unit 00: retrofitting forty units of mathematics into a different format is exactly the work nobody does.
  • The size, and what to cut first. If 333 h is too long, cut in this order: unit 17 (chaos), which nothing in the course depends on at all and which is a genuine detour from the Newton-to-Einstein line; then unit 40, which depends on branch 15, so that a chain of two branches is a lot of machinery for one payoff; then unit 09, accepting that unit 19's drum must take Bessel on trust and unit 20 loses its best debt to pay off.
  • Judged and deliberately not fixed. Unit 02 feeds almost nothing downstream and stays on the spine as a pedagogical bet you may disagree with. Unit 19 merges Fourier series and Sturm–Liouville into a 10.5 h unit, the largest size here and shared with units 20, 33, 36 and 37; splitting it is defensible and would make the count 42. Units 15 and 17 exist partly for pleasure, and this says so rather than dressing them up as prerequisites. A quantum coda would fit naturally after unit 21 and is excluded only because you did not ask.

Deliberately excluded

  • Quantum mechanics, in every form. The largest exclusion and the most tempting to breach: by unit 19 you own Sturm–Liouville theory and L², by unit 15 Hamilton–Jacobi and the eikonal, by unit 21 WKB — the Schrödinger equation is about two hours away at three separate points. Out because you named relativity as the destination and because quantum mechanics is a second course of comparable size. The seams are flagged in one sentence each and not pursued.
  • Thermodynamics and statistical mechanics, except the single adiabatic relation unit 22 needs for the speed of sound. Unit 14's Liouville theorem is where statistical mechanics would naturally begin, and the slide says so and stops. The cost is real: unit 40's black-hole thermodynamics has to be stated rather than understood.
  • Quantum field theory and everything downstream. Unit 29 deliberately builds classical field theory in the shape QFT later uses, and that preparation is the whole of the contribution. Unruh is gestured at by unit 30's Rindler horizon and Hawking radiation stated in unit 40, neither derived.
  • Laboratory work and error analysis as disciplines, per your request — with one qualification. Published measurements appear constantly (Pound–Rebka, Cassini, Hulse–Taylor, LIGO, Planck, MICROSCOPE, VLBI) with their uncertainties, because a prediction compared against a number with no error bar is not a test. You are asked to be right about nature, not to measure it.
  • Fluid dynamics past the linear-acoustic level: turbulence, vorticity dynamics, aerofoils, the whole of aerodynamics. Unit 22 takes the stress tensor and the sound wave and stops. What fluids teaches best about dimensional analysis — Reynolds number, Kolmogorov scaling, the boundary layer as a singular perturbation — is harvested into units 00 and 21 as problems.
  • Optics as a subject. Fermat (11), the eikonal (15, 21) and dispersion (20) are in because they are load-bearing. Lenses, interferometry, diffraction, coherence and imaging are out: a beautiful application of unit 19's Fourier analysis, and on no path to Einstein's equations.
  • Circuits, AC analysis, media, transmission lines, antennas, waveguides and plasma physics — the applied two-thirds of a standard electromagnetism course. Units 23–25 take Maxwell as far as radiation and then turn towards relativity, which is the only reason electromagnetism is here.
  • Spinors, the Dirac equation and SL(2,ℂ) → SO⁺(1,3). A genuine gap, and the exclusion this proposal is least comfortable with, since spinors are arguably the deepest thing special relativity contains. Out because nothing on the road to the Schwarzschild tests requires them and adding them honestly costs two units.
  • Gauge theory beyond the U(1) example in unit 29's second problem: fibre bundles, connections on principal bundles, the tetrad formalism. Unit 34 goes as far as Cartan's structure equations and stops.
  • Numerical relativity, the ADM 3+1 split, and the Cauchy problem for Einstein's equations. Named in units 35 and 38, taught in neither — and the subject that actually produces the waveform templates unit 38 compares against.
  • Alternative theories of gravity: Brans–Dicke, f(R), TeVeS. Unit 35's Lovelock argument is the right lens on the whole family, and it is one slide rather than a unit.
  • Measure-theoretic rigour and functional analysis proper. L² is used as an inner product space, completeness of eigenfunction systems is cited, Lebesgue integration is named where it matters and never used, distributions are introduced operationally in unit 20. You could prove these and would enjoy it; it would cost three units that buy no physics.
  • Numerical methods as a discipline — discretisation, stability, stiffness, symplectic integrators, convergence order. Several problems end in arithmetic; none end in a library.
  • History of science narrated for its own sake. Dates, names and corrections to the received story appear throughout, but only where the history is the argument: Newton's isothermal sound speed, Maxwell's displacement current, Michelson–Morley, Le Verrier's missing planet.
