# Unit 00 — Newton's second law is an ODE — and dimensions tell you the answer's shape before you solve it

- directory: `units/00-newton-as-ode/`
- part: Part A — the equation of motion, and the calculus it forces (data-trunk="a")
- part thesis: Newton's second law is a differential equation, and this part is one long answer to 'which equation, and how do you solve it?'. The mathematics arrives in five waves, each pulled in by a problem that stalls without it: dimensional analysis and quadrature for one-dimensional motion, qualitative theory for the equations with no formula, multivariable calculus for motion in space, line and flux integrals for work and gravity, and linear ODE theory plus series solutions for oscillators and orbits. It ends with the Kepler problem solved completely — shape and timetable — and the inclination of a real satellite predicted to within a degree.

## Physics
The three laws and what each actually asserts — the first is the definition of an inertial frame, not a special case of the second. Mass, momentum and force laws as constitutive input rather than theorem. One-dimensional motion under constant gravity, linear drag and quadratic drag; terminal velocity. Energy as a first integral; turning points, bounded and unbounded motion, equilibria read off V. Units, dimensions, and the habit this course will not let you drop: write the answer's dimensional form before you integrate anything.

## Mathematical tools
Second-order ODEs reducible to quadrature in exactly three cases: F(t), integrate twice; F(v), separable in v then invert; F(x), multiply by x-dot and integrate, which is where energy comes from. Separation of variables, implicit solutions, inverting t(x) and recognising when you cannot. Improper integrals at a turning point and when the singularity is integrable. The Buckingham pi theorem, stated and proved as a rank argument on the dimension matrix; similarity and scaling solutions. Elliptic integrals of the first kind named, not developed.

## The worked example the slides are built around
The plane pendulum, done twice on one slide. First dimensionally: the only groups available are T sqrt(g/l) and the amplitude, so T = sqrt(l/g) f(theta_0) with no mass dependence — two lines, no calculus, and everything except f is already settled. Then exactly: first-integrate theta'' = -(g/l) sin theta, reduce to T = 4 sqrt(l/g) K(sin(theta_0/2)), expand to T ≈ 2 pi sqrt(l/g)(1 + theta_0^2/16 + ...), and find the amplitude at which the small-angle formula is 1 per cent wrong (about 23 degrees). The calculus determined f and nothing else.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Reduction to quadrature in all three cases with the trap in each, and dimensional arguments carried to a number. "Taylor's blast wave": from E, rho and t alone derive R(t) = C (E t^2/rho)^{1/5}, read the fireball radius off the declassified Trinity photographs' scale bar, and recover a yield near 21 kilotons — a classified number obtained from dimensions and a photograph. "Period by quadrature": for V = k|x|^n derive T proportional to E^{1/n - 1/2}, evaluate the constant as a Beta function, and identify the unique n for which the period is amplitude-independent.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
Given any force of the form F(t), F(v) or F(x) you first write the answer's dimensional form and name the dimensionless group the calculus is left to determine, then reduce to quadrature and produce the explicit solution or the period integral — and you can classify which of the three forms a problem is in before starting it.
