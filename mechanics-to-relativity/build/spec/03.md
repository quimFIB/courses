# Unit 03 — Where things are: curves, frames, and the frame that lies to you

- directory: `units/03-frames/`
- part: Part A — the equation of motion, and the calculus it forces (data-trunk="a")
- part thesis: Newton's second law is a differential equation, and this part is one long answer to 'which equation, and how do you solve it?'. The mathematics arrives in five waves, each pulled in by a problem that stalls without it: dimensional analysis and quadrature for one-dimensional motion, qualitative theory for the equations with no formula, multivariable calculus for motion in space, line and flux integrals for work and gravity, and linear ODE theory plus series solutions for oscillators and orbits. It ends with the Kepler problem solved completely — shape and timetable — and the inclination of a real satellite predicted to within a degree.

## Physics
Motion in three dimensions. Projectile motion with drag. Uniform circular motion and why the acceleration points inward. Rotating reference frames: centrifugal, Coriolis and Euler terms as artefacts of differentiating in a frame that is itself turning. The Foucault pendulum, the eastward deflection of falling bodies, and why cyclones rotate.

## Mathematical tools
Partial derivatives; the total derivative of a map R^n -> R^m as the linear map best approximating it, and the multivariable chain rule stated once as composition of linear maps rather than as a page of subscripts. Clairaut's theorem with its counterexample. Vector-valued functions of one variable; arc length; the Frenet-Serret frame and the decomposition a = v' T + (v^2/rho) N. Time-dependent orthogonal matrices; antisymmetry of Omega = R^T R' in so(3); the identification of so(3) with R^3 by the cross product; the transport theorem (d/dt)_inertial = (d/dt)_rotating + omega cross.

## The worked example the slides are built around
Derive the full rotating-frame equation m a_rot = F - 2m omega x v_rot - m omega x (omega x r) - m omega' x r by differentiating x_in = R(t) x_rot twice, then apply it to a pendulum at latitude lambda to get the Foucault precession rate omega sin(lambda) — 32.7 h at the Pantheon's 48.9 N, against the observed roughly 32 h.

## What the problem sheet exercises
The transport theorem applied until it stops being surprising, plus Frenet computations. "Falling east": compute the eastward Coriolis deflection of a mass dropped 100 m at the equator (about 2.2 cm) and get the sign right. "Circular motion from scratch": show ||v|| constant is equivalent to a dot v = 0 and derive the Frenet decomposition of acceleration from it.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
Starting only from x_inertial = R(t) x_rotating with R in SO(3) you derive the Coriolis and centrifugal terms, identify omega from R^T R', and predict the Foucault period at a stated latitude to within an hour.
