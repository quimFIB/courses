# Unit 08 — The central-force problem, and the two conservation laws that solve it

- directory: `units/08-central-forces/`
- part: Part A — the equation of motion, and the calculus it forces (data-trunk="a")
- part thesis: Newton's second law is a differential equation, and this part is one long answer to 'which equation, and how do you solve it?'. The mathematics arrives in five waves, each pulled in by a problem that stalls without it: dimensional analysis and quadrature for one-dimensional motion, qualitative theory for the equations with no formula, multivariable calculus for motion in space, line and flux integrals for work and gravity, and linear ODE theory plus series solutions for oscillators and orbits. It ends with the Kepler problem solved completely — shape and timetable — and the inclination of a real satellite predicted to within a degree.

## Physics
Torque and angular momentum; conservation for central forces and the immediate consequence that motion is planar. The two-body problem reduced to one body of reduced mass mu. The effective potential V_eff = V(r) + L^2/2 mu r^2, from whose graph the entire qualitative theory of orbits can be read — bound, unbound, circular, and the centrifugal barrier. Kepler's three laws derived rather than quoted. The Laplace-Runge-Lenz vector, and the fact that it is *extra*: most central forces have nothing like it.

## Mathematical tools
The cross product and its identities. Conserved quantities as first integrals lowering the order of an ODE system, six to two here. Quadrature for r(t) and theta(r). The Binet substitution u = 1/r, turning the orbit into u'' + u = -(mu/L^2 u^2) F(1/u) — linear for the inverse-square law, and the single most useful change of variable in the course, since unit 37 adds exactly one term to it and gets general relativity. Bertrand's theorem cited; its computational half is a problem.

## The worked example the slides are built around
The Kepler problem end to end: from F = -k/r^2 obtain u'' + u = mu k/L^2, hence r = p/(1 + e cos theta) with p = L^2/mu k and e = sqrt(1 + 2 E L^2/mu k^2); classify by e and show e < 1 iff E < 0; derive T^2 = 4 pi^2 a^3/GM from the areal velocity. Then A = p x L - mu k r-hat: verify dA/dt = 0 by direct differentiation and read the orbit equation off A dot r in one line.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Effective potentials, stability of circular orbits, and closure. "Which power laws close?": show circular orbits of F proportional to r^n are stable for n > -3, that small radial oscillations have frequency omega_theta sqrt(3+n), and hence that orbits close only for n = -2 and n = 1. "Escape and deflection": compute escape speed, then the hyperbolic deflection angle for a given impact parameter, obtaining Rutherford's formula.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
From V(r) = -k/r alone you derive r(theta) = p/(1 + e cos theta), express e and p in terms of E and L, prove Kepler's second and third laws, and verify the Runge-Lenz vector is conserved by explicit differentiation.
