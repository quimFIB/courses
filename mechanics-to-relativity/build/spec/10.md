# Unit 10 — Real planets are not points: multipoles, Legendre, and secular perturbation

- directory: `units/10-multipoles-legendre/`
- part: Part A — the equation of motion, and the calculus it forces (data-trunk="a")
- part thesis: Newton's second law is a differential equation, and this part is one long answer to 'which equation, and how do you solve it?'. The mathematics arrives in five waves, each pulled in by a problem that stalls without it: dimensional analysis and quadrature for one-dimensional motion, qualitative theory for the equations with no formula, multivariable calculus for motion in space, line and flux integrals for work and gravity, and linear ODE theory plus series solutions for oscillators and orbits. It ends with the Kepler problem solved completely — shape and timetable — and the inclination of a real satellite predicted to within a degree.

## Physics
The potential of a non-spherical body. The Earth's oblateness and its J2 = 1.0826e-3, the largest non-spherical term in the solar system by effect. Its consequence: a satellite's orbital plane precesses, which is exploited rather than fought. Tides revisited as the l = 2 term; why the Moon is tidally locked.

## Mathematical tools
The generating function (1 - 2xt + t^2)^{-1/2} = sum P_n(x) t^n defining the Legendre polynomials; Legendre's equation and its polynomial solutions; Rodrigues' formula; orthogonality on [-1,1]. The multipole expansion of 1/|r - r'| with monopole, dipole and quadrupole terms and their coordinate-independent meanings. First-order perturbation theory for ODEs: osculating orbital elements, the method of averaging, and the distinction between secular and periodic terms — the first appearance of the idea unit 21 develops properly and unit 37 spends.

## The worked example the slides are built around
Expand Phi for an axially symmetric body, identify J2 with the quadrupole moment, and derive the nodal precession rate Omega-dot = -(3/2) J2 (R_E/a)^2 n cos(i)/(1-e^2)^2. Then solve Omega-dot = 360 degrees per year for a circular orbit at 800 km altitude and obtain i = 98.6 degrees — the actual inclination of every sun-synchronous satellite ever flown.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Multipole computations and secular perturbation. "Quadrupole of an ellipsoid": compute J2 for an oblate spheroid of given flattening and check the Earth's value against f = 1/298.257. "Apsidal precession": show the J2 term also precesses perigee, compute the rate, and find the critical inclination 63.4 degrees at which it vanishes — which is why Molniya orbits use exactly that.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
From J2 alone you predict the sun-synchronous inclination at a stated altitude to within a degree of the published value, and you can say which Legendre term produces each of the two precessions.
