# Unit 11 — The brachistochrone, and how to differentiate with respect to a curve

- part thesis: Nothing here says anything Newton did not, and that is the point: it is a change of language whose entire value is what it makes obvious. Extremising an integral gives the equations of motion; those equations are then invariant under any change of coordinates, which is the idea that ends the course as general covariance. Symmetry becomes conservation by one theorem. And the part quietly builds the two objects relativity needs — an action principle, and a real definition of a tensor, arriving with the inertia tensor because that is the first place nine numbers per basis will not do.

## Physics
Johann Bernoulli's 1696 challenge. Fermat's principle of least time, with Snell's law falling out of it. The hanging chain. The common structure: nature appears to extremise an integral, and the question is what equation that forces.

## Mathematical tools
Functionals on spaces of curves; admissible variations and the first variation as a Gateaux derivative. The fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations and the du Bois-Reymond form you actually need, both proved with bump functions. The Euler-Lagrange equation. The Beltrami identity when the integrand lacks explicit dependence on the independent variable. Natural boundary conditions at a free endpoint. Isoperimetric and holonomic constraints by Lagrange multipliers in function space. Several dependent variables; higher derivatives. The second variation, the Legendre condition, the accessory equation and Jacobi's conjugate points — because 'stationary action' is not 'least action' and unit 26 will need to know which. The direct method, and Weierstrass's demolition of Dirichlet's principle as the reason existence is a real question.

## The worked example the slides are built around
The brachistochrone in full: set up T[y] = integral sqrt((1+y'^2)/2gy) dx, apply Beltrami, integrate to the cycloid in parametric form, fix both constants from the endpoints, and confirm with explicit numbers that it beats the straight line and the circular arc. Then the catenary by the same route with the length constraint handled by a multiplier, noting the multiplier is physically the tension, and finish with a conjugate-point check that the cycloid really is a minimum.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Deriving Euler-Lagrange equations and probing their limits. "Snell from Fermat": derive refraction, then its continuous version for a medium with n(y), and solve the ray path in a linear gradient — a mirage, with a number. "Stationary but not minimal": exhibit a great-circle arc exceeding half the circle, show it fails the Jacobi condition, and identify the conjugate point as the antipode. "Geodesics on a surface of revolution": derive them and obtain Clairaut's relation r sin psi = const as a first integral, which unit 36 re-derives as a Killing vector.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
You derive the cycloid from scratch, and you produce a functional whose Euler-Lagrange solution is stationary but not a minimum, together with the test — conjugate point or second variation — that detects it.
