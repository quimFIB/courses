# Unit 14 — Hamilton: phase space, and flows that preserve volume

- part thesis: Nothing here says anything Newton did not, and that is the point: it is a change of language whose entire value is what it makes obvious. Extremising an integral gives the equations of motion; those equations are then invariant under any change of coordinates, which is the idea that ends the course as general covariance. Symmetry becomes conservation by one theorem. And the part quietly builds the two objects relativity needs — an action principle, and a real definition of a tensor, arriving with the inertia tensor because that is the first place nine numbers per basis will not do.

## Physics
The phase-space picture and what it makes visible that configuration space hides: the pendulum's separatrix from unit 02 now as a level set of H, the structure of the Kepler problem, and the fact that trajectories never cross. Liouville's theorem and its meaning — a cloud of initial conditions deforms without shrinking, which is why statistical mechanics is possible and why integrators for mechanics should be symplectic.

## Mathematical tools
The Legendre transform done properly as convex duality: definition by supremum, involutivity for convex functions, the degenerate case flagged in advance (unit 27's relativistic free particle is nearly one), and the relation to Young's inequality. Hamilton's equations as a first-order system on R^{2n}. Poisson brackets with bilinearity, antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity; {., H} generates time evolution, so conserved quantities are exactly those commuting with H — Noether's converse, now cheap. Canonical transformations and the four generating functions. Liouville's theorem as divergence-freeness of the Hamiltonian vector field. The symplectic form omega = dp wedge dq named here and unpacked in unit 34.

## The worked example the slides are built around
Legendre-transform the Lagrangians of units 12 and 08, then sketch the Kepler phase portrait in (r, p_r) at fixed L, identifying the separatrix at E = 0. Then compute {L_i, L_j} = epsilon_ijk L_k, and {L_i, A_j} and {A_i, A_j} for the Runge-Lenz vector, watching the algebra close on so(4) for bound orbits and so(3,1) for unbound ones — which is what 'hidden symmetry' meant in unit 13.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Canonicity tests and bracket algebra. "Is it canonical?": test a given transformation three ways — the symplectic condition M^T J M = J, preservation of all Poisson brackets, and an explicit generating function — and confirm the verdicts agree. "Liouville by hand": follow a rectangle of initial conditions through the free-particle and harmonic-oscillator flows, compute the area at time t, and watch the shear do all the work.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
You prove a given transformation canonical by two independent routes, perform a Legendre transform including its degenerate case, and prove Liouville's theorem from Hamilton's equations in three lines.
