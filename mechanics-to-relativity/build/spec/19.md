# Unit 19 — Fourier and Sturm-Liouville: the modes are a basis, and that is a theorem

- directory: `units/19-fourier-sturm-liouville/`
- part: Part C — the analytic toolkit, fields, and the crisis that ends Newtonian physics (data-trunk="c")
- part thesis: Take a chain of oscillators to the continuum and you get a partial differential equation, a field, and the need for four more pieces of mathematics: Fourier and Sturm-Liouville theory, the Fourier transform with the complex analysis that makes it usable, asymptotics for the problems with no exact answer, and tensor fields on space. Electromagnetism is here at full strength and on purpose: it is the only place vector calculus reaches its final form, it is the historical and logical road to relativity, and F_{mu nu} is the canonical tensor field that makes the Riemann tensor's index work familiar rather than alarming. The part ends by showing, with the chain rule and nothing else, that Maxwell's equations do not survive a Galilean boost — and by finding which transformation they do survive.

## Physics
Separation of variables on the finite string, and the claim that every initial shape is a superposition of its modes. Timbre: why a plucked and a struck string at the same pitch sound different, and why plucking at one seventh of the length silences the seventh harmonic — a prediction you can hear. Then the same pattern in three geometries: the string gives sines, the circular drum gives Bessel functions, the sphere gives Legendre polynomials and spherical harmonics. Heat conduction in a rod, which was Fourier's own problem. Why strings are tuneful and drums are not.

## Mathematical tools
Fourier sine, cosine and complex series; orthogonality; Bessel's inequality; Parseval; completeness in L^2 stated with the Fejer route sketched. Dirichlet's pointwise theorem for piecewise-C^1 functions and convergence to the midpoint at a jump; uniform convergence and its failure; Gibbs' phenomenon with its 8.9490 per cent overshoot computed; the dictionary between smoothness and coefficient decay, which makes truncation error predictable; term-by-term differentiation and exactly what it costs. Then the Sturm-Liouville operator (p u')' + q u = -lambda w u: formal self-adjointness by Lagrange's identity, the boundary conditions that make it genuinely self-adjoint, and each consequence proved — eigenvalues real and simple, eigenfunctions w-orthogonal, spectrum discrete and unbounded. Completeness via compactness of the resolvent, stated. Sturm's oscillation theorem. The Rayleigh quotient and the variational characterisation of eigenvalues, which is unit 11's calculus of variations in its second job. Bessel and Legendre recognised as two instances of one theorem; spherical harmonics from separating the Laplacian.

## The worked example the slides are built around
The circular drum: separate in polar coordinates, arrive at Bessel's equation, impose regularity at the centre and vanishing at the rim, and obtain frequency ratios 1 : 1.593 : 2.136 : 2.296 — irrational, therefore not a chord, which is why a drum has no pitch. Then bound the fundamental eigenvalue above with a one-parameter trial function via the Rayleigh quotient and compare with the exact first Bessel zero 2.4048.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Expansions, convergence rates, and proving the Sturm-Liouville properties. "Hear the coefficients": compute b_n proportional to (1/n^2) sin(n pi a/L) for the plucked string, show plucking at a = L/7 makes b_7 vanish, bound the L^2 truncation error by Parseval as N^{-3} against the struck string's N^{-1}, and say which sounds brighter and why. "Self-adjointness does the work": prove reality and orthogonality from Lagrange's identity, then find the boundary condition that breaks it and see which conclusion fails first. "Legendre from separation": put Legendre's equation in Sturm-Liouville form, identify p, q and w, and prove orthogonality without touching a generating function.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
Given a Sturm-Liouville problem you prove reality and orthogonality from self-adjointness, count the zeros of the third eigenfunction correctly, bound the lowest eigenvalue above to within a few per cent by a trial function, and expand a given initial profile with a correct statement of what kind of convergence you have.
