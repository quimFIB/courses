# Unit 20 — The Fourier transform, complex analysis, and why causality is a statement about poles

- directory: `units/20-fourier-transform-complex/`
- part: Part C — the analytic toolkit, fields, and the crisis that ends Newtonian physics (data-trunk="c")
- part thesis: Take a chain of oscillators to the continuum and you get a partial differential equation, a field, and the need for four more pieces of mathematics: Fourier and Sturm-Liouville theory, the Fourier transform with the complex analysis that makes it usable, asymptotics for the problems with no exact answer, and tensor fields on space. Electromagnetism is here at full strength and on purpose: it is the only place vector calculus reaches its final form, it is the historical and logical road to relativity, and F_{mu nu} is the canonical tensor field that makes the Riemann tensor's index work familiar rather than alarming. The part ends by showing, with the chain rule and nothing else, that Maxwell's equations do not survive a Galilean boost — and by finding which transformation they do survive.

## Physics
The response of a linear system to any input, not only periodic ones; the susceptibility of unit 06's oscillator. Causality — a system cannot respond before it is driven — and the discovery that this innocuous statement constrains the entire frequency response. Dispersion: why glass separates colours, and why refraction and absorption are not independent. Wave packets, phase and group velocity, packet spreading.

## Mathematical tools
The Fourier transform on R; inversion; Plancherel; the convolution theorem; differentiation becoming multiplication; the uncertainty relation proved by Cauchy-Schwarz. Test functions and distributions, introduced properly and retroactively legitimising unit 06's Green's function. Then complex analysis: holomorphy and Cauchy-Riemann, Goursat and Cauchy's theorem, the integral formula and its consequences (infinitely differentiable, analytic, Liouville, maximum modulus, Morera), Laurent series, isolated singularities, the residue theorem, Jordan's lemma, principal values, branch points and cuts, the argument principle and Rouche, analytic continuation. Contour integration as the tool for inverting transforms, and the Bromwich contour for the Laplace transform. Asymptotics of integrals belongs here and not in unit 21: Watson's lemma, Laplace's method, stationary phase, and steepest descent by contour deformation.

## The worked example the slides are built around
Invert the susceptibility 1/(omega_0^2 - omega^2 - i gamma omega) by residues: locate both poles, both in the lower half-plane, close upwards for t < 0 to get exactly zero and downwards for t > 0 to collect both residues — recovering precisely the Green's function built by hand in unit 06, now with its step function explained rather than imposed. Then the proved equivalence: G(t) = 0 for t < 0 iff the susceptibility is holomorphic in the upper half-plane iff the Kramers-Kronig relations hold between its real and imaginary parts.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Contour technique, with the debts of earlier units called in. "The Laplace limit, finally": locate the complex singularity of the inverse of Kepler's equation and prove unit 09's series has radius of convergence 0.6627434..., which unit 09 could only assert. "Kramers-Kronig": derive the dispersion relations from analyticity, verify them on the oscillator, and conclude that a medium which refracts must also absorb. "Fresnel by octant": evaluate the integrals of cos(x^2) and sin(x^2) with an honest arc estimate — the integral stationary phase generalises.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
You compute a retarded Green's function by contour integration and prove it vanishes for t < 0 by a contour-closing argument rather than by inspection, evaluate a real integral by steepest descent with every arc estimate justified, and settle unit 09's Laplace limit.
