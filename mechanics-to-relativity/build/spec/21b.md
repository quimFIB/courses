## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
The course was designed with quantum mechanics excluded, and its proposal said why: the destination is general relativity, and quantum mechanics is a second course of comparable size. But it also observed that by unit 21 the Schrödinger equation is a couple of hours away at three separate points — unit 15's Hamilton–Jacobi equation is the eikonal of some wave theory, unit 19's Sturm–Liouville theory is a spectral theory on L², and unit 21's WKB ansatz is the short-wavelength limit of a wave equation. This unit and 24b cash those three debts and stop. They are a coda, not a quantum course: no spin, no identical particles, no scattering theory beyond tunnelling, no Dirac equation, no field quantisation. Say so on the overview slide. Nothing in the main line depends on 21b or 24b: no unit numbered 22–40 may cite either as a prerequisite.

## Physics
Matter has a wavelength. Light comes in quanta, E = ħω and p = ħk (photoelectric effect, Compton scattering — named as evidence, not developed); de Broglie (1924) turned the relation round, and Davisson and Germer (1927) diffracted electrons off a nickel crystal at the wavelength h/p predicts. Unit 15 already showed that classical mechanics is the geometric-optics limit of a wave theory, with Hamilton's principal function S playing the eikonal; Schrödinger (1926) asked which wave equation has it as its limit. Answer it by running unit 21's WKB ansatz backwards: substitute psi = A exp(iS/ħ) into a candidate linear wave equation, demand that the leading order in ħ be the Hamilton–Jacobi equation and the next order a conservation law, and arrive at i ħ ∂psi/∂t = −(ħ²/2m)∇²psi + V psi. Then state the theory as mathematics, because a mathematician will ask what the objects are: a state is a unit vector in L²(ℝ³) up to a phase; |psi|² is a probability density (Born 1926), conserved by the continuity equation the derivation produced; observables are self-adjoint operators — position as multiplication, momentum as −iħ∇ — and measurement outcomes lie in their spectrum. The canonical commutator [x, p] = iħ as Dirac's rule replacing unit 14's Poisson bracket {x, p} = 1 by a commutator over iħ, with Groenewold's theorem (1946) stated: no quantisation map can do this consistently for all polynomials, so the rule is a heuristic and not a functor. Stationary states, energy quantisation as the discreteness of a Sturm–Liouville spectrum on a confining potential, zero-point energy, tunnelling. The repair of the stability-of-matter failure that unit 24 will record is 24b's job, and this unit says so at the end.

## Mathematical tools
L² as the Hilbert space unit 19 built, now with complex scalars. Self-adjoint versus symmetric operators: momentum on a half-line or a box as the example where the domain matters, stated with the deficiency-index criterion named and not developed. Stone's theorem stated: strongly continuous unitary groups correspond exactly to self-adjoint generators, so the Hamiltonian must be self-adjoint for probability to be conserved. Separation of variables, giving the time-independent equation as a Sturm–Liouville eigenproblem, and unit 19's theorems (real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenfunctions, completeness — cited there) doing the work. Free evolution solved by unit 20's Fourier transform: a Gaussian packet's width grows as sigma(t) = sigma_0 sqrt(1 + (ħt / 2m sigma_0²)²). The uncertainty relation Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2 twice: as Robertson's inequality from Cauchy–Schwarz on commutators, and as unit 20's Fourier uncertainty — the same theorem, which is the point. The harmonic oscillator by ladder operators a = (m omega x + i p)/sqrt(2 m ħ omega), [a, a†] = 1, N = a†a, and the spectrum ħ omega(n + 1/2) from the algebra alone, including the argument that the ladder must terminate below. Hermite functions as the eigenfunctions, matched to the series solution. WKB revisited from unit 21: Bohr–Sommerfeld quantisation ∮ p dq = 2πħ(n + 1/2) with the 1/2 coming from the connection formulae at the two turning points (the Maslov index, named); the tunnelling exponent exp(−(2/ħ)∫ sqrt(2m(V − E)) dx) through a barrier, with its validity condition.

## The worked example the slides are built around
The harmonic oscillator three times, with one answer. First as a Sturm–Liouville problem: the series solution, and the requirement that it lie in L² forcing the series to terminate, which is where the quantisation comes from. Second by ladder operators, getting ħ omega(n + 1/2) in a few lines of algebra and the ground state as the kernel of a. Third by Bohr–Sommerfeld, which happens to be exact here — and the 1/2 appears only because of unit 21's connection formulae, which is the cleanest possible demonstration that they are not decoration. Close by comparing, for large n, the oscillator's |psi_n|² averaged over a few wavelengths with the classical probability density 1/(pi sqrt(A² − x²)) of a particle spending time in proportion to 1/v: the correspondence principle is unit 21's asymptotics, not a philosophy.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Making the formalism produce numbers. "An electron in a box the size of an atom": the infinite square well E_n = n² pi² ħ²/(2mL²), and for L = 0.1 nm the ground state at about 37.6 eV — the right order for atomic energies, from dimensions plus one boundary condition. "How fast a free electron forgets where it is": an electron packet of initial width 0.1 nm doubles its width in about 0.3 fs. "Alpha decay and 24 orders of magnitude": with the WKB tunnelling exponent through the Coulomb barrier (the Gamow factor, Gamow and Gurney–Condon, 1928), compare uranium-238 (alpha energy 4.20 MeV, half-life 4.5 × 10⁹ years) with polonium-212 (8.78 MeV, 0.3 µs) — a factor of two in energy against about 24 orders of magnitude in half-life — find how much of that the bare exponent explains, and account for the residual with the finite nuclear radius; then derive the Geiger–Nuttall law's form. "Commutators from brackets": verify [x, p] = iħ on a dense domain, compute [H, x] and [H, p], and recover Ehrenfest's theorem — Newton's second law for expectation values — noting when it reduces to the classical equation and when it does not. At least one proof: that a symmetric operator's eigenvalues are real and its eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues orthogonal, or that the oscillator ladder must terminate.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
You derive the Schrödinger equation by running the WKB ansatz backwards, and recover from it the Hamilton–Jacobi equation at leading order and the continuity equation at the next; you solve the harmonic oscillator by ladder operators and by Bohr–Sommerfeld and say exactly where each one's 1/2 comes from; and you compute the WKB tunnelling exponent for alpha decay and use it to explain why a factor of two in energy spans about 24 orders of magnitude in half-life.
