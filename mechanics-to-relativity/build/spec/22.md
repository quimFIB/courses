# Unit 22 — Continuum mechanics: the stress tensor, and conservation laws in local form

- directory: `units/22-continuum-stress/`
- part: Part C — the analytic toolkit, fields, and the crisis that ends Newtonian physics (data-trunk="c")
- part thesis: Take a chain of oscillators to the continuum and you get a partial differential equation, a field, and the need for four more pieces of mathematics: Fourier and Sturm-Liouville theory, the Fourier transform with the complex analysis that makes it usable, asymptotics for the problems with no exact answer, and tensor fields on space. Electromagnetism is here at full strength and on purpose: it is the only place vector calculus reaches its final form, it is the historical and logical road to relativity, and F_{mu nu} is the canonical tensor field that makes the Riemann tensor's index work familiar rather than alarming. The part ends by showing, with the chain rule and nothing else, that Maxwell's equations do not survive a Galilean boost — and by finding which transformation they do survive.

## Physics
A deformable body, for which 'the force on it' is not a single vector. Cauchy's stress tensor and the tetrahedron argument that produces it; pressure as the isotropic part; the symmetry of the stress tensor as angular-momentum balance. Strain, and Hooke's law reduced by isotropy to two constants. Mass and momentum conservation in local form. Euler's equations for an inviscid fluid, with Navier-Stokes stated and the viscous term identified. Sound as the linearisation of Euler's equations about rest. P- and S-waves in an elastic solid, and why only solids carry shear waves — which is how the Earth's liquid outer core was found.

## Mathematical tools
The divergence theorem as the engine that converts every integral conservation statement into a local one: continuity, and momentum balance with the stress tensor supplying the flux. The material derivative and the transport theorem. Tensor fields on R^3, the first use of unit 16's index machinery on a field rather than a single object. Cauchy's stress theorem proved by the tetrahedron limit — the only place in classical physics where you *prove* rather than assume that a physical quantity is bilinear, and the best rehearsal available for what T^{mu nu} is. Flagged explicitly: the triple (energy density, momentum density, momentum flux) assembled here becomes T^{mu nu} in unit 35, and this local conservation law is exactly the statement that its divergence vanishes.

## The worked example the slides are built around
Derive the speed of sound: linearise continuity and Euler about rest, close the system with the adiabatic relation p proportional to rho^gamma, and obtain c = sqrt(gamma p/rho) = 343 m/s in air at 20 C. Then show Newton's isothermal assumption yields 280 m/s, 18 per cent low — a discrepancy that stood unexplained for a century until Laplace, and a genuine historical falsification with a number attached and a wrong physical hypothesis identifiable as its cause.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Constructing local conservation laws and linearising. "Cauchy's tetrahedron": prove the traction on a surface is linear in its normal, hence a tensor, then prove that tensor symmetric from angular-momentum balance and identify the assumption (no intrinsic body torques) that derivation quietly uses. "Seismology": compute P- and S-wave speeds from given Lame parameters, show c_L > c_T always, and explain why the S-wave shadow zone implies a liquid core; locate an epicentre from P-S arrival differences at three stations.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
From an integral conservation statement you derive its local form by the divergence theorem, you prove Cauchy's theorem rather than assuming stress is bilinear, and you compute the speed of sound in air to within 2 per cent — getting the thermodynamic closure right rather than lucky.
