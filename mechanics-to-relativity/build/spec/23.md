# Unit 23 — Static fields: electrostatics and magnetostatics, the same vector calculus twice

- directory: `units/23-static-fields/`
- part: Part C — the analytic toolkit, fields, and the crisis that ends Newtonian physics (data-trunk="c")
- part thesis: Take a chain of oscillators to the continuum and you get a partial differential equation, a field, and the need for four more pieces of mathematics: Fourier and Sturm-Liouville theory, the Fourier transform with the complex analysis that makes it usable, asymptotics for the problems with no exact answer, and tensor fields on space. Electromagnetism is here at full strength and on purpose: it is the only place vector calculus reaches its final form, it is the historical and logical road to relativity, and F_{mu nu} is the canonical tensor field that makes the Riemann tensor's index work familiar rather than alarming. The part ends by showing, with the chain rule and nothing else, that Maxwell's equations do not survive a Galilean boost — and by finding which transformation they do survive.

## Physics
Coulomb's law and its formal identity with Newton's, so that all of unit 05 transfers free of charge and the differences — two signs, and no shielding of gravity, so gravity accumulates while electricity screens — are the interesting part. Conductors and boundary conditions; the method of images; the multipole expansion of a charge distribution, reusing unit 10's Legendre machinery as a boundary-value tool. Magnetostatics: Biot-Savart, Ampere's law, the vector potential, gauge freedom, and the empirical fact that B has no sources, which has no electrostatic analogue. The Lorentz force, connecting back to unit 12's velocity-dependent potential.

## Mathematical tools
Stokes' theorem doing real work at last: Ampere's law is the curl theorem applied to B. Poisson's and Laplace's equations; Green's functions for the Laplacian in two and three dimensions, with the dimension-dependence derived rather than asserted; uniqueness by the maximum principle; Earnshaw's theorem. Separation of variables in spherical and cylindrical coordinates using unit 19's machinery. The existence of A from div B = 0 as the Poincare lemma, with the solenoid supplying the non-simply-connected obstruction — the same computation as unit 04's vortex, one degree up. The Helmholtz decomposition: a field decaying at infinity is determined by its divergence and its curl, which is why Maxwell's system has exactly four equations and is neither over- nor under-determined, a point almost never made explicitly.

## The worked example the slides are built around
A grounded conducting sphere in a uniform external field: solve Laplace's equation by Legendre expansion, extract the induced dipole moment and the surface charge density proportional to cos(theta); then obtain the identical answer by images with two image charges, and reconcile the two derivations term by term.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Boundary-value problems, the structure theorem, and the first cohomological hint. "Images and their limits": solve the point charge near a grounded plane and near a grounded sphere, then exhibit a geometry admitting no finite image set. "The solenoid": compute B inside and outside, find B = 0 outside but A nonzero with circulation equal to the enclosed flux, and identify this as unit 04's vortex problem with the field one degree higher — closed but not exact. "Helmholtz precisely": state the decomposition with its decay hypotheses, prove uniqueness, and use it to predict that Maxwell's system must have four equations before you have seen them.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
You solve a spherical boundary-value problem by Legendre expansion and independently by images and get the same answer, produce a vector potential in a stated gauge, and state the Helmholtz theorem with its hypotheses and use it to count Maxwell's equations in advance.
