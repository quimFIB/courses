## Why this unit exists
Unit 24 recorded a failure and walked past it: a classical electron orbiting a proton radiates by Larmor's formula and spirals in within about 10⁻¹¹ s, so classical electrodynamics cannot explain why atoms exist. This unit, which follows it directly, repairs it with unit 21b's wave mechanics, and in doing so cashes two more of the course's debts: unit 10's Legendre functions become the angular part of the wavefunction, and unit 08's Runge–Lenz vector — the hidden symmetry that makes Kepler orbits close — reappears as the reason hydrogen's energy levels are more degenerate than rotational symmetry alone explains. Same 1/r potential, same hidden symmetry, two theories. That is the unit's thesis; say it on the overview slide.

## Physics
Start with the failure, quantified: the classical collapse time a_0³/(4 r_e² c) ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻¹¹ s from unit 24's Larmor formula, with r_e the classical electron radius. Then the repair heuristically, in unit 00's style: an electron confined to radius r has momentum at least of order ħ/r, so E(r) ≈ ħ²/(2 m r²) − e²/(4 pi epsilon_0 r), which is bounded below and minimised at the Bohr radius a_0 = 4 pi epsilon_0 ħ²/(m e²) ≈ 0.529 Å with E = −13.6 eV — the uncertainty principle is what makes the energy bounded below. Then exactly: hydrogen as a central-force problem, separated in spherical coordinates as unit 08 separated the Kepler problem. Angular momentum conserved because the Hamiltonian commutes with rotations — unit 13's Noether theorem, quantum version. The bound spectrum E_n = −13.6 eV / n², degeneracy n² (spin ignored), the Rydberg formula, and the spectral series: Lyman, Balmer, Paschen. The degeneracy in l is "accidental" only until one finds the quantum Runge–Lenz vector, which Pauli used in 1926 to derive the spectrum algebraically, independently of and at the same time as Schrödinger's wave-mechanical solution: together with L it closes into the Lie algebra of SO(4) on bound states, and n² is the dimension of the representation each level carries. What is left out, stated: electron spin (the Stern–Gerlach experiment of 1922, named), fine structure as the place where unit 27's special relativity returns at order alpha², and the stability of bulk matter, which needs the Pauli exclusion principle (Dyson–Lenard 1967, Lieb–Thirring 1975, cited). The Dirac equation stays excluded, as the course's proposal excludes spinors.

## Mathematical tools
Angular momentum operators L = x × p and their commutators [L_i, L_j] = i ħ epsilon_ijk L_k, recognised as the Lie algebra of SO(3) from unit 16 — rotations generated by L as unit 21b's momentum generates translations. The ladder L± = L_x ± i L_y, and the spectrum of L² and L_z derived algebraically: ħ² l(l+1) and ħ m with |m| ≤ l; half-integer l allowed by the algebra and excluded for functions on the sphere by single-valuedness (the half-integers are spin, named). Spherical harmonics as unit 10's associated Legendre functions times e^{i m phi}, now as the simultaneous eigenfunctions. The radial equation with the effective potential — unit 08's centrifugal barrier, now with ħ² l(l+1) — solved by a Frobenius series (unit 09, reached through 21b's prerequisite 19) whose termination is the quantisation; Laguerre polynomials named. The variational principle (Rayleigh–Ritz): the ground-state energy is the infimum of ⟨psi|H|psi⟩, proved for an operator with a lowest eigenvalue and a complete eigenbasis. The quantum Runge–Lenz vector, its commutation with H stated with the algebra in the notes, and the SO(4) argument for the n² degeneracy given in structure with Pauli's derivation cited. Reduced mass, from unit 08's two-body reduction.

## The worked example the slides are built around
Hydrogen's ground state three ways. First the uncertainty-principle estimate, which lands on a_0 and −13.6 eV exactly — and the slides say why that is luck in the constant and not in the scaling. Second the exact 1s state from the radial equation, psi ∝ e^{−r/a_0}, with ⟨r⟩ = 3a_0/2. Third a variational calculation with a Gaussian trial function, which gives −(8/3pi) × 13.6 eV ≈ −11.5 eV: an upper bound, as the variational principle promises, and 15 per cent high. Then follow the running example to the light it emits: the Balmer H-alpha line, n = 3 → 2, computed with and without the reduced-mass correction and compared with the measured 656.3 nm in air.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Spectroscopy to four figures, and the algebra behind it. "H-alpha": the wavelength from the Rydberg formula with the reduced mass, and the step from vacuum to air (refractive index about 1.000277) needed to match the measured 656.28 nm. "How deuterium was found": the H-alpha shift between hydrogen and deuterium from the reduced mass alone, about 0.18 nm, which is how Urey, Brickwedde and Murphy identified deuterium in 1932 — history where the history is the argument. "The classical collapse": the 1.6 × 10⁻¹¹ s from unit 24's Larmor formula, done properly as an integral over the inspiral. "Counting states": the degeneracy n² from summing 2l + 1 over l < n, and again from the SO(4) structure. "Angular momentum algebra": derive the spectrum of L² and L_z from the commutators alone and prove the bound |m| ≤ l. At least one proof: the variational principle, or the termination argument for the radial series.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
You solve hydrogen's radial equation for the bound spectrum −13.6 eV / n², explain the n² degeneracy by the Runge–Lenz symmetry unit 08 met classically, and compute the H-alpha wavelength with the reduced-mass correction to four significant figures — 656.3 nm in air — together with the 0.18 nm deuterium shift that announced deuterium in 1932.
