# Unit 29 — Classical field theory: Noether again, and where T^{mu nu} actually comes from

- directory: `units/29-classical-field-theory/`
- part: Part D — Minkowski: relativity as the geometry of a quadratic form, and fields on it (data-trunk="d")
- part thesis: Special relativity is presented as the structure it is: a group derived from homogeneity, isotropy and one invariant speed, acting on a vector space carrying an indefinite bilinear form. Every paradox dissolves into a statement about that form — the twin paradox is the reversed triangle inequality — and every dynamical result follows from the free action being proper time. Electromagnetism is then recognised as having been relativistic all along, field theory supplies an honest definition of the stress-energy tensor, and the part closes by taking the equivalence principle seriously and showing, from inside flat spacetime, exactly which feature of gravity Minkowski space cannot represent.

## Physics
A field is a mechanical system whose coordinates are labelled by points of space rather than by an index. The action as an integral of a Lagrangian density over spacetime. Three Lagrangians done explicitly: the scalar wave equation, the Klein-Gordon field, and Maxwell's L = -(1/4 mu_0) F_{mu nu}F^{mu nu} - A_mu J^mu, from which the source equations follow in four lines. Gauge invariance as a property of the action, forcing current conservation. Noether for fields: continuous symmetries give conserved currents and hence charges. Spacetime translation gives the canonical energy-momentum tensor — which for Maxwell comes out neither symmetric nor gauge-invariant, a genuinely surprising defect — and the Belinfante-Rosenfeld improvement repairs it. Then the definition general relativity actually uses: T^{mu nu} = (2/sqrt(-g)) delta S_matter/delta g_{mu nu}, automatically symmetric and gauge-invariant. This unit exists so that the right-hand side of Einstein's equation is a definition rather than an incantation.

## Mathematical tools
Functional derivatives of an action over a spacetime region; the field Euler-Lagrange equations derived by unit 11's argument in four dimensions with the boundary term handled explicitly. Noether's theorem in the field-theoretic setting, proved in full. Variation with respect to a metric, introduced here in flat space with g held general, so that unit 35's Einstein-Hilbert variation is a continuation rather than a new technique. Tensor densities, sqrt(-g) and the invariant volume element.

## The worked example the slides are built around
Derive all of Maxwell's source equations by varying S with respect to A_mu, carefully, including the integration by parts and the boundary term. Then compute T^{mu nu} twice: once as the canonical Noether current from translation invariance, obtaining the non-symmetric non-gauge-invariant expression; once by metric variation, obtaining the familiar symmetric F^{mu alpha}F^nu_alpha - (1/4) eta^{mu nu} F^2 — and identify the Belinfante improvement term as exactly the difference. The two routes landing in the same place by different mathematics is the point.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Building actions and extracting currents. "The Klein-Gordon field": from L = -(1/2)(d phi)^2 - (1/2) m^2 phi^2 derive the field equation, the energy-momentum tensor and the dispersion relation, and interpret the mass term as what makes the wave equation dispersive. "A symmetry with no obvious charge": find the conserved current of the global phase symmetry of a complex scalar, then show that gauging it — letting the phase depend on x — forces the introduction of a field with exactly the transformation law of A_mu.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
Given a Lagrangian density you derive the field equations, construct the Noether current of every continuous symmetry and verify its conservation on shell, and produce the symmetric energy-momentum tensor by metric variation — and for Maxwell you can state precisely how the canonical and metric-variation tensors differ and why the difference is separately conserved.
