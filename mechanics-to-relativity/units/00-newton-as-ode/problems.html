the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. Given any force of the form \(F(t)\), \(F(v)\) or \(F(x)\): you classify which of the three forms it is before starting; you write the dimensional form of the answer and name the dimensionless group the calculus is left to determine; and you reduce to quadrature and produce either the explicit solution or the period integral, with the trap of that case handled.

P1 Classify, then take the first step routine

exercises: recognising the three integrable cases; the first reduction of each

For each equation below, say which of the three cases it falls into — \(F(t)\), \(F(v)\), \(F(x)\) — and carry out only the first reduction: the integral to be done, not the answer.

(a) \(m\ddot x = -mg - b\dot x\)

(b) \(m\ddot x = -\kappa x^3\)

(c) \(m\ddot x = F_0 e^{-t/\tau}\)

(d) \(m\ddot x = -b\dot x - \kappa x\)

(e) \(m\ddot x = -\kappa x\,\big(1 + \epsilon \cos\omega t\big)\)

Two of the five are in none of the three cases. Say which, say why, and say in one sentence what would have to be true for the method of the other three to apply.

Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1 — what the answer is

Five labels and three integrals. Each label is one of \(F(t)\), \(F(v)\), \(F(x)\), or "none of the three"; each of the three integrals is a single expression, not an evaluated one.

Rung 2 — the test

Cover up the left-hand side and read the right. Which of \(t\), \(\dot x\), \(x\) appears? If exactly one, that is the case. If two, no move works, and saying so is the whole answer for that part.

Rung 3 — what "first reduction" means

For \(F(t)\): integrate once, and keep the constant. For \(F(v)\): write the first-order equation in \(v\) and put it in separated form \(m\,\mathrm dv/F(v) = \mathrm dt\). For \(F(x)\): multiply by \(\dot x\) and write down the conserved quantity. Stop there.

Rung 4 — the first line

(a) \(m\dot v = -mg - bv\), so \(t = \int_{v_0}^v m\,\mathrm dw/(-mg - bw)\), and note where that integrand blows up.

Worked solution

Idea. The classification is decided by which of \(t, v, x\) actually appears on the right-hand side. If more than one appears, none of the three moves works.

(a) \(m\ddot x = -mg - b\dot x\). The right-hand side depends on \(\dot x\) only: case \(F(v)\). First reduction: write \(v = \dot x\), giving the first-order equation \(m\dot v = -mg - bv\), and separate, \[ t \;=\; \int_{v_0}^{v}\frac{m\,\mathrm dw}{-mg - bw}, \] valid on any interval avoiding the zero \(w = -mg/b\).

(b) \(m\ddot x = -\kappa x^3\). Position only: case \(F(x)\). First reduction: multiply by \(\dot x\) and integrate, \(\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac14\kappa x^4 = E\).

(c) \(m\ddot x = F_0 e^{-t/\tau}\). Time only: case \(F(t)\). First reduction: integrate once, \(\dot x(t) = v_0 + \frac{F_0\tau}{m}\big(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\big)\).

(d) \(m\ddot x = -b\dot x - \kappa x\) depends on both \(x\) and \(\dot x\). None of the three. Multiplying by \(\dot x\) gives \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12\kappa x^2\big) = -b\dot x^2\), which is not zero, so there is no first integral to exploit; separating in \(v\) is impossible because \(x\) is still present.

(e) \(m\ddot x = -\kappa x(1 + \epsilon\cos\omega t)\) depends on both \(x\) and \(t\). None of the three, for the mirror-image reason: the equation is not invariant under time translation, so there is no energy integral.

What would have to be true. For (d): \(b = 0\), which restores case \(F(x)\); or \(\kappa = 0\), which gives case \(F(v)\). For (e): \(\epsilon = 0\). Both equations are nevertheless solvable — (d) by unit 06's linear theory, (e) not in closed form at all (it is Mathieu's equation, and unit 21 says what can be done with it). "Not reducible to quadrature" is not the same as "hopeless"; it is the reason units 01, 02 and 06 exist.

Check. Dimensions of (c)'s first reduction: \([F_0\tau/m] = \mathsf{MLT^{-2}}\cdot\mathsf T/\mathsf M = \mathsf{LT^{-1}}\), a velocity. ✓ Limit of (c) as \(\tau\to\infty\): \(1 - e^{-t/\tau} \to t/\tau\), so \(\dot x \to v_0 + F_0 t/m\), constant force. ✓

P2 The dimensional form, three times routine

exercises: building a dimension matrix; computing its rank; counting and naming groups

For each of the following, write the dimension matrix in \((\mathsf M, \mathsf L, \mathsf T)\), compute its rank, say how many independent dimensionless groups there are, exhibit a basis for them, and state the strongest conclusion the Buckingham pi theorem allows.

(a) The period \(T\) of a mass \(m\) on a spring of stiffness \(\kappa\) (dimension \(\mathsf{MT^{-2}}\)), released from amplitude \(a\).

(b) The speed \(v\) of a long water wave of wavelength \(\lambda\) on deep water of density \(\rho\) under gravity \(g\).

(c) The escape speed \(v\) from a spherical body of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\), given Newton's constant \(G\) (dimension \(\mathsf{M^{-1}L^3T^{-2}}\)).

In (a), what does the answer say about the amplitude, and which slide of the deck proves the same thing a different way?

Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1 — what the answer is

Three matrices, three ranks, three kernels, and three formulas of the shape "quantity \(= C\times\) (a monomial)" with \(C\) an undetermined pure number.

Rung 2 — getting the dimensions right

Read them off a defining equation, never off a unit name. \(\kappa\) from \(F = \kappa x\); \(G\) is given; \(\rho\) is mass over volume. Write each column as a triple of exponents in \((\mathsf M, \mathsf L, \mathsf T)\).

Rung 3 — rank without row reduction

A quantity carrying a base dimension that nothing else carries is automatically independent of the rest — and it is therefore guaranteed to drop out of any dimensionless group. Spot those first; in all three parts they take you straight to \(r = 3\).

Rung 4 — the first line

(a) \([\kappa] = \mathsf{MT^{-2}}\), so the columns for \((T, m, \kappa, a)\) are \((0,0,1)\), \((1,0,0)\), \((1,0,-2)\), \((0,1,0)\). Now solve \(Da = 0\) one row at a time, starting with the \(\mathsf L\) row, which has only one nonzero entry.

Worked solution

Idea. Write the matrix, find the rank by spotting which columns are obviously independent, solve \(Da = 0\).

(a) mass on a spring

\([\kappa] = \mathsf{MT^{-2}}\) because \(\kappa x\) is a force. With quantities \((T, m, \kappa, a)\): \[ D = \begin{array}{c|cccc} & T & m & \kappa & a\\\hline \mathsf M & 0&1&1&0\\ \mathsf L & 0&0&0&1\\ \mathsf T & 1&0&-2&0 \end{array} \] Columns \(m, a, T\) are \(e_1, e_2, e_3\), so \(r = 3\) and \(n - r = 4 - 3 = 1\). Solving \(Da = 0\): the \(\mathsf L\) row gives \(a_a = 0\); the \(\mathsf M\) row gives \(a_m = -a_\kappa\); the \(\mathsf T\) row gives \(a_T = 2a_\kappa\). Taking \(a_\kappa = 1\), \[ \pi = \frac{T^2\kappa}{m} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = C\sqrt{m/\kappa}. \] The amplitude drops out entirely — the oscillator is isochronous, and with one group the conclusion is complete up to the single number \(C\) (which is \(2\pi\)). The deck proves the same thing twice more: from mechanical similarity, since \(-\kappa x\) is homogeneous of degree 1 so \(T\propto a^{0}\) (Mechanical similarity: scaling the equation, not the units), and by evaluating the period integral (The period integral).

(b) deep-water wave

Quantities \((v, g, \lambda, \rho)\): \[ D = \begin{array}{c|cccc} & v & g & \lambda & \rho\\\hline \mathsf M & 0&0&0&1\\ \mathsf L & 1&1&1&-3\\ \mathsf T & -1&-2&0&0 \end{array} \] \(\rho\) is the only column with a nonzero \(\mathsf M\) entry, so it is independent of the others; \(\lambda\) and \(v\) are independent in the \((\mathsf L,\mathsf T)\) plane. Hence \(r = 3\) and \(n - r = 1\). The \(\mathsf M\) row forces \(a_\rho = 0\); the \(\mathsf T\) row gives \(a_v = -2a_g\); the \(\mathsf L\) row then gives \(a_\lambda = -a_v - a_g = a_g\). With \(a_g = 1\), \[ \pi = \frac{g\lambda}{v^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = C\sqrt{g\lambda}. \] Density drops out, and long waves travel faster than short ones — the reason a distant storm announces itself with swell before chop.

(c) escape speed

Quantities \((v, G, M, R)\), \([G] = \mathsf{M^{-1}L^3T^{-2}}\): \[ D = \begin{array}{c|cccc} & v & G & M & R\\\hline \mathsf M & 0&-1&1&0\\ \mathsf L & 1&3&0&1\\ \mathsf T & -1&-2&0&0 \end{array} \] Columns \(M, R, v\) give \(\mathsf M\), then \(\mathsf L\), then \(\mathsf T\) in turn, so \(r = 3\) and \(n - r = 1\). \(\mathsf M\): \(a_M = a_G\). \(\mathsf T\): \(a_v = -2a_G\). \(\mathsf L\): \(a_v + 3a_G + a_R = 0\), so \(a_R = -a_G\). With \(a_G = 1\), \[ \pi = \frac{GM}{v^2R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v = C\sqrt{GM/R}. \] (\(C = \sqrt2\); unit 08 supplies it.)

Check. Dimensions of each conclusion: \([\sqrt{m/\kappa}] = (\mathsf M/\mathsf{MT^{-2}})^{1/2} = \mathsf T\) ✓; \([\sqrt{g\lambda}] = (\mathsf{LT^{-2}}\cdot\mathsf L)^{1/2} = \mathsf{LT^{-1}}\) ✓; \([\sqrt{GM/R}] = (\mathsf{M^{-1}L^3T^{-2}}\cdot\mathsf M/\mathsf L)^{1/2} = \mathsf{LT^{-1}}\) ✓.

P3 A bead thrown upward through oil medium

exercises: case F(v) with a sign change; separation and inversion; a limit check; two numbers

A bead of mass \(m = 5.00\) g is thrown vertically upward with speed \(v_0 = 2.00\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) through a fluid exerting a linear drag \(-b\dot x\) with \(b = 5.00\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}}\). Measure \(x\) upward from the throw.

(a) Solve for \(v(t)\) on the upward leg, and find the time \(t_{\uparrow}\) to the top of the flight and the height \(h\) reached. Give both as numbers.

(b) Prove that \(t_{\uparrow} < v_0/g\) for every \(b > 0\), and say in one sentence why that is physically obvious.

(c) Show that as \(b \to 0\) your \(h\) tends to \(v_0^2/2g\), and give the first correction in \(b\).

(d) The bead then falls back. Is the descent quicker or slower than the ascent? Answer with a number for these data, and with an argument that does not depend on them.

Hints · 5 rungs
Rung 1 — what the answer is

(a) a function \(v(t)\), then two numbers in seconds and metres; (b) an inequality proved for all \(b > 0\); (c) a two-term expansion; (d) a number and an argument.

Rung 2 — before any algebra

The dimensional form: the quantities are \(t_\uparrow, h, m, b, g, v_0\), so \(\tau = m/b\) is the only time in the problem and \(g\tau\) the only speed other than \(v_0\). Everything will come out as \(\tau\) times a function of \(v_0/(g\tau)\). Knowing that makes the algebra checkable.

Rung 3 — signs

Measure \(x\) upward. On the way up the bead moves upward, so drag points downward, the same way as gravity: \(m\dot v = -mg - bv\) with \(v > 0\). The right-hand side is more negative than \(-mg\), which is already the answer to (b).

Rung 4 — the shape of the integral

\(m\dot v = -b(v + v_\infty)\) with \(v_\infty = mg/b\). Separating gives a logarithm; the argument never vanishes on the upward leg, which is why the inversion is safe here and would not be on the way down.

Rung 5 — the first line

\(\displaystyle\int_{v_0}^{v}\frac{\mathrm dw}{w + v_\infty} = -\frac t\tau\), so \(v(t) = -v_\infty + (v_0 + v_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}\); set \(v = 0\) for \(t_\uparrow\) and integrate once more for \(h\).

Worked solution

Idea. Case \(F(v)\): separate, invert, integrate once more. Careful with signs — \(v\) is positive upward and both gravity and drag act downward on the way up.

(a)

With \(x\) measured upward, \(m\dot v = -mg - bv\). Write \(\tau = m/b\) and \(v_\infty = mg/b = g\tau\) (the terminal speed for the downward motion). Then \(\dot v = -(v + v_\infty)/\tau\), and separating, \[ \int_{v_0}^{v}\frac{\mathrm dw}{w + v_\infty} = -\frac{t}{\tau} \;\Longrightarrow\; \ln\frac{v + v_\infty}{v_0 + v_\infty} = -\frac t\tau \;\Longrightarrow\; v(t) = -v_\infty + (v_0 + v_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}. \] The inversion is legitimate because \(w + v_\infty > 0\) throughout the upward leg, so the integrand never blows up. Setting \(v = 0\), \[ t_\uparrow = \tau\ln\!\Big(1 + \frac{v_0}{v_\infty}\Big),\qquad h = \int_0^{t_\uparrow}\! v\,\mathrm dt = v_\infty\tau\left[\frac{v_0}{v_\infty} - \ln\!\Big(1+\frac{v_0}{v_\infty}\Big)\right]. \] (The second follows from \(\int_0^t v = -v_\infty t + \tau(v_0+v_\infty) (1 - e^{-t/\tau})\) together with \(e^{-t_\uparrow/\tau} = v_\infty/(v_0+v_\infty)\).)

Numbers. \(\tau = m/b = 0.00500/0.0500 = 0.1000\) s, \(v_\infty = g\tau = 0.9810\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(v_0/v_\infty = 2.0387\): \[ t_\uparrow = 0.1000\times\ln(3.0387) = \mathbf{0.1111\ s},\qquad h = 0.09810\times(2.0387 - 1.1113) = \mathbf{0.0910\ m}. \]

(b)

\(t_\uparrow = \tau\ln(1 + v_0/(g\tau))\) and \(\ln(1+z) < z\) for every \(z > 0\), so \(t_\uparrow < \tau\cdot v_0/(g\tau) = v_0/g\). Physically: on the way up, drag acts downward alongside gravity, so the deceleration is greater than \(g\) at every instant, so the bead stops sooner than it would in vacuum.

(c)

Put \(\epsilon = v_0/(g\tau) = bv_0/(mg)\). Then \(h = g\tau^2\big[\epsilon - \ln(1+\epsilon)\big]\) and \(\epsilon - \ln(1+\epsilon) = \tfrac12\epsilon^2 - \tfrac13\epsilon^3 + O(\epsilon^4)\), so \[ h = \frac{v_0^2}{2g} - \frac{b\,v_0^3}{3\,m\,g^2} + O(b^2). \] As \(b \to 0\), \(h \to v_0^2/2g\), the vacuum apex. ✓

Honest warning about the numbers. For these data \(\epsilon = 2.04\), which is not small: the vacuum apex is \(0.2039\) m, the first correction is \(0.2771\) m, and the "corrected" answer would be negative. The true \(h\) is \(0.0910\) m, less than half the vacuum value. The expansion is a statement about the limit \(b \to 0\) and says nothing useful here — a good illustration that an asymptotic correction is only a correction where the parameter is small. Unit 21 is about exactly this distinction.

(d)

Slower on the way down. The descent takes \(0.1754\) s against an ascent of \(0.1111\) s, a ratio of \(1.578\). (Solve \(v_\infty(t - \tau(1-e^{-t/\tau})) = h\) numerically for the descent time.)

Argument without the numbers. Energy. On the way up the bead does work against gravity and against drag, so it arrives at the top having lost \(\tfrac12 mv_0^2 - mgh\) to drag. On the way down it starts from rest and drag again removes energy, so it returns to the throw point with speed \(v_1 < v_0\). It therefore covers the same distance \(h\) with a smaller speed at every corresponding height — the downward journey is everywhere slower than the upward one at the same height — so it takes longer.

Check. Dimensions: \([\tau] = \mathsf M/(\mathsf{MT^{-1}}) = \mathsf T\), \([v_\infty\tau] = \mathsf L\). ✓ Limit \(b\to0\): \(t_\uparrow \to v_0/g\) and \(h \to v_0^2/2g\), both vacuum values. ✓

P4 The skydiver medium

exercises: case F(v) with quadratic drag; a hyperbolic quadrature; asymptotics; four numbers

A skydiver of mass \(M = 80.0\) kg falls from rest with quadratic drag, \(M\dot v = Mg - cv^2\) for \(v > 0\), and reaches a terminal speed of \(55.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

(a) Find \(c\). Then derive \(v(t)\) and \(x(t)\) from the quadrature, with no appeal to a remembered formula.

(b) At what time is she at 99 per cent of terminal speed, and how far has she fallen by then? Compare both with the drag-free answers at the same instant.

(c) Show that for large \(t\), \(x(t) = v_\infty t - (v_\infty^2/g)\ln 2 + o(1)\), compute the constant lag in metres, and say how good that approximation already is at the time found in (b).

Hints · 5 rungs
Rung 1 — what the answer is

A number for \(c\) in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}\), two functions \(v(t)\) and \(x(t)\), and four numbers: a time, a distance, and the two drag-free comparisons.

Rung 2 — where the constants come from

Terminal speed is the zero of the total force, so \(c\) follows from \(Mg = cv_\infty^2\) with no calculus at all. Do that first; then the ODE has only \(g\) and \(v_\infty\) in it.

Rung 3 — the integral

\(\displaystyle\int\frac{M\,\mathrm dw}{Mg - cw^2}\). Take \(Mg\) outside and it becomes \(\frac1g\int\frac{\mathrm dw}{1 - (w/v_\infty)^2}\), which is an inverse hyperbolic tangent — or partial fractions and two logarithms, if you prefer.

Rung 4 — the second integral

You need \(\int\tanh\). Ask yourself what function differentiates to \(\tanh\); the answer is one line long and gives the lag in part (c) for free.

Rung 5 — the first line

\(c = Mg/v_\infty^2 = 80.0\times9.81/55.0^2\), and \(t = \dfrac{v_\infty}{g}\operatorname{artanh}\dfrac{v}{v_\infty}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Case \(F(v)\), with a quadratic zero of the force at \(v_\infty\).

(a)

\(v_\infty = \sqrt{Mg/c}\), so \(c = Mg/v_\infty^2 = 80.0\times9.81/55.0^2 = \mathbf{0.2594\ kg\,m^{-1}}\). Separating, \[ t = \int_0^v\frac{M\,\mathrm dw}{Mg - cw^2} = \frac1g\int_0^v\frac{\mathrm dw}{1 - (w/v_\infty)^2} = \frac{v_\infty}{g}\operatorname{artanh}\frac{v}{v_\infty}, \] using \(M/(Mg - cw^2) = \big(g(1 - (w/v_\infty)^2)\big)^{-1}\) and \(\int_0^z(1-u^2)^{-1}\mathrm du = \operatorname{artanh} z\). This is valid for \(0 \le v < v_\infty\), where the integrand is finite; at \(v = v_\infty\) the integral diverges, which is the statement that terminal speed is never attained. Inverting (legitimate: \(\operatorname{artanh}\) is strictly increasing with nonvanishing derivative on \((-1,1)\)), \[ v(t) = v_\infty\tanh\frac{gt}{v_\infty},\qquad x(t) = \int_0^t v = \frac{v_\infty^2}{g}\Big[\ln\cosh\frac{gs}{v_\infty}\Big]_0^t = \frac{v_\infty^2}{g}\ln\cosh\frac{gt}{v_\infty}, \] since \(\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm du}\ln\cosh u = \tanh u\) and \(\ln\cosh 0 = 0\).

(b)

\(v = 0.99\,v_\infty\) needs \(gt/v_\infty = \operatorname{artanh}0.99 = 2.6467\), so with \(v_\infty/g = 5.6065\) s, \[ t_{99} = \mathbf{14.84\ s},\qquad x_{99} = \frac{55.0^2}{9.81}\ln\cosh(2.6467) = \mathbf{604\ m}. \] Drag-free at the same instant: \(v = gt_{99} = 145.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), against her actual \(0.99v_\infty = 54.45\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) — 2.67 times larger — and \(x = \tfrac12 gt_{99}^2 = 1080\) m against \(604\) m, 1.79 times larger.

(c)

\(\cosh u = \tfrac12(e^u + e^{-u}) = \tfrac12 e^u(1 + e^{-2u})\), so \(\ln\cosh u = u - \ln 2 + \ln(1 + e^{-2u}) = u - \ln2 + O(e^{-2u})\). Hence \[ x(t) = \frac{v_\infty^2}{g}\Big(\frac{gt}{v_\infty} - \ln2\Big) + O(e^{-2gt/v_\infty}) = v_\infty t - \frac{v_\infty^2\ln2}{g} + o(1), \] with the constant lag \(v_\infty^2\ln2/g = 55.0^2\times0.6931/9.81 = \mathbf{213.7\ m}\). At \(t_{99}\) the asymptotic formula gives \(55.0\times14.8385 - 213.7 = 602.4\) m against the exact \(603.9\) m: an error of \(1.5\) m, or 0.26 per cent, already.

Check. Dimensions: \([v_\infty^2/g] = \mathsf{L^2T^{-2}}/\mathsf{LT^{-2}} = \mathsf L\). ✓ Limit \(t \ll v_\infty/g\): \(\tanh u \approx u\) gives \(v \approx gt\) and \(\ln\cosh u \approx u^2/2\) gives \(x \approx \tfrac12gt^2\) — free fall. ✓ Limit \(c \to 0\): \(v_\infty \to \infty\) and the same expansion holds for all fixed \(t\). ✓

P5 The pi theorem, in pieces hard

exercises: proof; kernels and rank; what adding a quantity does to the count

Let \(q_1,\dots,q_n\) be positive quantities with dimension matrix \(D \in \mathbb{Q}^{d\times n}\) and \(r = \operatorname{rank} D\).

(a) Prove that \(\prod_j q_j^{a_j}\) is dimensionless if and only if \(Da = 0\), and that the dimensionless monomials form a group under multiplication isomorphic to \((\ker D, +)\).

(b) Suppose \(r = n\). Prove that the only dimensionless monomial is the constant 1. Deduce that if a further quantity \(Q\) is determined by the \(q_j\) and the enlarged matrix has the same rank \(n\), then \(Q = C\prod_j q_j^{c_j}\) for a pure number \(C\) and a unique exponent vector \(c\).

(c) Now add one more quantity \(q_{n+1}\) to the list. Prove that the number of independent groups either stays the same or increases by exactly one, and characterise which happens in terms of the column \(D_{\cdot\,n+1}\).

(d) Use (c) to explain, in two sentences, why adding an irrelevant quantity to the list weakens the conclusion, and why omitting a relevant one makes it false rather than weak.

Hints · 5 rungs
Rung 1 — what the answer is

Four proofs. No numbers. (a) is two short arguments, one about dimensions and one about groups; (b) and (c) are rank–nullity; (d) is two sentences of interpretation.

Rung 2 — the only fact about dimensions you need

The dimension vector of a product is the sum of the dimension vectors. Everything in (a) is that fact plus induction.

Rung 3 — for (b)

"Only the constant monomial" is \(\ker D = \{0\}\). For the second half, ask where the column \(D_{\cdot Q}\) can possibly live if the enlarged matrix has the same rank as \(D\), and remember that a vector in the column space of a full-column-rank matrix has unique coordinates.

Rung 4 — for (c)

The count is \((n+1) - r'\), and appending a column changes the rank by 0 or 1. Write both cases out and see which way the count moves; the answer is the opposite of what most people guess.

Rung 5 — the first line

(a) If \(q\) has dimension vector \(u\) and \(q'\) has \(u'\), then under \(\lambda\) the product \(qq'\) scales by \(\prod_i\lambda_i^{u_i}\prod_i\lambda_i^{u'_i} = \prod_i\lambda_i^{(u+u')_i}\).

Worked solution

(a)

The dimension of a product is the sum of the dimensions: if \(q\) has dimension vector \(u\) and \(q'\) has \(u'\), then under \(\lambda\) the product scales by \(\prod_i\lambda_i^{u_i}\prod_i\lambda_i^{u_i'} = \prod_i\lambda_i^{(u+u')_i}\). By induction the monomial \(\prod_j q_j^{a_j}\) has dimension vector \(\sum_j a_j D_{\cdot j} = Da\). A quantity is dimensionless exactly when its dimension vector is \(0\), that is, exactly when \(Da = 0\).

For the group statement: let \(\mathcal G\) be the set of formal monomials \(\prod_j q_j^{a_j}\) with \(a \in \mathbb{Q}^n\), under multiplication. The map \(\Phi : (\mathbb{Q}^n, +) \to \mathcal G\), \(a \mapsto \prod q_j^{a_j}\), is a surjective homomorphism, and it is injective if the \(q_j\) are treated as independent formal symbols. Restricting to dimensionless monomials restricts the domain to \(\ker D\) by the first paragraph, so \(\Phi|_{\ker D}\) is a group isomorphism \((\ker D, +) \cong (\text{dimensionless monomials}, \times)\). In particular the number of independent groups is \(\dim\ker D = n - r\), by rank–nullity.

(b)

If \(r = n\) then \(\ker D = \{0\}\), so by (a) the only dimensionless monomial is \(\prod q_j^0 = 1\).

Now let \(\tilde D = [\,D_{\cdot Q}\ \ D\,]\) be the enlarged matrix and suppose \(\operatorname{rank}\tilde D = n\). Since \(D\) already has rank \(n\) and is a submatrix, the column \(D_{\cdot Q}\) must lie in the column space of \(D\): there is \(c \in \mathbb{Q}^n\) with \(D_{\cdot Q} = Dc\), and it is unique because the columns of \(D\) are independent. Then \(\pi := Q\prod_j q_j^{-c_j}\) is dimensionless, since its dimension vector is \(D_{\cdot Q} - Dc = 0\). By Buckingham applied to the list \((Q, q_1,\dots,q_n)\) — which has \(n+1\) quantities and rank \(n\), hence one group — the relation determining \(Q\) is \(F(\pi) = 0\); as \(Q\) is determined by the \(q_j\), that relation fixes \(\pi\) at a constant value \(C\). Hence \(Q = C\prod_j q_j^{c_j}\), with \(c\) unique as shown. \(\square\)

(c)

Let \(D' = [\,D\ \ D_{\cdot\,n+1}\,]\) and \(r' = \operatorname{rank} D'\). Appending a column changes the rank by 0 or 1, and by 1 exactly when \(D_{\cdot\,n+1}\) is not in the column space of \(D\). The number of groups is \((n+1) - r'\).

  • If \(D_{\cdot\,n+1} \in \operatorname{col} D\): \(r' = r\), so the count goes from \(n - r\) to \(n + 1 - r\): one more group.
  • Otherwise: \(r' = r + 1\), so the count goes from \(n - r\) to \(n + 1 - (r+1) = n - r\): unchanged.

(d)

Adding an irrelevant quantity whose dimensions are already spanned by the others adds a group, by (c). Buckingham then gives \(F(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_{n-r}, \pi_{n+1-r}) = 0\), an unknown function of one more variable — which is a strictly weaker statement, because a function of \(m+1\) variables is far less constrained than one of \(m\). (The physics is not wrong: the true law simply does not depend on the extra group.)

Omitting a relevant quantity is different in kind. The remaining relation is then not dimensionally homogeneous in the reduced list — the hypothesis of the theorem fails — and the "conclusion" is a false statement rather than a weak one. The diagnostic is usually visible: the groups you get are vacuous, or the predicted scaling contradicts an experiment. The deck's example is the pendulum with \(g\) omitted, where the only group is \(\theta_0\) and the theorem says nothing at all about \(T\).

(c) Evaluate the numerical constant for \(n = 1\) and \(n = 4\), quoting \(B(1,\tfrac12) = 2\) and \(B(\tfrac14,\tfrac12) = 5.2441\).

(d) Check the \(E\)-exponent against mechanical similarity: \(V = k|x|^n\) has a force homogeneous of degree \(n-1\), so the deck's \(T \propto a^{(1-k_{\mathrm{hom}})/2}\) applies with \(k_{\mathrm{hom}} = n - 1\) — the deck writes that degree \(k\), a letter already taken here by the potential's coefficient. Hints · 5 rungs
Rung 1 — what the answer is

A formula for \(T(E)\) with a Beta function in it, a single integer \(n\), two numerical constants, and one consistency check against the deck.

Rung 2 — before integrating

Write the dimensional form. The quantities are \(T, m, k, E\); \([k]\) follows from \(k|x|^n\) being an energy. One group, so \(T\) is a pure number times a monomial — and the \(E\)-exponent that the problem asks about is visible at that stage, before any integral.

Rung 3 — the substitution

Scale out the turning point first: \(x = a\sigma\) with \(a = (E/k)^{1/n}\) turns the integral into \(\int_0^1(1-\sigma^n)^{-1/2}\mathrm d\sigma\), a pure number depending only on \(n\).

Rung 4 — getting the Beta function

The Beta integral has the form \(\int_0^1 u^{p-1}(1-u)^{q-1}\mathrm du\). You want \((1-u)^{-1/2}\), so \(q = 1/2\); to get \(u\) where you currently have \(\sigma^n\), substitute \(u = \sigma^n\) and be careful with \(\mathrm d\sigma\).

Rung 5 — the first line

\(\displaystyle T = 2\sqrt{2m}\int_0^{a}\frac{\mathrm dx}{\sqrt{E - kx^n}}\) with \(a = (E/k)^{1/n}\), using the evenness of \(V\) to fold the two halves together.

Worked solution

(a)

\(V\) is continuous, even, strictly increasing on \([0,\infty)\) with \(V(0) = 0\) and \(V\to\infty\); so for any \(E > 0\) the set \(\{V \le E\}\) is the bounded interval \([-a, a]\) with \(a = (E/k)^{1/n}\), and the motion is bounded with turning points \(\pm a\). By the period-integral theorem, \[ T(E) = \sqrt{2m}\int_{-a}^{a}\frac{\mathrm dx}{\sqrt{E - k|x|^n}} = 2\sqrt{2m}\int_{0}^{a}\frac{\mathrm dx}{\sqrt{E - kx^n}}, \] using the evenness. Substitute \(x = a\,\sigma\), so \(kx^n = E\sigma^n\): \[ T = 2\sqrt{2m}\;\frac{a}{\sqrt E}\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm d\sigma}{\sqrt{1-\sigma^n}} . \] Now put \(u = \sigma^n\), \(\sigma = u^{1/n}\), \(\mathrm d\sigma = \tfrac1n u^{1/n - 1}\mathrm du\): \[ \int_0^1\frac{\mathrm d\sigma}{\sqrt{1-\sigma^n}} = \frac1n\int_0^1 u^{\frac1n - 1}(1-u)^{-\frac12}\,\mathrm du = \frac1n B\!\Big(\frac1n,\frac12\Big). \] With \(a/\sqrt E = k^{-1/n}E^{1/n - 1/2}\), \[ T(E) = \frac{4}{n}\,\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\,\sqrt m\;k^{-1/n}\,E^{\frac1n-\frac12}\, B\!\Big(\frac1n,\frac12\Big), \] as claimed: the prefactor is \(2\sqrt{2m}\cdot\frac1n = \frac{2\sqrt2}{n}\sqrt m\), and \(2\sqrt2 = 4/\sqrt2\).

The integral is improper at \(\sigma = 1\) and converges, since near \(\sigma = 1\) the integrand is \(\asymp (1-\sigma)^{-1/2}\); equivalently \(B(p,\tfrac12)\) converges for \(p > 0\).

(b)

\(E^{1/n - 1/2}\) is constant in \(E\) exactly when \(1/n = 1/2\), that is \(n = 2\) — *the harmonic oscillator is the unique isochronous power-law potential*. Then \(B(\tfrac12,\tfrac12) = \int_0^1\mathrm du/\sqrt{u(1-u)} = \pi\) (substitute \(u = \sin^2\varphi\), which turns it into \(2\int_0^{\pi/2}\mathrm d\varphi\)), and \[ T = \frac42\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\sqrt m\,k^{-1/2}\,\pi = \pi\sqrt{2m/k}. \] For the standard form \(V = \tfrac12\kappa x^2\) we have \(k = \kappa/2\), so \(T = \pi\sqrt{4m/\kappa} = 2\pi\sqrt{m/\kappa}\). ✓

(c)

The constant is \((4/n)(1/\sqrt2)B(1/n,1/2)\):

  • \(n = 1\): \(4\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\cdot 2 = 4\sqrt2 = \mathbf{5.6569}\), and \(T = 5.6569\sqrt m\,k^{-1}E^{1/2}\). (This is the bouncing-ball potential; the period grows like \(\sqrt E\), i.e. like the drop height's square root.)
  • \(n = 4\): \(1\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\cdot 5.2441 = \mathbf{3.7081}\), and \(T = 3.7081\sqrt m\,k^{-1/4}E^{-1/4}\): a quartic well runs faster the more energy it has.

(d)

\(V = k|x|^n\) gives \(F = -V' = -nk|x|^{n-1}\operatorname{sgn}x\), homogeneous of degree \(n - 1\). The deck's result with exponent \(k_{\text{hom}} = n-1\) gives \(T\propto a^{(1 - (n-1))/2} = a^{1 - n/2}\). Since \(E = ka^n\), \(a\propto E^{1/n}\), so \(T\propto E^{(1/n)(1 - n/2)} = E^{1/n - 1/2}\). ✓ The two agree, and the relation that makes them agree is \(a \propto E^{1/n}\).

Check. Dimensions: \([k] = \mathsf{ML^{2-n}T^{-2}}\) (so that \(k|x|^n\) is an energy), hence \([\sqrt m k^{-1/n}E^{1/n-1/2}] = \mathsf M^{1/2}\cdot \mathsf M^{-1/n}\mathsf L^{(n-2)/n}\mathsf T^{2/n}\cdot \mathsf M^{1/n - 1/2}\mathsf L^{2/n-1}\mathsf T^{1-2/n} = \mathsf T\). ✓ Limit/special case: \(n = 2\) reproduces \(2\pi\sqrt{m/\kappa}\). ✓ Number: \(4\sqrt2 = 5.6569\) and \(5.2441/\sqrt2 = 3.7081\). ✓

(a) Prove that if \(V'(a) \neq 0\) the integral converges, and give an explicit upper bound for it in terms of \(|V'(a)|\), \(\sup|V''|\) and \(\delta\).

(b) Prove that if \(V'(a) = 0\) and \(V''(a) < 0\) it diverges.

(c) Deduce that for the plane pendulum the period is finite for every \(0 < \theta_0 < \pi\) and infinite for \(\theta_0 = \pi\).

Hints · 5 rungs
Rung 1 — what the answer is

Two convergence proofs with an explicit bound in one of them, a two-line deduction, and an asymptotic rate confirmed against three given numbers.

Rung 2 — the shape of the argument

Everything is comparison with \(\int_0^\delta s^{-p}\,\mathrm ds\), which converges for \(p < 1\) and diverges for \(p \ge 1\). So the whole problem is: what power of \(s = a - x\) is \(E - V(a-s)\)?

Rung 3 — the tool

Taylor's theorem with remainder about \(a\), using \(V(a) = E\). If \(V'(a)\ne0\) the leading term is linear in \(s\) and the integrand goes like \(s^{-1/2}\); if \(V'(a) = 0\) it is quadratic and the integrand goes like \(s^{-1}\). For the bound in (a) you must make "leading term" quantitative: choose \(\delta\) small enough that the remainder is at most half the leading term.

Rung 4 — for (d)

At energy \(E_c - \Delta\) the motion stops short of the top, at \(s_t = \sqrt{\Delta/\kappa}\). The integral is now proper, and \(\int \mathrm ds/\sqrt{s^2 - s_t^2}\) is an inverse hyperbolic cosine. Use \(\operatorname{arcosh}z = \ln(2z) + O(z^{-2})\) and the logarithm appears.

Rung 5 — the first line

With \(s = a-x\) and \(V'(a) = c > 0\), Taylor gives \(E - V(a-s) = cs - \tfrac12V''(\xi)s^2\); bound \(|V''|\) by \(M\) on the interval and restrict to \(\delta \le c/M\).

Worked solution

Idea. Taylor with remainder turns \(E - V\) into a power of \(x - a\); the comparison test does the rest. The exponent \(-1/2\) is integrable and \(-1\) is not, and everything hinges on which one you get.

Write \(I(\delta) := \int_{a-\delta}^{a}\mathrm dx\big/\sqrt{\tfrac2m(E-V(x))} = \sqrt{m/2}\int_{a-\delta}^{a}(E - V(x))^{-1/2}\mathrm dx\), and put \(s = a - x \in (0,\delta]\).

(a) \(V'(a) \neq 0\): convergent

Since \(V < E = V(a)\) to the left of \(a\), \(V\) is increasing there, so \(V'(a) \ge 0\); with \(V'(a)\ne0\) write \(V'(a) = c > 0\). Taylor with Lagrange remainder about \(a\): \[ E - V(a - s) = V(a) - V(a-s) = c\,s - \tfrac12 V''(\xi)\,s^2 \quad\text{for some }\xi\in(a-s,a). \] Let \(M = \sup_{[a-\delta_0,a]}|V''|\) and choose \(\delta \le \min(\delta_0, c/M)\). Then \(\tfrac12|V''(\xi)|s^2 \le \tfrac12 Ms\cdot s \le \tfrac12 cs\), so \[ \tfrac12 c\,s \;\le\; E - V(a-s) \;\le\; \tfrac32 c\,s . \] Hence \((E-V)^{-1/2} \le \sqrt{2/(cs)}\) and \[ I(\delta) \;\le\; \sqrt{\frac m2}\int_0^\delta\sqrt{\frac{2}{c\,s}}\,\mathrm ds = \sqrt{\frac mc}\int_0^\delta s^{-1/2}\mathrm ds = 2\sqrt{\frac{m\delta}{c}} \;<\;\infty, \] valid for \(\delta \le \min(\delta_0, |V'(a)|/\sup|V''|)\). That is the required bound, and the comparison test gives convergence.

(b) \(V'(a) = 0\), \(V''(a) < 0\): divergent

Write \(V''(a) = -2\kappa\) with \(\kappa > 0\). Taylor to third order (using \(V \in C^3\)): \(E - V(a-s) = \kappa s^2 + R(s)\) with \(|R(s)| \le M's^3\) near 0. Choose \(\delta\) with \(M'\delta \le \kappa\); then \(E - V(a-s) \le 2\kappa s^2\), so \[ I(\delta) \;\ge\; \sqrt{\frac m2}\int_0^\delta\frac{\mathrm ds}{\sqrt{2\kappa}\,s} = \frac12\sqrt{\frac m\kappa}\int_0^\delta\frac{\mathrm ds}{s} = \infty . \]

(c)

For the pendulum, \(V(\theta) = mgl(1-\cos\theta)\) and the turning point is \(\theta_0\) with \(E = V(\theta_0)\).

  • \(0 < \theta_0 < \pi\): \(V'(\theta_0) = mgl\sin\theta_0 \ne 0\), so (a) applies and the quarter-period is finite; the period is four times it, hence finite.
  • \(\theta_0 = \pi\): \(V'(\pi) = 0\) and \(V''(\pi) = -mgl < 0\), so (b) applies and the time is infinite. This is the separatrix.

(d) the rate

Near the top write \(s = a - x\) and \(V = E_c - \kappa s^2 + O(s^3)\). At energy \(E = E_c - \Delta\) the turning point is at \(s_t\) with \(\kappa s_t^2 = \Delta\), i.e. \(s_t = \sqrt{\Delta/\kappa}\), and \(E - V = \kappa s^2 - \Delta\) to leading order. Numerical confirmation on the pendulum. Also \(\Delta = 2mgl - mgl(1-\cos\theta_0) = 2mgl\cos^2(\theta_0/2)\), so each factor of 10 in \(\pi - \theta_0\) is a factor of 100 in \(\Delta\) and should add \(2\sqrt{l/g}\ln 100 = 2\times0.31928\times4.6052 = 2.941\) s. Observed: \(10.766 - 7.826 = 2.940\) s and \(13.707 - 10.766 = 2.941\) s. ✓

\(E_c = V(\pm x_\star) = \tfrac12\kappa\frac{\kappa}{\alpha} - \tfrac14\alpha\frac{\kappa^2}{\alpha^2} = \frac{\kappa^2}{4\alpha} = \frac{8.00^2}{4\times50.0} = \mathbf{0.320\ J}.\)

For \(E < E_c\) the component of \(\{V \le E\}\) containing the origin is an interval strictly inside \((-x_\star, x_\star)\): bounded, periodic motion. For \(E > E_c\) there is no turning point on either side of the origin (\(V\to-\infty\) as \(|x|\to\infty\)), so the particle escapes and in fact accelerates away for ever. \(E = E_c\) is the separatrix, reaching \(x_\star\) in infinite time by P8.

(c)

Starting at the origin, \(E = \tfrac12 mv_0^2\). Oscillation for \(\tfrac12 mv_0^2 < E_c\), escape for \(>\). The threshold is \[ v_c = \sqrt{2E_c/m} = \sqrt{2\times0.320/0.200} = \mathbf{1.789\ m\,s^{-1}} . \]

(d)

Near \(x = 0\), \(V \approx \tfrac12\kappa x^2\), so \(m\ddot x \approx -\kappa x\) with angular frequency \(\omega = \sqrt{\kappa/m} = \sqrt{8.00/0.200} = 6.3246\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\) and \[ T \approx 2\pi/\omega = \mathbf{0.9935\ s}. \] It is only an approximation because the quartic term was dropped: the true potential is flatter than the parabola (the \(-\tfrac14\alpha x^4\) term is negative), so the restoring force is weaker and the true period is longer, growing without bound as \(E\to E_c\). By P7's method the amplitude dependence enters at order \((a/x_\star)^2\).

Check. Dimensions: \([\kappa^2/\alpha] = (\mathsf{MT^{-2}})^2/ (\mathsf{ML^{-2}T^{-2}}) = \mathsf{ML^2T^{-2}}\), an energy. ✓ Limit \(\alpha\to0\): \(x_\star\to\infty\) and \(E_c\to\infty\) — the lid disappears and every motion is bounded, as it must be for a pure harmonic well. ✓

P10 The pendulum's period, in numbers medium

exercises: using the amplitude series; error at a given order; a clock that drifts

Use \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\big(1 + \theta_0^2/16 + 11\theta_0^4/3072 + \cdots\big)\), with \(\theta_0\) in radians, and \(l = 1.000\) m.

(a) Compute the period at \(\theta_0 = 45^\circ\) keeping two terms, then three. The exact value is \(2.086256\) s. How large is the three-term error, in milliseconds, and roughly what size of term would you expect next?

(b) A pendulum clock is regulated to keep perfect time at an amplitude of \(6^\circ\). Friction reduces the amplitude to \(4^\circ\). Does it then gain or lose, and by how many seconds per day?

(c) Find the amplitude at which the small-angle formula is wrong by 0.1 per cent, using the leading term only, and say to what accuracy you trust that estimate given the deck's exact 1-per-cent answer of \(22.81^\circ\).

P11 Mechanical similarity, and Kepler's third law hard

exercises: proof; homogeneous forces; scaling solutions; a prediction checked against two planets

(a) Let \(F : \mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) satisfy \(F(\alpha x) = \alpha^k F(x)\) for all \(\alpha > 0\) and all \(x\). Prove that if \(x(t)\) solves \(m\ddot x = F(x)\), then so does \(\tilde x(t) = \alpha\,x(t/\beta)\), provided \(\beta^2 = \alpha^{1-k}\).

(b) Deduce that a bounded motion of amplitude \(a\) has period proportional to \(a^{(1-k)/2}\), and check the result against the harmonic oscillator and against your answer to P7.

(c) Prove the same statement componentwise for the three-dimensional inverse-square law \(m\ddot x_i = -GMm\,x_i/|x|^3\), \(i = 1,2,3\): find the relation between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\), and state the conclusion as Kepler's third law.

(d) Mars has semi-major axis \(1.523679\) AU and Jupiter \(5.2044\) AU. Predict each period in years and compare with the observed \(686.980\) d and \(4332.589\) d (take 1 yr \(= 365.25\) d). One agreement is far better than the other: quantify both, and identify which assumption of the scaling argument the worse one violates.

Hints · 5 rungs
Rung 1 — what the answer is

Two proofs by substitution, one scaling exponent, one relation \(\beta^2 = \alpha^3\), and four numbers: two predicted periods and two relative errors.

Rung 2 — what to substitute

Do not try to solve anything. Define \(\tilde x(t) := \alpha x(t/\beta)\), compute \(m\ddot{\tilde x}\) and \(F(\tilde x)\) separately, and see what relation between \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) makes them equal.

Rung 3 — from solutions to periods

If \(x\) has period \(T\) then \(\tilde x(t) = \alpha x(t/\beta)\) has period \(\beta T\) — check that directly from the definition. Amplitudes scale by \(\alpha\). That is the whole of (b).

Rung 4 — for (c)

The only new step is \(|\tilde x(t)| = \alpha|x(t/\beta)|\), which lets you pull \(\alpha^{-3}\) out of the denominator. The rest is (a) with \(k = -2\).

Rung 5 — for (d), and the first line

Compute \(a^{3/2}\) and compare; then ask which quantity in \(m\ddot x_i = -GMm x_i/|x|^3\) was assumed not to move, and look up the two planets' masses relative to the Sun before deciding which agreement should have been worse. First line of (a): \(\ddot{\tilde x}(t) = (\alpha/\beta^2)\ddot x(t/\beta)\) by the chain rule, twice.

Worked solution

(a)

Let \(\tilde x(t) = \alpha x(t/\beta)\). Differentiating twice by the chain rule, \(\ddot{\tilde x}(t) = (\alpha/\beta^2)\,\ddot x(t/\beta)\). Since \(x\) solves the equation, \(\ddot x(t/\beta) = F(x(t/\beta))/m\), so \[ m\ddot{\tilde x}(t) = \frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}\,F\big(x(t/\beta)\big). \] On the other hand \(F(\tilde x(t)) = F(\alpha x(t/\beta)) = \alpha^k F(x(t/\beta))\) by homogeneity. The two agree for all \(t\) exactly when \(\alpha/\beta^2 = \alpha^k\), i.e. \(\beta^2 = \alpha^{1-k}\). \(\square\)

(b)

If \(x\) is a bounded periodic motion of amplitude \(a\) and period \(T\), then \(\tilde x\) has amplitude \(\alpha a\) and period \(\beta T\), since \(\tilde x(t + \beta T) = \alpha x(t/\beta + T) = \alpha x(t/\beta) = \tilde x(t)\). So amplitudes scale by \(\alpha\) and periods by \(\beta = \alpha^{(1-k)/2}\): \(T\propto a^{(1-k)/2}\).

  • Harmonic oscillator, \(F = -\kappa x\), \(k = 1\): \(T\propto a^0\). ✓ (And \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/\kappa}\) confirms it.)
  • P7's \(V = k|x|^n\), \(F\) homogeneous of degree \(n-1\): \(T\propto a^{1 - n/2}\), and with \(E\propto a^n\) this is \(T\propto E^{1/n - 1/2}\), exactly P7(a). ✓

(c)

Put \(\tilde x_i(t) = \alpha x_i(t/\beta)\), so that \(|\tilde x(t)| = \alpha|x(t/\beta)|\). Then \[ m\ddot{\tilde x}_i(t) = \frac{m\alpha}{\beta^2}\ddot x_i(t/\beta) = -\frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}\,\frac{GMm\,x_i(t/\beta)}{|x(t/\beta)|^3}, \] while the equation demands \[ -\frac{GMm\,\tilde x_i(t)}{|\tilde x(t)|^3} = -\frac{GMm\,\alpha x_i(t/\beta)}{\alpha^3|x(t/\beta)|^3} = -\frac{1}{\alpha^2}\cdot\frac{GMm\,x_i(t/\beta)}{|x(t/\beta)|^3}. \] These agree for all \(t\) exactly when \(\alpha/\beta^2 = \alpha^{-2}\), that is \[ \boxed{\ \beta^2 = \alpha^3\ } \] — which is the case \(k = -2\) of (a), as it should be. So scaling every distance in an orbit by \(\alpha\) gives another orbit with all times scaled by \(\alpha^{3/2}\): *the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the linear size*, Kepler's third law.

Which assumption fails. The equation \(m\ddot x_i = -GMm x_i/|x|^3\) treats the Sun as fixed, i.e. assumes the planet's mass is negligible. Jupiter's mass is \(9.546\times10^{-4}\) of the Sun's, against \(3.2\times10^{-7}\) for Mars. The correct statement (unit 08, cited, where the two-body problem is reduced) has \(GM\) replaced by \(G(M + m)\), giving a period smaller by \((1 + m/M)^{-1/2}\): \[ 11.87288\times(1 + 9.546\times10^{-4})^{-1/2} = 11.8672\ \mathrm{yr}, \] which removes half the discrepancy and leaves \(4.4\times10^{-4}\) — the residue being perturbations by Saturn and the precision of the quoted semi-major axis. The scaling argument itself is exact; what it was applied to was an approximate equation.

Another way

(a) can be read as Buckingham on the equation rather than on the answer. Write the homogeneous force as \(F(x) = -\kappa x^{k}\), so that \(\kappa\) has dimension \(\mathsf{MT^{-2}L^{1-k}}\); the quantities are \(T, a, m, \kappa\). The rank is 3, so there is one group, and solving \(Da = 0\) gives \(\pi = T^2\kappa a^{k-1}/m\), hence \(T = C\sqrt{m/\kappa}\,a^{(1-k)/2}\) — the same exponent, now with the mass dependence visible too. Similarity and Buckingham are the same invariance read two ways; use whichever is quicker.
