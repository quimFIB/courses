Phase portraits: what you know when there is no formula

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. Given a planar nonlinear system you have not seen before, you locate and classify every equilibrium, decide at each whether linearisation is conclusive and say which theorem you are invoking, construct a Lyapunov function where it is not, and either prove or rule out a periodic orbit — with a trapping region and Poincaré–Bendixson, or the index theorem, or a strictly monotone quantity.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about four hours. Work them on paper first. Every number asked for is a number you can get exactly or to four figures by hand.

P1 The flow of \(\dot x = x^2\) routine

exercises: flows, the one-parameter group property, maximal intervals

For \(\dot x = x^2\) on \(\mathbb{R}\):

  1. Solve it by separation (unit 00) and show the flow is \(\varphi_t(x_0) = x_0/(1 - x_0 t)\).
  2. Verify the group property \(\varphi_t(\varphi_s(x_0)) = \varphi_{t+s}(x_0)\) directly from that formula, and say for which \((t, s, x_0)\) both sides are defined.
  3. Give the maximal interval of existence for \(x_0 > 0\), for \(x_0 = 0\) and for \(x_0 < 0\), and explain in one sentence why \(\{\varphi_t\}\) is a local flow and not a group of maps of \(\mathbb{R}\).
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is a formula \(\varphi_t(x_0)\); to part 2 an identity between two rational functions of \(t, s, x_0\); to part 3 three intervals, each with one finite endpoint or none.

Rung 2

Unit 00's first case: \(\dot x = F(x)\) is separable. Integrate \(\mathrm{d}x/x^2\) and remember to treat \(x_0 = 0\) on its own, by uniqueness rather than by the formula.

Rung 3

For part 2, put the inner expression in and clear the compound fraction by multiplying numerator and denominator by \(1 - x_0 s\). For part 3, the maximal interval ends where the denominator vanishes, and its sign tells you on which side of \(t = 0\) that happens.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{x^2} = \int\mathrm{d}t \implies -\frac1x = t + C\).

Worked solution

Idea. One separable equation (unit 00), then the group property is pure algebra, and the maximal interval is read off the formula's pole.

Working.

  1. Away from \(x = 0\), \(\mathrm{d}x/x^2 = \mathrm{d}t\) gives \(-1/x = t + C\), so \(x(t) = -1/(t+C)\); imposing \(x(0) = x_0 \ne 0\) gives \(C = -1/x_0\) and \[\varphi_t(x_0) = \frac{-1}{t - 1/x_0} = \frac{x_0}{1 - x_0 t}.\] For \(x_0 = 0\) the constant solution \(x \equiv 0\) is the unique one (Picard–Lindelöf; \(f(x) = x^2\) is \(C^\infty\)), and the formula gives it too.
  2. \[\varphi_t(\varphi_s(x_0)) = \frac{\dfrac{x_0}{1-x_0 s}}{1 - t\,\dfrac{x_0}{1-x_0 s}} = \frac{x_0}{1 - x_0 s - x_0 t} = \varphi_{t+s}(x_0),\] multiplying numerator and denominator by \(1 - x_0 s\). Both sides are defined exactly when \(1 - x_0 s \ne 0\) and \(1 - x_0(s+t) \ne 0\); the second condition alone does not suffice, because the intermediate point must exist.
  3. \(x_0 > 0\): the denominator vanishes at \(t = 1/x_0 > 0\), and \(x(t) \to +\infty\) there, so the maximal interval is \((-\infty,\ 1/x_0)\). \(x_0 = 0\): all of \(\mathbb{R}\). \(x_0 < 0\): the pole is at \(t = 1/x_0 < 0\), so the interval is \((1/x_0,\ \infty)\). Hence no single \(t > 0\) gives a map defined on all of \(\mathbb{R}\): for fixed \(t > 0\), \(\varphi_t\) is undefined at \(x_0 = 1/t\). This is a local flow, not a one-parameter group of maps of \(\mathbb{R}\).

Check. Dimensions: if \([x] = \mathsf{L}\) then \([\dot x] = \mathsf{L}\mathsf{T}^{-1}\) forces \(x^2\) to be read as \(x^2/\ell\) for some length \(\ell\); with \(\ell = 1\) the product \(x_0 t\) is dimensionless, as the formula requires. Limit: for small \(t\), \(\varphi_t(x_0) = x_0(1 + x_0t + \dots) = x_0 + x_0^2 t + O(t^2)\), and \(\dot x(0) = x_0^2\) — the equation, recovered. Number: from \(x_0 = 2\), blow-up at \(t = 0.5\); at \(t = 0.4\), \(x = 2/(1-0.8) = 10\).

P2 Six linear systems routine

exercises: the trace–determinant classification, degenerate cases

For each matrix, compute \(\tau\), \(\Delta\) and the eigenvalues, name the type of the origin, and say whether it is stable, asymptotically stable or unstable: \[A_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&2\end{pmatrix},\quad A_2 = \begin{pmatrix}-3&1\\1&-3\end{pmatrix},\quad A_3 = \begin{pmatrix}1&-5\\1&-1\end{pmatrix},\] \[A_4 = \begin{pmatrix}-1&-2\\2&-1\end{pmatrix},\quad A_5 = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix},\quad A_6 = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.\] Two of the six are not hyperbolic. Identify them and say what goes wrong if you use them as a guide to a nonlinear system.

Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Six rows of a table: two numbers, two eigenvalues, a name, a stability verdict.

Rung 2

\(\lambda^2 - \tau\lambda + \Delta = 0\). Locate \((\tau,\Delta)\) in the plane of “Planar linear systems: two numbers decide” before solving: the sign of \(\Delta\), then the sign of \(\tau^2-4\Delta\), then the sign of \(\tau\).

Rung 3

Two of the six sit exactly on a boundary of that picture — one on the positive \(\Delta\) axis, one at the origin. Those are the non-hyperbolic pair. For the one with \(\Delta = 0\), do not stop at "the eigenvalues are zero": solve the system outright, it is two lines.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\det(A_1 - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 - 3\lambda - 4 = (\lambda-4)(\lambda+1)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Compute \(\tau\) and \(\Delta\), locate the point in the trace–determinant plane, and only then solve the quadratic.

Working.

\(A_5\) is not \(2I\), so it is defective: the solution is \(e^{2t}(c_1\mathbf v + c_2(\mathbf w + t\mathbf v))\) with \(\mathbf v = (1,0)\). \(A_6\) gives \(\dot x = y\), \(\dot y = 0\), so \(x(t) = x_0 + y_0 t\), \(y \equiv y_0\): every point of the \(x\)-axis is an equilibrium and every other orbit is a horizontal line traversed at constant speed.

The two non-hyperbolic ones are \(A_3\) (\(\operatorname{Re}\lambda = 0\)) and \(A_6\) (\(\lambda = 0\)). Using them as a guide to a nonlinear system is illegitimate: Hartman–Grobman does not apply, and the deck's family \(\dot r = ar^3\) shows that a linear centre is compatible with a nonlinear sink, source or centre. For \(A_6\) the failure is worse still — an arbitrarily small nonlinear term can destroy the line of equilibria entirely, leaving none.

Check. For each row, the eigenvalues multiply to \(\Delta\) and add to \(\tau\): \(4\times(-1) = -4\) and \(4-1 = 3\); \((-2)(-4) = 8\), \(-6\); \((2i)(-2i) = 4\), \(0\); \((-1)^2 + 2^2 = 5\), \(-2\); \(2\times2 = 4\), \(4\); and \(0\times0 = 0\), \(0+0 = 0\). All six check.

P3 The damped pendulum for every \(\gamma\) medium

exercises: linearisation of the running example, reading a parameter off the τ–Δ plane

For \(\ddot\theta + \gamma\dot\theta + \sin\theta = 0\) with \(\gamma \ge 0\):

  1. Classify \((0,0)\) and \((\pi,0)\) for every \(\gamma > 0\), stating the ranges of \(\gamma\) in which the type changes.
  2. Find the value of \(\gamma\) at which the hanging equilibrium stops being a spiral, and say what it is called mechanically.
  3. Give the eigenvalues at \(\gamma = 0.1\) and at \(\gamma = 2.5\) to four decimal places, and check each pair against \(\tau\) and \(\Delta\).
  4. Is either equilibrium ever non-hyperbolic for \(\gamma > 0\)? Say why this matters.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 are a classification with threshold values of \(\gamma\); part 3 is eight numbers; part 4 is yes or no with a reason.

Rung 2

Both Jacobians have the same trace. Write \(\tau\) and \(\Delta\) for each equilibrium and then ask which of the three regions of the \(\tau\)–\(\Delta\) plane the point \((-\gamma, \pm1)\) is in as \(\gamma\) grows from \(0\).

Rung 3

The spiral-to-node threshold is where the discriminant changes sign. For part 4, "hyperbolic" means no eigenvalue has zero real part — check the two cases separately, using \(\Delta \ne 0\) for one and \(\tau \ne 0\) for the other.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12\big(-\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2 - 4\cos\theta^\ast}\big)\), with \(\cos\theta^\ast = +1\) below and \(-1\) above.

Worked solution

Idea. Both Jacobians have \(\tau = -\gamma\); only \(\Delta = \cos\theta^*\) differs. One parameter moves both points horizontally in the \(\tau\)–\(\Delta\) plane.

Working.

  1. At \((0,0)\): \(\tau = -\gamma\), \(\Delta = 1\), so \(\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12(-\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2-4})\).

    • \(0 < \gamma < 2\): complex with real part \(-\gamma/2 < 0\) — a *stable spiral*.
    • \(\gamma = 2\): double root \(\lambda = -1\), and the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&-2\end{pmatrix}\) is not \(-I\), so a *degenerate stable node*.
    • \(\gamma > 2\): two distinct negative real roots — a stable node (\(\lambda_+\lambda_- = 1 > 0\) and \(\lambda_++\lambda_- = -\gamma < 0\)).

    At \((\pi, 0)\): \(\Delta = -1 < 0\) for every \(\gamma\), so the roots are real of opposite sign: a saddle, always.

  2. \(\gamma = 2\): critical damping, \(b = 2m\sqrt{gl^3}\) in physical units. It is the least damping that returns the pendulum without overshoot, and the boundary between spiral and node in the \(\tau\)–\(\Delta\) plane.
  3. \(\gamma = 0.1\): down, \(\lambda = -0.0500 \pm 0.9987\,i\) (sum \(-0.1 = \tau\), modulus squared \(0.0025 + 0.9975 = 1.0000 = \Delta\)); up, \(\lambda = 0.9512,\ -1.0512\) (sum \(-0.1000\), product \(-1.0000\)). \(\gamma = 2.5\): down, \(\lambda = -0.5000,\ -2.0000\) (sum \(-2.5\), product \(1.0000\)); up, \(\lambda = 0.3508,\ -2.8508\) (sum \(-2.5000\), product \(-1.0000\)).
  4. No. At the hanging position \(\Delta = 1 > 0\) and \(\tau = -\gamma < 0\), so both roots have real part \(-\gamma/2 \ne 0\); at the upright position \(\Delta = -1 \ne 0\), so neither root is zero and both are real. Hence Hartman–Grobman applies at both for every \(\gamma > 0\): the linear picture is the true local picture, and no bifurcation can occur as \(\gamma\) varies over \((0,\infty)\) — the transition at \(\gamma = 2\) changes the shape of the approach but not the topology, which is why it is not a bifurcation.

Check. Limit \(\gamma \to 0^+\): the down eigenvalues tend to \(\pm i\), the linearised small-amplitude frequency, and the period \(2\pi\) in units of \(\sqrt{l/g}\) — unit 00's \(2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\). Limit \(\gamma \to \infty\): \(\lambda_+ \to -1/\gamma \to 0^-\) and \(\lambda_- \to -\gamma\), the overdamped creep, slower and slower as the fluid thickens, as it should be.

P4 When linearisation lies hard

exercises: the failure of linearisation at a centre, constructing a Lyapunov function

Consider \(\dot x = -y - x^3,\quad \dot y = x - y^3\).

  1. Show the origin is the only equilibrium and that its linearisation is a centre, so Hartman–Grobman says nothing.
  2. Prove that the origin is asymptotically stable, by exhibiting a Lyapunov function and verifying every hypothesis of the theorem.
  3. Show that \(\dot x = -y + x^3,\ \dot y = x + y^3\) has the same linearisation and an unstable origin, and state precisely what this pair of examples proves about the linearisation of a non-hyperbolic equilibrium.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 ends in a \(2\times2\) matrix and the word "centre"; part 2 is a proof, whose answer is one function plus four verified hypotheses; part 3 is a proof of instability plus one sentence of interpretation.

Rung 2

For the equilibria, substitute one equation into the other and use that \(1 + x^8 > 0\). For the Lyapunov function, do not be clever: the simplest positive definite function on the plane is the one to try first.

Rung 3

\(\dot V = 2x\dot x + 2y\dot y\); the linear parts cancel, exactly as they did in the deck's “When linearisation lies”, and what survives is a sum of fourth powers. For the unstable case, \(V\) increasing is not by itself instability — you must show the orbit leaves a fixed ball, so bound \(\dot V\) below on a compact annulus.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\dot V = 2x(-y-x^3) + 2y(x-y^3) = -2(x^4+y^4)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The linearisation is a centre for both systems, so it decides nothing. The radial derivative decides everything, and \(V = x^2+y^2\) computes it.

Working.

  1. Equilibria: \(-y = x^3\) and \(x = y^3\) give \(x = (-x^3)^3 = -x^9\), i.e. \(x(1 + x^8) = 0\), so \(x = 0\) and then \(y = 0\): the origin only. The Jacobian is \(\begin{pmatrix}-3x^2 & -1\\ 1 & -3y^2\end{pmatrix}\), which at the origin is \(\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\): \(\tau = 0\), \(\Delta = 1\), a centre. Both eigenvalues are \(\pm i\), purely imaginary, so the equilibrium is not hyperbolic and Hartman–Grobman is silent.
  2. Take \(V(x,y) = x^2 + y^2\) on \(N = \mathbb{R}^2\). Hypotheses, one at a time: \(V\) is \(C^1\); \(V(0,0) = 0\); \(V > 0\) elsewhere; and \[\dot V = 2x(-y - x^3) + 2y(x - y^3) = -2xy + 2xy - 2x^4 - 2y^4 = -2(x^4+y^4),\] which is \(< 0\) for every \((x,y) \ne (0,0)\). Lyapunov's theorem therefore gives asymptotic stability. Since \(\dot V < 0\) off the origin on the whole plane and every sublevel set \(\{V \le c\}\) is compact, the argument runs from any initial point: the origin is globally asymptotically stable.

  3. For \(\dot x = -y + x^3\), \(\dot y = x + y^3\) the Jacobian at the origin is again \(\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\). Now \(\dot V = +2(x^4+y^4) > 0\) off the origin. Instability: fix a small \(\varepsilon\) and a starting point with \(a := V(\mathbf x_0) > 0\) inside \(B_\varepsilon\). While the orbit remains in \(B_\varepsilon\) it stays in the compact set \(\{a \le V \le \varepsilon^2\}\), on which \(\dot V \ge k > 0\) by continuity and compactness; so \(V \ge a + kt\), which exceeds \(\varepsilon^2\) in finite time. The orbit must leave \(B_\varepsilon\), and it does so however small \(a\) was: the origin is unstable.

    What the pair proves. Two systems with identical linearisations, one with a globally asymptotically stable origin and one with an unstable origin. So at a non-hyperbolic equilibrium the linearisation carries no information about stability whatsoever — not "weak" information, none. The hypothesis in Hartman–Grobman is not an artefact of its proof.

Check. Dimensions: \(V\) has the dimensions of a squared length, \(\dot V\) of (length\(^4\))/time, so the cubic terms carry the compensating length\(^{-2}\)time\(^{-1}\) — the systems are already written in dimensionless variables. Limit: drop the cubic terms and \(\dot V = 0\), the linear centre, consistent. Number: from \(\mathbf x_0 = (1,0)\) the first system has \(\dot V = -2\) initially, so \(V\) falls from \(1\) at rate \(2\) — a fast start and, since \(\dot V \sim -2V^2\) near the axes, a slow \(t^{-1}\)-like tail, which is not exponential and so not something a linearisation could have predicted.

P5 A spring with quadratic damping medium

exercises: LaSalle's invariance principle, non-smooth damping, basins

A mass on a hardening spring in a fluid obeys \(\ddot x + \dot x|\dot x| + x^3 = 0\).

  1. Write it as a planar system and find all its equilibria.
  2. Show that \(V = \tfrac12 v^2 + \tfrac14 x^4\) has \(\dot V = -|v|^3\), and explain why Lyapunov's asymptotic-stability test alone does not apply.
  3. Use LaSalle on a suitable compact forward invariant set to prove that every solution converges to the origin, and state the basin you obtain.
  4. Why can this argument not be run on the damped pendulum to get the whole cylinder as the basin?
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1: one equilibrium. Part 2: one identity and one sentence about a hypothesis. Part 3: a set \(\Omega\), a set \(E\), a set \(M\), a conclusion. Part 4: a comparison, not a computation.

Rung 2

\(v|v|\) is differentiable with derivative \(2|v|\), so Picard–Lindelöf still applies — say so before using uniqueness anywhere. Then compute \(\dot V\) with the orbital-derivative formula.

Rung 3

For LaSalle you need \(\Omega\) compact and forward invariant: sublevel sets of \(V\) are both, and here they are bounded because \(V\) grows in every direction. Finding \(M\) is the standard two-line argument — assume \(v \equiv 0\) on a whole orbit and differentiate.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\dot V = v\dot v + x^3\dot x = v(-v|v| - x^3) + x^3 v = -|v|^3\).

Worked solution

Idea. Energy again, but \(\dot V\) vanishes on a whole line, so Lyapunov's asymptotic test fails and LaSalle is the right instrument. The potential grows without bound, so the sublevel sets are compact and the basin is everything.

Working.

  1. \(\dot x = v\), \(\dot v = -v|v| - x^3\). The right-hand side is \(C^1\) (the derivative of \(v|v|\) is \(2|v|\), continuous), so Picard–Lindelöf applies. Equilibria need \(v = 0\) and \(x^3 = 0\): the origin alone.
  2. \(\dot V = v\dot v + x^3\dot x = v(-v|v| - x^3) + x^3 v = -v^2|v| = -|v|^3 \le 0\). Lyapunov's asymptotic half requires \(\dot V < 0\) on a punctured neighbourhood, but \(\dot V = 0\) on the entire line \(\{v = 0\}\), which meets every neighbourhood of the origin. So that test gives stability only.
  3. Fix \(c > 0\) and let \(\Omega = \{V \le c\}\). It is closed, and bounded because \(V \ge \tfrac14x^4\) and \(V \ge \tfrac12 v^2\) force \(|x| \le (4c)^{1/4}\), \(|v| \le \sqrt{2c}\); hence compact. It is forward invariant since \(\dot V \le 0\). Now \(E = \{v = 0\} \cap \Omega\). If a solution lies in \(E\) for all \(t\), then \(v \equiv 0\), so \(\dot v \equiv 0\), and \(\dot v = -0 - x^3\) forces \(x \equiv 0\). So \(M = \{(0,0)\}\), and LaSalle gives \(\varphi_t(\mathbf x_0) \to (0,0)\) for every \(\mathbf x_0 \in \Omega\). Since \(c\) was arbitrary and every point of the plane lies in some \(\Omega\), the basin is all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\): the origin is globally asymptotically stable.
  4. Because the pendulum's \(M\) is bigger. Running the same argument on the cylinder with \(\Omega = \{V \le c\}\), \(c > 2\), the set is still compact and forward invariant and \(E\) is still \(\{\omega = 0\}\); but now \(\dot\omega = -\sin\theta\) vanishes at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\), so \(M = \{(0,0), (\pi,0)\}\) and the conclusion is only that every orbit approaches the pair. That is the truth: the saddle's stable manifold really does end upright, so the hanging position's basin is not the whole cylinder. Taking \(c < 2\) excludes the saddle and recovers the deck's result.

Check. Dimensions: \(v|v|\) is an acceleration, so the damping coefficient hidden in it carries \(\mathsf{L}^{-1}\); \(V\) is then an energy per unit mass, and \(\dot V = -|v|^3\) is a power per unit mass. Limit: replace \(v|v|\) by \(\gamma v\) and \(\dot V = -\gamma v^2\), the linear case, with the same conclusion by the same argument. Number: starting from rest at \(x = 2\), \(V = \tfrac14\cdot16 = 4\), so the motion is forever confined to \(|x| \le (16)^{1/4} = 2\) and \(|v| \le \sqrt 8 = 2.8284\).

P6 Van der Pol has a limit cycle hard

exercises: building an explicit trapping region, Poincaré–Bendixson

The Van der Pol oscillator \(\ddot x - (1-x^2)\dot x + x = 0\) is equivalent to the Liénard system \[\dot x = y - F(x), \qquad \dot y = -x, \qquad F(x) = \tfrac13 x^3 - x .\]

  1. Verify the equivalence, and show the origin is the only equilibrium and is an unstable spiral.
  2. With \(W = \tfrac12(x^2+y^2)\), show \(\dot W = x^2(1 - \tfrac13 x^2)\), and deduce that \(\{x^2 + y^2 \ge 1\}\) is forward invariant.
  3. Let \(K\) be the hexagon with vertices \((0, \tfrac{52}{3})\), \((4, \tfrac{52}{3})\), \((4, 0)\), \((0, -\tfrac{52}{3})\), \((-4, -\tfrac{52}{3})\), \((-4, 0)\). Prove that no orbit leaves \(K\). (Use the symmetry \((x,y)\mapsto(-x,-y)\) to halve the work; three edges then remain, together with the boundary points at which the field is tangent rather than strictly inward — handle those separately.)
  4. Conclude that the Van der Pol equation has a periodic orbit, without computing it. Where exactly in your argument was two-dimensionality used?
Hints · 5 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a compact set — an annulus-shaped region between an explicit circle and an explicit hexagon — plus the sentence "Poincaré–Bendixson applies". No formula for the cycle exists.

Rung 2

Part 1: differentiate \(\dot x = y - F(x)\) once and substitute \(\dot y\). Part 2: compute \(\dot W\) and notice that the \(xy\) terms cancel, leaving \(-xF(x)\), which is \(\ge 0\) precisely where \(|x| \le \sqrt3\).

Rung 3

For part 2 do not argue "the field points outward on the circle" — at \((0,\pm r)\) it does not. Argue instead that \(W\) cannot decrease while the orbit is inside the disc, and look at a would-be last crossing time.

Rung 4

For each edge of the hexagon write the constraint as \(g \le 0\) with \(g\) affine, and compute \(\dot g\) from the system. Two edges give \(\dot g = -x\) and \(\dot g = y - F(4)\) immediately. The slanted edge gives a cubic in \(x\); maximise it by setting its derivative to zero, and the maximum is comfortably negative.

Rung 5 — the first line

\(\dot W = x\dot x + y\dot y = x\big(y - F(x)\big) + y(-x) = -xF(x) = x^2\big(1 - \tfrac13x^2\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. An inner boundary from the energy \(W\), an outer boundary from three straight edges chosen so that the field crosses each inwards, and Poincaré–Bendixson applied to the annulus between them.

Working.

  1. Differentiating \(\dot x = y - F(x)\) and using \(\dot y = -x\): \(\ddot x = \dot y - F'(x)\dot x = -x - (x^2-1)\dot x\), i.e. \(\ddot x - (1-x^2)\dot x + x = 0\). Equilibria: \(\dot y = 0\) gives \(x = 0\), and then \(\dot x = 0\) gives \(y = F(0) = 0\) — the origin only. Its Jacobian is \(\begin{pmatrix}-F'(x) & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}_{(0,0)} = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}\), so \(\tau = 1\), \(\Delta = 1\), \(\tau^2 - 4\Delta = -3 < 0\): an unstable spiral, eigenvalues \(0.5 \pm 0.8660\,i\).
  2. \(\dot W = x\dot x + y\dot y = x\big(y - F(x)\big) - xy = -xF(x) = -x\big(\tfrac13x^3 - x\big) = x^2\big(1 - \tfrac13x^2\big)\). On the closed unit disc \(|x| \le 1 < \sqrt3\), so \(\dot W \ge 0\) there. Suppose an orbit had \(W(t_0) \ge \tfrac12\) and \(W(t_1) < \tfrac12\) for some \(t_1 > t_0\). Let \(t_2\) be the last time before \(t_1\) with \(W = \tfrac12\); on \([t_2, t_1]\) the orbit lies in the disc \(\{W \le \tfrac12\}\), where \(\dot W \ge 0\), so \(W(t_1) \ge W(t_2) = \tfrac12\) — a contradiction. Hence \(\{x^2+y^2 \ge 1\}\) is forward invariant.

  3. Write \(c = F(4) = \tfrac{64}{3} - 4 = \tfrac{52}{3}\) and \(s = c/4 = \tfrac{13}{3}\). The field satisfies \(f(-x,-y) = -f(x,y)\) (because \(F\) is odd), so the symmetry \(\sigma(x,y) = (-x,-y)\) maps orbits to orbits, and \(K\) is \(\sigma\)-invariant: it suffices to check three edges.

    • Edge A, \(y = c\), \(0 \le x \le 4\). With \(g = y - c\), \(\dot g = \dot y = -x \le 0\), strictly for \(x > 0\).
    • Edge B, \(x = 4\), \(0 \le y \le c\). With \(g = x - 4\), \(\dot g = \dot x = y - F(4) = y - c \le 0\), strictly for \(y < c\).
    • Edge C, the segment from \((4,0)\) to \((0,-c)\), i.e. \(y = sx - c\) with \(0 \le x \le 4\); the region lies above it, \(g = sx - c - y\). Then \[\dot g = s\dot x - \dot y = s\big(y - F(x)\big) + x = \underbrace{s\big(sx - c - \tfrac13x^3 + x\big) + x}_{=:h(x)} .\] \(h'(x) = s^2 + s + 1 - sx^2\) vanishes at \(x^\ast = \sqrt{(s^2+s+1)/s} = \sqrt{217/39} = 2.3588\), the only maximum on \([0,4]\), and \(h(x^\ast) = -sc + x^\ast(s^2+s+1) - \tfrac13 s (x^\ast)^3 = -75.111 + 56.874 - 18.958 = -37.195 < 0\). (Endpoints, for reassurance: \(h(0) = -sc = -75.111\) and \(h(4) = -71.111\).) So \(\dot g < 0\) strictly along the whole edge.

    Tangential points. Two boundary points have \(\dot g = 0\) for one of their active constraints, and by the symmetry it is enough to treat one of each kind. At \((0, c)\), edge A is tangent; but there \(\dot x = c - F(0) = c > 0\), so \(x(t) > 0\) immediately afterwards and hence \(\dot y = -x < 0\): the orbit moves strictly below \(y = c\). At the corner \((4, c)\), edge B is tangent (\(\dot x = c - c = 0\)); there \(\ddot x = \dot y - F'(x)\dot x = -4 - 0 < 0\), so \(x\) strictly decreases, and edge A is strictly inward (\(\dot y = -4 < 0\)). In both cases the orbit enters the interior. Every other boundary point has every active constraint strictly decreasing, so a first exit time cannot exist: at such a time some \(g\) would have to be non-decreasing. Hence \(K\) is forward invariant.

  4. Let \(A = K \cap \{x^2+y^2 \ge 1\}\). It is compact (closed subset of the compact \(K\)), forward invariant (an intersection of two forward invariant sets), non-empty, and contains no equilibrium, since the only equilibrium is the origin and the unit disc has been removed — note the disc is inside \(K\), because \(K\) is convex and the origin's distances to the three edge lines are \(4\), \(c = 17.3333\) and \(c/\sqrt{s^2+1} = 3.8976\), all greater than \(1\). Any orbit starting in \(A\) therefore stays in \(A\) forever, and by Poincaré–Bendixson its \(\omega\)-limit set is a periodic orbit contained in \(A\). So the Van der Pol equation has a periodic solution, and we have not computed it.

    Where the plane was used: only in Poincaré–Bendixson, whose proof needs the Jordan curve theorem. Steps 1–3 are valid in any dimension and prove only that the orbits are trapped — which in \(\mathbb{R}^3\) would allow chaos instead of a cycle.

Check. Dimensions: the Liénard variables are already dimensionless; \(W\) has the dimensions of \(x^2\) and \(\dot W\) of \(x^2\) per unit time, matching \(x^2(1-x^2/3)\) once the \(1\) is read as an inverse time. Limit: the sign change of \(\dot W\) at \(|x| = \sqrt3 = 1.7321\) predicts a cycle crossing that value; the true Van der Pol cycle at \(\mu = 1\) has maximum \(|x| = 2.0086\), comfortably outside, as the inequality only requires. Number: the region \(A\) is enormous compared with the cycle — \(K\) reaches \(|y| = 17.33\) — which is the usual price of an explicit trapping region: it proves existence, not location.

P7 Index arithmetic medium

exercises: the index of a closed curve, the Poincaré index theorem, non-existence of cycles

  1. For \(f(x,y) = (x^2 - y^2,\ 2xy)\), compute the index of the circle of radius \(\rho\) about the origin directly from the definition, for any \(\rho > 0\). (Substitute \(x = \rho\cos\phi\), \(y = \rho\sin\phi\) and find the angle of \(f\).)
  2. Deduce that the origin is not a hyperbolic equilibrium of \(\dot{\mathbf x} = f(\mathbf x)\), without computing \(Df\). Then compute \(Df(0,0)\) and check.
  3. A planar system has exactly three equilibria inside a closed curve \(\Gamma\): two spiral sinks and one saddle. What is \(I_f(\Gamma)\)? Can \(\Gamma\) be a periodic orbit?
  4. Prove: a periodic orbit of a planar system cannot enclose exactly one equilibrium if that equilibrium is a saddle.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an integer. Parts 2 and 4 are one-line arguments by contradiction. Part 3 is an integer and a yes/no with a caveat.

Rung 2

Substitute \(x = \rho\cos\phi\), \(y = \rho\sin\phi\) and look for a double-angle formula in what comes out. The index is then read off without any integration.

Rung 3

For parts 2 and 4, the only facts needed are: a hyperbolic equilibrium has index \(\operatorname{sign}\det Df\), and a periodic orbit has index \(+1\). Both are on the deck's “Indices of equilibria, and what they count”.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(x^2 - y^2 = \rho^2\cos2\phi\) and \(2xy = \rho^2\sin2\phi\).

Worked solution

Idea. The index is an angle count: write the field in polar form and read off how fast its angle turns.

Working.

  1. Put \(x = \rho\cos\phi\), \(y = \rho\sin\phi\). Then \(x^2 - y^2 = \rho^2\cos 2\phi\) and \(2xy = \rho^2\sin 2\phi\), so \(f = \rho^2(\cos2\phi, \sin2\phi)\): the angle of \(f\) is \(\vartheta = 2\phi\). As \(\phi\) runs from \(0\) to \(2\pi\), \(\vartheta\) increases by \(4\pi\), so \(I_f = 4\pi/2\pi = 2\), for every \(\rho > 0\). (\(f\) is the map \(z \mapsto z^2\) in complex notation, and its index is its degree.)
  2. A hyperbolic equilibrium has \(\operatorname{ind} = \operatorname{sign}\det Df \in \{+1,-1\}\), and \(2\) is neither; so the origin is not hyperbolic. Indeed every entry of \(Df = \begin{pmatrix}2x & -2y\\ 2y & 2x\end{pmatrix}\) vanishes at the origin, so \(Df(0,0) = 0\), which is as non-hyperbolic as a matrix can be.
  3. By the Poincaré index theorem, \(I_f(\Gamma) = (+1) + (+1) + (-1) = +1\). That is consistent with \(\Gamma\) being a periodic orbit, since the Umlaufsatz requires index \(+1\) — so the index test does not rule it out. It does not prove one exists either: the index is a necessary condition, never a sufficient one.
  4. Suppose \(\Gamma\) is a periodic orbit enclosing exactly one equilibrium, a saddle. The Umlaufsatz gives \(I_f(\Gamma) = +1\); the index theorem gives \(I_f(\Gamma) = \operatorname{ind}(\text{saddle}) = -1\). Since \(+1 \ne -1\), no such \(\Gamma\) exists. \(\square\)

Check. Consistency: a field whose index is \(2\) must, under any small perturbation that makes the equilibria hyperbolic, break into equilibria whose indices sum to \(2\) — for instance two sinks, or two spirals. Trying \(f_\epsilon = (x^2-y^2-\epsilon,\ 2xy)\) gives equilibria at \((\pm\sqrt\epsilon, 0)\); their determinants are \(4x^2 = 4\epsilon > 0\), so each has index \(+1\) and the total is \(2\), as the homotopy invariance of the index on a large circle demands.

P8 A gradient system, drawn routine

exercises: gradient flows, classification, index bookkeeping

Let \(V(x,y) = x^2(x-1)^2 + y^2\) and consider \(\dot x = -\partial_x V,\ \dot y = -\partial_y V\).

  1. Find all equilibria and classify each from the Jacobian.
  2. Compute the index of each, check the total against what a large circle must give, and state why the system can have no periodic orbit — twice, once from the index and once from \(\dot V\).
  3. Sketch the phase portrait, marking the two basins and the curve that separates them.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Three equilibria, three Jacobians (all diagonal), three indices, one separating curve.

Rung 2

Differentiate \(V\) once in each variable and factorise \(V_x\) completely — the three roots are the three equilibria's \(x\)-coordinates.

Rung 3

The Jacobian is minus the Hessian, and here the Hessian is diagonal, so no eigenvalue computation is needed. For the separatrix, notice that the two equations do not talk to each other.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(V_x = 2x(x-1)^2 + 2x^2(x-1) = 2x(x-1)(2x-1)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The system is \(\dot x = -V_x\), \(\dot y = -V_y\), and here it decouples, so everything reduces to a one-dimensional picture times a decaying \(y\).

Working.

  1. \(V_x = 2x(x-1)^2 + 2x^2(x-1) = 2x(x-1)(2x-1)\) and \(V_y = 2y\). So \[\dot x = -2x(x-1)(2x-1), \qquad \dot y = -2y,\] with equilibria \((0,0)\), \((\tfrac12, 0)\), \((1,0)\). The Jacobian is the diagonal matrix \(\operatorname{diag}(-(12x^2-12x+2),\ -2)\), giving

    • \((0,0)\): \(\operatorname{diag}(-2,-2)\) — a stable star node;
    • \((\tfrac12,0)\): \(\operatorname{diag}(+1,-2)\) — a saddle;
    • \((1,0)\): \(\operatorname{diag}(-2,-2)\) — a stable star node.
  2. Indices \(\operatorname{sign}\det\): \(+1\), \(-1\), \(+1\), total \(+1\). A large circle must give \(+1\), and it does: for large \(|x|\) the field points inward along \(x\) (\(\dot x \approx -4x^3\)) and along \(y\), and tracing the angle of \(f\) once round a circle of radius \(R\) gives one full turn. No periodic orbit: (i) a cycle would need to enclose equilibria of total index \(+1\), which is possible only by enclosing a sink alone or all three — but (ii) along any orbit \(\dot V = -\big(V_x^2 + V_y^2\big) \le 0\) with equality only at equilibria, so \(0 = \oint\dot V\,\mathrm{d}t < 0\) for a non-constant periodic orbit, a contradiction. The second argument is decisive on its own; the first shows how far index bookkeeping gets you without it.
  3. The portrait: \(y\) decays to \(0\) exponentially everywhere, and the \(x\)-dynamics is one-dimensional with stable points \(0\) and \(1\) and an unstable point \(\tfrac12\). The separatrix is the vertical line \(x = \tfrac12\) — the saddle's stable manifold, which here is exact rather than approximate because the equations decouple. Basins: \(\{x < \tfrac12\}\) drains to \((0,0)\), \(\{x > \tfrac12\}\) to \((1,0)\). Orbits cross the level curves of \(V\) at right angles, as in any gradient system.

Check. \(V\) has a double well with minima \(V = 0\) at \(x = 0, 1\) and a local maximum \(V(\tfrac12, 0) = \tfrac1{16} = 0.0625\) between them, and the equilibria of the flow are exactly the critical points of \(V\) — as they must be, since \(\nabla V = 0\) is the equilibrium condition. Limit: drop the \(y\) equation and the remaining one-dimensional flow has the stability pattern stable–unstable–stable that any smooth double well forces.

P9 A pitchfork in disguise medium

exercises: locating bifurcations, identifying a normal form, sub- versus supercritical

Consider the one-dimensional family \(\dot x = \mu x - \sin x\) on \((-\pi, \pi)\), with \(\mu > 0\).

  1. Show \(x = 0\) is an equilibrium for every \(\mu\), and find for which \(\mu\) it is stable.
  2. Show that a symmetric pair of further equilibria exists for \(\mu\) on one side of \(\mu = 1\) only, and determine which side. Identify the bifurcation at \(\mu = 1\) and say whether it is supercritical or subcritical.
  3. Expand \(\sin x\) to third order to obtain the approximate branch \(x \approx \pm\sqrt{6(1-\mu)}\). At \(\mu = 0.9\) compare it with the exact root to four decimal places.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

A threshold value of \(\mu\), a side, a word (super- or subcritical), and one four-figure comparison.

Rung 2

Do not solve \(\mu x = \sin x\). Divide by \(x\) and ask for which \(\mu\) the horizontal line \(\mu\) meets the graph of \(\sin x / x\) on \((0,\pi)\).

Rung 3

"Subcritical" means the new branches live on the side of the threshold where the trivial branch is stable; check the stability of the outer branches from the sign of \(f'(x^\ast) = \mu - \cos x^\ast\), substituting \(\mu = \sin x^\ast/x^\ast\).

Rung 4 — the first line

\(f'(0) = \mu - 1\), so the origin changes stability exactly at \(\mu = 1\).

Worked solution

Idea. Solve \(\mu x = \sin x\) by comparing \(\sin x / x\) with \(\mu\); the symmetry \(x \mapsto -x\) is what makes the bifurcation a pitchfork rather than a saddle-node.

Working.

  1. \(f(x,\mu) = \mu x - \sin x\) vanishes at \(x = 0\) for every \(\mu\). \(f'(x) = \mu - \cos x\), so \(f'(0) = \mu - 1\): the origin is stable for \(\mu < 1\) and unstable for \(\mu > 1\).
  2. Non-zero equilibria satisfy \(\mu = \sin x / x\). On \((0,\pi)\) the function \(\sin x/x\) is strictly decreasing from \(1\) (as \(x \to 0^+\)) to \(0\), so there is exactly one positive root when \(0 < \mu < 1\) and none when \(\mu \ge 1\); the odd symmetry of \(f\) gives the mirror root. So the pair exists below the threshold, on the side where the origin is stable: a subcritical pitchfork at \(\mu = 1\). The outer branches are unstable: at a root \(x^\ast \in (0,\pi)\), \(f'(x^\ast) = \mu - \cos x^\ast = \frac{\sin x^\ast - x^\ast\cos x^\ast}{x^\ast} > 0\), since \(\tan x^\ast > x^\ast\) on \((0,\pi/2)\) and \(\cos x^\ast < 0\) on \([\pi/2, \pi)\).
  3. \(\mu x = x - x^3/6 + O(x^5)\) gives \(x^2 = 6(1-\mu)\), i.e. \(x \approx \pm\sqrt{6(1-\mu)}\). At \(\mu = 0.9\): the approximation is \(\sqrt{0.6} = 0.7746\), the exact root of \(0.9x = \sin x\) is \(0.7867\). The error is \(0.0121\), about \(1.5\%\). Keeping the next term, \(\mu x = x - x^3/6 + x^5/120\) gives \(x^2 = 6(1-\mu) + x^4/20\), a relative correction of \(x^2/40 = 0.0155\) — which is the \(1.5\%\) observed, so the discrepancy is exactly the \(O(x^5)\) truncation and nothing else.

Check. Limit \(\mu \to 1^-\): both branches tend to \(0\) like \(\sqrt{6(1-\mu)}\), the square-root law of every pitchfork. Limit \(\mu \to 0^+\): the roots tend to \(\pm\pi\), where \(\sin x/x \to 0\) — correct, since \(\dot x = -\sin x\) has equilibria at \(0\) and \(\pm\pi\). Sanity: \(\sin(0.7867)/0.7867 = 0.9000\), so the quoted root really does solve the equation at \(\mu = 0.9\).

P10 A Hopf bifurcation, with an amplitude medium

exercises: the Hopf normal form, polar reduction, amplitude scaling

For \(\mu \in \mathbb{R}\), \[\dot x = \mu x - y - x(x^2+y^2), \qquad \dot y = x + \mu y - y(x^2+y^2).\]

  1. Show that in polar coordinates \(\dot r = \mu r - r^3\) and \(\dot\theta = 1\), and classify the origin for \(\mu < 0\), \(\mu = 0\) and \(\mu > 0\).
  2. Show that for \(\mu > 0\) there is exactly one periodic orbit, give its radius and period, and prove it is attracting. Evaluate both at \(\mu = 0.36\).
  3. Replace \(-x(x^2+y^2)\) and \(-y(x^2+y^2)\) by \(+x(x^2+y^2)\) and \(+y(x^2+y^2)\). Show the cycle now exists for \(\mu < 0\) and is repelling, and say what an experimenter slowly increasing \(\mu\) through zero would observe in each of the two cases.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Two scalar equations in \((r, \theta)\), then a radius, a period, and two sentences about what an experiment shows.

Rung 2

\(r\dot r = x\dot x + y\dot y\) and \(r^2\dot\theta = x\dot y - y\dot x\). Both computations have cancelling cross terms, as in the deck's “When linearisation lies” and “Periodic orbits and limit cycles”.

Rung 3

Uniqueness of the cycle is not "the only root of a cubic" — it is that \(r\) is strictly monotone wherever \(\dot r \ne 0\), so no other orbit can return to its starting radius. Attraction is the sign of \(\partial_r\dot r\) at the cycle.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(r\dot r = \mu(x^2+y^2) - (x^2+y^2)^2 = \mu r^2 - r^4\).

Worked solution

Idea. The system is the Hopf normal form in disguise; polar coordinates separate it completely.

Working.

  1. \(r\dot r = x\dot x + y\dot y = \mu(x^2+y^2) - (x^2+y^2)^2 = \mu r^2 - r^4\), so \(\dot r = \mu r - r^3\); and \(r^2\dot\theta = x\dot y - y\dot x = x^2 + y^2 = r^2\), so \(\dot\theta = 1\) (the cross terms cancel in both computations). At the origin the Jacobian is \(\begin{pmatrix}\mu & -1\\ 1 & \mu\end{pmatrix}\), eigenvalues \(\mu \pm i\): a stable spiral for \(\mu < 0\), a centre in the linearisation for \(\mu = 0\) — where the cubic decides, and \(\dot r = -r^3 < 0\) makes it an asymptotically stable non-hyperbolic sink — and an unstable spiral for \(\mu > 0\).
  2. For \(\mu > 0\), \(\dot r = 0\) with \(r > 0\) forces \(r = \sqrt\mu\), so the circle \(r = \sqrt\mu\) is an orbit and it is the only periodic one: any periodic orbit must have \(r\) returning to its initial value, and \(r\) is strictly monotone off \(r = \sqrt\mu\). The period is \(2\pi\), since \(\dot\theta = 1\). It attracts because \(\partial_r(\mu r - r^3)\big|_{r=\sqrt\mu} = \mu - 3\mu = -2\mu < 0\), so \(\rho = r - \sqrt\mu\) obeys \(\dot\rho = -2\mu\rho + O(\rho^2)\). At \(\mu = 0.36\): radius \(\sqrt{0.36} = 0.6\) exactly, period \(2\pi = 6.2832\).
  3. With the signs flipped, \(\dot r = \mu r + r^3\). For \(\mu < 0\) the cycle is at \(r = \sqrt{-\mu}\), and \(\partial_r(\mu r + r^3) = \mu + 3r^2 = -2\mu > 0\): repelling. It is the boundary of the basin of the (still stable) origin, and it shrinks to nothing as \(\mu \to 0^-\). What the experimenter sees. Supercritical: nothing until \(\mu = 0\), then a smooth oscillation whose amplitude grows as \(\sqrt\mu\) — reversible, no hysteresis. Subcritical: nothing until \(\mu = 0\), then a sudden jump to whatever large-amplitude behaviour exists elsewhere in the system, with no small oscillation in between, and usually hysteresis on the way back down.

Check. Dimensions: \(\mu\) is an inverse time (it is a growth rate), so the cubic term must be \(k r^3\) with \([k] = \mathsf{T}^{-1}\mathsf{L}^{-2}\), and the cycle radius \(\sqrt{\mu/k}\) is then a length, as an amplitude must be. Writing \(k = 1\), as the statement does, is a choice of length unit. Limit \(\mu \to 0^+\): the cycle radius \(\to 0\) and its period stays \(2\pi\) — the cycle is born from the equilibrium, not from infinity. Number: at \(\mu = 0.36\) an orbit starting at \(r_0 = 0.1\) has \(\dot r = 0.036 - 0.001 = 0.035 > 0\), heading outward toward \(0.6\), as claimed.

P11 Gradient systems cannot spiral hard

exercises: proof from structure, the spectral theorem, non-existence arguments

Let \(V : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\) be \(C^2\) and let \(\dot{\mathbf x} = -\nabla V(\mathbf x)\), meaning \(\dot x_i = -\partial_i V\). Prove all four:

  1. \(\dot V \le 0\), with equality exactly at the equilibria.
  2. There is no periodic orbit other than an equilibrium.
  3. There is no orbit joining an equilibrium to itself in infinite time — no homoclinic orbit — and no cycle of orbits joining distinct equilibria.
  4. At every equilibrium, the Jacobian is symmetric, hence its eigenvalues are real. Conclude that a gradient system has no spirals and no centres, and that in the plane its hyperbolic equilibria are only saddles and nodes.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Four proofs. Three of them use the same identity; the fourth uses a theorem from linear algebra you already have.

Rung 2

The identity is \(\dot V = -|\nabla V|^2\). Parts 2 and 3 are both "a strictly decreasing function cannot return to a value it has left", applied once to a period and once to a pair of limits.

Rung 3

For part 4, write down the Jacobian of the map \(\mathbf x \mapsto -\nabla V\) and ask what kind of matrix it is. Symmetry of the mixed partials is Clairaut's theorem (unit 03); after that it is the spectral theorem, and complex eigenvalues become impossible.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\dot V = \sum_i \partial_i V\,\dot x_i = -\sum_i(\partial_i V)^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. Everything follows from one identity, \(\dot V = -|\nabla V|^2\), except the last part, which is the spectral theorem.

Working.

  1. Along a solution, \(\dot V = \sum_i \partial_i V \cdot \dot x_i = -\sum_i (\partial_i V)^2 = -|\nabla V|^2 \le 0\), with equality exactly when \(\nabla V(\mathbf x) = 0\), which is exactly the equilibrium condition.
  2. If \(\mathbf x(\cdot)\) is \(T\)-periodic, then \(0 = V(\mathbf x(T)) - V(\mathbf x(0)) = \int_0^T \dot V\,\mathrm{d}t = -\int_0^T|\nabla V|^2\,\mathrm{d}t \le 0\), so the integrand vanishes identically, so \(\nabla V \equiv 0\) along the orbit and the orbit is a single equilibrium. \(\square\)
  3. Suppose an orbit is homoclinic to \(\mathbf p\): \(\mathbf x(t) \to \mathbf p\) as \(t \to \pm\infty\), with \(\mathbf x(t) \ne \mathbf p\). Then \(V(\mathbf x(t))\) is strictly decreasing (part 1, since \(\nabla V \ne 0\) along a non-constant orbit), while continuity gives \(\lim_{t\to-\infty}V(\mathbf x(t)) = V(\mathbf p) = \lim_{t\to+\infty}V(\mathbf x(t))\). A strictly decreasing function cannot have equal limits at \(\pm\infty\). For a cycle of heteroclinic orbits \(\mathbf p_1 \to \mathbf p_2 \to \dots \to \mathbf p_k \to \mathbf p_1\) the same monotonicity gives \(V(\mathbf p_1) > V(\mathbf p_2) > \dots > V(\mathbf p_k) > V(\mathbf p_1)\), which is impossible. \(\square\)
  4. The Jacobian of \(-\nabla V\) is \(-\big(\partial_i\partial_j V\big)\), the negative Hessian. For \(V \in C^2\) the mixed partials commute — Clairaut's theorem, proved in unit 03 — so the matrix is symmetric, and the spectral theorem (linear algebra) gives an orthonormal eigenbasis with real eigenvalues. A spiral needs a non-real pair and a centre needs a purely imaginary one, so neither can occur. In the plane a hyperbolic equilibrium then has two real non-zero eigenvalues: same sign gives a node, opposite signs a saddle. \(\square\)

Check. Test on P8: \(V = x^2(x-1)^2 + y^2\) gave two star nodes and a saddle, with no spiral and no cycle — exactly the menu this problem says is available. Test on the pendulum: the damped pendulum has a spiral, so it is not a gradient system, however much it behaves like one; the deck called it gradient-like for precisely this reason. Limit: for \(V\) a positive definite quadratic form \(\tfrac12\mathbf x^\top P\mathbf x\), the flow is \(\dot{\mathbf x} = -P\mathbf x\) with \(P\) symmetric positive definite — a stable node in every eigendirection, and never a spiral.

P12 Reading \(\gamma\) off a photograph medium

exercises: the logarithmic decrement, inverting a classification, dimensions

A pendulum of length \(l = 1\) m and mass \(m = 0.2\) kg swings with small amplitude, and successive maxima of \(\theta\) are measured to be in the ratio \(0.1974\).

  1. From the linearisation at the hanging position, show that successive maxima of \(\theta\) are in the ratio \(e^{-\Lambda}\) with \(\Lambda = \gamma\pi/\sqrt{1-\gamma^2/4}\), the logarithmic decrement.
  2. Invert this to get \(\gamma = 2\Lambda/\sqrt{4\pi^2 + \Lambda^2}\), and compute \(\gamma\) to four decimal places.
  3. Recover the physical damping coefficient \(b\) in \(m l^2\ddot\theta + b\dot\theta + mgl\sin\theta = 0\), in N m s, and give the observed period in seconds. Take \(g = 9.81\) m s\(^{-2}\).
  4. Check your \(\gamma\) against the qualitative picture: is the motion a spiral or a node, and by what factor does the energy fall per period?
Hints · 5 rungs
Rung 1

A dimensionless \(\gamma\) to four decimals, then a \(b\) in N m s and a period in seconds.

Rung 2

Work with the linearisation at the hanging position, whose solution is on slide 14: an exponential times a cosine, with \(\alpha = -\gamma/2\) and \(\beta = \sqrt{1-\gamma^2/4}\). Successive maxima of \(\theta\) are one full period apart, not half of one.

Rung 3

At successive maxima the cosine factor takes the same value, so the ratio is purely the exponential. Then square the resulting relation to clear the square root before solving for \(\gamma\).

Rung 4

The physical damping is \(\gamma = b/(m\sqrt{gl^3})\), from the overview slide's note, and the time unit is \(\sqrt{l/g}\). Energy goes as the square of the amplitude for small oscillations.

Rung 5 — the first line

\(\theta_{n+1}/\theta_n = e^{-\gamma T/2}\) with \(T = 2\pi/\beta\), so \(\Lambda = \gamma\pi/\sqrt{1-\gamma^2/4}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Successive maxima of a decaying oscillation are one full period apart, so their ratio is \(e^{-(\gamma/2)T}\) with \(T = 2\pi/\beta\); inverting gives \(\gamma\).

Working.

  1. Small oscillations obey the linearisation \(\ddot\theta + \gamma\dot\theta + \theta = 0\), whose solutions are (deck, “Solving \(\dot{\boldsymbol\xi} = A\boldsymbol\xi\), all three cases”, with \(\alpha = -\gamma/2\) and \(\beta = \sqrt{1-\gamma^2/4}\)) \(\theta(t) = Ae^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\beta t + \phi)\). Maxima of \(\theta\) occur where \(\dot\theta = 0\) with \(\ddot\theta < 0\); differentiating, \(\dot\theta = 0\) requires \(\tan(\beta t + \phi) = -\gamma/(2\beta)\), whose solutions are spaced by \(\pi/\beta\) and alternate between maxima and minima. Successive maxima are therefore \(T = 2\pi/\beta\) apart, and at each the cosine factor repeats, so \[\frac{\theta_{n+1}}{\theta_n} = e^{-\gamma T/2} = e^{-\Lambda}, \qquad \Lambda = \frac{\gamma}{2}\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\beta} = \frac{\gamma\pi}{\sqrt{1-\gamma^2/4}} .\]
  2. Squaring, \(\Lambda^2(1 - \gamma^2/4) = \gamma^2\pi^2\), so \(\Lambda^2 = \gamma^2(\pi^2 + \Lambda^2/4)\) and \(\gamma = \Lambda/\sqrt{\pi^2 + \Lambda^2/4} = 2\Lambda/\sqrt{4\pi^2+\Lambda^2}\). With the measured ratio \(0.1974\), \(\Lambda = \ln(1/0.1974) = 1.6225\), so \[\gamma = \frac{2(1.6225)}{\sqrt{4\pi^2 + 1.6225^2}} = \frac{3.2450}{6.4893} = 0.5001 .\]
  3. The scaling of the deck's overview note is \(\gamma = b/(m\sqrt{gl^3})\), so \[b = \gamma\, m\sqrt{gl^3} = 0.5001 \times 0.2 \times \sqrt{9.81} = 0.3132 \ \text{N m s}.\] The period in dimensionless time is \(2\pi/\beta\) with \(\beta = \sqrt{1-\gamma^2/4} = 0.9682\), giving \(6.4893\) — which is \(\sqrt{4\pi^2+\Lambda^2}\) again, and not by accident: squaring the definition of \(\Lambda\) gives \(1-\gamma^2/4 = 4\pi^2/(4\pi^2+\Lambda^2)\), so \(2\pi/\beta = \sqrt{4\pi^2+\Lambda^2}\) exactly. Multiplying by the time unit \(\sqrt{l/g} = 0.3193\) s gives \(T = 2.0719\) s.
  4. \(\gamma = 0.5001 < 2\), so the equilibrium is a stable spiral and the motion oscillates — consistent with there being successive maxima to measure at all. Energy is quadratic in the amplitude for small oscillations, so it falls by \(0.1974^2 = 0.0390\) per period, a loss of \(96.1\%\) — a heavily damped pendulum, which is why only a handful of swings are visible.
