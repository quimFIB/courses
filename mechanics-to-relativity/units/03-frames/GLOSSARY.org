The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: Foucault's pendulum in the Panthéon — wire \(L = 67\) m,
latitude \(\lambda = 48.8462^\circ\) N, plane of swing turning once every
\(31.79\) h.

* Differentiating in several variables

- partial derivative :: The derivative of \(f\) along one coordinate line,
  \(\partial_i f(a) = \lim_{h\to0}\big(f(a+he_i)-f(a)\big)/h\). Existence of all
  of them says almost nothing on its own: \(xy/(x^2+y^2)\) has both at the
  origin and is not even continuous there. /Slide: Partials are not enough./
- directional derivative :: The same limit with an arbitrary unit vector \(u\) in
  place of \(e_i\). Existence for every \(u\), plus continuity, still does not
  give differentiability: \(x^3/(x^2+y^2)\) has \(D_ug(0) = u_1^3\), which is not
  linear in \(u\).
- total derivative (differential) :: The linear map
  \(Df(a):\mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m\) with
  \(f(a+h) = f(a) + Df(a)h + o(\lVert h\rVert)\): the linear map best
  approximating \(f\) near \(a\). Unique when it exists, and it is a
  *map*, not a matrix — the matrix needs a basis. /Slide: The derivative is a
  linear map./
- Jacobian matrix :: The matrix of \(Df(a)\) in the standard bases, with entries
  \(\partial_j f_i(a)\). That it exists and has those entries is a theorem about
  the total derivative, not the definition of it. /Slide: The Jacobian, and when
  partials suffice./
- continuously differentiable, \(C^1\) :: All partials exist near \(a\) and are
  continuous at \(a\). Sufficient for differentiability, strictly stronger than
  it, and the licence used silently for every map in this course.
- multivariable chain rule :: \(D(g\circ f)(a) = Dg(f(a)) \circ Df(a)\): the
  derivative of a composite is the composite of the derivatives. In components
  it is the \((i,k)\) entry of a matrix product and not worth memorising in that
  form. /Slide: The chain rule, once./
- Clairaut's theorem (Schwarz's theorem) :: If both mixed second partials exist
  near \(a\) and are continuous at \(a\), they are equal. Peano's
  \(xy(x^2-y^2)/(x^2+y^2)\) fails the hypothesis and has
  \(\partial_2\partial_1 f(0,0) = -1\), \(\partial_1\partial_2 f(0,0) = +1\).
  /Slide: Clairaut, and the function that breaks it./

* Curves

- vector-valued function :: A map \(\gamma : I \to \mathbb R^3\) from an
  interval, differentiable iff each component is. Its total derivative is
  \(t\mapsto t\dot\gamma\); the product rule holds for the dot product, the
  cross product and every other bilinear operation, with the order kept. /Slide:
  Vector-valued functions and two product rules./
- arc length :: \(s(t) = \int_a^t\lVert\dot\gamma\rVert\), so \(\dot s = v\). The
  one parameter a curve fixes by itself, which is why curvature is defined with
  respect to it. /Slide: Arc length and the unit tangent./
- unit-speed parametrisation :: \(\gamma\) reparametrised by \(s\); it exists
  wherever \(v > 0\), because then \(s(t)\) is strictly increasing and so
  invertible — unit 00's move, used for a different purpose.
- unit tangent :: \(\hat T = \vec v/v = \mathrm d\gamma/\mathrm ds\). Depends on
  the curve and the direction of travel, not on the speed.
- curvature :: \(\kappa = \lVert\hat T'\rVert\), the rate the direction of travel
  turns per unit distance travelled. \(\kappa = 1/\rho\) for a circle of radius
  \(\rho\), which is what earns \(\rho\) its name. /Slide: Curvature, and the
  frame a curve carries./
- radius of curvature :: \(\rho = 1/\kappa\): the radius of the osculating
  circle, the circle sharing the curve's point, tangent and curvature.
- Frenet–Serret frame :: The orthonormal right-handed basis
  \((\hat T, \hat N, \hat B)\) attached to each point of a curve with
  \(\kappa > 0\).
- principal normal :: \(\hat N = \hat T'/\kappa\), a unit vector perpendicular to
  \(\hat T\) — perpendicular because \(\lVert\hat T\rVert\) is constant — pointing
  towards the centre of the osculating circle.
- binormal :: \(\hat B = \hat T\times\hat N\), completing the frame.
- osculating plane :: The plane spanned by \(\hat T\) and \(\hat N\): the plane
  the curve is momentarily bending in. A curve is planar iff its torsion
  vanishes.
- torsion :: \(\tau = -\hat B'\cdot\hat N\), the rate at which the curve leaves
  its osculating plane. \(\tau \equiv 0\) iff the curve is planar. Sign
  conventions differ between books; here a right-handed helix has \(\tau > 0\).
  /Slide: The Frenet–Serret formulas./
- Frenet–Serret formulas :: \(\hat T' = \kappa\hat N\), \(\hat N' = -\kappa\hat
  T
  + \tau\hat B\), \(\hat B' = -\tau\hat N\). They say no more than: the matrix
    \(Q^{\!\top}Q'\) of an orthonormal moving frame is antisymmetric, so it has
    three free entries, and the definition of \(\hat N\) kills one of them.
- tangential and normal acceleration :: The two — and only two — parts of
  \(\vec a = \dot v\hat T + (v^2/\rho)\hat N\). Speeding up is tangential,
  turning is normal, and \(\vec a\cdot\hat B = 0\) always. /Slide: Acceleration
  has exactly two parts./
- centripetal acceleration :: The \(v^2/\rho\) term, a corollary of the Frenet
  decomposition rather than a postulate, and derived without assuming the path
  is a circle.
- terminal velocity :: The velocity at which drag balances gravity, found by
  setting \(\vec{\dot v} = 0\): \(m\vec g/b\) for linear drag,
  \(\sqrt{mg/b}\) straight down for quadratic. /Slide: Three dimensions are not
  three one-dimensional problems./
- Reynolds number :: \(\mathcal R = \rho_{\text{air}}vd/\mu\), dimensionless, and
  it decides which drag law applies: \(\mathcal R \lesssim 1\) linear (and the
  three components separate), \(\mathcal R \gtrsim 10^3\) quadratic (and they do
  not). A football at \(20\) m/s has \(\mathcal R \approx 3\times10^5\).

* Frames that turn

- frame :: An orthonormal right-handed basis of \(\mathbb R^3\) with an origin.
  /Slide: What a frame is, and what \(SO(3)\) is./
- inertial frame :: One in which \(m\vec a = \vec F\) holds with \(\vec F\) built
  only from real interactions. No global one exists — the laboratory turns with
  the Earth — and computing the size of that error is what this unit is for.
- special orthogonal group, \(SO(3)\) :: \(\{R : R^{\!\top}R = I,\ \det R = +1\}\),
  a group under matrix multiplication. Two frames sharing an origin are related
  by exactly one element of it, and \(x_{\text{in}} = Rx_{\text{rot}}\) is this
  unit's fixed convention.
- antisymmetric matrix :: \(A^{\!\top} = -A\). For a differentiable curve \(R(t)\)
  in \(SO(3)\), \(\Omega = R^{\!\top}\dot R\) is one — differentiate
  \(R^{\!\top}R = I\). /Slide: \(\Omega = R^{\!\top}\dot R\) is antisymmetric./
- \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\), so(3) :: The space of antisymmetric \(3\times3\) matrices, of
  dimension 3. That \(\Omega\) lands in a three-dimensional space is why an
  angular velocity has three components and no more.
- angular velocity vector :: \(\vec\omega\), the unique vector with
  \(\hat\omega = R^{\!\top}\dot R\). Its direction is the instantaneous axis, its
  magnitude the rate of turn, and its components here are in the *rotating*
  frame.
- hat map :: The linear isomorphism \(\mathbb R^3 \to \mathfrak{so}(3)\) sending
  \(\vec\omega\) to the antisymmetric matrix \(\hat\omega\). Being an
  isomorphism is the whole content: every antisymmetric \(3\times3\) matrix is
  "cross with a unique vector". /Slide: \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\) is \(\mathbb R^3\):
  the hat map and the cross product./
- cross product :: \(\vec u\times\vec w := \hat u\,w\) — defined as the action of
  the matrix, not by a mnemonic determinant. It exists only in three dimensions,
  since \(\dim\mathfrak{so}(n) = n(n-1)/2\) equals \(n\) only for \(n = 3\). Its
  further identities arrive in unit 08.
- transport theorem ::
  \((\mathrm d\vec u/\mathrm dt)_{\text{in}} = (\mathrm d\vec u/\mathrm dt)_{\text{rot}} + \vec\omega\times\vec u\),
  for any vector \(\vec u\) whatsoever, with both sides in rotating-frame
  components. One line from \(\dot R = R\Omega\), and everything in sections 4
  and 5 is this applied twice. /Slide: The transport theorem./

* The frame that lies to you

- fictitious force (inertial force) :: One of the three terms
  \(-2m\vec\omega\times\vec v\), \(-m\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r)\),
  \(-m\dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r\) appearing in a rotating frame. Nothing
  exerts them and they have no third-law partner; they are proportional to \(m\),
  which is the hint unit 30 collects. /Slide: The rotating-frame equation./
- centrifugal force :: \(-m\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r)\), of
  magnitude \(m\omega^2 r_\perp\) directed away from the *axis*, not from the
  centre. On Earth it is \(0.346\%\) of \(g\) at the equator. /Slide:
  Centrifugal: what a plumb line points at./
- effective gravity ::
  \(\vec g_{\text{eff}} = \vec g_{\text{true}} - \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r)\)
  — what a plumb line and a spring balance measure. It is not radial, and it
  defines "vertical", which is why the deviation is invisible from inside a
  building and shows up in the planet's shape instead.
- Coriolis force :: \(-2m\vec\omega\times\vec v_{\text{rot}}\). On a horizontal
  velocity its horizontal part is \(2m\omega\sin\lambda\,v\) to the right of the
  motion in the northern hemisphere, to the left in the southern, and zero at the
  equator. /Slide: Coriolis: the force that turns cyclones./
- Eötvös effect :: The vertical part of the Coriolis force,
  \(2m\omega\cos\lambda\,v_{\text{east}}\): moving east makes you lighter. It is
  absorbed into \(g_{\text{eff}}\) and is \(2.3\times10^{-5}\) of \(g\) at the
  Panthéon pendulum's peak speed.
- inertial circle :: The circular path a body follows when Coriolis is the only
  horizontal force, of radius \(v/(2\omega\sin\lambda)\) — \(291\) km for a
  \(30\) m/s wind at \(45^\circ\) N — and of period half the frame's rotation
  period.
- Rossby number :: \(\mathcal Ro = v/(2\omega\sin\lambda\,L)\), comparing inertia
  with Coriolis. About \(0.29\) for a \(1000\) km storm, about \(10^4\) for a
  bath: weather is Coriolis, plugholes are not.
- Euler force :: \(-m\dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r\), present only while the frame
  is spinning up or down. On Earth it is smaller than the centrifugal term by
  \(10^{-7}\) and is ignored throughout. /Slide: Euler: only while the frame is
  spinning up./

* The Foucault pendulum

- Foucault pendulum :: A long pendulum free to swing in any vertical plane, whose
  plane of swing turns relative to the ground at \(\omega\sin\lambda\), clockwise
  from above in the northern hemisphere. The small-oscillation equations are
  \(\ddot{\vec r} = -\omega_0^2\vec r - 2\omega_z\hat z\times\dot{\vec r}\) with
  \(\omega_0^2 = g/L\) and \(\omega_z = \omega\sin\lambda\). /Slide: The
  pendulum's two horizontal equations./
- Foucault period :: \(T = 2\pi/(\omega\sin\lambda) =\) one sidereal day divided
  by \(\sin\lambda\). At the Panthéon, \(23.9345\,\mathrm h/0.752946 = 31.79\) h;
  \(23.93\) h at the pole, infinite at the equator. /Slide: Worked: 31.8 hours at
  the Panthéon./
