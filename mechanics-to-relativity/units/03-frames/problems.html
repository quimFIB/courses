Several-variable calculus

P1 Partials are not enough routine

exercises: partial and directional derivatives, why the total derivative is defined as it is

Let \[ f(x,y) = \frac{xy}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad g(x,y) = \frac{x^3}{x^2+y^2}, \] both extended by the value \(0\) at the origin.

  1. Show that \(\partial_1 f(0,0)\) and \(\partial_2 f(0,0)\) both exist, and that \(f\) is nevertheless not continuous at the origin.
  2. Show that \(g\) is continuous at the origin, that its directional derivative \(D_u g(0,0)\) exists for every unit vector \(u\), and that \(g\) is still not differentiable at the origin.
  3. State, in one sentence, which clause of the definition of the total derivative each example violates.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two different things are being asked. Part 1 wants a number (two of them, both zero) and then a counterexample to a limit. Part 2 wants a *function of the direction* \(u\), and then an argument that this function is not what a derivative would give.

Rung 2

For part 1, restrict to a line through the origin and ask what \(f\) is on it. Try the axes; then try \(y = x\). For part 2, note that if \(Df(0)\) existed, it would be a linear map \(\Lambda\) and every directional derivative would be \(\Lambda u\) — linear in \(u\).

Rung 3

Part 1, first line: \(f(x,0) = 0\) for every \(x\), so the whole difference quotient along the \(x\)-axis is identically zero. Part 2, first line: \(\dfrac{g(tu)-g(0)}{t} = \dfrac1t\cdot\dfrac{t^3u_1^3}{t^2}\) for a unit \(u\).

Worked solution

Idea. A partial derivative sees one line. A function can behave perfectly on every line through a point and still be wrong on a curve through it, so "differentiable" has to be defined by a statement about all directions at once.

1. \(f(x,y) = xy/(x^2+y^2)\). On the \(x\)-axis \(f(x,0) = 0\) for all \(x\), so \(\partial_1 f(0,0) = 0\); by symmetry \(\partial_2 f(0,0) = 0\). But along \(y = x\), \[ f(x,x) = \frac{x^2}{2x^2} = \tfrac12 \qquad (x \ne 0), \] while along \(y = 0\) the value is \(0\). Two paths to the origin give two limits, so \(\lim_{(x,y)\to 0}f\) does not exist and \(f\) is not continuous at the origin. In particular it is not differentiable there.

2. \(g(x,y) = x^3/(x^2+y^2)\). Continuity: \(|g| = |x|\cdot x^2/(x^2+y^2) \le |x| \to 0\), and \(g(0,0) = 0\). Directional derivatives: for a unit vector \(u = (u_1,u_2)\), \[ \frac{g(tu)-g(0)}{t} = \frac1t\cdot\frac{t^3u_1^3}{t^2(u_1^2+u_2^2)} = u_1^3 , \] so \(D_ug(0,0) = u_1^3\) exists for every \(u\). If \(g\) were differentiable at the origin with derivative \(\Lambda\), then \(D_ug(0,0) = \Lambda u\) would be linear in \(u\). Taking \(u = (1,0)\) gives \(\Lambda u = 1\), so \(\Lambda = (1,0)\) and \(\Lambda u = u_1\); but at \(u = (1/\sqrt2, 1/\sqrt2)\), \(u_1^3 = 0.3536 \ne 0.7071 = u_1\). Contradiction.

3. \(f\) fails the definition at the first hurdle: differentiability implies continuity, and \(f\) is not continuous. \(g\) fails the clause that the error be \(o(\lVert h\rVert)\) as \(h \to 0\) in any manner — it is \(o(|t|)\) along each fixed ray, at a rate that depends on the ray, and the uniformity over directions that a single linear map would give is exactly what is missing.

Check. Both examples are homogeneous of degree \(0\) and \(1\) respectively: \(f(tx,ty) = f(x,y)\) and \(g(tx,ty) = t\,g(x,y)\). A degree-0 function that is not constant on rays cannot be continuous at the origin, and a degree-1 function is differentiable at the origin exactly when its restriction to the unit circle is the restriction of a linear map. Both failures were predictable from the scaling alone.

P2 Clairaut's counterexample medium

exercises: mixed partials, the hypothesis of Clairaut's theorem

Let \(f(x,y) = xy(x^2-y^2)/(x^2+y^2)\) with \(f(0,0) = 0\).

  1. Compute \(\partial_1 f(0,y)\) for all \(y\) and \(\partial_2 f(x,0)\) for all \(x\), and deduce \(\partial_2\partial_1 f(0,0)\) and \(\partial_1\partial_2 f(0,0)\).
  2. Compute \(\partial_2\partial_1 f(x,y)\) away from the origin and show it has no limit as \((x,y)\to(0,0)\) — so the theorem's hypothesis fails, and the theorem is not being contradicted.
  3. Why does this not happen for any function built from polynomials, exponentials and trigonometric functions by composition?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Do not differentiate the quotient rule twice and then substitute \((0,0)\) — the formula you get is not valid at the origin. Go back to the definition of a partial derivative as a limit.

Rung 2

You need \(\partial_1 f\) along the whole \(y\)-axis (not just at the origin) before you can differentiate it in \(y\). And note the symmetry \(f(y,x) = -f(x,y)\): it halves the work and predicts the sign.

Rung 3

First line: \(\partial_1 f(0,y) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac1h\cdot\frac{hy(h^2-y^2)}{h^2+y^2}\). For part 2, the answer you are heading for is \(\partial_2\partial_1 f = (x^6+9x^4y^2-9x^2y^4-y^6)/(x^2+y^2)^3\); approach the origin along each axis in turn.

Worked solution

Idea. Compute the two mixed partials at the origin from the definition — not from a formula valid only off it — and then show the formula off the origin has no limit, so the theorem's hypothesis, not its conclusion, is what fails.

1. For \(y \ne 0\), \[ \partial_1 f(0,y) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(h,y)-f(0,y)}{h} = \lim_{h\to0}\frac1h\cdot\frac{hy(h^2-y^2)}{h^2+y^2} = \frac{y(-y^2)}{y^2} = -y , \] and \(\partial_1 f(0,0) = 0\) too, so \(\partial_1 f(0,y) = -y\) for every \(y\) and \[ \partial_2\partial_1 f(0,0) = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dy}(-y) = -1 . \] Since \(f(y,x) = -f(x,y)\), the same computation with the roles swapped gives \(\partial_2 f(x,0) = +x\) and \(\partial_1\partial_2 f(0,0) = +1\).

2. Away from the origin, writing \(f = (x^3y - xy^3)/(x^2+y^2)\) and differentiating twice, \[ \partial_1 f = \frac{y(x^4 + 4x^2y^2 - y^4)}{(x^2+y^2)^2}, \qquad \partial_2\partial_1 f = \frac{x^6 + 9x^4y^2 - 9x^2y^4 - y^6}{(x^2+y^2)^3} . \] Along the \(x\)-axis this is \(x^6/x^6 = +1\); along the \(y\)-axis it is \(-y^6/y^6 = -1\). So \(\partial_2\partial_1 f\) has no limit at the origin and is not continuous there. Clairaut's hypothesis fails, and nothing has been contradicted.

3. Any function assembled from polynomials, \(\exp\), \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) by composition, addition and multiplication is \(C^\infty\) wherever it is defined, so all its mixed partials are continuous and Clairaut always applies. The counterexample needs a quotient with a vanishing denominator — a point where smoothness genuinely breaks down.

Check. The signs are forced by antisymmetry: \(f(y,x) = -f(x,y)\) makes \(\partial_1\partial_2 f(0,0) = -\partial_2\partial_1 f(0,0)\), so the two mixed partials at the origin are negatives of each other and could only be equal if both were \(0\). They are \(\pm1\). The formula in part 2 is likewise antisymmetric under \(x \leftrightarrow y\) ✓.

P3 The chain rule as composition routine

exercises: Jacobians, composing linear maps, the polar map

  1. Let \(f(u,v) = (u^2-v^2,\ 2uv)\) and \(g(x,y) = x^2+y^2\). Compute \(D(g\circ f)(1,2)\) twice: once by composing Jacobians, once by simplifying \(g\circ f\) first and differentiating. They must agree.
  2. Let \(\Phi(r,\theta) = (r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta)\). Write down \(D\Phi(r,\theta)\) and \(\det D\Phi\). Where is \(D\Phi\) not invertible, and what goes wrong there geometrically?
  3. Show that the columns of \(D\Phi\) are orthogonal, and that dividing each by its length gives an element of \(SO(2)\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is a \(1\times2\) matrix — a row — because \(g\) is scalar-valued and \(f\) has two inputs. Part 2's answer is a \(2\times2\) matrix and a scalar.

Rung 2

Order matters: \(D(g\circ f)(a) = Dg(f(a))\,Df(a)\), with \(Dg\) evaluated at \(f(a)\), not at \(a\). Compute \(f(1,2)\) before anything else.

Rung 3

\(f(1,2) = (-3,4)\), so \(Dg(-3,4) = (-6,8)\); and the columns of \(D\Phi\) are \(\partial\Phi/\partial r\) and \(\partial\Phi/\partial\theta\), in that order.

Worked solution

Idea. Compose the linear maps; the subscripts are the matrix product and are never needed as a separate rule.

1. \(Df(u,v) = \begin{pmatrix} 2u & -2v \\ 2v & 2u\end{pmatrix}\), so \(Df(1,2) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -4 \\ 4 & 2\end{pmatrix}\). Also \(f(1,2) = (1-4,\ 4) = (-3,4)\), and \(Dg(x,y) = (2x,\ 2y)\), so \(Dg(-3,4) = (-6,\ 8)\). Composing, \[ D(g\circ f)(1,2) = (-6,\ 8)\begin{pmatrix}2 & -4\\ 4 & 2\end{pmatrix} = (-12+32,\ 24+16) = (20,\ 40). \] Directly: \(g(f(u,v)) = (u^2-v^2)^2 + 4u^2v^2 = (u^2+v^2)^2\), whose derivative is \(\big(4u(u^2+v^2),\ 4v(u^2+v^2)\big)\); at \((1,2)\) that is \((4\cdot5,\ 8\cdot5) = (20,\ 40)\) ✓.

2. \(D\Phi(r,\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\), \(\det D\Phi = r\cos^2\theta + r\sin^2\theta = r\). It is singular exactly at \(r = 0\). Geometrically the whole line \(\{r = 0\}\) is crushed to the single point \((0,0)\): \(\Phi\) is not injective on any neighbourhood of such a point, so it has no local inverse there, and \(\theta\) is undefined at the origin however the map is patched.

3. The columns are \((\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) and \(r(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\); their inner product is \(-r\cos\theta\sin\theta + r\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0\). Dividing by the lengths \(1\) and \(r\) leaves \(\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}\), which is orthogonal with determinant \(+1\).

Check. \(\det D\Phi = r\) has dimensions of length, as a map from \((\mathrm L, 1)\) to \((\mathrm L,\mathrm L)\) must ✓. It is the factor that will reappear in unit 05 as the area element \(r\,\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta\), and it vanishing at \(r=0\) is why polar coordinates have a coordinate singularity there.

P4 Polar coordinates are a rotating frame medium

exercises: differentiating a moving basis, the transport theorem in two dimensions

A particle in the plane has position \(\vec r(t) = r(t)\,\hat e_r(\theta(t))\) where \(\hat e_r(\theta) = (\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) and \(\hat e_\theta(\theta) = (-\sin\theta,\cos\theta)\).

  1. Show \(\dot{\hat e}_r = \dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta\) and \(\dot{\hat e}_\theta = -\dot\theta\,\hat e_r\), and derive \[ \vec v = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta, \qquad \vec a = (\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\,\hat e_r + (r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\,\hat e_\theta . \]
  2. Now get the same result a second way: treat \((\hat e_r, \hat e_\theta)\) as a frame rotating at \(\vec\omega = \dot\theta\,\hat z\) and apply the rotating-frame equation, reading off which term is centrifugal, which Coriolis and which Euler.
  3. Which term of \(\vec a\) is responsible for the fact that a skater pulling her arms in spins faster?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Both \(\hat e_r\) and \(\hat e_\theta\) depend on \(t\) through \(\theta(t)\), so every product rule has three terms, not two. The answer has exactly two components in each of parts 1 and 2, and they must match term for term.

Rung 2

\(\hat e_r\) and \(\hat e_\theta\) are unit vectors, so each derivative is perpendicular to the vector itself — which in two dimensions leaves only one possibility up to a factor. That factor is \(\dot\theta\).

Rung 3

For part 2: in the rotating frame the particle's position list is just \((r, 0)\), because \(\hat e_r\) is the frame's first basis vector by construction. Its rotating-frame velocity is \((\dot r, 0)\) and its rotating-frame acceleration \((\ddot r, 0)\). Everything else in \[ \vec a_{\text{in}} = \vec a_{\text{rot}} + 2\vec\omega\times\vec v_{\text{rot}} +\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) + \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r \] is a cross product with \(\hat z\).

Worked solution

Idea. \((\hat e_r,\hat e_\theta)\) is a frame that turns at \(\dot\theta\). The familiar polar acceleration formula and the rotating-frame equation are the same statement.

1. \(\hat e_r = (\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\) gives \(\dot{\hat e}_r = \dot\theta(-\sin\theta,\cos\theta) = \dot\theta\hat e_\theta\), and \(\dot{\hat e}_\theta = \dot\theta(-\cos\theta,-\sin\theta) = -\dot\theta\hat e_r\). Then \[ \vec v = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(r\hat e_r) = \dot r\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\hat e_\theta, \] \[ \vec a = \ddot r\hat e_r + \dot r\dot\theta\hat e_\theta +(\dot r\dot\theta + r\ddot\theta)\hat e_\theta + r\dot\theta(-\dot\theta\hat e_r) = (\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2)\hat e_r + (r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta)\hat e_\theta . \]

2. In the frame carried by \((\hat e_r,\hat e_\theta)\) the particle sits at \(\vec r_{\text{rot}} = (r,0)\), with \(\vec v_{\text{rot}} = (\dot r, 0)\) and \(\vec a_{\text{rot}} = (\ddot r, 0)\), and the frame turns at \(\vec\omega = \dot\theta\hat z\). The inertial acceleration is \[ \vec a_{\text{in}} = \vec a_{\text{rot}} + 2\vec\omega\times\vec v_{\text{rot}} +\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) + \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r , \] and term by term: \(\vec a_{\text{rot}} = \ddot r\hat e_r\); \(2\dot\theta\hat z\times\dot r\hat e_r = 2\dot r\dot\theta\hat e_\theta\) (Coriolis); \(\dot\theta\hat z\times(\dot\theta\hat z\times r\hat e_r) = -r\dot\theta^2\hat e_r\) (centrifugal); \(\ddot\theta\hat z\times r\hat e_r = r\ddot\theta\hat e_\theta\) (Euler). Adding gives part 1 exactly.

3. The Coriolis term \(2\dot r\dot\theta\). With no tangential force, \(r\ddot\theta + 2\dot r\dot\theta = 0\), i.e. \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(r^2\dot\theta) = 0\): pulling the arms in (\(\dot r < 0\)) forces \(\ddot\theta > 0\). It is the same conserved quantity that unit 08 will call angular momentum.

Check. Dimensions: \(r\dot\theta^2\) is \(\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}\) ✓, \(\dot r\dot\theta\) is \(\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}\) ✓. Limit \(\dot\theta = 0\): \(\vec a = \ddot r\hat e_r\), straight-line motion along a fixed ray ✓. Limit \(r\) constant, \(\dot\theta\) constant: \(\vec a = -r\dot\theta^2\hat e_r\), which is \(-v^2/r\) towards the centre with \(v = r\dot\theta\) ✓.

P5 Circular motion from scratch medium

exercises: proof; the constant-norm lemma, and the Frenet decomposition derived from it

Let \(\gamma\) be a twice-differentiable trajectory with speed \(v = \lVert\dot\gamma\rVert > 0\) everywhere.

  1. Prove that \(v\) is constant on an interval if and only if \(\vec a\cdot\vec v = 0\) there.
  2. Without quoting the Frenet–Serret formulas, prove \(\vec a = \dot v\,\hat T + (v^2/\rho)\,\hat N\), defining \(\hat N\) and \(\rho\) as you go. (Differentiate \(\vec v = v\hat T\), then use part 1 on \(\hat T\).)
  3. Deduce that a particle moving at constant speed on a circle of radius \(r\) has \(\lVert\vec a\rVert = v^2/r\) directed at the centre — and say precisely where "circle" was used, given that parts 1 and 2 never mention one.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an equivalence, so two implications — but one computation does both, because the same derivative appears on each side. Part 2 is an identity between vectors, and \(\hat N\) and \(\rho\) are yours to define.

Rung 2

Differentiate \(\lVert u\rVert^2 = u\cdot u\) rather than \(\lVert u\rVert\); the square root is not differentiable at zero and you do not need it. For part 2, write \(\vec v = v\hat T\) and differentiate the product.

Rung 3

Part 2's key step: \(\hat T\) is a unit vector, so part 1 applies to it and \(\dot{\hat T}\perp\hat T\). Define \(\hat N\) as the unit vector along \(\dot{\hat T}\), and define \(\kappa\) by \(\lVert\dot{\hat T}\rVert = \kappa v\) — the \(v\) converts "per unit time" into "per unit arc length".

Worked solution

Idea. One lemma about constant-norm vectors does all the work, twice: once for \(\vec v\), once for \(\hat T\).

1. \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}v^2 = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\vec v\cdot\vec v) = 2\vec v\cdot\vec a\) by the product rule for the dot product. If \(v\) is constant then \(v^2\) is, so the derivative vanishes and \(\vec a\cdot\vec v = 0\). Conversely if \(\vec a\cdot\vec v = 0\) on an interval then \(v^2\) has zero derivative there, hence is constant, hence so is \(v = \sqrt{v^2} \ge 0\). \(\square\)

2. Write \(\vec v = v\hat T\) with \(\hat T = \vec v/v\), which is defined because \(v > 0\), and is a unit vector. Differentiating, \[ \vec a = \dot v\,\hat T + v\,\dot{\hat T} . \] By part 1 applied to \(\hat T\) (constant norm \(1\)), \(\dot{\hat T}\cdot\hat T = 0\): \(\dot{\hat T}\) is perpendicular to \(\hat T\). Where \(\dot{\hat T} \ne 0\) define \[ \hat N = \frac{\dot{\hat T}}{\lVert\dot{\hat T}\rVert}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{\lVert\dot{\hat T}\rVert}{v} = \frac1\rho, \] the division by \(v\) turning "per unit time" into "per unit arc length", since \(\mathrm ds = v\,\mathrm dt\). Then \(\dot{\hat T} = \kappa v\hat N\) and \[ \vec a = \dot v\hat T + \kappa v^2\hat N = \dot v\hat T + \frac{v^2}{\rho}\hat N . \] There is no third term, and \(\vec a\cdot\hat B = 0\) follows because \(\vec a\) lies in \(\mathrm{span}(\hat T,\hat N)\) by construction. \(\square\)

3. On a circle of radius \(r\) at constant speed, \(\dot v = 0\) kills the first term, and \(\rho = r\) — a circle is its own osculating circle — leaves \(\lVert\vec a\rVert = v^2/r\) along \(\hat N\), which for a circle points at the centre. "Circle" was used in exactly one place: to evaluate \(\rho\). Parts 1 and 2 are statements about an arbitrary trajectory, and the familiar formula is what they become when the radius of curvature happens to be constant and equal to the radius of the path.

Check. Dimensions \(\mathrm{L\,T^{-2}}\) on both terms ✓. A straight line has \(\dot{\hat T} = 0\), so \(\rho = \infty\) and only the tangential term survives, recovering unit 00's one-dimensional problem ✓. A number: the helix of P6 has \(\dot v = 0\) and \(v^2/\rho = 36\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), which is \(\lVert\ddot\gamma\rVert = R\omega^2 = 4\times 9 = 36\) ✓.

P6 The Frenet frame of a helix routine

exercises: arc length, curvature, torsion, the two-part acceleration, with numbers

A cable car runs along \(\gamma(t) = (4\cos 3t,\ 4\sin 3t,\ 2t)\), metres and seconds.

  1. Find the speed, the arc length per full turn, and the rise per turn.
  2. Compute \(\hat T\), \(\hat N\), \(\hat B\), \(\kappa\), \(\rho\) and \(\tau\).
  3. Verify \(\vec a = \dot v\hat T + (v^2/\rho)\hat N\) numerically, and check that \(\rho\) exceeds the radius \(4\) m of the shadow circle. By what factor, and why that factor?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

\(\kappa\) and \(\tau\) are numbers with units \(\mathrm m^{-1}\), and for this curve both are constant. \(\hat T, \hat N, \hat B\) are vectors depending on \(t\).

Rung 2

The speed is constant, which kills the tangential acceleration and makes \(\ddot\gamma = \kappa v^2\hat N\): you get \(\hat N\) and \(\kappa\) from \(\ddot\gamma\) alone, without differentiating \(\hat T\).

Rung 3

Primes are \(\mathrm d/\mathrm ds\) and dots are \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dt\), with \(\mathrm ds = v\,\mathrm dt\); so \(\tau = -\hat B'\cdot\hat N = -\dot{\hat B}\cdot\hat N/v\). First line: \(\dot\gamma = (-12\sin3t,\ 12\cos3t,\ 2)\), so \(v = \sqrt{148}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Everything follows from \(\dot\gamma\) and \(\ddot\gamma\); the only subtlety is remembering that primes are \(\mathrm d/\mathrm ds\) and dots are \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dt\), differing by a factor \(v\).

1. \(\dot\gamma = (-12\sin3t,\ 12\cos3t,\ 2)\), so \[ v = \sqrt{144 + 4} = \sqrt{148} = 12.1655\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}, \] constant. A full turn takes \(2\pi/3 = 2.0944\) s, in which the arc length is \(v\cdot 2\pi/3 = 25.479\) m and the rise is \(2\cdot2\pi/3 = 4.1888\) m.

2. \(\hat T = \dot\gamma/v = (-0.98639\sin3t,\ 0.98639\cos3t,\ 0.16440)\). Next \(\ddot\gamma = (-36\cos3t,\ -36\sin3t,\ 0)\), of norm \(36\), so \(\hat N = (-\cos3t,\ -\sin3t,\ 0)\) — horizontal, pointing at the axis — and \[ \kappa = \frac{\lVert\ddot\gamma\rVert}{v^2} = \frac{36}{148} = 0.24324\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}, \qquad \rho = \frac{148}{36} = 4.1111\ \mathrm m. \] (The middle step uses \(\dot{\hat T} = \kappa v\hat N\) with \(\dot v = 0\), so \(\ddot\gamma = \kappa v^2\hat N\).) Then \(\hat B = \hat T\times\hat N = (0.16440\sin3t,\ -0.16440\cos3t,\ 0.98639)\), and \(\dot{\hat B} = (3\cdot0.16440)(\cos3t,\sin3t,0) = -(0.49320)\hat N\), so \[ \tau = -\hat B'\cdot\hat N = -\frac{\dot{\hat B}\cdot\hat N}{v} = \frac{0.49320}{12.1655} = 0.040541\ \mathrm{m^{-1}} = \frac{6}{148}. \]

3. \(\dot v = 0\) and \(v^2/\rho = 148/4.1111 = 36.000\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = \lVert\ddot\gamma\rVert\) ✓, and the direction matches \(\hat N\) ✓. The shadow circle has radius \(4\) m, and \[ \frac{\rho}{4} = \frac{148}{144} = 1.02778 = \frac{v^2}{(R\omega)^2}: \] the factor is exactly the squared ratio of the true speed to the speed of the shadow. Stretching a circle into a helix straightens it, so \(\rho > R\); in the limit of a very steep helix \(\rho \to \infty\) and the curve is nearly a straight line.

Check. Dimensions: \(\kappa, \tau\) in \(\mathrm m^{-1}\) ✓. Limit: with the rise set to zero the curve is the circle of radius 4, and indeed \(\kappa \to R\omega^2/(R\omega)^2 = 1/R = 0.25\) and \(\tau \to 0\) ✓. Orthonormality: \(\hat T\cdot\hat N = 0.98639\sin3t\cos3t - 0.98639\cos3t\sin3t = 0\) ✓, and \(\lVert\hat B\rVert^2 = 0.16440^2 + 0.98639^2 = 1.0000\) ✓.

P7 Curvature you can feel routine

exercises: curvature of a graph, normal acceleration, a number

A dip in a road has profile \(y = x^2/(2L)\) with \(L = 400\) m, driven at a constant \(30\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

  1. Show that the curvature of the graph of a \(C^2\) function \(y = h(x)\) is \(\kappa = |h''|/(1+h'^2)^{3/2}\).
  2. Evaluate \(\kappa\), \(\rho\) and the normal acceleration at the bottom \(x = 0\), and again at \(x = 200\) m. Give both in units of \(g\).
  3. At the bottom of the dip, does the driver feel heavier or lighter, and by what percentage?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1's answer is a formula; parts 2 and 3 are numbers. Parametrise the graph by \(x\) itself — the parametrisation is not unit speed and that is fine, provided you never confuse \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dx\) with \(\mathrm d/\mathrm ds\).

Rung 2

You do not need \(\hat T'\). Use \(\vec a = \dot v\hat T + \kappa v^2\hat N\) and take the component of the second derivative perpendicular to the first: the tangential part drops out and \(\kappa v^2\) is what is left.

Rung 3

With \(\gamma(x) = (x, h(x))\): \(\gamma' = (1,h')\), \(\gamma'' = (0,h'')\), and the unit normal is \((-h',1)/\sqrt{1+h'^2}\). For part 3, ask which way \(\hat N\) points at the bottom of a dip, and what the seat must therefore push with.

Worked solution

Idea. A graph is the curve \(t \mapsto (t, h(t))\); push it through the definition of \(\kappa\) once and the formula is yours for good.

1. Parametrise by \(x\): \(\gamma(x) = (x, h(x))\), \(\gamma' = (1, h')\), \(v = \sqrt{1+h'^2}\), \(\gamma'' = (0, h'')\). Since \(\vec a = \dot v\hat T + \kappa v^2\hat N\) and \(\hat N \perp \hat T\), take the component of \(\gamma''\) perpendicular to \(\gamma'\): the unit normal is \((-h', 1)/\sqrt{1+h'^2}\), so \[ \kappa v^2 = \gamma''\cdot\hat N = \frac{h''}{\sqrt{1+h'^2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \kappa = \frac{|h''|}{(1+h'^2)^{3/2}} . \]

2. Here \(h = x^2/(2L)\), \(h' = x/L\), \(h'' = 1/L\) with \(L = 400\) m.

  • At \(x = 0\): \(h' = 0\), so \(\kappa = 1/L = 2.500\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(\rho = 400\) m, and \(a_N = v^2/\rho = 900/400 = 2.250\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 0.2294\,g\).
  • At \(x = 200\) m: \(h' = 0.5\), \((1+0.25)^{3/2} = 1.39754\), so \(\kappa = 1.7889\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\), \(\rho = 559.0\) m, and \(a_N = 900/559.0 = 1.6100\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} = 0.1641\,g\).

3. At the bottom the road is concave up, so \(\hat N\) points upwards and the seat must supply \(m(g + a_N)\): the driver feels heavier, by \(2.250/9.81 = 22.9\%\).

Check. Dimensions: \([\kappa] = \mathrm L^{-1}\) and the formula's numerator \(h''\) is \(\mathrm L^{-1}\) with a dimensionless denominator ✓. Limit: a straight road, \(h'' = 0\), gives \(\kappa = 0\) and no normal acceleration ✓. Sanity: \(\kappa\) decreases away from the bottom, as it must — the parabola is flattest, hence most curved, at its vertex, and asymptotically straight far out ✓.

P8 The hat map, and why \(\vec\omega\) is a vector hard

exercises: proof; so(3), the conjugation identity, body versus space angular velocity

Write \(\hat a\) for the antisymmetric matrix with \(\hat a\,w = \vec a\times\vec w\).

  1. Prove \((\vec u\times\vec v)\cdot\vec w = \det(\vec u,\vec v,\vec w)\), the determinant of the matrix with those columns.
  2. Deduce that \(R(\vec u\times\vec v) = (R\vec u)\times(R\vec v)\) for every \(R \in SO(3)\), and hence that \(R\,\hat a\,R^{\!\top} = \widehat{R\vec a}\). Where exactly is \(\det R = +1\) used, and what happens for \(\det R = -1\)?
  3. Let \(R : I \to SO(3)\) and put \(\Omega = R^{\!\top}\dot R\), \(\tilde\Omega = \dot R R^{\!\top}\). Prove \(\tilde\Omega = R\Omega R^{\!\top}\) and conclude that the angular velocity read in the inertial frame is \(R\vec\omega\): the two frames report the same arrow.
  4. Prove that \(\dot{\vec\omega}\) has the same rotating-frame components whether it is differentiated in the rotating or in the inertial frame.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an identity between two scalars; part 2 between two vectors and then between two matrices; part 3 between two matrices; part 4 between two vectors. Nothing here needs coordinates except part 1.

Rung 2

Two matrices are equal iff they agree on every vector. That turns the matrix statement \(R\hat aR^{\!\top} = \widehat{R\vec a}\) into a vector statement you can attack with part 2's cross-product identity.

Rung 3

For part 2, the trick is to write \(\vec w = R(R^{\!\top}\vec w)\) so that all three columns of the determinant carry an \(R\), and then pull \(\det R\) out. For part 3, start from \(\dot R = R\Omega\). For part 4, apply the transport theorem to \(\vec\omega\) itself and remember \(\vec\omega\times\vec\omega = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. The cross product is tied to the determinant; the determinant is invariant under \(SO(3)\); everything else is bookkeeping.

1. Expanding, \[ (\vec u\times\vec v)\cdot\vec w = (u_2v_3-u_3v_2)w_1 + (u_3v_1-u_1v_3)w_2 +(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)w_3 , \] which is precisely the cofactor expansion of \(\det[\vec u\ \vec v\ \vec w]\) along its third column. \(\square\)

2. For \(R \in SO(3)\) and any \(\vec w\), writing \(\vec w = R(R^{\!\top}\vec w)\), \[ \big((R\vec u)\times(R\vec v)\big)\cdot\vec w = \det[R\vec u\ R\vec v\ RR^{\!\top}\vec w] = \det R\cdot\det[\vec u\ \vec v\ R^{\!\top}\vec w] = (\vec u\times\vec v)\cdot R^{\!\top}\vec w = \big(R(\vec u\times\vec v)\big)\cdot\vec w . \] True for every \(\vec w\), so \((R\vec u)\times(R\vec v) = R(\vec u\times\vec v)\). Then for any \(\vec w\), \[ R\,\hat a\,R^{\!\top}\vec w = R\big(\vec a\times(R^{\!\top}\vec w)\big) = (R\vec a)\times(RR^{\!\top}\vec w) = (R\vec a)\times\vec w = \widehat{R\vec a}\,\vec w , \] so \(R\hat aR^{\!\top} = \widehat{R\vec a}\). \(\square\)

\(\det R = +1\) is used exactly once, in the middle equality of the first display. For \(\det R = -1\) it gives \(-1\) instead, so \((R\vec u)\times(R\vec v) = -R(\vec u\times\vec v)\): under a reflection the cross product acquires a sign, which is the precise content of calling it a pseudovector.

3. From \(\Omega = R^{\!\top}\dot R\) we get \(\dot R = R\Omega\), hence \[ \tilde\Omega = \dot RR^{\!\top} = R\Omega R^{\!\top} = \widehat{R\vec\omega} \] by part 2. So the antisymmetric matrix built in the inertial frame corresponds to the vector \(R\vec\omega\) — and \(x_{\text{in}} = Rx_{\text{rot}}\) is exactly how the components of one vector in the two frames are related. The two frames report the same arrow. \(\square\)

4. Apply the transport theorem to \(\vec u = \vec\omega\): \[ \Big(\frac{\mathrm d\vec\omega}{\mathrm dt}\Big)_{\text{in}} = \Big(\frac{\mathrm d\vec\omega}{\mathrm dt}\Big)_{\text{rot}} + \vec\omega\times\vec\omega = \Big(\frac{\mathrm d\vec\omega}{\mathrm dt}\Big)_{\text{rot}} . \] Equivalently, in inertial components, \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(R\vec\omega) = \dot R\vec\omega + R\dot{\vec\omega} = R(\vec\omega\times\vec\omega) + R\dot{\vec\omega} = R\dot{\vec\omega}\). \(\square\)

Check. Part 2 with \(R\) a rotation by \(\pi/2\) about \(\hat z\): \(\hat e_1\times\hat e_2 = \hat e_3\) becomes \(\hat e_2\times(-\hat e_1) = \hat e_3\) ✓. Part 3's dimensions: both sides of \(\tilde\Omega = R\Omega R^{\!\top}\) are \(\mathrm T^{-1}\) ✓. Part 4 is why the Euler term needs no frame label in the rotating-frame equation — a fact used silently on the slides.

P9 The transport theorem, twice medium

exercises: derivation; the rotating-frame equation without matrices

Derive \[ m\vec a_{\text{rot}} = \vec F - 2m\vec\omega\times\vec v_{\text{rot}} -m\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) - m\dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r \] using only the transport theorem, applied first to \(\vec r\) and then to the resulting \(\vec v_{\text{in}}\) — no matrices anywhere.

Then say, in one sentence each: why the Coriolis term carries a factor \(2\) and the others do not; and what changes if the two frames' origins differ by a time-dependent \(\vec d(t)\).

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The theorem converts one inertial derivative into a rotating one. You need two inertial derivatives, so you apply it twice — the second time to a vector that is itself a sum of two things.

Rung 2

Write the theorem as an operator identity, \((\mathrm d/\mathrm dt)_{\text{in}} = (\mathrm d/\mathrm dt)_{\text{rot}} + \vec\omega\times\), and apply it to \(\vec r\) first. Keep the result as a sum of two terms; do not simplify it.

Rung 3

First line: \(\vec v_{\text{in}} = \dot{\vec r} + \vec\omega\times\vec r\). Now apply the operator to that, using the product rule on \(\vec\omega\times\vec r\) — which is where the second copy of \(\vec\omega\times\dot{\vec r}\), and hence the factor 2, comes from.

Worked solution

Idea. Apply \((\mathrm d/\mathrm dt)_{\text{in}} = (\mathrm d/\mathrm dt)_{\text{rot}} + \vec\omega\times\) to \(\vec r\), then to what comes out. Every term appears exactly once, which is why counting them is easy.

Working. First application, to the position vector: \[ \vec v_{\text{in}} = \dot{\vec r} + \vec\omega\times\vec r , \] where the dot is the rotating-frame derivative, i.e. \(\vec v_{\text{rot}}\). Second application, to \(\vec v_{\text{in}}\): \[ \vec a_{\text{in}} = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Big|_{\text{rot}}\!\big(\dot{\vec r} + \vec\omega\times\vec r\big) +\vec\omega\times\big(\dot{\vec r} + \vec\omega\times\vec r\big) . \] Expanding the first bracket with the product rule for \(\times\), and using \(\dot{\vec\omega}\) for the rotating-frame derivative of \(\vec\omega\) (which by P8.4 is unambiguous): \[ \vec a_{\text{in}} = \ddot{\vec r} + \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r +\vec\omega\times\dot{\vec r} + \vec\omega\times\dot{\vec r} +\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) = \vec a_{\text{rot}} + 2\vec\omega\times\vec v_{\text{rot}} +\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) + \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r . \] Multiply by \(m\) and substitute \(m\vec a_{\text{in}} = \vec F\), then solve for \(m\vec a_{\text{rot}}\): \[ m\vec a_{\text{rot}} = \vec F - 2m\vec\omega\times\vec v_{\text{rot}} -m\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) - m\dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r . \qquad\square \]

The factor 2. \(\vec\omega\times\dot{\vec r}\) is produced twice: once by differentiating \(\vec\omega\times\vec r\) inside the rotating frame (the \(\vec\omega\times\dot{\vec r}\) half of the product rule), and once by the \(\vec\omega\times\) that the second application of the theorem adds to \(\dot{\vec r}\). The centrifugal and Euler terms each arise from one source only.

Translating origins. If \(\vec x_{\text{in}} = \vec d(t) + R\vec r\), the extra term differentiates independently, giving \(\vec a_{\text{in}} = \ddot{\vec d} + (\text{everything above})\), so the rotating-frame equation acquires one more fictitious term, \(-m\ddot{\vec d}\). Nothing else changes, because translation and rotation act on separate parts of the expression.

Check. Dimensions: every term \(\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}\) ✓. Limit \(\vec\omega \to 0\): all three vanish and \(m\vec a = \vec F\) ✓. Special case \(\vec r\) fixed in the rotating frame, \(\vec\omega\) constant: only the centrifugal term survives, of magnitude \(m\omega^2r_\perp\) outward ✓. The matrix derivation on the slides gives the identical four terms, which is the strongest check available.

P10 Falling east medium

exercises: Coriolis with a sign, one-step iteration, a number

A mass is dropped from rest down a mineshaft of depth \(h = 80\) m at latitude \(\lambda = 51.0^\circ\) N.

  1. Show that the horizontal deflection is \(x = \tfrac13\,\omega g t^3\cos\lambda\) with \(t = \sqrt{2h/g}\), and say which way it points and why that direction is right in both hemispheres.
  2. Evaluate it. Compare with the naive angular-momentum estimate "the mass starts with the eastward speed of a point \(h\) higher, so it gains \(\omega h t\)", and explain the ratio between the two.
  3. Estimate the size of the term you dropped by iterating once, and say why that justifies stopping.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a length of order centimetres. You are asked for one component of a vector equation, so set up the local east–north–up frame first and write \(\vec\omega\) in it.

Rung 2

Solve the problem you can solve — free fall — and use its answer inside the Coriolis term. That gives an explicit function of \(t\) on the right-hand side, which you integrate twice.

Rung 3

At latitude \(\lambda\), \(\vec\omega = \omega(0,\cos\lambda,\sin\lambda)\) and to zeroth order \(\vec v = (0,0,-gt)\). First line: \(-2\vec\omega\times\vec v = (2\omega g t\cos\lambda,\ 0,\ 0)\). For part 2, express the naive estimate as a multiple of \(\omega g t^3\) and compare the numerical coefficients.

Worked solution

Idea. Solve to zeroth order, feed the answer back in once, and check that the next round would be negligible.

1. In the local east–north–up frame at latitude \(\lambda\), \(\vec\omega = \omega(0,\cos\lambda,\sin\lambda)\) and the equation, with centrifugal already absorbed into \(g\) and \(\dot{\vec\omega} = 0\), is \(\vec a = -g\hat z - 2\vec\omega\times\vec v\). To zeroth order \(\vec v = (0,0,-gt)\), so \[ -2\vec\omega\times\vec v = -2\omega(0,\cos\lambda,\sin\lambda)\times(0,0,-gt) = (2\omega gt\cos\lambda,\ 0,\ 0), \] due east. Integrating \(\ddot x = 2\omega g t\cos\lambda\) twice from rest gives \(x = \tfrac13\omega gt^3\cos\lambda\), and the drop time is \(t = \sqrt{2h/g}\).

The direction is east in both hemispheres because the deflection carries the factor \(\cos\lambda > 0\) for every \(\lambda \in (-90^\circ, 90^\circ)\) — unlike the horizontal Coriolis deflection, which carries \(\sin\lambda\) and does change sign at the equator.

2. \(t = \sqrt{160/9.81} = 4.03855\) s, so \[ x = \tfrac13(7.29212\times10^{-5})(9.81)(4.03855)^3\cos 51.0^\circ = 0.015706\times0.62932 = 9.88\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm m \approx 0.99\ \mathrm{cm}. \] The naive estimate: the mass begins with the eastward speed of a point \(h\) further from the axis, an excess \(\omega h\cos\lambda\), which over \(t\) seconds gives \(\omega h t\cos\lambda = 7.29212\times10^{-5}\times80\times4.03855 \times 0.62932 = 1.48\ \mathrm{cm}\). The ratio is exactly \(2/3\), since \(h = \tfrac12gt^2\) makes the naive answer \(\tfrac12\omega gt^3\cos\lambda\) against the true \(\tfrac13\omega gt^3\cos\lambda\). The naive estimate overshoots because the mass also falls inwards, shrinking its distance from the axis and so giving up part of its initial lead.

3. Feeding the first-order eastward velocity \(\dot x = \omega gt^2\cos\lambda\) back into \(-2\vec\omega\times\vec v\) produces a correction smaller by a factor of order \(\omega t = 7.29\times10^{-5}\times4.04 = 2.9\times10^{-4}\), i.e. about \(3\ \mu\mathrm m\). That is three and a half orders of magnitude below the answer and far below any achievable measurement, so one iteration is enough. (Unit 21 calls this a regular perturbation expansion in the small parameter \(\omega t\).)

Check. Dimensions: \(\omega gt^3 = \mathrm{T^{-1}\,L\,T^{-2}\,T^3} = \mathrm L\) ✓. Limit \(\omega \to 0\): no deflection ✓. Limit \(\lambda \to 90^\circ\): no deflection, correct, since at the pole the fall is along the rotation axis ✓. Cross-check against the slide's worked example: at the equator with \(h = 100\) m the same formula gives \(2.20\) cm, and Reich's 1831 measurements down a \(158\) m shaft at Freiberg agree with the predicted \(2.75\) cm ✓.

P11 The plumb line does not point at the centre medium

exercises: centrifugal decomposition in the local frame, a number, a maximisation

  1. Decompose \(-\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r)\) for a point on a spherical Earth of radius \(R_{\mathrm E}\) at latitude \(\lambda\) into local vertical and local northward components.
  2. Show that the angle between \(\vec g_{\text{eff}}\) and the true radial direction is \(\approx \omega^2R\sin\lambda\cos\lambda/g\), find the latitude where it is largest, and evaluate it there in degrees and in arcminutes.
  3. By what percentage does rotation reduce the measured \(g\) at the equator? Measured \(g\) is \(9.780\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) at the equator and \(9.832\) at the poles — how much of that difference have you explained, and what accounts for the rest?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 2 is an angle of order a tenth of a degree; part 3 is a percentage and then a fraction. The whole problem is one double cross product resolved in the local frame.

Rung 2

Put \(\vec r = R\hat z\) (straight up, at the point in question) and \(\vec\omega = \omega(0,\cos\lambda,\sin\lambda)\), and compute \(\vec\omega\times\vec r\) first — it points east, and its magnitude is the speed of the ground at that latitude.

Rung 3

\(-\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) = \omega^2R(0,\ -\sin\lambda\cos\lambda,\ \cos^2\lambda)\). The tilt is the ratio of the horizontal part to \(g\), and \(\sin\lambda\cos\lambda = \tfrac12\sin 2\lambda\) tells you where it peaks without any calculus.

Worked solution

Idea. Resolve one cross product in the local frame, then maximise a product of a sine and a cosine.

1. Take the local frame \(\hat x\) east, \(\hat y\) north, \(\hat z\) up, so \(\vec\omega = \omega(0,\cos\lambda,\sin\lambda)\) and \(\vec r = R\hat z\). Then \(\vec\omega\times\vec r = \omega R(\cos\lambda, 0, 0)\) — pointing east — and \[ -\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r) = -\omega^2R\,(0,\cos\lambda,\sin\lambda)\times(\cos\lambda,0,0) = \omega^2R\,(0,\ -\sin\lambda\cos\lambda,\ \cos^2\lambda). \] So the centrifugal term is \(\omega^2R\cos^2\lambda\) upward and \(\omega^2R\sin\lambda\cos\lambda\) southward (towards the equator) in the northern hemisphere. Its magnitude is \(\omega^2R\cos\lambda\), directed away from the polar axis, as it must be.

2. The tilt angle satisfies \[ \tan\alpha = \frac{\omega^2R\sin\lambda\cos\lambda}{g - \omega^2R\cos^2\lambda} \approx \frac{\omega^2R\sin\lambda\cos\lambda}{g}, \] the approximation costing a relative \(\omega^2R\cos^2\lambda/g = 0.17\%\) at \(45^\circ\). Since \(\sin\lambda\cos\lambda = \tfrac12\sin2\lambda\), the tilt is largest at \(\lambda = 45^\circ\), where \[ \alpha \approx \frac{0.0339157\times0.5}{9.81} = 1.72863\times10^{-3}\ \text{rad} = 0.09904^\circ = 5.94' . \] (Here \(\omega^2R_{\mathrm E} = (7.29212\times10^{-5})^2\times6.378137\times10^6 = 0.0339157\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).)

3. At the equator the whole centrifugal term is vertical and equal to \(0.0339157\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), a reduction of \(0.0339157/9.81 = 0.346\%\). The measured equator-to-pole difference is \(9.832 - 9.780 = 0.052\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), of which rotation explains \(0.0339\), i.e. \(65\%\). The remaining \(0.018\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) is the equatorial bulge: the Earth's equatorial radius exceeds its polar radius by \(21\) km, so an equatorial observer is further from the bulk of the mass, which lowers \(g\) further — but the nearby bulge mass itself raises it, and the net effect of the shape is what the residual measures. Unit 10 computes the bulge's own field from its quadrupole moment.

Check. Dimensions: \(\omega^2R/g\) is dimensionless ✓. Limits: the tilt is zero at the pole (\(\cos\lambda = 0\)) and at the equator (\(\sin\lambda = 0\)), both correct by symmetry ✓. Size: \(6\) arcminutes is a tenth of a degree — a metre of horizontal error over \(580\) m of plumb line, invisible to a builder and unmissable to a geodesist ✓.

exercises: the checkpoint formula, limits, numbers

  1. Compute the Foucault period at \(45.00^\circ\) N, at \(30.00^\circ\) N and at \(5.00^\circ\) N.
  2. A pendulum is to be built whose plane turns through a visible \(1^\circ\) in fifteen minutes. What latitude does that require?
  3. Two pendulums, at \(48.85^\circ\) N and \(48.85^\circ\) S, are started at the same instant in the same plane. How long until their planes are perpendicular?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything is one formula, \(T = 2\pi/(\omega\sin\lambda)\). Part 2 inverts it; part 3 needs one further thought about what happens to the sign in the southern hemisphere.

Rung 2

Use the sidereal day: \(T = 23.93447\ \mathrm h/\sin\lambda\). For part 2, convert "\(1^\circ\) in 15 minutes" into a full period before doing anything else.

Rung 3

Part 3: the two planes turn in opposite senses, so their relative rate is \(2\omega\sin\lambda\). And a plane of swing is a line, not a ray, so "perpendicular" is a relative turn of \(\pi/2\), not \(\pi\).

Worked solution

Idea. One formula: \(T = T_{\text{sid}}/\sin\lambda\), with \(T_{\text{sid}} = 86\,164.09\ \mathrm s = 23.93447\) h.

1.

latitude\(\sin\lambda\)\(T\) (h)
\(45.00^\circ\)\(0.70711\)\(33.848\)
\(30.00^\circ\)\(0.50000\)\(47.869\)
\(5.00^\circ\)\(0.08716\)\(274.62\) (11.4 days)

2. \(1^\circ\) in \(15\) min is a rate of \(\tfrac{\pi}{180}/900 = 1.93925\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\). Setting \(\omega\sin\lambda\) equal to it, \[ \sin\lambda = \frac{1.93925\times10^{-5}}{7.29212\times10^{-5}} = 0.265939 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda = 15.42^\circ , \] north or south. P13 Inertial circles hard

exercises: proof; constant curvature implies a circle, Coriolis alone, a number

A puck slides without friction on a dish that rotates at constant \(\vec\omega = \Omega\hat z\), the dish being shaped so that the normal force exactly cancels gravity and the centrifugal term. The rotating-frame equation then reduces to \(\vec a = -2\vec\omega\times\vec v\), with \(\vec v\) horizontal.

  1. Prove that \(\lVert\vec v\rVert\) is constant.
  2. Prove that the path has constant curvature \(\kappa = 2\Omega/v\), and hence constant radius \(\rho = v/(2\Omega)\).
  3. Prove that a plane curve with constant \(\kappa > 0\) is an arc of a circle of radius \(1/\kappa\). (Consider \(\vec c = \vec r + \rho\hat N\) and differentiate.)
  4. For \(\Omega = 2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(v = 0.20\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), give the radius and the time to go once round, and compare that time with the dish's own rotation period. The ratio is exact — say why.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 are each one line once you have the right lemma from the deck. Part 3 is a proof that a constant-curvature plane curve is a circle — the object to invent is the centre. Part 4 is two numbers and a ratio.

Rung 2

Part 1: what is \(\vec v\cdot(\vec\omega\times\vec v)\)? Part 2: with the speed constant, \(\vec a = \dot v\hat T + (v^2/\rho)\hat N\) has only one term, so \(\lVert\vec a\rVert\) gives \(\rho\) immediately — and \(\lVert\vec a\rVert = 2\Omega v\) because \(\vec\omega \perp \vec v\).

Rung 3

Part 3, first line: in the plane, \(\hat N' = -\kappa\hat T\) (prove it by differentiating \(\hat T\cdot\hat N = 0\) and \(\hat N\cdot\hat N = 1\)). Then put \(\vec c = \vec r + \rho\hat N\) and differentiate with respect to arc length; if \(\vec c\,{}' = 0\) you are finished. For part 4, write the circuit time as \(2\pi\rho/v\) and watch \(v\) cancel.

Worked solution

Idea. Coriolis alone does no work, so the speed is fixed; a force of fixed magnitude always perpendicular to a fixed-speed velocity gives fixed curvature; and fixed curvature in a plane means a circle.

1. \(\vec v\cdot\vec a = \vec v\cdot(-2\vec\omega\times\vec v) = 0\), because \(\vec\omega\times\vec v\) is perpendicular to \(\vec v\). By P5.1, \(\lVert\vec v\rVert\) is constant. \(\square\)

2. Since \(\vec v\) is horizontal and \(\vec\omega = \Omega\hat z\) is vertical, \(\lVert\vec\omega\times\vec v\rVert = \Omega v\), so \(\lVert\vec a\rVert = 2\Omega v\). But \(\vec a = \dot v\hat T + (v^2/\rho)\hat N\) with \(\dot v = 0\) by part 1, so \[ \frac{v^2}{\rho} = 2\Omega v \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho = \frac{v}{2\Omega}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{2\Omega}{v}, \] both constant, since \(v\) and \(\Omega\) are. The motion also stays horizontal: \(\vec a = -2\Omega\hat z\times\vec v\) has no vertical component when \(\vec v\) has none. \(\square\)

3. Take the curve unit-speed in the plane. \(\hat T\) is a unit vector so \(\hat T' = \kappa\hat N\) with \(\hat N \perp \hat T\); and \(\hat N\) is a unit vector so \(\hat N' \perp \hat N\), i.e. \(\hat N' = \alpha\hat T\) for some \(\alpha\). Differentiating \(\hat T\cdot\hat N = 0\) gives \(\kappa + \alpha = 0\), so \(\hat N' = -\kappa\hat T\). Now set \(\vec c = \vec r + \rho\hat N\) with \(\rho = 1/\kappa\) constant: \[ \vec c\,{}' = \hat T + \rho(-\kappa\hat T) = \hat T - \hat T = 0 . \] So \(\vec c\) is a fixed point, and \(\vec r - \vec c = -\rho\hat N\) has constant length \(\rho\), since \(\hat N\) is a unit vector: the curve lies on the circle of centre \(\vec c\) and radius \(\rho\). \(\square\)

4. \(\rho = 0.20/(2\times2) = 0.050\) m, and one circuit takes \[ T_{\text{circle}} = \frac{2\pi\rho}{v} = \frac{2\pi}{2\Omega} = \frac{\pi}{\Omega} = 1.5708\ \mathrm s, \] against the dish's own period \(2\pi/\Omega = 3.1416\) s. The ratio is exactly \(1/2\), and exactly, for every \(v\): the speed cancels between the circumference \(2\pi v/(2\Omega)\) and the speed \(v\). The circuit is traversed clockwise seen from above when \(\Omega > 0\) — opposite to the dish — because \(-2\Omega\hat z\times\vec v\) always points to the right of \(\vec v\).

Check. Dimensions: \(v/\Omega\) is \(\mathrm{L\,T^{-1}\,T} = \mathrm L\) ✓. Limit \(\Omega\to0\): \(\rho\to\infty\), a straight line, correct for a free puck on a non-rotating table ✓. The same formula with \(\Omega \to \omega_{\mathrm E}\sin\lambda\) is the atmospheric inertial circle of the Coriolis slide: \(30/(2\times5.157\times10^{-5}) = 291\) km at \(45^\circ\) N, with a period of \(\pi/(\omega\sin45^\circ) = 16.9\) h — the "inertial oscillation" that oceanographers see in drifter tracks ✓.

Another way (part 3)

Without any Frenet machinery: a unit-speed plane curve can be written \(\hat T(s) = (\cos\phi(s), \sin\phi(s))\) for a differentiable angle \(\phi\), and then \(\kappa = |\phi'|\). Constant \(\kappa\) gives \(\phi = \phi_0 \pm \kappa s\), and integrating \(\vec r\,{}' = \hat T\) gives \(\vec r(s) = \vec c \pm \rho(\sin\phi, -\cos\phi)\), a circle of radius \(\rho\) traversed at unit speed. The version on the slide generalises to space curves; this one does not, but it is the quickest route in the plane.
