03

Where things are: curves, frames, and the frame that lies to you

Differentiation in several variables, the frame a curve carries with it, and the exact price of doing mechanics in a room that is turning.

needs 00~2½ h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./mr problems 03

Unit 00 solved $m\ddot x = F$ in one dimension. Three dimensions are not three copies of that: the force at a point depends on where the point is, and "where" is now a vector. So the first half of this unit builds the calculus of maps $\mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ — the derivative as a linear map, the chain rule as composition, Clairaut's theorem — and the geometry of a single curve in space.

The second half asks a question unit 00 could not even phrase. Newton's law holds in an inertial frame. The room you are in is bolted to a planet that turns once a day. What exactly goes wrong, and by how much? The answer is three extra terms, each of which falls out of differentiating $x_{\mathrm{in}} = R(t)\,x_{\mathrm{rot}}$ twice. Nothing is postulated; the fictitious forces are theorems about $\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}$.

03 · overview

One pendulum, five sections

A 67-metre pendulum hangs in the Panthéon. Released, it swings in a vertical plane — and that plane slowly turns, once every $31.79$ hours. Nothing pushes it sideways.

The floor from above: the swing line 0 to 8 hours after release, turning clockwise at $11.33^\circ$ per hour (computed).

the route, in four steps

1. The room's attitude is a rotation $R(t)$, with $x_{\mathrm{in}} = R(t)\,x_{\mathrm{rot}}$. Differentiating $R^{\!\top}R = I$ shows its rate of turning is one vector, $\vec\omega$.

2. Differentiate $x_{\mathrm{in}}$ twice: in the room, $m\vec a = \vec F$ gains three terms. For a moving bob the one that matters is Coriolis, $-2m\,\vec\omega\times\vec v$.

3. Split $\vec\omega$ at latitude $\lambda$ into a vertical part $\omega_{\mathrm E}\sin\lambda$ and a horizontal part. Only the vertical part turns a horizontal swing; the other only presses up or down.

4. In a frame turning back at $\omega_{\mathrm E}\sin\lambda$ the Coriolis term cancels and the plane is fixed; so on the floor it turns once per $2\pi/(\omega_{\mathrm E}\sin\lambda) = 31.79$ h.

The route, slide by slide. Step 1 is section 3: "What a frame is, and what $SO(3)$ is" sets up $x_{\mathrm{in}} = R\,x_{\mathrm{rot}}$, "$\Omega = R^{\!\top}\dot R$ is antisymmetric" proves the antisymmetry, and the hat-map slide turns the antisymmetric matrix into the vector $\vec\omega$. Step 2 is "Differentiate twice" and "The rotating-frame equation" in section 4. Step 3 is "Coriolis: the force that turns cyclones", whose figure draws the split. Step 4 is "Undo the rotation, and Coriolis is gone" in section 5, and the number is "Worked: 31.8 hours at the Panthéon". Sections 1 and 2 supply the calculus all of this uses: the derivative of a map, the product rule, and the fact that a unit vector can only turn.

Why step 3 is the heart of it. A horizontal velocity crossed with a horizontal vector is vertical, so the horizontal part of $\vec\omega$ can only push the bob up or down, never sideways. Crossed with the vertical part it stays horizontal and is turned through a right angle. That is why the answer has $\sin\lambda$ in it, why it vanishes on the equator, and why at the pole it is the whole of $\omega_{\mathrm E}$.

The problem sheet follows the same order: problems 1–3 are the several-variable calculus, 4–7 the curve geometry, 8–9 the frame algebra, 10–13 the consequences with numbers. Three of them — 5, 8 and 13 — are proofs rather than computations.

The point he was making was not subtle and not new — everyone had believed the Earth turned for two centuries — but it was the first demonstration that used no astronomy at all. 03 · derivatives

Partials are not enough

definition

For $f : \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$, the partial derivative at $a$ is $$\partial_i f(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h e_i) - f(a)}{h},$$ the ordinary derivative of $f$ along the $i$-th coordinate line. The directional derivative $D_uf(a)$ along a unit vector $u$ replaces $e_i$ by $u$.

both partials exist, $f$ is not even continuous

$$f(x,y) = \frac{xy}{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad f(0,0) = 0.$$ On each axis $f \equiv 0$, so $\partial_1 f(0) = \partial_2 f(0) = 0$. On the line $y = x$, $f \equiv \tfrac12$. So $f$ has no limit at the origin.

$f$ depends only on the angle: in polar coordinates it is $\tfrac12\sin 2\varphi$. Every disc round the origin, however small, contains the values $0$, $\tfrac12$ and $-\tfrac12$. The partials only look along the two dark axes.

Reading the figure. Put $x = r\cos\varphi$, $y = r\sin\varphi$: then $f = r^2\cos\varphi\sin\varphi/r^2 = \tfrac12\sin2\varphi$, with no $r$ left. So $f$ is constant along each line through the origin and the value depends on which line. The partial derivatives ask about two of those lines only, where $f \equiv 0$, and are perfectly happy. Each partial derivative restricts $f$ to one line and asks a single-variable question; that is exactly the weakness.

Why this matters here and not in unit 00. In one variable there is one difference quotient and it either converges or does not. In several variables "differentiable" has to mean something stronger than "differentiable along every line", because a function can be smooth along every line through a point and still be badly behaved on a curve through it. The definition on "The derivative is a linear map" is the right one, and the counterexamples on this slide and the next are the reason it is phrased that way.

These two functions come back as problem 1.

03 · derivatives

Even every direction is not enough

every directional derivative exists, still not differentiable

$$g(x,y) = \frac{x^3}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad g(0,0) = 0 .$$ For a unit vector $u = (u_1,u_2)$, $\big(g(tu) - g(0)\big)/t = u_1^3$ exactly, so $D_ug(0) = u_1^3$ for every $u$.

The partials are $\partial_1 g(0) = 1$, $\partial_2 g(0) = 0$. If $g$ had a derivative, it would be the linear map with those entries, and it would give $D_ug(0) = u_1$. Writing $u = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$, the truth is $\cos^3\varphi$, not $\cos\varphi$.

And $g$ is continuous at $0$, since $|g| \le |x|$. Continuity plus every directional derivative still does not make a derivative.

The directional derivative of $g$ at the origin, against the direction $\varphi$ of $u$. A linear map would have to give the dashed $\cos\varphi$; the actual $\cos^3\varphi$ agrees with it only on the axes ($\varphi = 0, \pi/2, \pi, \dots$).

The example, in full. For a unit vector $u = (u_1, u_2)$, $\frac{g(tu) - g(0)}{t} = \frac{1}{t}\cdot\frac{t^3u_1^3}{t^2(u_1^2+u_2^2)} = u_1^3$, so every directional derivative exists and equals $u_1^3$. If $g$ were differentiable at $0$ with derivative $\Lambda$, then $D_ug(0) = \Lambda u$ would be linear in $u$; $u_1^3$ is not. Concretely, linearity would force $D_ug(0) = u_1\,D_{e_1}g(0) + u_2\,D_{e_2}g(0) = u_1$, and at $u = (1,1)/\sqrt2$ that is $0.707$ against the actual $0.354$.

What to look for in the plot. The two curves touch at the four axis directions, where the partials were computed, and nowhere else between them. That is the picture of "the partials exist but do not assemble into a linear map".

03 · derivatives

The derivative is a linear map

definition · the total derivative

$f : U \subseteq \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ is differentiable at $a \in U$ if there is a linear map $Df(a) : \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R^m$ with $$f(a + h) = f(a) + Df(a)\,h + r(h), \qquad \frac{\lVert r(h)\rVert}{\lVert h\rVert} \longrightarrow 0 \ \text{ as } h \to 0 .$$ $Df(a)$ is the linear map that best approximates $f$ near $a$. It is a map, not a number and not a matrix.

uniqueness

There is at most one such $Df(a)$.

Proof. If $\Lambda_1, \Lambda_2$ both work then $(\Lambda_1-\Lambda_2)h = o(\lVert h\rVert)$. Put $h = tu$, $t \to 0$: $(\Lambda_1-\Lambda_2)u = 0$ for every $u$. $\square$

The physics motive: a force field $F : \mathbb R^3 \to \mathbb R^3$ near a point of equilibrium is its derivative, to first order — a $3\times3$ matrix. Every "small oscillations" calculation in this course is that sentence.

For the pendulum that matrix is $-m\omega_0^2 I$ on the horizontal plane; section 5 starts there.

Differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous: $\lVert f(a+h)-f(a)\rVert \le \lVert Df(a)\rVert\lVert h\rVert + \lVert r(h)\rVert \to 0$.

Reading the definition. "$r(h)/\lVert h\rVert \to 0$" is the whole content: the error is not merely small, it is small compared with $\lVert h \rVert$, and in every direction at once, uniformly. That last clause is what the counterexamples of the two previous slides fail. The one-variable case is included: $Df(a)$ is multiplication by $f'(a)$.

On the norm. Which norm on $\mathbb R^n$ does not matter — all norms on a finite-dimensional space are equivalent, so the condition $r(h) = o(\lVert h\rVert)$ is the same statement in any of them. The learner has the metric-space topology this needs.

Why "linear map" and not "matrix" is worth insisting on. The matrix depends on the basis; the map does not. This distinction is quietly doing nothing here and will be doing everything in unit 16, where a tensor is defined as a multilinear map and the component transformation law becomes a theorem about change of basis. The habit starts now: define the object, then compute its components.

The operator norm $\lVert \Lambda \rVert = \sup_{\lVert u \rVert = 1}\lVert \Lambda u\rVert$ is finite for a linear map on a finite-dimensional space, which is what the continuity argument on the slide uses.

03 · derivatives

The Jacobian, and when partials suffice

components

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$ then every $\partial_j f_i(a)$ exists, and the matrix of $Df(a)$ in the standard bases is the Jacobian matrix $$\big[Df(a)\big]_{ij} = \partial_j f_i(a).$$

Proof. Put $h = te_j$ in the definition and divide by $t$: $\big(f(a+te_j)-f(a)\big)/t \to Df(a)e_j$, whose $i$-th entry is $\partial_j f_i(a)$. $\square$

converse, with a hypothesis

If every $\partial_j f_i$ exists on a neighbourhood of $a$ and is continuous at $a$, then $f$ is differentiable at $a$. Such an $f$ is called $C^1$.

full proof in the notes (N)

So in practice: compute partials, check they are continuous, and you may use the Jacobian as the derivative. The counterexamples exist to keep the check honest, not to make life hard.

Full proof of the $C^1$ criterion

Take $m = 1$ (do each component separately). Write $h = (h_1,\dots,h_n)$ and walk from $a$ to $a + h$ one coordinate at a time: with $p_k = a + \sum_{j \le k} h_j e_j$, so $p_0 = a$ and $p_n = a + h$, $$f(a+h) - f(a) = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\big(f(p_k) - f(p_{k-1})\big).$$ Each bracket changes one coordinate only, so the single-variable mean value theorem gives $f(p_k) - f(p_{k-1}) = h_k\,\partial_k f(q_k)$ for some $q_k$ on the segment between $p_{k-1}$ and $p_k$; in particular $\lVert q_k - a \rVert \le \lVert h\rVert$. Hence $$f(a+h) - f(a) - \sum_k h_k \partial_k f(a) = \sum_k h_k\big(\partial_k f(q_k) - \partial_k f(a)\big),$$ whose absolute value is at most $\lVert h\rVert \sum_k |\partial_k f(q_k) - \partial_k f(a)|$. By continuity of the partials at $a$, the sum tends to $0$ as $h \to 0$. So the remainder is $o(\lVert h\rVert)$ and $Df(a)h = \sum_k h_k \partial_k f(a)$. $\square$

The hypothesis is not vacuous: $x^2\sin(1/x)$ extended by $0$ is differentiable everywhere with a discontinuous derivative, so "differentiable" is strictly weaker than $C^1$ even in one variable. In several variables there is a further gap below that one — partials existing without any derivative at all — which is what "Partials are not enough" exhibited.

Every map in this course is $C^\infty$ where it is used, so this theorem is the licence to stop worrying. It is invoked silently from here on.

03 · derivatives

The chain rule, once

theorem · the multivariable chain rule

If $f$ is differentiable at $a$ and $g$ is differentiable at $f(a)$, then $g \circ f$ is differentiable at $a$ and $$D(g\circ f)(a) \;=\; Dg\big(f(a)\big) \circ Df(a) .$$ The derivative of a composite is the composite of the derivatives. In matrices: multiply the Jacobians, in that order.

full proof in the notes (N)

Written in components it is the familiar page of subscripts, $$\partial_k (g_i \circ f)(a) = \sum_{j} \partial_j g_i\big(f(a)\big)\, \partial_k f_j(a),$$ which is just the $(i,k)$ entry of a matrix product. Learn the line above; derive this one when you need it.

the case used constantly below

$\gamma : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R^n$ a curve, $f : \mathbb R^n \to \mathbb R$: then $$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,f(\gamma(t)) = Df(\gamma(t))\,\dot\gamma(t).$$ A $1\times n$ matrix times an $n\times 1$ one: a number.

Full proof of the chain rule

Write $A = Df(a)$, $B = Dg(b)$ with $b = f(a)$. By definition $f(a+h) = b + Ah + r(h)$ with $r(h) = o(\lVert h\rVert)$, and $g(b+k) = g(b) + Bk + s(k)$ with $s(k) = o(\lVert k\rVert)$. Put $k = k(h) = Ah + r(h)$. Then $$g(f(a+h)) = g(b) + B\big(Ah + r(h)\big) + s(k(h)) = g(b) + BAh + \underbrace{B\,r(h) + s(k(h))}_{\text{claim: } o(\lVert h\rVert)} .$$ The first term: $\lVert B r(h)\rVert \le \lVert B\rVert\,\lVert r(h)\rVert = o(\lVert h\rVert)$. The second: $\lVert k(h)\rVert \le (\lVert A\rVert + 1)\lVert h\rVert$ for $h$ small, so $k \to 0$ with $h$, and given $\varepsilon > 0$ we have $\lVert s(k)\rVert \le \varepsilon \lVert k \rVert \le \varepsilon(\lVert A\rVert+1)\lVert h\rVert$ once $\lVert h \rVert$ is small enough. Hence the whole remainder is $o(\lVert h\rVert)$, and by uniqueness $D(g\circ f)(a) = BA$. $\square$

The one place to be careful is that $s$ must be evaluated at $k(h)$, which is not simply $Ah$; the bound $\lVert k(h)\rVert = O(\lVert h \rVert)$ is what makes that harmless.

Where this is used in this unit. Twice, both times invisibly: to differentiate $t \mapsto R(t)x(t)$ (a bilinear map composed with a curve, hence a product rule), and to change to polar coordinates on the problem sheet. It is used visibly from unit 04 onwards, where $\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}V(\gamma(t)) = \nabla V \cdot \dot\gamma$ is the whole reason potential energy works.

Clairaut, and the function that breaks it

Clairaut's theorem

If $\partial_1\partial_2 f$ and $\partial_2\partial_1 f$ exist on a neighbourhood of $a$ and are continuous at $a$, then $$\partial_1\partial_2 f(a) = \partial_2\partial_1 f(a).$$

full proof in the notes (N)

Partial derivatives commute — when the second derivatives are continuous. This is the hypothesis that will later make $\mathrm d^2 = 0$ in unit 34 and kill half the candidate terms in unit 33's curvature identities.

and when they are not

$$f(x,y) = \frac{xy(x^2-y^2)}{x^2+y^2}, \qquad f(0,0) = 0 .$$ Then $\partial_1 f(0,y) = -y$ and $\partial_2 f(x,0) = x$, so $$\partial_2\partial_1 f(0,0) = -1, \qquad \partial_1\partial_2 f(0,0) = +1 .$$

Both mixed partials exist everywhere; neither is continuous at the origin. The theorem is not being evaded, its hypothesis is being failed.

Full proof of Clairaut's theorem

Fix $a = (a_1,a_2)$ and for small $h, k$ define the second difference $$\Delta(h,k) = f(a_1+h,a_2+k) - f(a_1+h,a_2) - f(a_1,a_2+k) + f(a_1,a_2).$$ It is symmetric under swapping the roles of the two coordinates — the same four values with the same signs. Read it one way: with $\varphi(s) = f(s, a_2+k) - f(s, a_2)$ we have $\Delta = \varphi(a_1+h)-\varphi(a_1) = h\,\varphi'(\xi) = h\big(\partial_1 f(\xi, a_2+k) - \partial_1 f(\xi, a_2)\big)$ by the mean value theorem, and a second application in the other variable gives $\Delta = hk\,\partial_2\partial_1 f(\xi,\eta)$ for some $(\xi,\eta)$ in the rectangle. Reading it the other way gives $\Delta = hk\,\partial_1\partial_2 f(\xi',\eta')$ with $(\xi',\eta')$ in the same rectangle. Divide by $hk$ and let $(h,k) \to (0,0)$; both interior points tend to $a$, and by continuity of the two mixed partials at $a$ the limits are $\partial_2\partial_1 f(a)$ and $\partial_1\partial_2 f(a)$. $\square$

The counterexample, computed. For $y \ne 0$, $\partial_1 f(0,y) = \lim_{h\to0} \frac{1}{h}\cdot\frac{hy(h^2-y^2)}{h^2+y^2} = \frac{y(-y^2)}{y^2} = -y$; it is $0$ at $y = 0$ too, so $\partial_1 f(0,y) = -y$ for all $y$ and $\partial_2\partial_1 f(0,0) = -1$. The function is antisymmetric, $f(y,x) = -f(x,y)$, so $\partial_2 f(x,0) = +x$ and $\partial_1\partial_2 f(0,0) = +1$. This is problem 2, where you also check that the mixed partials are genuinely discontinuous at the origin rather than merely unequal there.

The example is Peano's, from 1884; Clairaut had stated the theorem in 1740 without the hypothesis, and Schwarz gave the modern proof. Some books call it Schwarz's theorem for that reason.

Curves

A trajectory is a map $\mathbb R \to \mathbb R^3$. It carries a frame of its own, and that frame splits acceleration in two.

03 · curves

Vector-valued functions and two product rules

definition

A vector-valued function $\gamma : I \to \mathbb R^3$ on an interval is differentiable iff each component is, and then $\dot\gamma = (\dot\gamma_1,\dot\gamma_2,\dot\gamma_3)$. This is the total derivative of $\gamma$: the linear map $t \mapsto t\,\dot\gamma$.

For a trajectory, $\vec v = \dot\gamma$ is the velocity, $v = \lVert\vec v\rVert$ the speed, $\vec a = \ddot\gamma$ the acceleration.

product rules

For differentiable $u, w : I \to \mathbb R^3$ and scalar $f$, $$(f u)^{\textstyle\cdot} = \dot f u + f\dot u, \qquad (u\cdot w)^{\textstyle\cdot} = \dot u\cdot w + u\cdot\dot w,$$ and the same for any bilinear map in place of $\cdot$ — including the cross product of section 3, where the order must be kept.

Proof. Every bilinear $B$ on finite-dimensional spaces satisfies $B(u(t+h),w(t+h)) - B(u,w) = B(\Delta u, w) + B(u, \Delta w) + B(\Delta u, \Delta w)$; divide by $h$ and let $h \to 0$, the last term going as $h$. $\square$

Why bilinearity is the right level of generality. Writing the dot-product rule out in components proves it, but proves it once per bilinear operation. The argument on the slide proves it for all of them at once — dot, cross, matrix product, the action of a matrix on a vector — and the only thing it needs is that $B$ is bilinear and continuous, which every bilinear map on a finite-dimensional space is. The $B(\Delta u, \Delta w)$ term is $O(h^2)$ because $\lVert B(p,q)\rVert \le C \lVert p\rVert \lVert q\rVert$ for some constant $C$ depending only on $B$.

Order matters for the cross product, since $u \times w = -w\times u$. The rule reads $(u\times w)^{\textstyle\cdot} = \dot u \times w + u \times \dot w$, and swapping either factor's position changes the sign. Nearly every sign error in rotating-frame mechanics is a swapped cross product.

Notation: an overdot always means $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$ in this course, and $\vec{\ }$ marks a 3-vector. Later units drop both when the objects stop being 3-vectors.

03 · curves

Constant speed means sideways acceleration

lemma

For a differentiable $u : I \to \mathbb R^3$: $\ \lVert u\rVert$ is constant $\iff u \cdot \dot u = 0$ on $I$.

Proof. $\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\lVert u \rVert^2 = \tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(u\cdot u) = 2\,u\cdot\dot u$ by the product rule. A differentiable function on an interval is constant iff its derivative vanishes, and $\lVert u\rVert$ is constant iff $\lVert u\rVert^2$ is. $\square$

Applied to velocity: a particle moves at constant speed $\iff$ its acceleration is perpendicular to its velocity at every instant.

That is the whole content of "circular motion has centripetal acceleration". No circle has been assumed — only $v = \text{const}$.

The pendulum is the opposite case: at the bottom of its swing $\dot v = 0$ and all of $\vec a$ is normal; towards the ends $v^2/\rho$ dies away and what is left is tangential.

Applied to a unit vector: if $\lVert e(t)\rVert = 1$ then $\dot e \perp e$. A unit vector can only turn.

Used three times below: for $\hat T$, for $\hat N$, and for the columns of a rotation matrix — which is where the antisymmetry of $R^{\!\top}\dot R$ comes from.

This one-line lemma is the smallest true statement in the unit and the most used. It is problem 5's first half; the second half asks you to get from it to the two-term decomposition of acceleration without quoting the Frenet formulas.

A dimension check on the applied version. $u \cdot \dot u$ has units of $[u]^2/\mathrm T$. For $u = \vec v$ that is $\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-3}}$ — which is, up to the mass, power. Constant speed means the force does no work, and unit 04 will say exactly that. The lemma is the kinematic shadow of a statement about energy that cannot yet be phrased.

A trap. "$\vec a \perp \vec v$" is an instantaneous statement and does not by itself make the path a circle: a helix has constant speed and acceleration everywhere perpendicular to velocity, and it is not closed, let alone circular. Its curvature is constant too, so even that is not the missing condition: constant curvature forces a circle only for a curve that also stays in a plane, which is what problem 13 proves.

03 · curves

Arc length and the unit tangent

definition

For a $C^1$ curve $\gamma$ on $[a,b]$ the arc length is $$s(t) = \int_a^t \lVert\dot\gamma(\tau)\rVert\,\mathrm d\tau ,$$ so $\dot s = v \ge 0$. If $v > 0$ throughout, $s$ is strictly increasing, hence invertible, and $\tilde\gamma = \gamma\circ t(s)$ is the unit-speed parametrisation: $\lVert\tilde\gamma'(s)\rVert = 1$.

The unit tangent is $\hat T = \vec v / v = \mathrm d\gamma/\mathrm ds$. It depends on the curve and the direction of travel, not on how fast you go.

check

By the one-variable chain rule and the inverse function rule, $\tilde\gamma'(s) = \dot\gamma\,\frac{\mathrm dt}{\mathrm ds} = \dot\gamma/v$, of norm 1. ✓

Inverting $s(t)$ is exactly unit 00's move: there we inverted $t(x)$ to get $x(t)$ after a quadrature. Here we invert $s(t)$ to reparametrise. In both cases monotonicity is what licenses it, and in both cases the inverse rarely has a formula.

Why arc length is the right parameter. A curve is a set of points; a parametrisation is a way of walking along it. Curvature ought to be a property of the set, so it must be defined using a parameter fixed by the set itself. Arc length is that parameter, unique up to the choice of starting point and direction.

That the integral is the length. The definition above is a definition, and it deserves a word of justification: for a partition $a = t_0 < \dots < t_N = b$ the inscribed polygon has length $\sum \lVert \gamma(t_i)-\gamma(t_{i-1})\rVert$, and each term is $\lVert \int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}\dot\gamma\rVert$, which for a $C^1$ curve tends to $\int \lVert \dot\gamma \rVert$ as the mesh goes to zero. The supremum over partitions — the honest definition of length — agrees with the integral for $C^1$ curves. Cited: this is the rectifiability theorem, proved in any real analysis text (Rudin, Principles, Thm 6.27); nothing later in this course depends on the proof.

Dimensions. $[s] = \mathrm L$, $[\mathrm d/\mathrm ds] = \mathrm L^{-1}$, $\hat T$ dimensionless. Every formula below can be checked by counting how many $\mathrm d/\mathrm ds$ it carries.

03 · curves

Curvature, and the frame a curve carries

definition

At a point where $\hat T' \ne 0$ (primes are $\mathrm d/\mathrm ds$): $$\kappa = \lVert \hat T'\rVert, \qquad \hat N = \hat T'/\kappa, \qquad \hat B = \hat T \times \hat N .$$ $\kappa$ is the curvature and the radius of curvature is $\rho = 1/\kappa$. $\hat B = \hat T\times\hat N$ is the unit vector perpendicular to both that makes the triple right-handed (the cross product is defined properly in section 3). $(\hat T, \hat N, \hat B)$ is the Frenet–Serret frame: an orthonormal, right-handed basis attached to each point of the curve.

$\hat T' \perp \hat T$ by the lemma, so $\hat N$ really is perpendicular to $\hat T$: the principal normal. $\hat B$ is the binormal, and $\hat T, \hat N$ span the osculating plane — the plane the curve is momentarily bending in.

The osculating circle: same point, same tangent, same curvature as the curve (schematic). $\hat N$ points at its centre, a distance $\rho$ away; $\hat B$ points out of the page.

Why $\kappa = \lVert\hat T'\rVert$ deserves the name. $\hat T$ is a unit vector, so $\hat T'$ measures only how fast the direction of travel turns, per unit of distance travelled. For a circle of radius $\rho$ traversed at unit speed, $\hat T$ goes once round the unit sphere per circuit, of length $2\pi\rho$, so $\lVert \hat T'\rVert = 2\pi/(2\pi\rho) = 1/\rho$. Hence the name "radius of curvature", and hence $\rho = 1/\kappa$ is the radius of the circle that best fits the curve there.

Dimensions. $[\kappa] = \mathrm L^{-1}$, $[\rho] = \mathrm L$. A straight line has $\kappa = 0$ and $\rho = \infty$, and the frame is undefined — which is correct, since a straight line does not single out any perpendicular direction.

The degenerate points. $\hat N$ and $\hat B$ are undefined wherever $\kappa = 0$, and can jump across an isolated zero of $\kappa$. That is a real nuisance in differential geometry and irrelevant here: every trajectory in this unit has $\kappa > 0$ where the frame is used. Unit 32 replaces this entire apparatus with a connection, which has no such defect.

The frame is named for Jean Frédéric Frenet (1847) and Joseph Serret (1851), who found it independently.

The Frenet–Serret formulas

theorem

Along a unit-speed curve with $\kappa > 0$, with $\tau = -\hat B'\cdot\hat N$ the torsion: $$\hat T' = \kappa\,\hat N, \qquad \hat N' = -\kappa\,\hat T + \tau\,\hat B, \qquad \hat B' = -\tau\,\hat N .$$

proof

Each of $\hat T', \hat N', \hat B'$ is expanded in the orthonormal basis $(\hat T,\hat N,\hat B)$, so the whole content is the matrix $M$ with $M_{ij} = (\text{$i$-th vector})' \cdot (\text{$j$-th vector})$. Differentiating $e_i \cdot e_j = \delta_{ij}$ gives $e_i'\cdot e_j + e_i \cdot e_j' = 0$: $M$ is antisymmetric, so only three entries are free. $\hat T' = \kappa\hat N$ is the definition of $\kappa, \hat N$, which fixes $M_{12} = \kappa$ and $M_{13} = \hat T'\cdot\hat B = 0$. Naming $M_{23} = \tau$ leaves nothing else. $\square$

The Frenet–Serret formulas say: the frame rotates, and the rotation is described by two numbers. That antisymmetry argument is the same one that will produce $\vec\omega$ in section 3 — watch for it returning.

Reading the proof again. Three orthonormal vectors evolving in $s$ are three columns of an orthogonal matrix $Q(s)$, and $M = Q^{\!\top}Q'$ is antisymmetric for exactly the reason given. An antisymmetric $3\times3$ matrix has three independent entries; the definition of $\hat N$ forces one of them to vanish and names another $\kappa$, leaving $\tau$. So a space curve has exactly two local invariants, and cited: the fundamental theorem of curves says $\kappa(s) > 0$ and $\tau(s)$ determine the curve up to a rigid motion (do Carmo, Differential Geometry of Curves and Surfaces, §1-5).

Torsion, in words. $\tau$ measures the rate at which the curve leaves its osculating plane. $\tau \equiv 0$ iff the curve is planar: if $\tau = 0$ then $\hat B' = 0$, so $\hat B$ is a fixed vector $\hat B_0$, and $(\gamma \cdot \hat B_0)' = \hat T\cdot\hat B_0 = 0$, so $\gamma$ lies in a fixed plane. Dimensions: $[\tau] = \mathrm L^{-1}$, like $\kappa$.

Sign conventions. Some books define $\tau$ with the opposite sign ($\hat B' = +\tau\hat N$). The convention here — the common one, and do Carmo's — makes a right-handed helix have $\tau > 0$. Check any formula you import against a right-handed helix before believing it.

Notice what is not claimed: nothing about $\kappa$ or $\tau$ requires the curve to be a trajectory, and neither of them knows about time. The next slide puts time back.

Acceleration has exactly two parts

theorem

For any trajectory with $v = \lVert\vec v\rVert > 0$, $$\vec a \;=\; \dot v\,\hat T \;+\; \frac{v^2}{\rho}\,\hat N .$$ Speeding up is tangential; turning is normal. There is no third component: $\vec a \cdot \hat B = 0$ always.

The two pieces are the tangential and normal acceleration.
Proof. $\vec v = v\hat T$, so $\vec a = \dot v \hat T + v\dot{\hat T}$; and $\dot{\hat T} = \hat T' \dot s = \kappa v \hat N$ by the chain rule and Frenet. $\square$

The acceleration at one point (schematic): a part along the curve, $\dot v\,\hat T$, and a part towards the centre of the osculating circle, $(v^2/\rho)\,\hat N$. Nothing sticks out of the page.

Reading the figure. The dark arrow is $\vec a$; the two coloured arrows are its components in the Frenet frame, completed by the dashed parallelogram. Change the speed and only the tangential arrow changes; tighten the bend (smaller $\rho$) at the same speed and only the normal arrow grows. The binormal $\hat B$ points out of the page, and the theorem says the acceleration never has a component there: the curve is always, at that instant, accelerating within its own osculating plane.

The physics. Multiply by $m$: the component of force along the motion changes the speed, the component across it changes the direction, and nothing changes the binormal component because there is nothing there to change. A car's engine and brakes act along $\hat T$; its tyres act along $\hat N$; and the friction budget is shared between them, which is the entire content of the "friction circle" that racing drivers are taught.

Three dimensions are not three one-dimensional problems

A projectile with drag: $m\vec{\dot v} = m\vec g - b\lVert\vec v\rVert^{\,n-1}\vec v$.

$n = 1$: linear drag decouples

$m\dot v_i = mg_i - b v_i$ for each $i$ separately: three copies of unit 00's $F(v)$ case, each solved by separation of variables. The horizontal motion knows nothing about the vertical.

$n = 2$: quadratic drag does not

$m\dot v_x = -b\sqrt{v_x^2+v_z^2}\,v_x$ contains $v_z$. Falling faster slows the horizontal motion. No separation, no closed form, and unit 02's qualitative methods or unit 21's asymptotics are what is left.

Which case is real? The dimensionless Reynolds number $\mathcal R = \rho_{\text{air}} v d/\mu$ decides, with $d$ the ball's diameter, $\rho_{\text{air}}$ the air's density and $\mu$ its viscosity: $\mathcal R \lesssim 1$ gives linear drag, $\mathcal R \gtrsim 10^3$ quadratic. For a $22\ \mathrm{cm}$ football at $20\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $\mathcal R \approx 3\times 10^5$ — firmly quadratic.

The lesson for this unit: going to three dimensions is not bookkeeping. It is why a geometric language — curves, frames, linear maps — is worth building before the equations get harder.

The terminal velocity limit still works in both cases: set $\vec{\dot v} = 0$ and solve. For $n=1$, $\vec v_\infty = m\vec g/b$; for $n = 2$, $v_\infty = \sqrt{mg/b}$, straight down.

The Reynolds number, computed. With $\rho_{\text{air}} = 1.2\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, $\mu = 1.8\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s}$, $d = 0.22\ \mathrm m$, $v = 20\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$: $\mathcal R = 1.2\times20\times0.22/1.8\times10^{-5} = 2.9\times10^5$. Quoted above as $3\times10^5$. A falling mist droplet, $d = 20\ \mu\mathrm m$ at $0.01\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, gives $\mathcal R = 0.013$: linear, which is why Stokes' law describes fog and not footballs.

Why $n=1$ separates and $n=2$ does not. The drag force is antiparallel to $\vec v$ in both cases, but only in the linear case is its magnitude a function of one component at a time. Coupling through $\lVert \vec v\rVert$ is the generic situation; the decoupled case is the accident.

03 · frames

What a frame is, and what $SO(3)$ is

definition

A frame is an orthonormal, right-handed basis of $\mathbb R^3$ together with an origin. An inertial frame is one in which Newton's second law holds in the form $m\vec a = \vec F$ with $\vec F$ built only from real interactions.

definition

$$SO(3) = \{R \in \mathbb R^{3\times3} : R^{\!\top}R = I,\ \det R = +1\},$$ a group under matrix multiplication: the special orthogonal group. $R^{\!\top}R = I$ says $R$ preserves the inner product; $\det R = +1$ says it preserves orientation.

Two frames sharing an origin are related by exactly one $R \in SO(3)$: if $x_{\mathrm{rot}}$ are a vector's components in one and $x_{\mathrm{in}}$ in the other, then $$\boxed{\;x_{\mathrm{in}} = R\,x_{\mathrm{rot}}\;}$$ and if the frames move relative to each other, $R = R(t)$.

The names anticipate the use: "in" for an inertial frame, "rot" for one that turns with the room.

Why $\det = +1$ and not $-1$. $O(3)$ has two connected components. A frame that moves continuously from another cannot change handedness on the way, so a physically realisable relative motion lives in the identity component, $SO(3)$. The learner's group theory says the rest: $SO(3)$ is a normal subgroup of index 2 in $O(3)$, with quotient $\{\pm1\}$ recording handedness.

Does an inertial frame exist? Strictly, no: the laboratory rotates with the Earth, the Earth orbits the Sun, the Sun orbits the galaxy. What the definition means in practice is "inertial to the accuracy you need", and this unit's business is to compute exactly how large the error is for the first of those. Unit 30 reopens the question and answers it differently: there is no global inertial frame in the presence of gravity, only local ones, and that is the equivalence principle.

The convention. Different books put $R$ or $R^{\!\top}$ in $x_{\mathrm{in}} = R x_{\mathrm{rot}}$. Fix one and never change it; every sign in section 4 depends on this choice. Here: $R$ maps rotating-frame components to inertial-frame components. With the other convention every $\Omega$ below picks up a minus sign and $\vec\omega$ reverses.

$\Omega = R^{\!\top}\dot R$ is antisymmetric

theorem

Let $R : I \to SO(3)$ be differentiable. Then $\Omega(t) = R(t)^{\!\top}\dot R(t)$ is antisymmetric: $\Omega^{\!\top} = -\Omega$.

Proof. Differentiate $R^{\!\top}R = I$ with the product rule: $\dot R^{\!\top}R + R^{\!\top}\dot R = 0$, i.e. $(R^{\!\top}\dot R)^{\!\top} = -R^{\!\top}\dot R$. $\square$

definition

$$\mathfrak{so}(3) = \{A \in \mathbb R^{3\times3} : A^{\!\top} = -A\},$$ the space of antisymmetric matrices, of dimension 3: the diagonal vanishes and the three entries above it are free.

$\dot R$ is not in $SO(3)$ and is not a rotation. But $R^{\!\top}\dot R$ lies in a 3-dimensional space — and three numbers is exactly what an angular velocity vector should be.

This is the lemma on "constant speed" again, in disguise: the columns of $R$ are unit vectors, so each column's derivative is perpendicular to it — the diagonal of $\Omega$ vanishes — and each pair is orthogonal, which pairs up the off-diagonal entries.

Same argument as the Frenet proof, third appearance. The moving-frame trade: orthonormality plus differentiation gives antisymmetry.

The other one. $\tilde\Omega = \dot R R^{\!\top}$ is antisymmetric too, by the same computation applied to $RR^{\!\top} = I$, and $\tilde\Omega = R\Omega R^{\!\top}$. They are the body and space angular velocities, and they differ: $\Omega$ has components in the rotating frame, $\tilde\Omega$ in the inertial one. Problem 8 proves $\tilde\Omega$ corresponds to $R\vec\omega$, which is exactly the statement that $\vec\omega$ is a genuine vector rather than a triple of numbers that happens to have three entries.

Group-theoretic context, for the reader who wants it. $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is the tangent space to $SO(3)$ at the identity — its Lie algebra — and $\Omega$ is $\dot R$ transported back to the identity by left translation. None of that language is needed here, and it is not used again until unit 31. What is needed is the dimension count: a curve in $SO(3)$ has, at each instant, three degrees of freedom of velocity, no more.

Why $R^{\!\top}\dot R$ and not $\dot R$ alone. $\dot R$ is a general $3\times3$ matrix with nine entries and no structure. Multiplying by $R^{\!\top}$ undoes the rotation already accumulated and leaves only the rate, which is where the structure is.

$\mathfrak{so}(3)$ is $\mathbb R^3$: the hat map and the cross product

definition · the hat map

$$\widehat{\ \cdot\ } : \mathbb R^3 \to \mathfrak{so}(3), \qquad \hat\omega = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega_3 & \omega_2 \\ \omega_3 & 0 & -\omega_1 \\ -\omega_2 & \omega_1 & 0\end{pmatrix},$$ and the cross product is the bilinear map $\ \vec u \times \vec w := \hat u\,w$.

theorem

$\widehat{\ \cdot\ }$ is a linear isomorphism $\mathbb R^3 \to \mathfrak{so}(3)$. Consequently every antisymmetric $3\times3$ matrix is "cross with a unique vector".

Proof. Linear by inspection; injective since $\hat\omega = 0$ forces every $\omega_i = 0$; and $\dim\mathbb R^3 = \dim\mathfrak{so}(3) = 3$. $\square$

Immediate consequences, each one line from the matrix:

  • $\vec u\times\vec w = -\vec w\times\vec u$, so $\vec u\times\vec u = 0$;
  • $\vec u \cdot (\vec u \times \vec w) = 0$, since $u^{\!\top}\hat u\,w = -u^{\!\top}\hat u^{\!\top}w = 0$;
  • $\lVert \vec u\times\vec w\rVert = \lVert\vec u\rVert\lVert\vec w\rVert\sin\theta$;
  • $\hat e_1 e_2 = e_3$: right-handed.

The further identities — $\vec u\times(\vec w\times \vec z)$ and friends — arrive in unit 08, where they earn their keep. Only the four above are used here.

Definition first, formula second. The cross product is usually introduced by a mnemonic determinant. Here it is defined as the action of the unique antisymmetric matrix attached to a vector, which is why it exists only in three dimensions: $\dim\mathfrak{so}(n) = n(n-1)/2$, and that equals $n$ only when $n = 3$. In every other dimension "the axis of a rotation" is not a vector, and the cross product does not exist. Unit 28 meets the general object — an antisymmetric $(0,2)$-tensor — and unit 34 names it a 2-form.

Full proof of the norm identity

Expand $\lVert \hat u w\rVert^2$ directly: it equals $(u_2w_3-u_3w_2)^2 + (u_3w_1-u_1w_3)^2 + (u_1w_2-u_2w_1)^2$, and multiplying out and adding $(\vec u\cdot\vec w)^2 = (u_1w_1+u_2w_2+u_3w_3)^2$ gives $\lVert\vec u\rVert^2\lVert\vec w\rVert^2$ — every cross term cancels. This is Lagrange's identity, $\lVert\vec u\times\vec w\rVert^2 + (\vec u\cdot\vec w)^2 = \lVert\vec u\rVert^2\lVert\vec w\rVert^2$. With $\vec u\cdot\vec w = \lVert\vec u\rVert\lVert\vec w\rVert\cos\theta$ it gives $\lVert\vec u\times\vec w\rVert = \lVert\vec u\rVert\lVert\vec w\rVert|\sin\theta|$, and $\theta \in [0,\pi]$ makes the modulus unnecessary. $\square$

Dimensions. $\times$ multiplies dimensions like a product: $[\vec\omega\times\vec r] = \mathrm{T^{-1}\,L} = \mathrm{L\,T^{-1}}$, a velocity, as the transport theorem needs.

Handedness. The cross product changes sign under a reflection, which is why it is sometimes called a pseudovector. The honest statement is that $\hat u$ is the real object and the vector $\vec u$ is its image under an isomorphism that depends on an orientation. Unit 28 makes this precise.

The transport theorem

theorem · the transport theorem

Let $u(t)$ be the rotating-frame components of a vector, $R(t) \in SO(3)$, and $\vec\omega$ the vector with $\hat\omega = R^{\!\top}\dot R$. Then the inertial-frame rate of change, expressed in rotating-frame components, is $$\left(\frac{\mathrm d\vec u}{\mathrm dt}\right)_{\!\text{in}} = \left(\frac{\mathrm d\vec u}{\mathrm dt}\right)_{\!\text{rot}} + \vec\omega\times\vec u .$$

Proof. The inertial components are $Ru$; differentiate: $(Ru)^{\textstyle\cdot} = \dot Ru + R\dot u = R(R^{\!\top}\dot R\,u + \dot u) = R(\dot u + \vec\omega\times\vec u)$. Multiplying back by $R^{\!\top}$ gives the statement. $\square$

dimensions

$[\vec\omega\times\vec u] = [u]/\mathrm T$ ✓, matching both derivatives.

limit $\vec\omega \to 0$

The two derivatives agree: a non-rotating frame tells the truth. ✓

test $\vec u = \vec\omega$

$\vec\omega\times\vec\omega = 0$, so $\dot{\vec\omega}$ is the same in both frames. ✓

What the theorem is really saying. "The rate of change of a vector" is not well defined until you say which frame's basis is being held fixed. The next slide draws the special case that makes it memorable.

It applies to any vector, not just position: velocity, angular momentum, a magnetic field. Applying it to $\vec v$ is the second route through "Differentiate twice" in section 4, and applying it to the angular momentum of a rigid body is how unit 16 gets Euler's equations.

The third check is not a triviality. It says the angular acceleration needs no frame label — a fact used silently in every textbook and proved here in four characters.

Notation warning. The right-hand side mixes two things that look alike: $(\mathrm d\vec u/\mathrm dt)_{\text{rot}}$ means "differentiate the component list $u(t)$", full stop. The subscripts are not decoration, and dropping them is how the theorem gets misremembered as $\dot u = \dot u + \omega\times u$.

A vector whose rotating components change has two contributions to its inertial rate: the change you see in the room, $(\mathrm d\vec u/\mathrm dt)_{\text{rot}}$, and the turning of the room's axes underneath it, $\vec\omega\times\vec u$. They add.

Reading the figure. $\vec\omega$ points out of the page (the right-hand rule for an anticlockwise turn). $\vec\omega\times\vec u$ is then $u$ turned by $+90^\circ$ in the plane, scaled by $\omega$ — exactly the direction the tip of $u$ is being carried. For a point at distance $r_\perp$ from the axis the speed is $\omega r_\perp$, the familiar rigid-rotation formula, now a corollary.

Why the general case is just addition. Write the inertial components as $Ru$. The product rule splits $(Ru)^{\textstyle\cdot}$ into $\dot R u$ (the axes turning, $u$ held fixed — this slide) and $R\dot u$ (the components changing, the axes held fixed — the room's own derivative). That is the one-line proof on the previous slide, read as a picture.

Differentiate twice

Write $x$ for $x_{\mathrm{rot}}$, so $x_{\mathrm{in}} = R(t)x$. Differentiating twice with the product rule: $$\ddot x_{\mathrm{in}} = \ddot R x + 2\dot R\dot x + R\ddot x .$$ Multiply by $R^{\!\top}$ to get inertial acceleration in rotating components: $$R^{\!\top}\ddot x_{\mathrm{in}} = \big(R^{\!\top}\ddot R\big)x + 2\Omega\dot x + \ddot x .$$

the one identity needed

$$R^{\!\top}\ddot R = \dot\Omega + \Omega^2 .$$

Proof. $\dot\Omega = \tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(R^{\!\top}\dot R) = \dot R^{\!\top}\dot R + R^{\!\top}\ddot R$, and $\dot R^{\!\top}\dot R = (R\Omega)^{\!\top}(R\Omega) = \Omega^{\!\top}\Omega = -\Omega^2$ using $\dot R = R\Omega$ and antisymmetry. $\square$

Substituting, and translating every $\Omega$ into a cross product:

$$R^{\!\top}\ddot x_{\mathrm{in}} = \ddot x + 2\vec\omega\times\dot{\vec x} + \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec x) + \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec x .$$

Newton's law in the inertial frame is $m\,\ddot x_{\mathrm{in}} = F_{\mathrm{in}}$; multiplying by $R^{\!\top}$ turns both sides into rotating components. Rearranged, that is the boxed result on the next slide.

Four terms, and every one of them came from the product rule. Nothing was postulated.

The step that is easy to fumble. $\Omega^2 x = \hat\omega(\hat\omega x) = \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec x)$, and $\dot\Omega x = \dot{\hat\omega}x = \widehat{\dot{\vec\omega}}\,x = \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec x$ because the hat map is linear, so it commutes with $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$.

A second route, for cross-checking. Apply the transport theorem twice instead. With $\vec v_{\text{in}} = \dot{\vec x} + \vec\omega\times\vec x$ (transport applied to $\vec x$), apply transport again to $\vec v_{\text{in}}$: $$\vec a_{\text{in}} = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\dot{\vec x}+\vec\omega\times\vec x\big) + \vec\omega\times\big(\dot{\vec x}+\vec\omega\times\vec x\big) = \ddot{\vec x} + \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec x + 2\vec\omega\times\dot{\vec x} + \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec x),$$ the same four terms. The factor 2 in the Coriolis term appears because $\vec\omega\times\dot{\vec x}$ arises once from each application. That is problem 9, and it is worth doing both ways once: the matrix route shows where the terms come from, the transport route shows why there are exactly four.

A frame that also translates. If the origins differ by $d(t)$, add $-m\ddot d$ to the right-hand side of the next slide's equation. Nothing else changes, because translation commutes with the whole calculation. A lift accelerating upwards is that term alone, and it is the seed of unit 30's equivalence principle.

The rotating-frame equation

theorem

In a frame whose attitude relative to an inertial frame is $R(t)$, with $\hat\omega = R^{\!\top}\dot R$, a particle of mass $m$ obeys $$m\,\vec a_{\mathrm{rot}} \;=\; \vec F \;\underbrace{-\;2m\,\vec\omega\times\vec v_{\mathrm{rot}}}_{\text{\small Coriolis}} \;\underbrace{-\;m\,\vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec r)}_{\text{\small centrifugal}} \;\underbrace{-\;m\,\dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec r}_{\text{\small Euler}} ,$$ with $\vec F$ the real force in rotating-frame components. The three extra terms are the fictitious forces.

dimensions

$m\omega v = \mathrm{M\,T^{-1}\,L\,T^{-1}}$ ✓; $m\omega^2 r$ ✓; $m\dot\omega r$ ✓. All $\mathrm{M\,L\,T^{-2}}$.

limit $\vec\omega\to0$

All three vanish: $m\vec a = \vec F$ returns. ✓

case: at rest, $\vec\omega$ constant

Only centrifugal survives, magnitude $m\omega^2 r_\perp$, pointing away from the axis. ✓

they are not forces

Nothing exerts them; no third-law partner exists; they are proportional to $m$ for the same reason weight is, which is a hint unit 30 will collect. They are the price of writing $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$ in a basis that is itself moving.

Every symbol, once. $\vec r$ is the particle's position in rotating-frame components; $\vec v_{\mathrm{rot}} = \dot{\vec r}$ and $\vec a_{\mathrm{rot}} = \ddot{\vec r}$ are its component-wise derivatives — what an observer holding a stopwatch and a ruler in the room measures; $\vec F$ is the real force (gravity, tension, normal force), written in the same basis.

Why the names. Coriolis published the $2\vec\omega\times\vec v$ term in 1835 in a paper about waterwheels, not about weather. "Centrifugal" is Huygens' word, from 1659. The Euler term has no agreed name; some books call it the azimuthal or transverse force.

The structure worth remembering. Coriolis depends on velocity but not position; centrifugal on position but not velocity; Euler on position and on $\dot{\vec\omega}$ only. So on a steadily rotating Earth the Euler term is zero, and the two that remain split cleanly by what they depend on.

A number for the third check. On the equator, $r_\perp = R_{\mathrm E} = 6.378\times10^6\ \mathrm m$ and $\omega_{\mathrm E}^2 R_{\mathrm E} = 0.033916\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, which is $0.346\%$ of $g$. Small, and not zero: it is the next slide.

definition

Coriolis: the force that turns cyclones

In the local frame (east, north, up), $\vec\omega = \omega(0,\cos\lambda,\sin\lambda)$. For a horizontal velocity $\vec v = (v_x, v_y, 0)$, $$-2\vec\omega\times\vec v = 2\omega\sin\lambda\,(v_y,\,-v_x,\,0) \;+\; 2\omega\cos\lambda\,(0,0,v_x).$$ The first part rotates $\vec v$ by $-90^\circ$ in the horizontal plane: in the northern hemisphere, a Coriolis force always to the right of the motion, of magnitude $2m\omega\sin\lambda\,v$. The second is vertical — the Eötvös effect — and is absorbed into $g_{\text{eff}}$.

Only $\omega\sin\lambda$, the vertical component of $\vec\omega$, turns a horizontal plane. Hold onto that: it is the whole of section 5.

$\vec\omega$ is a free vector, so copy it to Paris and split it there. The part along the local vertical, $\omega\sin\lambda$, turns horizontal motion sideways; the part along local north, $\omega\cos\lambda$, lies in the horizontal plane and can only push up or down.

The cross product, done slowly. With $\vec\omega = \omega(0,\cos\lambda,\sin\lambda)$ and $\vec v = (v_x,v_y,0)$: $\vec\omega\times\vec v = \omega(\cos\lambda\cdot 0 - \sin\lambda\cdot v_y,\ \sin\lambda\cdot v_x - 0,\ 0 - \cos\lambda\cdot v_x) = \omega(-v_y\sin\lambda,\ v_x\sin\lambda,\ -v_x\cos\lambda)$. Multiply by $-2$ to get the slide's expression.

Reading the figure. The angle between the polar axis and the local horizontal at latitude $\lambda$ is $\lambda$ itself — the same angle as at the centre, because the local vertical is the radius and the axis meets the equatorial plane at a right angle. So the components are $\omega\cos\lambda$ along north and $\omega\sin\lambda$ along up, which is the $(0, \cos\lambda, \sin\lambda)$ above. A horizontal $\vec v$ crossed with the northward part gives a vertical vector (the Eötvös term); crossed with the upward part it gives a horizontal one at right angles to $\vec v$.

Why "right" is not a convention. Take $\vec v$ due north, $(0,v,0)$, in the northern hemisphere. The horizontal Coriolis term is $2\omega\sin\lambda(v, 0, 0)$: due east, which is to the right of north. The sign flips with $\sin\lambda$, so it is to the left in the southern hemisphere.

Coriolis: why storms turn anticlockwise

a storm, in numbers

A $30\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ wind at $45^\circ$ N: Coriolis acceleration $2\omega v\sin\lambda = 3.09\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Left alone it would bend the air onto an inertial circle of radius $$\frac{v}{2\omega\sin\lambda} = 291\ \mathrm{km}.$$

Air pushed inward by a low-pressure centre and deflected right instead circulates anticlockwise (northern hemisphere), clockwise in the south, and not at all at the equator, where $\sin\lambda = 0$.

The Rossby number $\mathcal{R}o = v/(2\omega\sin\lambda\,\ell)$, with $\ell$ the size of the flow, compares inertia with Coriolis: $0.29$ for a $1000\ \mathrm{km}$ storm, $\sim 10^4$ for water leaving a bath. Bathtubs are not Coriolis.

Parcels of air released round a low L (computed, with friction; parameters schematic). Each is pushed straight in, is turned to its own right the whole way, and the four together circle L anticlockwise.

What the figure computes. Each parcel obeys $\ddot{\vec r} = -G\hat r - 2\omega_z\hat z\times\dot{\vec r} - \gamma\dot{\vec r}$: a constant pull towards L, the horizontal Coriolis term of the previous slide, and a friction term that lets it spiral in rather than orbit. The constants are chosen to make the picture legible, not to match a real storm. What does not depend on them is the sense: deflection to the right of an inward push is anticlockwise circulation.

Numbers, recomputed. $2\omega v\sin 45^\circ$ with $v = 30$: $3.0937\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$; radius $v/(2\omega\sin45^\circ) = 290.9\ \mathrm{km}$; Rossby number with $\ell = 10^6\ \mathrm m$: $0.291$.

The bathtub. With $v \sim 0.1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and $\ell \sim 0.1\ \mathrm m$, $\mathcal{R}o \sim 10^4$: Coriolis is four orders of magnitude below the residual swirl left by filling the bath. It has been measured, in 1962, by Shapiro at MIT — in a symmetric tank, filled and then left still for 24 hours. The circulation is real and it is about $10^{-4}$ of the relevant scale.

a roundabout

The pendulum's two horizontal equations

A Foucault pendulum is a bob on a wire of length $L$, free to swing in any vertical plane. Displace it horizontally by $\vec r = (x,y)$, with $\lVert\vec r\rVert \ll L$. The wire's tension is $T \approx mg$, and its horizontal component is $-T\vec r/L$: a restoring force $-m\omega_0^2\vec r$ with $\omega_0^2 = g/L$.

Adding the horizontal Coriolis term and writing $\omega_z = \omega\sin\lambda$: $$\ddot{\vec r} = -\omega_0^2\,\vec r \;-\; 2\omega_z\,\hat z\times\dot{\vec r}. $$ In components, $\ \ddot x = -\omega_0^2x + 2\omega_z\dot y$, $\ \ddot y = -\omega_0^2 y - 2\omega_z\dot x$.

$\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$ is forced by dimensions alone (unit 00): $g$ and $L$ admit exactly one frequency. That the motion is sinusoidal is unit 06's business, and is not needed here.

Side view, angle exaggerated. $T$ and its horizontal part form a triangle similar to the one made by $L$ and $r$, so the horizontal part is $-T\,r/L$.

Reading the figure. The wire, the vertical through the pivot and the horizontal displacement $r$ make a right triangle with hypotenuse $L$; the tension and its two components make a similar one with hypotenuse $T$. So the horizontal component is $T\sin\theta = T\,r/L$, pointing back towards the vertical, exactly — the small-angle approximation enters only in $T \approx mg$.

The tension, more carefully. With the wire at angle $\theta$ to the vertical, the vertical balance is $T\cos\theta = mg$ (to lowest order in $\dot\theta^2$) and the horizontal force is $-T\sin\theta\,\hat r = -mg\tan\theta\,\hat r$. For small $\theta$, $\tan\theta \approx \lVert \vec r\rVert/L$, giving $-mg\vec r/L$. The neglected corrections are relative $O(\theta_0^2)$, and $\theta_0 = 6/67 = 0.0896$ gives $\theta_0^2 = 0.8\%$ — far larger than the Coriolis corrections above. The frequency shift they produce is smaller, $\theta_0^2/16 = 0.05\%$ (the standard anharmonic result, derived in unit 21), but it depends on amplitude, and that is why real Foucault pendulums need long wires and small swings: an amplitude-dependent frequency makes an elliptical orbit precess on its own, with no connection to the Earth's rotation at all.

Notation. $\hat z\times\dot{\vec r}$ for a horizontal $\dot{\vec r} = (\dot x,\dot y,0)$ is $(-\dot y,\dot x,0)$: a $+90^\circ$ rotation. The minus sign in front makes the deflection clockwise from above in the northern hemisphere.

Undo the rotation: the proof

proof

Apply the transport theorem with the ground frame in the "inertial" role. With $\vec\Omega_F = -\omega_z\hat z$ constant, $\vec a_{\text{ground}} = \vec a_F + 2\vec\Omega_F\times\vec v_F + \vec\Omega_F\times(\vec\Omega_F\times\vec r)$ and $\vec v_{\text{ground}} = \vec v_F + \vec\Omega_F\times\vec r$. Substitute both into $\vec a_{\text{ground}} = -\omega_0^2\vec r - 2\omega_z\hat z\times\vec v_{\text{ground}}$:

$2\vec\Omega_F\times\vec v_F = -2\omega_z\hat z\times\vec v_F$ on the left cancels $-2\omega_z\hat z\times\vec v_F$ on the right. What is left uses $\hat z\times(\hat z\times\vec r) = -\vec r$ for horizontal $\vec r$: the two remaining terms are $-\omega_z^2\vec r_F$ on the left and $-2\omega_z^2\vec r_F$ on the right. Collecting, $\ddot{\vec r}_F = -(\omega_0^2+\omega_z^2)\vec r_F$. $\square$

why "a fixed plane"

Let $\hat n$ be horizontal with $\hat n\cdot\vec r_F(0) = \hat n\cdot\dot{\vec r}_F(0) = 0$, and $f = \hat n\cdot\vec r_F$. Then $\ddot f = -\omega_1^2 f$, writing $\omega_1^2 = \omega_0^2+\omega_z^2$, with $f(0)=\dot f(0) = 0$. Unit 00's trick — multiply by $\dot f$ and integrate — gives $\dot f^2 + \omega_1^2f^2 = 0$, constant and equal to $0$. So $f\equiv 0$. ✓

The correction $\omega_z^2$ is a relative $(\omega_z/\omega_0)^2 = 2.1\times10^{-8}$ on the swing frequency. It is written down because it is free, and then never used again.

The cancellation, written out. Left-hand side after substituting: $\vec a_F - 2\omega_z\hat z\times\vec v_F + \omega_z^2\hat z\times(\hat z\times\vec r)$. Right-hand side: $-\omega_0^2\vec r - 2\omega_z\hat z\times\vec v_F - 2\omega_z\hat z\times(-\omega_z\hat z\times\vec r)$. The middle terms are identical and cancel. With $\hat z\times(\hat z\times\vec r) = -\vec r$, the left keeps $-\omega_z^2\vec r$ and the right keeps $-2\omega_z^2\vec r$, so $\vec a_F = -\omega_0^2\vec r - 2\omega_z^2\vec r + \omega_z^2\vec r$.

The energy trick is unit 00's, exactly. There it turned $m\ddot x = F(x)$ into a first integral by multiplying by $\dot x$. Here the same multiplication proves a uniqueness statement without any theory of ODEs: $\dot f^2 + \omega_1^2 f^2$ is a non-negative constant that starts at zero. Unit 01's Picard–Lindelöf would also do it, and is not a prerequisite of this unit.

What Foucault actually saw

the pendulum does not swing in a plane

Released from rest relative to the ground, the bob is not at rest in $F$: $\vec v_F(0) = -\vec\Omega_F\times\vec r(0) = \omega_z\hat z\times\vec r(0)$, of size $\omega_z A$. So in $F$ the orbit is a thin ellipse, not a segment, with

$$\frac{\text{minor}}{\text{major}} \approx \frac{\omega_z}{\omega_0} = 1.4\times10^{-4},$$ which at $A = 6\ \mathrm m$ is a minor semi-axis of $0.86\ \mathrm{mm}$. (That the orbit is an ellipse is unit 06's isotropic oscillator; only its aspect ratio is needed here, and it follows from the initial data.)

The bob never passes through the centre: each pass misses it by the minor semi-axis, and each turning point is a sharp cusp, where the bob is momentarily at rest on the floor.

The path on the floor when the bob is released from rest (computed; the turning rate exaggerated about 1000-fold, which fattens the ellipse to match). Cusps at the ends; the dashed circle at the centre is never entered.

The initial velocity, from the transport theorem. $\vec v_{\text{ground}} = \vec v_F + \vec\Omega_F\times\vec r$. Setting $\vec v_{\text{ground}}(0) = 0$ with $\vec\Omega_F = -\omega_z\hat z$ gives $\vec v_F(0) = \omega_z\hat z\times\vec r(0)$, perpendicular to the displacement and of magnitude $\omega_z A = 5.4906\times10^{-5}\times 6 = 3.29\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. For an oscillator of frequency $\omega_1 \approx \omega_0$, a transverse velocity $u$ at the turning point produces a transverse excursion $u/\omega_0 = 8.6\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm m$ — the $0.86\ \mathrm{mm}$ quoted.

Reading the figure. In $F$ the path is the thin ellipse $x_F = A\cos\omega_1t$, $y_F = (\omega_z A/\omega_1)\sin\omega_1 t$, the isotropic oscillator's orbit for this initial data (that it is an ellipse is quoted from unit 06). Carried back to the floor by the turn $-\omega_z t$, it becomes the rosette. At each end the ellipse's sideways velocity $\omega_z A$ is exactly cancelled by the floor's turning, which is why the bob comes to rest there and the path has a cusp. The ratio $\omega_z/\omega_1$ is drawn as $0.15$; the real $1.4\times10^{-4}$ would make the central circle $0.86\ \mathrm{mm}$ in radius inside a $12\ \mathrm m$ swing.

Honest labelling. The claim that the orbit is exactly an ellipse requires solving $\ddot{\vec r} = -\omega_1^2\vec r$, which needs unit 06. What is proved here is the statement of "Undo the rotation, and Coriolis is gone" — the swing plane is fixed in $F$ when the initial velocity is along the initial displacement — plus the size of the initial transverse velocity, which is exact.

03 · foucault

Burning the thread, and the Charron ring

The bob never passes through the centre. Foucault knew, and launched his pendulum by burning a thread holding it aside — the only way to release it with no sideways velocity at all.

Even then, the amplitude-dependent frequency of a real pendulum — a $0.05\%$ shift here — makes any residual ellipse precess on its own, in a direction set by which way the bob was nudged. Every serious installation fights this: the Paris pendulum uses a "Charron ring", a metal collar that the wire touches at the bottom of each swing and which bleeds off the elliptical component.

The physics of "Undo the rotation" is exact to the order stated. This slide is why a demonstration is harder than a derivation.

the drift that competes

A real pendulum's frequency depends on its amplitude, so an elliptical orbit precesses on its own, at a rate proportional to its area: $\tfrac38\,ab\,\omega_0/L^2$ for semi-axes $a$, $b$ (cited, Airy 1851). A $1\ \mathrm{cm}$ minor axis at the Panthéon gives $3.5\%$ of the Foucault rate; the $0.86\ \mathrm{mm}$ one forced by release, $0.3\%$.

Why the ellipse is a problem and not a curiosity. An anharmonic pendulum's frequency depends on amplitude, so the two perpendicular components of an elliptical orbit run at slightly different rates, and the ellipse's axis rotates at a rate proportional to its area: $\tfrac38\,ab\,\omega_0/L^2$ for semi-axes $a$ and $b$. Cited: that is Airy's result of 1851, and it is the anharmonic perturbation theory of unit 21 applied to two coupled coordinates. At the Panthéon, a $1\ \mathrm{cm}$ minor axis against the $6\ \mathrm m$ major one gives $1.92\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ — $3.5\%$ of the Foucault rate, already fatal to a careful measurement — and since it grows in proportion to the minor axis it reaches the Foucault rate itself at about $30\ \mathrm{cm}$. The $0.86\ \mathrm{mm}$ ellipse forced by the release above contributes only $0.3\%$. The Charron ring works by interrupting the orbit once per swing near the centre, where the elliptical component is fastest.
