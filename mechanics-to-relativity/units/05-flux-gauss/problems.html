Integrating over regions

P1 Swapping the order routine

exercises: Fubini on a non-box region, redescribing a region by its other coordinate

  1. Evaluate \[ \int_0^1\!\!\int_x^1 e^{y^2}\,\mathrm{d}y\,\mathrm{d}x . \] The inner integral has no elementary antiderivative, so the order must be reversed. Sketch the region first, then describe it the other way round.
  2. Let \(D\) be the region bounded by \(y = x^2\), \(y = 1\) and \(x = 0\). Compute \(\int_D xy\,\mathrm{d}A\) in both orders and check the two agree.
  3. In part 2 the two sets of limits are different functions. State, in one sentence, what it is that the two computations have in common — that is, what Fubini's theorem actually asserts is equal.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a number. Nothing will make \(\int e^{y^2}\mathrm dy\) elementary, so the inner variable must be the one that comes out easily — and there is only one other choice.

Rung 2

Draw the region. \(\{0 \le x \le 1,\ x \le y \le 1\}\) is a triangle; describe the same triangle by fixing \(y\) first and asking which \(x\) belong to it. After the swap the inner integral has a constant integrand.

Rung 3

First line: the triangle is \(\{0 \le x \le y \le 1\}\), so \(\int_0^1\!\int_0^y e^{y^2}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy\), and the inner integral is \(y\,e^{y^2}\), which is a substitution away.

Worked solution

Idea. Fubini says the double integral equals either iterated integral. The limits are not part of the theorem — they are a description of the region, and a region has two descriptions. Choosing the second is free, and sometimes it is the difference between an elementary answer and none.

1. The region is \(\{(x,y) : 0 \le x \le 1,\ x \le y \le 1\}\), the triangle with vertices \((0,0)\), \((0,1)\), \((1,1)\) — equivalently \(\{0 \le x \le y \le 1\}\). Read by \(y\) first: \[ \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^y e^{y^2}\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy = \int_0^1 y\,e^{y^2}\,\mathrm dy = \Big[\tfrac12e^{y^2}\Big]_0^1 = \tfrac12(e-1) = 0.8591 . \] The \(x\) integral supplied the factor \(y\) that the substitution \(u = y^2\) needed.

2. \(D = \{0 \le x \le 1,\ x^2 \le y \le 1\} = \{0 \le y \le 1,\ 0 \le x \le \sqrt y\}\). \[ \int_0^1\!\!\int_{x^2}^1 xy\,\mathrm dy\,\mathrm dx = \int_0^1 x\,\frac{1-x^4}{2}\,\mathrm dx = \frac12\Big(\frac12 - \frac16\Big) = \frac16 , \] \[ \int_0^1\!\!\int_0^{\sqrt y} xy\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dy = \int_0^1 y\cdot\frac{y}{2}\,\mathrm dy = \frac16 . \]

3. Both equal \(\int_D xy\,\mathrm dA\), which is by definition \(\int_B (xy)_D\) over any box \(B \supseteq D\), with the integrand extended by zero. Fubini asserts that this single number equals each iterated integral; the differing limits are two ways of saying where the extension is nonzero.

Check. On \(D\) the integrand \(xy\) lies in \([0,1]\) and \(|D| = 1 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac23\), so the answer must lie in \([0, \tfrac23]\); \(\tfrac16\) does. For part 1, \(1 \le e^{y^2} \le e\) on a triangle of area \(\tfrac12\), so the answer lies in \([0.5, 1.359]\); \(0.8591\) does.

P2 Jacobians, and three volumes routine

exercises: the change of variables theorem, Jacobian determinants, linear maps and det

  1. Derive \(|\det D\Psi| = r^2\sin\theta\) for spherical coordinates, using that a matrix with mutually orthogonal columns has \((\det A)^2 = \prod_i\lVert c_i\rVert^2\). Say where the formula degenerates and why that does not invalidate its use over a ball.
  2. Show that the solid ellipsoid \(x^2/a^2 + y^2/b^2 + z^2/c^2 \le 1\) has volume \(\tfrac43\pi abc\), using a linear change of variables and nothing else.
  3. A ball of radius \(R\) has density \(\rho(r) = \rho_0(1 - r/R)\). Find its mass and its mean density.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 ends in a function of \((r,\theta)\); part 2 in a number times \(abc\); part 3 in a mass and a density. Part 2 needs no integral at all beyond the volume of the unit ball.

Rung 2

For part 1, you are told the determinant identity to use, so the work is computing the three columns and checking they are pairwise orthogonal. For part 2, ask which linear map carries the unit ball onto the ellipsoid, and what the change of variables theorem then says.

Rung 3

Part 1, first line: \(\partial_r\Psi\) is a unit vector, \(\partial_\theta\Psi\) has length \(r\), \(\partial_\varphi\Psi\) has length \(r\sin\theta\). Part 2, first line: \(T = \operatorname{diag}(a,b,c)\) and \(|\det T| = abc\). Part 3, first line: \(M = \int_0^R 4\pi r^2\rho_0(1-r/R)\,\mathrm dr\).

Worked solution

Idea. The change of variables theorem turns a hard region into an easy one at the cost of a determinant. For a linear map the determinant is the whole story; for spherical coordinates it is a product of three lengths, because the columns happen to be orthogonal.

1. The columns of \(D\Psi\) are \[ \partial_r\Psi = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi, \sin\theta\sin\varphi, \cos\theta),\quad \partial_\theta\Psi = r(\cos\theta\cos\varphi, \cos\theta\sin\varphi, -\sin\theta),\quad \partial_\varphi\Psi = r\sin\theta(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi, 0), \] of lengths \(1\), \(r\), \(r\sin\theta\). Their pairwise dot products vanish: the first two give \(\sin\theta\cos\theta(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi) - \cos\theta\sin\theta = 0\), and the third is orthogonal to both because its third component is zero and its first two are \((-\sin\varphi,\cos\varphi) \perp (\cos\varphi,\sin\varphi)\). Hence \(A^{\!\top}A = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)\) and \((\det A)^2 = r^4\sin^2\theta\), so \(|\det D\Psi| = r^2\sin\theta\).

It degenerates where \(r = 0\) or \(\sin\theta = 0\): the origin and the polar axis, where the chart is also non-injective, since every \(\varphi\) gives the same point. That set is a line, of measure zero, and the change of variables theorem tolerates a failure on a null set — remove a thin closed neighbourhood of the axis, apply the theorem, and let its thickness go to zero; both sides move by at most (bound on the integrand) × (volume removed).

2. Let \(T = \operatorname{diag}(a,b,c)\), so \(T\) maps the unit ball \(B_1\) onto the ellipsoid \(E\). With \(f = 1\), \[ |E| = \int_E 1 = \int_{B_1}|\det T| = abc\,|B_1| = \tfrac43\pi abc . \]

3. \(M = \int_0^R 4\pi r^2\rho_0(1 - r/R)\,\mathrm dr = 4\pi\rho_0\big(\tfrac{R^3}{3} - \tfrac{R^3}{4}\big) = \tfrac13\pi\rho_0R^3\), and the mean density is \(M/(\tfrac43\pi R^3) = \rho_0/4\).

Check. Part 2 with \(a=b=c=R\) gives \(\tfrac43\pi R^3\) ✓. Part 3: the density runs from \(\rho_0\) at the centre to \(0\) at the surface, so the mean must lie strictly between; \(\rho_0/4\) does, and it is below \(\rho_0/2\) because most of the volume is near the surface, where the density is small ✓. Dimensions: a mass \(\rho_0R^3\) ✓.

P3 The centre of the ball, the hard way medium

exercises: superposition, the shell theorem used as a building block, gravitational binding energy

  1. Using only the shell result \(\Phi_{\text{shell}}(r) = -Gm/a\) for \(r < a\), together with superposition, compute the potential at the centre of a uniform ball of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\) by integrating over its shells. Confirm it equals \(-3GM/2R\), the value the slides got from Gauss's law.
  2. Assemble the ball shell by shell from infinity, the shell of radius \(r\) arriving on top of the mass \(M(r)\) already present. Show that the gravitational potential energy of the finished ball is \[ U = -\frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R} , \] and evaluate it for the Earth.
  3. Why is the coefficient in part 2 (\(3/5\)) different from the one in part 1 (\(3/2\))? Answer in terms of which potential each is.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Both parts are single integrals over \(r\), not triple integrals: the angular work has already been done, by the shell theorem. Part 1 is a potential (units \(\mathrm{L^2T^{-2}}\)); part 2 is an energy (units \(\mathrm{ML^2T^{-2}}\)).

Rung 2

For part 1 you need the interior half of the shell theorem, since the centre is inside every shell. For part 2 you need the exterior half, since the arriving shell is outside everything already assembled. Notice which half each uses — that is most of what the problem is teaching.

Rung 3

Part 1, first line: \(\mathrm dm = 4\pi\rho r^2\mathrm dr\) contributes \(-G\,\mathrm dm/r\) at the centre. Part 2, first line: \(\mathrm dU = -\dfrac{GM(r)}{r}\mathrm dm\) with \(M(r) = \tfrac43\pi\rho r^3\). For part 3, compute \(2U/M\) and compare it with \(\Phi(0)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The potential is linear in the mass, so decompose the ball into shells and add. Each shell's contribution at the centre is known from the shell theorem without any further integration — which is the entire point of proving that theorem first.

1. A shell of radius \(r\) and thickness \(\mathrm dr\) has mass \(\mathrm dm = 4\pi\rho r^2\,\mathrm dr\), and at any interior point — in particular at the centre — contributes \(-G\,\mathrm dm/r\). So \[ \Phi(0) = -G\!\int_0^R\frac{4\pi\rho r^2}{r}\,\mathrm dr = -4\pi G\rho\,\frac{R^2}{2} = -2\pi G\rho R^2 = -\frac{3GM}{2R}, \] using \(\rho = 3M/4\pi R^3\). This agrees with the slides' \(\Phi(0) = -3GM/2R\).

2. Build the ball outwards. When the shell of radius \(r\) arrives from infinity, the mass already present is \(M(r) = \tfrac43\pi\rho r^3\), a sphere, which by the shell theorem acts on the incoming shell as a point mass at the centre: the potential the shell falls through is \(-GM(r)/r\). So \[ U = -\int_0^R \frac{GM(r)}{r}\,\mathrm dm(r) = -G\!\int_0^R\frac{\tfrac43\pi\rho r^3}{r}\,4\pi\rho r^2\,\mathrm dr = -\frac{16\pi^2G\rho^2}{3}\!\int_0^R\! r^4\,\mathrm dr = -\frac{16\pi^2G\rho^2R^5}{15} . \] Substituting \(\rho^2 = 9M^2/16\pi^2R^6\) gives \(U = -\tfrac35 GM^2/R\). For the Earth, \(U = -0.6\times(3.986004418\times10^{14})\times(5.9722\times10^{24})/6.371\times10^6\), that is \[ U = -2.2419\times10^{32}\ \mathrm J . \]

3. They are different quantities. \(\Phi(0)\) is a potential per unit test mass at one point — the deepest point of the well. \(U\) is the self-energy of the whole distribution, \(U = \tfrac12\int\rho\Phi\,\mathrm dV\) — each pair of mass elements \(\mathrm dm, \mathrm dm'\) at separation \(s\) contributes \(-G\,\mathrm dm\,\mathrm dm'/s\) to \(U\) once, but to \(\int\Phi\,\mathrm dm\) twice, once from each end. It is an average of \(\Phi\) over all the mass, not just the centre, and halved because each pair of mass elements would otherwise be counted twice. Explicitly \(\langle\Phi\rangle = 2U/M = -\tfrac65 GM/R\), which is \(\tfrac45\) of \(\Phi(0)\) — the mass-weighted average is shallower than the centre — and the remaining factor \(\tfrac12\) is the double counting.

Check. Dimensions of \(U\): \(\mathrm{L^3M^{-1}T^{-2}}\cdot\mathrm M^2/\mathrm L = \mathrm{ML^2T^{-2}}\), an energy ✓. The sign is negative: work must be done to disperse the ball ✓. Comparison: a thin shell of the same mass and radius has \(U = -GM^2/2R\), coefficient \(\tfrac12 < \tfrac35\), so the ball is more tightly bound — as it should be, its mass being on average closer together ✓.

Flux and solid angle

P4 The solid angle of a disc medium

exercises: surface integrals, solid angle, limits of a formula, a number

  1. A disc of radius \(a\) is viewed from a point on its axis at distance \(h\). Show that the solid angle it subtends is \[ \Omega = 2\pi\Big(1 - \frac{h}{\sqrt{h^2+a^2}}\Big) . \]
  2. Check the two limits \(h \to 0\) and \(h \gg a\), saying in each case what answer you expected before computing.
  3. The Moon has radius \(1737.4\) km and mean distance \(3.844\times10^5\) km. Compute the solid angle it subtends at the Earth, and the fraction of the whole sky it covers. (Treat it as a disc; then say how large an error that makes.)
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is dimensionless and lies in \([0, 2\pi)\). Part 3 is a number in steradians, around \(10^{-5}\).

Rung 2

Solid angle is the flux of \(\hat r/r^2\), so write down the vector area element of a flat disc — the normal is constant — and use plane polar coordinates in the disc's own plane. Put the viewing point at the origin, not the disc's centre.

Rung 3

First line: with the disc at height \(h\), \(\mathrm d\vec A = \hat z\,s\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm d\varphi\), \(r = \sqrt{s^2+h^2}\) and \(\hat r\cdot\hat z = h/r\), so the integrand is \(hs/(s^2+h^2)^{3/2}\). For part 3, a sphere of radius \(R\) at distance \(d\) subtends the tangent cone, \(\sin\alpha = R/d\), not the disc's \(\tan\alpha = R/d\); compare the two.

Worked solution

Idea. Solid angle is a flux, of the field \(\hat r/r^2\), and the disc is a flat surface with a constant normal — so the integral is elementary in plane polar coordinates.

1. Put the viewing point at the origin and the disc in the plane \(z = h\), parametrised by \(\Sigma(s,\varphi) = (s\cos\varphi, s\sin\varphi, h)\) with \(0 \le s \le a\). Then \(\mathrm d\vec A = \hat z\,s\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm d\varphi\), \(r = \sqrt{s^2+h^2}\) and \(\hat r\cdot\hat z = h/r\). Hence \[ \Omega = \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^a \frac{h}{(s^2+h^2)^{3/2}}\,s\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm d\varphi = 2\pi h\Big[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{s^2+h^2}}\Big]_0^a = 2\pi\Big(1 - \frac{h}{\sqrt{h^2+a^2}}\Big). \]

2. \(h \to 0\): \(\Omega \to 2\pi\). Expected: from a point just above its centre, an infinite plane fills exactly half the sky, and \(4\pi/2 = 2\pi\) ✓. \(h \gg a\): \(1 - (1 + a^2/h^2)^{-1/2} = \tfrac{a^2}{2h^2} + O(a^4/h^4)\), so \(\Omega \approx \pi a^2/h^2\) — the area divided by the square of the distance, which is what a small distant object must give ✓.

3. \(a/h = 1737.4/384400 = 4.51978\times10^{-3}\), so \[ \Omega = 2\pi\Big(1 - \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (a/h)^2}}\Big) = 6.4177\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{sr}, \] and the fraction of the sky is \(\Omega/4\pi = 5.107\times10^{-6}\), about one part in \(196\,000\).

The Moon is a sphere, not a disc. The correct cone is the tangent cone, with half-angle \(\alpha\) given by \(\sin\alpha = R/d\) rather than \(\tan\alpha = R/d\), and \(\Omega = 2\pi(1-\cos\alpha) = 6.41778\times10^{-5}\) sr. The disc value is \(6.41765\times10^{-5}\) sr: a relative error of \(2.0\times10^{-5}\), of order \((R/d)^2\), and irrelevant at any plausible precision.

Check. Dimensionless ✓. Both values agree with the small-object estimate \(\pi(R/d)^2 = 6.41775\times10^{-5}\) to four figures, as they must when \(R/d \ll 1\) ✓.

P5 Which power has no sources routine

exercises: divergence in Cartesian components, flux through spheres, the role of dimension

  1. Show that for a radial field \(\vec F = \vec x\,f(r)\) in \(\mathbb R^3\), \(\operatorname{div}\vec F = 3f(r) + rf'(r)\). (You will need \(\partial_ir = x_i/r\).)
  2. Deduce \(\operatorname{div}(\hat r/r^p) = (2-p)r^{-p-1}\) and compute the flux of \(\hat r/r^p\) through the sphere of radius \(R\) about the origin. Show the two calculations agree about which \(p\) is special.
  3. Repeat in \(\mathbb R^n\) and find the exponent there.
  4. For \(p = 2\) the flux through every sphere is \(4\pi\), while the divergence vanishes everywhere the field is defined. Explain precisely why this is not a counterexample to the divergence theorem.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 end in formulas; part 3 in a single integer; part 4 in a sentence about a hypothesis.

Rung 2

Everything rests on \(\partial_ir = x_i/r\), which is the chain rule applied to \(r = (x_jx_j)^{1/2}\). For part 4, write down the divergence theorem's hypotheses and check each one against the region you would like to apply it to.

Rung 3

Part 1, first line: \(\partial_i(x_if(r)) = (\partial_ix_i)f + x_i f'\partial_ir\), summed over \(i\). Part 4, first line: the region between two concentric spheres is where the theorem does apply — and see what it tells you there.

Worked solution

Idea. Two independent calculations — a volume one and a surface one — single out the same exponent, and the divergence theorem is the statement that this was never a coincidence.

1. With \(r = (x_jx_j)^{1/2}\), the chain rule gives \(\partial_ir = x_i/r\). Then, summing over \(i\), \[ \operatorname{div}(\vec x f(r)) = \partial_i\big(x_if(r)\big) = (\partial_ix_i)f + x_i f'(r)\frac{x_i}{r} = 3f(r) + rf'(r). \]

2. \(\hat r/r^p = \vec x\,r^{-p-1}\), so \(f = r^{-p-1}\), \(rf' = -(p+1)r^{-p-1}\), and \(\operatorname{div} = (3 - p - 1)r^{-p-1} = (2-p)r^{-p-1}\), zero on \(r>0\) iff \(p = 2\). The flux through the sphere of radius \(R\) is \(R^{-p}\cdot 4\pi R^2 = 4\pi R^{2-p}\), independent of \(R\) iff \(p = 2\). Same exponent.

3. In \(\mathbb R^n\), \(\partial_ix_i = n\), so \(\operatorname{div}(\vec xf) = nf + rf'\) and \(\operatorname{div}(\hat r/r^p) = (n-1-p)r^{-p-1}\). The sphere of radius \(R\) has area \(c_nR^{n-1}\), so the flux is \(c_nR^{n-1-p}\). Both single out \(p = n-1\): the inverse-square law is the three-dimensional member of a family.

4. The divergence theorem requires \(\vec F\) to be \(C^1\) on an open set containing the whole region. For \(p = 2\) and a ball containing the origin, \(\vec F\) is undefined at the centre, so the theorem does not apply and there is nothing to contradict. Applied where it is legitimate — the region between two concentric spheres, on which \(\vec F\) is smooth — it gives \(0 = (\text{flux through the outer sphere}) - (\text{flux through the inner one})\), that is, the flux is the same through every sphere. That is exactly the constancy found in part 2, now derived rather than computed.

Check. \(p = 0\), a field of constant length pointing outward: flux \(4\pi R^2\), growing with \(R\), and \(\operatorname{div} = 2/r > 0\) — sources everywhere, consistently ✓. \(n = 2\), \(p = 1\): the two-dimensional inverse-first-power field is the divergence-free one, which is why a line mass in three dimensions has a \(1/s\) field ✓.

P6 The solid angle of a closed surface hard

exercises: the divergence theorem applied to a region with a puncture, a proof

Let \(S\) be a closed, piecewise-\(C^1\) surface bounding a compact region \(D\), oriented outward, and let the origin not lie on \(S\). Prove that \[ \oint_S \frac{\hat r\cdot\mathrm{d}\vec A}{r^2} = \begin{cases} 4\pi & \text{if } 0 \in \operatorname{int}D\\ 0 & \text{if } 0 \notin D .\end{cases} \] State clearly, at each step, where the hypotheses of the divergence theorem are checked — in particular, what goes wrong if you apply it to \(D\) itself in the first case. This is the one missing step in the slides' proof of Gauss's law.

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

This is a proof, and the answer has two cases because the field has one bad point. Nothing here needs a coordinate computation except on one small sphere, where the integrand is constant.

Rung 2

When the origin is outside \(D\), the field is smooth on all of \(D\) and the divergence theorem applies at once. When it is inside, it does not — so change the region until it does, by removing a small ball, and keep careful track of which way the new piece of boundary is oriented.

Rung 3

First line of the second case: pick \(\varepsilon\) with \(\overline{B_\varepsilon} \subset \operatorname{int}D\) and apply the theorem to \(D' = D\setminus B_\varepsilon\), whose boundary is \(S \cup S_\varepsilon\). The outward normal of \(D'\) on \(S_\varepsilon\) is \(-\hat r\), and \(\vec F\cdot\hat r = 1/\varepsilon^2\) there.

Worked solution

Idea. Where the field is smooth, the divergence theorem gives zero immediately. Where it is not, excise the bad point with a small sphere: the theorem then applies to what is left, and the small sphere's flux is computable exactly because the integrand was built to be radius-independent.

Setting. \(\vec F(\vec x) = \vec x/\lVert\vec x\rVert^3\) is \(C^\infty\) on \(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}\), with \(\operatorname{div}\vec F = 0\) there (P5 with \(p = 2\)).

Case \(0 \notin D\). \(D\) is compact and does not contain the origin, so \(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}\) is an open set containing \(D\) on which \(\vec F\) is \(C^1\). The divergence theorem applies directly: \[ \oint_S\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = \int_D\operatorname{div}\vec F\,\mathrm dV = 0 . \]

Case \(0 \in \operatorname{int}D\). Choose \(\varepsilon > 0\) with \(\overline{B_\varepsilon} \subset \operatorname{int}D\), possible because the interior is open. Let \(D' = D\setminus B_\varepsilon\), compact with piecewise-\(C^1\) boundary \(S \cup S_\varepsilon\). On \(S_\varepsilon\) the normal pointing out of \(D'\) points towards the origin, so it is \(-\hat r\). \(\vec F\) is \(C^1\) on an open set containing \(D'\), so \[ 0 = \int_{D'}\operatorname{div}\vec F\,\mathrm dV = \oint_S\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec A + \oint_{S_\varepsilon}\vec F\cdot(-\hat r)\,\mathrm dA . \] On \(S_\varepsilon\), \(\vec F\cdot\hat r = 1/\varepsilon^2\) and the area is \(4\pi\varepsilon^2\), so the second term is \(-4\pi\). Hence \(\oint_S\vec F\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = 4\pi\), for every \(\varepsilon\) and so independently of it.

What goes wrong on \(D\) itself. \(\vec F\) is not defined at the origin, is unbounded near it, and \(\operatorname{div}\vec F\) does not exist there. The hypothesis "\(C^1\) on an open set containing \(D\)" fails at exactly one point — and that one point carries the entire \(4\pi\). Writing \(\int_D\operatorname{div}\vec F = 0\) would "prove" the flux is zero, which is false for a sphere about the origin by direct computation.

Check. For \(S\) a sphere of any radius about the origin, direct evaluation gives \(\Omega = 4\pi\), matching ✓. For \(S\) far from the origin, the projection reading says every ray either misses \(S\) or crosses it an even number of times with alternating signs of \(\hat r\cdot\hat n\), so the signed projected area is zero ✓. Dimensionless throughout ✓.

Gauss's law, and what symmetry buys

P7 A hollow ball routine

exercises: Gauss's law with symmetry, matching at an interface, limits of a result

A shell of uniform density \(\rho\) occupies \(a \le r \le b\).

  1. Find \(\vec g(r)\) in all three regions \(r < a\), \(a < r < b\), \(r > b\).
  2. Find \(\Phi(r)\) in all three regions, fixing the constants by \(\Phi(\infty) = 0\) and continuity.
  3. Where is \(\lVert\vec g\rVert\) largest, and what is its value there?
  4. Check the limits \(a \to 0\) and \(a \to b\) (at fixed total mass in the second case), saying what each should reproduce.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three regions, so three formulas for \(\vec g\) and three for \(\Phi\). The answers are all elementary; the difficulty is entirely bookkeeping.

Rung 2

Use \(g(r) = -GM(r)/r^2\) with \(M(r)\) the mass strictly inside radius \(r\), and write that down separately in each region before integrating anything. For part 2, integrate \(\Phi' = -g\) inwards from infinity, fixing each constant by continuity at the interface above it.

Rung 3

First line: \(M(r) = \tfrac43\pi\rho(r^3 - a^3)\) for \(a \le r \le b\), which already contains the two other cases as \(r = a\) and \(r = b\). For part 3, differentiate \(r - a^3/r^2\) and note the sign.

Worked solution

Idea. Spherical symmetry plus Gauss gives \(g(r) = -GM(r)/r^2\) with no computation; the only work is bookkeeping \(M(r)\) in three regions, and then integrating once with the constants fixed by continuity.

1. \(M(r) = 0\) for \(r \le a\); \(M(r) = \tfrac43\pi\rho(r^3-a^3)\) for \(a \le r \le b\); \(M(r) = M = \tfrac43\pi\rho(b^3-a^3)\) for \(r \ge b\). Hence \[ \vec g = \vec 0\ (r<a), \qquad \vec g = -\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\Big(r - \frac{a^3}{r^2}\Big)\hat r\ (a<r<b), \qquad \vec g = -\frac{GM}{r^2}\hat r\ (r>b). \]

2. Outside, \(\Phi = -GM/r\). In the shell, \(\Phi' = -g\) gives \(\Phi(r) = \tfrac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\big(\tfrac{r^2}{2} + \tfrac{a^3}{r}\big) + C\), and matching at \(r = b\) to \(-GM/b = -\tfrac{4\pi G\rho}{3}(b^3-a^3)/b\) gives \[ C = -\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\cdot\frac{3b^2}{2} = -2\pi G\rho b^2 . \] Inside the cavity \(\vec g = 0\), so \(\Phi\) is constant and equals its value at \(r = a\): \[ \Phi(r\le a) = \frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\cdot\frac{3a^2}{2} - 2\pi G\rho b^2 = -2\pi G\rho\,(b^2 - a^2). \]

3. On \(a<r<b\), \(\tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dr}(r - a^3/r^2) = 1 + 2a^3/r^3 > 0\), so \(\lVert\vec g\rVert\) increases through the shell; outside it falls as \(1/r^2\). The maximum is at \(r = b\), where \(\lVert\vec g\rVert = GM/b^2 = \tfrac{4\pi G\rho}{3}(b^3-a^3)/b^2\).

4. \(a \to 0\): the shell formula becomes \(-\tfrac{4\pi G\rho}{3}r\,\hat r\) and \(\Phi = \tfrac{2\pi G\rho}{3}r^2 - 2\pi G\rho b^2\), which is exactly \(-GM(3b^2-r^2)/2b^3\) with \(M = \tfrac43\pi\rho b^3\) — the solid ball ✓. \(a \to b\) at fixed \(M\): \(\vec g = 0\) inside and \[ \Phi(r<a) = -2\pi G\rho(b^2-a^2) = -\frac{3GM}{2}\cdot\frac{b^2-a^2}{b^3-a^3} = -\frac{3GM}{2}\cdot\frac{b+a}{b^2+ab+a^2} \longrightarrow -\frac{3GM}{2}\cdot\frac{2b}{3b^2} = -\frac{GM}{b}, \] the thin shell ✓.

Check. \(\Phi\) is continuous everywhere and \(\vec g\) is continuous everywhere (no surface densities here), as they must be for a bounded, piecewise continuous \(\rho\) ✓. Dimensions: \(G\rho b^2\) has \(\mathrm{L^3M^{-1}T^{-2}}\cdot\mathrm{ML^{-3}}\cdot\mathrm L^2 = \mathrm{L^2T^{-2}}\) ✓.

P8 The off-centre cavity medium

exercises: superposition used to subtract a region, the interior field of a uniform ball

A ball of radius \(R\) and uniform density \(\rho\) has a spherical cavity of radius \(c\) hollowed out, centred at \(\vec d\) with \(\lVert\vec d\rVert + c \le R\).

  1. Prove that the field everywhere inside the cavity is uniform, and equal to \[ \vec g = -\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\,\vec d . \]
  2. Check the limits \(\vec d = \vec 0\) and \(c \to 0\).
  3. Nothing in part 1 used \(c\). Say why not, and what that tells you about how large the cavity may be.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a constant vector — the same at every point of the cavity — of the form (something) times \(\vec d\).

Rung 2

Do not integrate over the hollowed body. Write it as a sum of two solid balls, one of which has negative density, and use that both interior fields are known and both are linear in position.

Rung 3

First line: for \(\vec x\) in the cavity, \(\vec g = -\tfrac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\vec x + \tfrac{4\pi G\rho}{3}(\vec x - \vec d)\), and now look at what cancels.

Worked solution

Idea. A cavity is not a new problem. It is a full ball plus a ball of negative density in the hole, and superposition is exactly the statement that adding the two fields is legitimate.

1. Let \(\vec g_+\) be the field of the complete ball of density \(\rho\) and radius \(R\), and \(\vec g_-\) that of a ball of density \(-\rho\) and radius \(c\) centred at \(\vec d\). Their sum has density \(\rho\) everywhere in the body and \(0\) in the cavity, so by uniqueness of the defining integral it is the field of the hollowed body.

Take \(\vec x\) inside the cavity. Then \(\lVert\vec x\rVert < R\) and \(\lVert\vec x - \vec d\rVert < c\), so both interior formulas apply: \[ \vec g_+(\vec x) = -\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\vec x, \qquad \vec g_-(\vec x) = +\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}(\vec x - \vec d), \] and adding, \[ \vec g(\vec x) = -\frac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\,\vec d , \] which does not depend on \(\vec x\): the field is uniform in the cavity, points from the cavity's centre towards the ball's centre, and has magnitude proportional to the offset.

2. \(\vec d = \vec 0\): \(\vec g = \vec 0\), which is the shell theorem — a concentric cavity has no field, since the mass outside it is a set of complete shells ✓. \(c \to 0\): the cavity shrinks to the single point \(\vec d\), where the intact ball's own field is \(-\tfrac{4\pi G\rho}{3}\vec d\) — the same answer ✓.

3. The two interior fields are each linear in position and their coefficients depend only on \(\rho\), not on the radius of the ball producing them. In the subtraction the position-dependent parts cancel identically, leaving only the offset. The radius \(c\) enters only through the requirement that \(\vec x\) be inside both spheres, so the result holds for any cavity contained in the ball — including one almost as large as the ball itself, where the remaining matter is a thin crescent and the field inside it is still exactly uniform.

Check. Dimensions: \(G\rho d\) is \(\mathrm{L^3M^{-1}T^{-2}}\cdot\mathrm{ML^{-3}} \cdot\mathrm L = \mathrm{LT^{-2}}\) ✓. Numerically, with \(G = \rho = R = 1\), \(c = 0.3\), \(\vec d = (0.4,0,0)\): at the point \(\vec x = (0.45, 0.05, 0)\), in spherical coordinates centred on \(\vec x\) the radial integral of the defining integral is trivial (the \(s^2\) of the volume element cancels the \(s^{-2}\)), leaving \(\vec g = G\rho\int \hat u\,\ell(\hat u)\,\mathrm d\Omega\) with \(\ell(\hat u)\) the length of matter along the ray from \(\vec x\) in direction \(\hat u\). Evaluating that angular integral numerically gives \((-1.67552, 0.00000, 0.00000)\), against the predicted \(-\tfrac{4\pi}{3}(0.4,0,0) = (-1.67552, 0, 0)\) ✓.

P9 Where Gauss's law is true and useless hard

exercises: equivariance of the field under a symmetry group, stabilisers and fixed subspaces, a proof

  1. Let \(G \subseteq O(3)\) and suppose \(\rho\circ A = \rho\) for every \(A \in G\). Prove that \(\vec g(A\vec x) = A\,\vec g(\vec x)\), and deduce that \(\vec g(\vec x) \in \operatorname{Fix}(G_{\vec x})\), where \(G_{\vec x}\) is the stabiliser of \(\vec x\) in \(G\).
  2. For \(\rho\) a function of \(r\) alone, compute \(\operatorname{Fix}(G_{\vec x})\) with \(G = O(3)\) and deduce \(\vec g = g(r)\hat r\). State separately which further property of the group is needed before \(g\) can be taken outside the flux integral.
  3. Now take \(\rho\) uniform on a cube of side \(L\), and a concentric cubical surface of side \(L' > L\). Write down exactly what Gauss's law gives, and compute the average of \(\vec g\cdot\hat n\) over that surface.
  4. Compute the stabiliser of a point of a face of the cube in three cases — a generic point, a point on one of the face's mirror lines, and the face centre — and give the dimension of its fixed subspace in each. Conclude that the direction of \(\vec g\) is pinned down at the face centre and nowhere else on the face, and say which of the two conditions in part 2 fails even at the centre.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a proof by change of variables; part 2 a computation of a fixed subspace; part 3 a number; part 4 three stabilisers and three dimensions.

Rung 2

In part 1, substitute \(\vec y = A\vec z\) in the defining integral and use two properties of an orthogonal matrix: \(|\det A| = 1\) and \(\lVert A\vec u\rVert = \lVert\vec u\rVert\). In part 4, a stabiliser is the set of cube symmetries that leave the chosen point where it is — try a point with no special position on its face, then one on a mirror line, then the centre.

Rung 3

Part 1, first line: \(\vec g(A\vec x) = -G\int\rho(A\vec z)\,A(\vec x-\vec z)/\lVert\vec x-\vec z\rVert^3\,\mathrm dV(\vec z)\). Part 2: a vector fixed by every rotation of a plane has no component in that plane. Part 3: the surface area is \(6L'^2\) and the enclosed mass is \(\rho L^3\).

Worked solution

Idea. "By symmetry the field is radial" is a theorem with a hypothesis, and the hypothesis is about the stabiliser of a point. Gauss's law supplies one equation per surface; symmetry must reduce the unknown to one number before that equation can solve anything.

1. Substitute \(\vec y = A\vec z\) in the defining integral. The change of variables theorem contributes \(|\det A| = 1\), and \(\lVert A\vec u\rVert = \lVert\vec u\rVert\) since \(A\) is orthogonal: \[ \vec g(A\vec x) = -G\!\int\!\rho(\vec y)\frac{A\vec x-\vec y}{\lVert A\vec x-\vec y\rVert^3}\mathrm dV(\vec y) = -G\!\int\!\rho(A\vec z)\frac{A(\vec x-\vec z)}{\lVert\vec x-\vec z\rVert^3}\mathrm dV(\vec z) = A\,\vec g(\vec x), \] using \(\rho\circ A = \rho\) and the linearity of \(A\). If \(A \in G_{\vec x}\), that reads \(\vec g(\vec x) = A\vec g(\vec x)\), so \(\vec g(\vec x) \in \operatorname{Fix}(G_{\vec x})\).

2. \(G = O(3)\), \(\vec x \ne \vec 0\). An \(A\) fixing \(\vec x\) fixes the line \(\mathbb R\hat r\) pointwise and maps the orthogonal plane to itself, acting there as an arbitrary element of \(O(2)\). A vector fixed by all of them must have zero component in that plane, since no nonzero plane vector is fixed by every rotation of the plane. So \(\operatorname{Fix}(G_{\vec x}) = \mathbb R\hat r\) and \(\vec g = g(r)\hat r\).

The further property needed is transitivity: \(G\) must carry any point of the surface to any other, so that \(g\) takes the same value everywhere on it and can be taken outside \(\oint\vec g\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = g(r)\cdot4\pi r^2\). \(O(3)\) is transitive on each sphere, so this holds.

3. Gauss's law gives one number and nothing more: \[ \oint_{\text{cube }L'}\vec g\cdot\mathrm d\vec A = -4\pi G\rho L^3 . \] The surface has area \(6L'^2\), so the average of \(\vec g\cdot\hat n\) over it is \[ \langle\vec g\cdot\hat n\rangle = -\frac{4\pi G\rho L^3}{6L'^2} = -\frac{2\pi G\rho L^3}{3L'^2} . \] This is an average, not a value: it is attained at no particular point and cannot be used to predict \(\vec g\) anywhere.

4. Write \(G\) for the full symmetry group of the cube, of order \(48\).

  • A generic point of a face is moved by every non-identity element, so \(G_{\vec x} = \{e\}\) and \(\operatorname{Fix} = \mathbb R^3\), dimension \(3\).
  • A point on one of the face's four mirror lines (two joining midpoints of opposite edges, two along the diagonals) is fixed by the identity and by the reflection in the plane through that line normal to the face: \(\operatorname{Fix}\) is that plane, dimension \(2\).
  • The face centre is fixed by the order-8 group \(C_{4v}\) — four rotations about the face normal and four reflections — whose fixed subspace is the normal axis, dimension \(1\).

So the direction of \(\vec g\) is determined by symmetry only at the face centre, and nowhere else on the face. Even there, the second condition fails: \(G\) does not act transitively on the face (the centre goes only to the centres of the six faces), so the magnitude of \(\vec g\) remains an unknown function of two variables on each face, and one scalar equation cannot determine it.

Check. The three fixed-subspace dimensions are \(3, 2, 1\) for stabilisers of orders \(1, 2, 8\) — larger stabiliser, smaller fixed subspace, as it must be ✓. The average in part 3 has dimensions \(\mathrm{LT^{-2}}\) ✓, and letting \(L' \to \infty\) it falls as \(1/L'^2\), matching the inverse-square field of a distant point mass ✓.

Empty space, and tides

P10 Harmonic functions do not have holes to fall into hard

exercises: Laplace's equation, the mean value property, the maximum principle, a proof

  1. Show by direct differentiation that \(\nabla^2(1/r) = 0\) for \(r > 0\).
  2. Assume the mean value property and prove the strong maximum principle: a harmonic function on a connected open \(U\) which attains its maximum at an interior point is constant.
  3. Deduce Earnshaw's theorem for gravity: no point of empty space is a strict local minimum of \(\Phi\), so a test mass cannot be held in stable equilibrium by a static gravitational field alone.
  4. A student objects: the centre of the Earth is a minimum of \(\Phi\), and a body released there stays. Resolve the objection precisely, naming the hypothesis that fails and computing \(\nabla^2\Phi\) there.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a two-line computation; parts 2 and 3 are proofs; part 4 is a sentence and a number.

Rung 2

For part 2, consider the set where the maximum is attained and show it is both open and closed in \(U\); openness is where the mean value property is used. For part 3, ask what the average of a function over a small sphere must be if the function is strictly larger than its centre value everywhere on that sphere.

Rung 3

Part 1, first line: \(\partial_i(1/r) = -x_i/r^3\), then differentiate again and sum over \(i\). Part 2, first line: let \(E = \{x \in U : \Phi(x) = M\}\), nonempty and closed by continuity. Part 4: compute \(4\pi G\rho\) with the Earth's mean density, and check it against \(3GM/R^3\).

Worked solution

Idea. Everything here follows from one fact: a harmonic function equals its own spherical average. A strict interior extremum would make a function differ from its average, so there are none.

1. \(\partial_i(1/r) = -x_i/r^3\) using \(\partial_ir = x_i/r\), and then, summing over \(i\), \[ \nabla^2\frac1r = \partial_i\Big(-\frac{x_i}{r^3}\Big) = -\frac{3}{r^3} + 3\frac{x_ix_i}{r^5} = -\frac{3}{r^3} + \frac{3}{r^3} = 0 \qquad (r>0). \]

2. Let \(M = \sup_U\Phi\) be attained at an interior point, and let \(E = \{x \in U : \Phi(x) = M\}\). \(E\) is nonempty by hypothesis and closed in \(U\) because \(\Phi\) is continuous. \(E\) is open: take \(a \in E\) and \(r\) small enough that \(\overline{B(a,r)} \subset U\). The mean value property gives, for every \(s \le r\), \(M = \Phi(a) = \langle\Phi\rangle_{S(a,s)}\) with \(\Phi \le M\) pointwise. If \(\Phi(p) < M\) at some \(p\) on that sphere, continuity gives a patch of positive area on which \(\Phi \le M - \epsilon\), and then the average would be strictly below \(M\). So \(\Phi \equiv M\) on every such sphere, hence on \(B(a,r) \subseteq E\). A nonempty subset of a connected set that is both open and closed is the whole set, so \(E = U\) and \(\Phi \equiv M\).

3. Suppose \(\Phi\) is harmonic near \(a\) and \(a\) is a strict local minimum: \(\Phi(x) > \Phi(a)\) for all \(x \ne a\) in some ball. Then on any small sphere about \(a\) the average is strictly greater than \(\Phi(a)\), contradicting the mean value property. (Equivalently, apply part 2 to \(-\Phi\), which is also harmonic.) A test mass has potential energy \(m\Phi\), so a stable equilibrium requires a strict local minimum of \(\Phi\), and in vacuum there is none.

4. The hypothesis that fails is harmonicity. At the centre of the Earth \(\rho \ne 0\), so \[ \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho = 4\pi(6.67430\times10^{-11})(5513.4) = 4.624\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}} \ne 0 . \] Earnshaw's theorem says nothing about points inside matter, and the interior potential \(\Phi = \text{const} + GMr^2/2R^3\) indeed has a genuine minimum at \(r = 0\).

Check. The interior Laplacian can be computed a second way: \(\nabla^2(GMr^2/2R^3) = (GM/2R^3)\cdot6 = 3GM/R^3 = 3(1.5414\times10^{-6}) = 4.624\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), the same number ✓ — and \(GM/R^3 = \omega^2\) is exactly the tunnel frequency of worked example 3, so \(\nabla^2\Phi = 3\omega^2\) inside a uniform ball ✓.

P11 The trace-free tidal tensor medium

exercises: second derivatives of 1/r, symmetric bilinear forms, eigenvalues and eigenvectors, two numbers

  1. For a point mass \(M\) at the origin, compute \(T_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi\) and show it equals \((GM/r^3)(\delta_{ij} - 3n_in_j)\) with \(\hat n = \vec x/r\).
  2. Show \(\operatorname{tr}T = 0\), and find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(T\). Verify the eigenvalues sum to zero.
  3. Using \(\ddot\xi_i = -T_{ij}\xi_j\), say what happens to a small cloud of free particles released at rest near a point at distance \(r\), and deduce that there are two bulges along one axis and a squeeze around it.
  4. Compute the tidal stretch across an Earth radius due to the Moon, and due to the Sun. Which dominates, and by how much? Compare with the ratio of the two direct pulls, and explain the difference.
  5. Repeat part 1 for a point inside a uniform ball, where \(\Phi = \text{const} + GMr^2/2R^3\). Show \(T\) is isotropic there, find its trace, and say why there is no tidal axis.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

\(T\) is a symmetric \(3\times3\) matrix at each point, so it has three real eigenvalues; the physics is in their signs. Part 4 wants two accelerations in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), both around \(10^{-6}\).

Rung 2

Differentiate \(GMx_i/r^3\) with respect to \(x_j\), remembering \(\partial_jr = x_j/r\). Then, rather than solving a characteristic polynomial, guess the eigenvectors: the problem has exactly one distinguished direction.

Rung 3

First line: \(T_{ij} = GM(\delta_{ij}r^{-3} - 3x_ix_jr^{-5})\). Apply the matrix \(\delta - 3\hat n\hat n^{\!\top}\) to \(\hat n\) and then to anything orthogonal to \(\hat n\); the two answers are the eigenvalues. For part 5, use \(\partial_i\partial_j(x_kx_k) = 2\delta_{ij}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Differentiate \(-GM/r\) twice, and then stop computing and start using the spectral theorem: the physics is in the eigenvalues, and the two bulges are one eigenvector.

1. \(\Phi = -GM/r\), so \(\partial_i\Phi = GM\,x_i/r^3\) and \[ T_{ij} = \partial_j\partial_i\Phi = GM\Big(\frac{\delta_{ij}}{r^3} + x_i\cdot\big(-3r^{-4}\big)\frac{x_j}{r}\Big) = GM\Big(\frac{\delta_{ij}}{r^3} - \frac{3x_ix_j}{r^5}\Big) = \frac{GM}{r^3}\big(\delta_{ij} - 3n_in_j\big), \] with \(\hat n = \vec x/r\). It is symmetric in \(i \leftrightarrow j\), as Clairaut's theorem requires.

2. \(\operatorname{tr}T = \frac{GM}{r^3}(\delta_{ii} - 3n_in_i) = \frac{GM}{r^3}(3-3) = 0\). For the eigenvectors, note \((\delta - 3\hat n\hat n^{\!\top})\hat n = \hat n - 3\hat n = -2\hat n\), and for any \(\vec v \perp \hat n\), \((\delta - 3\hat n\hat n^{\!\top})\vec v = \vec v\). So the eigenvalues are \[ \lambda_\parallel = -\frac{2GM}{r^3}\ \text{along } \hat n, \qquad \lambda_\perp = +\frac{GM}{r^3}\ \text{twice, in the plane} \perp \hat n, \] and \(-2 + 1 + 1 = 0\) ✓.

3. \(\ddot{\vec\xi} = -T\vec\xi\). Along \(\hat n\): \(\ddot\xi = +2GM\xi/r^3\), of the same sign as \(\xi\) — the separation grows, and it grows for \(\xi > 0\) and for \(\xi < 0\) alike, since both \(\pm\hat n\) are eigenvectors with the same eigenvalue. So the cloud is stretched outward at both ends of the line to the source: two bulges. Transversely, \(\ddot\xi = -GM\xi/r^3\), of the opposite sign to \(\xi\): the cloud is squeezed around a waist. The volume of the cloud is unchanged at leading order: released at rest, each separation moves as \(\vec\xi(t) \approx (I - \tfrac12t^2T)\vec\xi(0)\), so volumes scale by \(\det(I - \tfrac12t^2T) = 1 - \tfrac12t^2\operatorname{tr}T + O(t^4)\), and \(\operatorname{tr}T = 0\).

4. Moon: \(GM_{\mathrm m}/d^3 = 4.9029\times10^{12}/(3.844\times10^8)^3 = 8.632\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), so the stretch across an Earth radius is \[ 2\,\frac{GM_{\mathrm m}}{d^3}R_\oplus = 1.100\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} . \] Sun: \(2GM_\odot R_\oplus/d_\odot^3 = 5.051\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The Moon dominates by a factor \(2.18\). The two direct pulls go the other way: \(GM_\odot/d_\odot^2 \big/ GM_{\mathrm m}/d^2 = 179\), the Sun winning by two orders of magnitude. The difference is one power of distance: a tide is a derivative of the field and so falls as \(M/d^3\), while the field itself falls as \(M/d^2\). The Sun is \(389\) times further away, and \(389\) is roughly the ratio \(179 \times 2.18\).

5. Inside a uniform ball, \(\Phi = \text{const} + GMr^2/2R^3\), so \[ T_{ij} = \frac{GM}{2R^3}\,\partial_i\partial_j(x_kx_k) = \frac{GM}{R^3}\,\delta_{ij} , \] isotropic, with \(\operatorname{tr}T = 3GM/R^3 = 4\pi G\rho \ne 0\). Every direction is an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue, so there is no preferred axis and no bulge: the deviation equation reads \(\ddot{\vec\xi} = -(GM/R^3)\vec\xi\), an isotropic compression in all three directions at once. That is the pure trace part of the tidal tensor, and it exists only where there is matter.

Check. Dimensions of \(T\): \(\mathrm{L^3T^{-2}}/\mathrm L^3 = \mathrm{T^{-2}}\), the square of a frequency ✓. The Moon's stretch is \(1.120\times10^{-7}\) of \(g_0\) — one part in \(8.9\) million, which is why the solid Earth barely notices and the ocean, free to flow, does ✓. And the equilibrium tide range this implies is \(\tfrac32GM_{\mathrm m}R^2/d^3g_0 = 0.535\) m for the Moon and \(0.246\) m for the Sun, in the right range for a real tide once the ocean's dynamic response is allowed for ✓.

P12 Two tunnels medium

exercises: the interior field of a ball, reduction to quadrature, a non-harmonic planet, two numbers

  1. For the uniform ball, show that the period of oscillation in a straight tunnel whose line passes at perpendicular distance \(b\) from the centre does not depend on \(b\), and find the maximum speed as a function of \(b\). Evaluate it for \(b = 0\) and \(b = R/2\).
  2. A planet of radius \(R\) has \(\rho(r) = A/r\). Find \(M(r)\) and show that \(\lVert\vec g\rVert\) is constant throughout the interior.
  3. For that planet the motion in a tunnel through the centre is not simple harmonic. Reduce it to quadrature (unit 00's third case) and show the period is \(4\sqrt{2R/g_0}\). Evaluate it for \(R = 6371\) km and \(g_0 = 9.8203\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and compare with the uniform ball's \(84.3\) min.
  4. The real Earth is centrally condensed. Which of the two models is the better guide, and what does that say about the sign of the correction to \(84.3\) min?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 ends in a period (independent of \(b\)) and a speed. Part 3 ends in a number of minutes, and it is not \(2\pi/\omega\) for any \(\omega\), because the motion is not sinusoidal.

Rung 2

For part 1, resolve \(\vec g = -(GM/R^3)\vec r\) along the tunnel: write \(\vec r\) as a perpendicular offset plus a displacement along \(\hat t\), and take the dot product. For part 2, compute \(M(r)\) first and only then \(g\). For part 3, the force has constant magnitude and flips sign at the centre, so multiply by \(\dot x\) and integrate, as in unit 00's third case.

Rung 3

Part 1, first line: \(\vec r\cdot\hat t = x\) exactly, so \(\ddot x = -(GM/R^3)x\). Part 2, first line: \(M(r) = \int_0^r 4\pi r'^2 (A/r')\,\mathrm dr'\). Part 3, first line: \(\dot x^2 = 2g_0(R - |x|)\), and a quarter period is \(\int_0^R\mathrm dx/\sqrt{2g_0(R-x)}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Inside a uniform ball the field is linear in the position vector, so its component along any straight line is linear in the distance along that line — and simple harmonic motion follows with no reference to the line's position. For a different density profile the field is different and so is the answer; the method, reduction to quadrature, is unit 00's and does not change.

1. Write \(\vec r = b\,\hat n_\perp + x\,\hat t\) with \(\hat n_\perp \perp \hat t\). Inside, \(\vec g = -(GM/R^3)\vec r\), so the component along the tunnel is \(\vec g\cdot\hat t = -(GM/R^3)\,x\), and \[ \ddot x = -\omega^2x, \qquad \omega^2 = \frac{GM}{R^3}, \] with no \(b\). Hence \(T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{R^3/GM} = 84.3\) min for every tunnel. The tunnel runs from \(x = -x_0\) to \(x = +x_0\) with \(x_0 = \sqrt{R^2-b^2}\), so the first integral \(\dot x^2 = \omega^2(x_0^2-x^2)\) gives \[ v_{\max} = \omega\sqrt{R^2-b^2}. \] For \(b = 0\): \(v_{\max} = \omega R = 7909.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). For \(b = R/2\): \(v_{\max} = \omega R\sqrt{3}/2 = 6850.1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

2. \(M(r) = \int_0^r 4\pi r'^2\frac{A}{r'}\,\mathrm dr' = 2\pi Ar^2\), so \[ \lVert\vec g(r)\rVert = \frac{GM(r)}{r^2} = 2\pi GA, \] constant throughout the interior. The total mass is \(M = 2\pi AR^2\), so \(2\pi GA = GM/R^2 = g_0\): the constant interior value is the surface value.

3. Through the centre, \(\ddot x = -g_0\operatorname{sgn}(x)\), which is not linear in \(x\), so the motion is not simple harmonic. Multiply by \(\dot x\) and integrate (unit 00's third case): with release from rest at \(x = R\), \[ \tfrac12\dot x^2 + g_0|x| = g_0R \implies \dot x^2 = 2g_0(R - |x|), \] and a quarter period is the time from \(R\) to \(0\): \[ \frac T4 = \int_0^R\frac{\mathrm dx}{\sqrt{2g_0(R-x)}} = \Big[\frac{-2\sqrt{R-x}}{\sqrt{2g_0}}\Big]_0^R = \sqrt{\frac{2R}{g_0}}, \qquad T = 4\sqrt{\frac{2R}{g_0}} . \] With \(R = 6.371\times10^6\) m and \(g_0 = 9.8203\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(T = 4556.4\ \mathrm s = 75.94\) min, against the uniform ball's \(84.3\) min.

4. Seismology says the Earth is strongly centrally condensed: the core is about twice the mean density, and the measured interior \(g\) stays near \(9.8\)–\(10.7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) through the whole mantle instead of falling linearly to zero. The constant-\(g\) model is therefore much the better guide over most of the depth, and since it gives a larger restoring force at every depth than the uniform model, the correction to \(84.3\) min is negative: the true period is shorter, and of order \(76\) min.
