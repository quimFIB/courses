the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You construct \(G(t-t')\) for \(\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = f\) from its defining property — zero before the kick, continuous, a unit jump in the derivative — solve an arbitrary drive by convolution, verify the answer against a directly computed piecewise solution including at the corners, and say why the solution space is two-dimensional by citing unit 01's theorem, not by counting constants.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about five hours. Work them on paper first. Every number asked for can be got exactly or to four figures by hand.

The instrument. Several problems use the deck's galvanometer: \(I\ddot\theta + b\dot\theta + \kappa\theta = \alpha\,i(t)\) with \(I = 1.0\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), \(\kappa = 6.4\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{N\,m\,rad^{-1}}\), \(\alpha = 2.0\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N\,m\,A^{-1}}\), coil resistance \(r = 5\,\Omega\). Hence \(\omega_0 = 8\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\) and \(\gamma = 400/(R+r)\, \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) with \(R\) the external resistance. Full scale is \(1.5625\,\mathrm{rad} = 89.5^\circ\), reached by a steady \(0.50\) mA.

Throughout, \(L x = \ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x\), \(p(\lambda) = \lambda^2 + \gamma\lambda + \omega_0^2\), \(\lambda_\pm = -\gamma/2 \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2/4-\omega_0^2}\), \(\omega_\mathrm{d} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}\), \(\chi(\omega) = 1/p(i\omega)\) and \(G\) is the impulse response.

P1 Turning the knob routine

exercises: the characteristic polynomial, classification of regimes, decay times

For the galvanometer at \(R = 995\,\Omega\), \(R = 75\,\Omega\), \(R = 20\,\Omega\) and \(R = 0\):

  1. Compute \(\gamma\), the discriminant \(\gamma^2 - 4\omega_0^2\), both roots of \(p\) to four figures, the regime, and \(Q\).
  2. Write down a basis of \(\ker L\) in each case, real-valued.
  3. Give the time constant of the slowest-decaying term in each case, and say which setting brings the needle to rest soonest.
  4. Check every row against \(\lambda_+\lambda_- = \omega_0^2\) and \(\lambda_+ + \lambda_- = -\gamma\).
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

A four-row table: two numbers, two roots, a word, and a time constant per row. Part 3's answer is one of the four settings.

Rung 2

\(\gamma = 400/(R+r)\) with \(r = 5\,\Omega\), and the only comparison that matters is \(\gamma\) against \(2\omega_0 = 16\). Do that comparison for all four rows before you solve a single quadratic.

Rung 3

For the underdamped rows the slow decay is the envelope \(e^{-\gamma t/2}\), so the time constant is \(2/\gamma\). For the overdamped row it is set by the root nearer zero, not the larger one — and that root is the surprise of the problem.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(R = 995\,\Omega \Rightarrow \gamma = 400/1000 = 0.4\), and \(\gamma^2-4\omega_0^2 = 0.16-256 < 0\).

P2 The complex exponential, used routine

exercises: Euler's formula, real and imaginary parts of complex solutions

  1. From \(\exp(z+w) = \exp z\exp w\) and Euler's formula, derive the addition formulas for \(\cos(a+b)\) and \(\sin(a+b)\) in two lines.
  2. Evaluate \((1+i)^8\) and \(\exp(i\pi/3)\) exactly, and \(|\exp(2 - 3i)|\).
  3. Prove that if \(L\) has real coefficients and \(z : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}\) satisfies \(Lz = 0\), then \(\operatorname{Re}z\) and \(\operatorname{Im}z\) are both in \(\ker L\). Then use it to turn the complex basis \(\{e^{\lambda_+t}, e^{\lambda_-t}\}\) of the underdamped case into a real one, and compute that real pair's Wronskian.
  4. Where exactly does the argument in part 3 fail if \(L\) is allowed a complex coefficient? Give an \(L\) and a \(z\) that break it.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is two identities; part 2 is three numbers, two of them exact; part 3 is a proof followed by a pair of functions and one more number; part 4 is a counterexample.

Rung 2

For part 1, write \(e^{i(a+b)}\) two ways and equate real and imaginary parts — legitimate only because \(a\) and \(b\) are real. For part 2, square \(1+i\) before doing anything else.

Rung 3

For part 3, the whole proof is the observation that \(L\) has real coefficients, so \(Lz = Lu + iLv\) with \(Lu\) and \(Lv\) real-valued. Part 4 asks you to break exactly that sentence: take an \(L\) with a genuinely complex coefficient and a \(z\) in its kernel.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\cos(a+b)+i\sin(a+b) = e^{i(a+b)} = e^{ia}e^{ib}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Everything follows from \(\exp(z+w) = \exp z\exp w\) and Euler.

Working.

  1. \(\cos(a+b) + i\sin(a+b) = e^{i(a+b)} = e^{ia}e^{ib} = (\cos a + i\sin a)(\cos b + i\sin b) = (\cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b) + i(\sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b)\). Both \(a, b\) real, so the real and imaginary parts may be equated, giving both formulas at once.
  2. \((1+i)^2 = 2i\), so \((1+i)^8 = (2i)^4 = 16\,i^4 = 16\). \(e^{i\pi/3} = \cos60^\circ + i\sin60^\circ = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i\). \(|e^{2-3i}| = e^2 = 7.38906\).

  3. Proof. Write \(z = u + iv\) with \(u, v\) real-valued. Since differentiation acts componentwise and \(\gamma, \omega_0^2\) are real, \(Lz = (\ddot u + \gamma\dot u + \omega_0^2u) + i(\ddot v + \gamma\dot v + \omega_0^2v) = Lu + i\,Lv\), and both \(Lu\) and \(Lv\) are real-valued. A complex number vanishes exactly when its real and imaginary parts do, so \(Lz = 0 \Rightarrow Lu = Lv = 0\). \(\square\)

    With \(\lambda_+ = -\gamma/2 + i\omega_\mathrm{d}\), \(e^{\lambda_+t} = e^{-\gamma t/2}(\cos\omega_\mathrm{d}t + i\sin\omega_\mathrm{d}t)\), so \(x_1 = e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos\omega_\mathrm{d}t\) and \(x_2 = e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\omega_\mathrm{d}t\). At \(t = 0\): \(x_1 = 1\), \(\dot x_1 = -\gamma/2\), \(x_2 = 0\), \(\dot x_2 = \omega_\mathrm{d}\), so \(W(0) = 1\cdot\omega_\mathrm{d} - 0 = \omega_\mathrm{d} \ne 0\) and by Abel \(W(t) = \omega_\mathrm{d}e^{-\gamma t}\). A basis.

  4. The step that fails is "\(Lu\) and \(Lv\) are real". Take \(Lx = \ddot x + 2i\dot x - x\), whose characteristic polynomial is \((\lambda+i)^2\), so \(z = e^{-it} \in \ker L\). But \(\operatorname{Re}z = \cos t\) gives \(L(\cos t) = -\cos t - 2i\sin t - \cos t = -2\cos t - 2i\sin t \ne 0\).

Check. Part 1 at \(a = b = \pi/4\): \(\cos(\pi/2) = \tfrac12-\tfrac12 = 0\) ✓. Part 3 in the limit \(\gamma \to 0\): \(W = \omega_0\), constant — Abel with \(\gamma = 0\) ✓. Number: for the galvanometer at \(R = 995\,\Omega\), \(W(0) = \omega_\mathrm{d} = 7.9975\).

P3 Abel's identity, and reduction of order medium

exercises: the Wronskian, Abel's identity, producing the second solution without guessing

  1. Prove that for any \(x_1, x_2 \in \ker L\) the Wronskian satisfies \(\dot W = -\gamma W\), and deduce Abel's identity. State precisely where the equation \(Lx = 0\) is used.
  2. Prove the equivalence: \(W(t_0) \ne 0\) for some \(t_0\) \(\iff\) \(W(t) \ne 0\) for all \(t\) \(\iff\) \(\{x_1,x_2\}\) is a basis of \(\ker L\). Your proof must use \(\dim\ker L = 2\) and say where.
  3. Take \(\gamma = 2\omega_0\) and the known solution \(x_1 = e^{-\omega_0t}\). Without guessing the second solution, use Abel's identity as a first-order equation for \(x_2\) — that is, solve \(x_1\dot x_2 - x_2\dot x_1 = W(0) e^{-\gamma t}\) by unit 00's methods — and recover \(x_2 = te^{-\omega_0t}\).
  4. Repeat part 3 for a general \(\gamma < 2\omega_0\) with \(x_1 = e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos\omega_\mathrm{d}t\), on an interval where \(x_1 \ne 0\), and recover the sine solution.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 are proofs; parts 3 and 4 each end with a named function you have already seen, obtained without guessing it.

Rung 2

For part 1, differentiate \(W = x_1\dot x_2-x_2\dot x_1\) and watch two of the four terms cancel before you substitute anything. For part 2, the three implications are (i)\(\Rightarrow\)(ii)\(\Rightarrow\)(iii)\(\Rightarrow\)(i), and exactly one of them needs \(\dim\ker L = 2\).

Rung 3

For parts 3 and 4, treat Abel's identity as a first-order equation for the unknown \(x_2\) with \(x_1\) known. Dividing through by \(x_1^2\) turns the left side into a derivative of a quotient — that is the whole trick, and it is unit 00's integrating-factor idea.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\dfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big(\dfrac{x_2}{x_1}\Big) = \dfrac{x_1\dot x_2-x_2\dot x_1}{x_1^{2}} = \dfrac{W}{x_1^{2}}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Differentiate \(W\) once; everything else is bookkeeping. Then use Abel backwards as a first-order equation for the unknown second solution.

Working.

  1. \(\dot W = \dot x_1\dot x_2 + x_1\ddot x_2 - \dot x_2\dot x_1 - x_2\ddot x_1 = x_1\ddot x_2 - x_2\ddot x_1\). Here and only here substitute \(\ddot x_i = -\gamma\dot x_i - \omega_0^2x_i\): \[\dot W = x_1(-\gamma\dot x_2-\omega_0^2x_2) - x_2(-\gamma\dot x_1-\omega_0^2x_1) = -\gamma(x_1\dot x_2 - x_2\dot x_1) = -\gamma W,\] the \(\omega_0^2\) terms cancelling identically. Then \((We^{\gamma t})^{\boldsymbol\cdot} = e^{\gamma t}(\dot W + \gamma W) = 0\), so \(We^{\gamma t}\) is constant: \(W(t) = W(t_0)e^{-\gamma(t-t_0)}\). \(\square\)
  2. (i) \(\Rightarrow\) (ii): the exponential never vanishes. (ii) \(\Rightarrow\) (iii): \(W(t_0) \ne 0\) makes \(M = \begin{pmatrix}x_1(t_0)&x_2(t_0)\\\dot x_1(t_0)&\dot x_2(t_0)\end{pmatrix}\) invertible, so \(\mathrm{ev}(c_1x_1+c_2x_2) = M(c_1,c_2)^{\!\top}\) covers all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\); since \(\mathrm{ev}\) is a bijection onto \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and \(\dim\ker L = 2\), the span is all of \(\ker L\), and two spanning elements of a two-dimensional space are a basis. (iii) \(\Rightarrow\) (i): contrapositive — if \(W(t_0) = 0\), choose \((c_1,c_2) \ne 0\) in \(\ker M\); then \(x = c_1x_1+c_2x_2\) has \(\mathrm{ev}(x) = 0\), hence \(x \equiv 0\) by injectivity of \(\mathrm{ev}\), so \(x_1, x_2\) are linearly dependent. \(\square\)
  3. \(\gamma = 2\omega_0\), \(x_1 = e^{-\omega_0t}\). Normalise \(W(0) = 1\), so \(W = e^{-2\omega_0t}\). Divide \(x_1\dot x_2 - \dot x_1x_2 = W\) by \(x_1^2 = e^{-2\omega_0t}\): \[\Big(\frac{x_2}{x_1}\Big)^{\boldsymbol\cdot} = \frac{W}{x_1^2} = \frac{e^{-2\omega_0t}}{e^{-2\omega_0t}} = 1 \implies \frac{x_2}{x_1} = t + C.\] Take \(C = 0\): \(x_2 = te^{-\omega_0t}\). No guessing was involved, and the factor \(t\) appears because \(W/x_1^2\) happens to be constant — which is exactly the statement \(\lambda_+ = \lambda_-\).
  4. \(x_1 = e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos\omega_\mathrm{d}t\), \(W = W(0)e^{-\gamma t}\), so on an interval where \(\cos\omega_\mathrm{d}t \ne 0\), \((x_2/x_1)^{\boldsymbol\cdot} = W(0)e^{-\gamma t}/(e^{-\gamma t}\cos^2\omega_\mathrm{d}t) = W(0)\sec^2\omega_\mathrm{d}t\), whence \(x_2/x_1 = W(0)\tan(\omega_\mathrm{d}t)/\omega_\mathrm{d}\) and \(x_2 = \frac{W(0)}{\omega_\mathrm{d}}e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\omega_\mathrm{d}t\). Choosing \(W(0) = \omega_\mathrm{d}\) gives the sine solution exactly; it extends to all \(t\) by continuity, or by noting that it solves \(Lx = 0\) everywhere.

Check. Dimensions: \([W] = [x]^2\mathrm{T^{-1}}\) and the exponent \(\gamma(t-t_0)\) is a pure number ✓. Limit \(\gamma \to 0\): \(W\) constant, the undamped flow preserving phase-space area ✓. Number: at \(\gamma = 0.4\), \(W(1\,\mathrm{s})/W(0) = e^{-0.4} = 0.6703\).

P4 Critical damping is genuinely special medium

exercises: the repeated root, the Jordan-block degeneracy, an extremal claim

Take \(x(0) = x_0 > 0\), \(\dot x(0) = 0\).

  1. Show that \(\gamma = 2\omega_0\) gives a repeated root and that \(\{e^{-\omega_0t}, te^{-\omega_0t}\}\) is a basis, and write \(x(t)\) explicitly for these initial conditions.
  2. Prove that for every \(\gamma > 2\omega_0\) the slow root satisfies \(|\lambda_+| < \omega_0\), so the approach to equilibrium is strictly slower than in the critical case; and that \(|\lambda_+| \to 0\) as \(\gamma \to \infty\).
  3. Prove that for every \(\gamma < 2\omega_0\) the solution crosses zero, infinitely often. Conclude: critical damping is the unique fastest return without overshoot.
  4. For the galvanometer, compute the time to settle permanently within \(1\%\) of \(x_0\) at \(\gamma = 16\) and at \(\gamma = 80\), to three figures, and give their ratio.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an explicit function; parts 2 and 3 are inequalities proved about the roots; part 4 is two times and their ratio.

Rung 2

For part 2, do not compute \(\lambda_+\) from the square root. Use \(\lambda_+\lambda_- = \omega_0^2\) together with the fact that \(\lambda_-\) is the more negative of the two.

Rung 3

For part 3, write the solution in the form \(Ce^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega_\mathrm{d}t-\psi)\) by combining the cosine and sine into one; zeros of a cosine are easy to count, zeros of a sum of two are not. For part 4, note that in the overdamped case one of the two exponentials is irrelevant after a few hundredths of a second.

Rung 4 — the first line

Critical, from rest: \(x(t) = x_0e^{-\omega_0t}(1+\omega_0t)\); solve \((1+u)e^{-u} = 0.01\) with \(u = \omega_0t\).

Worked solution

Idea. The degenerate root is where the two exponentials collide; the extremal claim is two inequalities about where the roots sit.

Working.

  1. \(\gamma = 2\omega_0\) makes the discriminant \(\gamma^2-4\omega_0^2 = 0\), so \(\lambda = -\omega_0\) twice, \(p(\lambda) = p'(\lambda) = 0\), and \(L(te^{\lambda t}) = p(\lambda)te^{\lambda t}+p'(\lambda)e^{\lambda t} = 0\). The Wronskian at \(t = 0\) is \(1\cdot1 - 0\cdot(-\omega_0) = 1 \ne 0\), so they are a basis. With \(x(0) = x_0\), \(\dot x(0) = 0\): \[x(t) = x_0\,e^{-\omega_0t}(1+\omega_0t), \qquad \dot x = -x_0\,\omega_0^2\,t\,e^{-\omega_0t}.\]
  2. Proof. For \(\gamma > 2\omega_0\) both roots are real and negative, with \(\lambda_+\lambda_- = \omega_0^2\) and \(|\lambda_-| > |\lambda_+|\) (because \(\lambda_- < \lambda_+ < 0\)). Hence \(|\lambda_+|^2 < |\lambda_+||\lambda_-| = \omega_0^2\), so \(|\lambda_+| < \omega_0\): every overdamped solution decays strictly more slowly than \(e^{-\omega_0t}\). And \(|\lambda_+| = \omega_0^2/|\lambda_-| \le \omega_0^2/(\gamma/2) \to 0\) as \(\gamma \to \infty\). \(\square\)

  3. Proof. For \(\gamma < 2\omega_0\), the solution from rest is \[x(t) = x_0e^{-\gamma t/2}\Big(\cos\omega_\mathrm{d}t + \tfrac{\gamma}{2\omega_\mathrm{d}}\sin\omega_\mathrm{d}t\Big) = x_0A\,e^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega_\mathrm{d}t-\psi),\] with \(A = \sqrt{1+\gamma^2/4\omega_\mathrm{d}^2} > 0\) and \(\tan\psi = \gamma/2\omega_\mathrm{d}\). The cosine vanishes at \(t = (\psi + (n+\tfrac12)\pi)/\omega_\mathrm{d}\) for every integer \(n\), and the exponential prefactor never does; so \(x\) changes sign infinitely often. \(\square\)

    Combining: the non-overshooting cases are exactly \(\gamma \ge 2\omega_0\), and among them the eventual decay rate is \(\omega_0\) at \(\gamma = 2\omega_0\) and \(|\lambda_+| < \omega_0\) for every larger \(\gamma\). It is therefore maximised at \(\gamma = 2\omega_0\): critical damping is the unique fastest return without overshoot.

  4. \(\gamma = 16\): solve \((1+8t)e^{-8t} = 0.01\). Putting \(u = 8t\), \((1+u)e^{-u} = 0.01\) gives \(u = 6.6384\), so \(t = 0.830\) s. \(\gamma = 80\): \(x/x_0 = (\lambda_-e^{\lambda_+t}-\lambda_+e^{\lambda_-t})/ (\lambda_--\lambda_+) = 1.0103\,e^{-0.80816t} - 0.0103\,e^{-79.19t}\); the fast term is negligible after a few hundredths of a second, and \(1.0103\,e^{-0.80816t} = 0.01\) gives \(t = 5.71\) s. Ratio \(5.711/0.8298 = 6.88\).

Check. Dimensions: \(\omega_0t\) is a pure number ✓. Limit: as \(\gamma \to 2\omega_0^+\), \(|\lambda_+| \to \omega_0\) and the overdamped settling time tends continuously to the critical one ✓. Number: at \(\gamma = 80\) the instrument is \(6.88\) times slower than at \(\gamma = 16\), which is the whole reason a shorted galvanometer is useless as a meter.

P5 Resonance is not at \(\omega_0\) medium

exercises: the frequency response, three different resonant frequencies, the exact half-power width

With \(\chi(\omega) = 1/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2+i\gamma\omega)\):

  1. Show that \(|\chi|\) is maximal at \(\omega_{\text{peak}} = \sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/2}\), that this exists only for \(Q > 1/\sqrt2\), and that the maximum value is \(1/(\gamma\omega_\mathrm{d})\).
  2. Show that the velocity amplitude \(\omega|\chi|\) and the mean absorbed power both peak exactly at \(\omega_0\), for every \(\gamma\).
  3. Show that the absorbed-power curve's full width at half maximum is exactly \(\gamma\), with no approximation, and that \(\omega_0/\Delta\omega = Q\).
  4. For the galvanometer at \(R = 995\,\Omega\), give \(\omega_0\), \(\omega_\mathrm{d}\) and \(\omega_{\text{peak}}\) to six figures, and the two half-power frequencies to four. Which of the three does a physicist mean by "the resonant frequency", and why?
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is one frequency and one maximum value; part 2 is a proof that a maximum sits at a specific point; part 3 is an exact width; part 4 is five numbers and a sentence.

Rung 2

Never differentiate with respect to \(\omega\) here. Set \(u = \omega^2\): the amplitude's denominator becomes a quadratic in \(u\), and finding a quadratic's vertex needs no calculus at all.

Rung 3

For parts 2 and 3, divide the denominator through by \(\omega^2\): the power curve depends on \(\omega\) only through \(g(\omega) = \omega_0^2/\omega - \omega\), and \(g\) vanishes at exactly one positive frequency. The half-power condition is then \(g^2 = \gamma^2\), a pair of quadratics whose roots differ by \(\gamma\).

Rung 4 — the first line

\(D(u) = (\omega_0^2-u)^2+\gamma^2u\), so \(\mathrm{d}D/\mathrm{d}u = -2(\omega_0^2-u)+\gamma^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. Maximise \(|\chi|^2\) in the variable \(u = \omega^2\); then notice that the velocity and power curves are a different function of \(\omega\), whose maximum is exact.

Working.

  1. \(|\chi|^{-2} = D(\omega) = (\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2\). With \(u = \omega^2 \ge 0\), \(D = (\omega_0^2-u)^2 + \gamma^2u\) is a quadratic in \(u\) with positive leading coefficient, minimised where \(\mathrm{d}D/\mathrm{d}u = -2(\omega_0^2-u)+\gamma^2 = 0\), i.e. \(u = \omega_0^2-\gamma^2/2\). That is admissible (\(u > 0\)) exactly when \(\gamma^2 < 2\omega_0^2\), i.e. \(Q > 1/\sqrt2\); otherwise the minimum over \(u \ge 0\) is at \(u = 0\) and \(|\chi|\) decreases from the start. At the interior minimum, \[D = \Big(\frac{\gamma^2}{2}\Big)^2 + \gamma^2\Big(\omega_0^2-\frac{\gamma^2}{2}\Big) = \gamma^2\Big(\omega_0^2-\frac{\gamma^2}{4}\Big) = \gamma^2\omega_\mathrm{d}^2,\] so \(|\chi|_{\max} = 1/(\gamma\omega_\mathrm{d})\).
  2. The velocity amplitude and the mean absorbed power are \[\omega|\chi| = \Big[\frac{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2}{\omega^2}+\gamma^2\Big]^{-1/2}, \qquad \bar P = \tfrac12\gamma F_0^2\Big[\frac{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2}{\omega^2} + \gamma^2\Big]^{-1}.\] Both are decreasing functions of \(g(\omega)^2\), where \(g(\omega) = \omega_0^2/\omega - \omega\). Since \(g^2 \ge 0\) with equality only at \(\omega = \omega_0\), both are maximal exactly there, for every \(\gamma\) and with no approximation.
  3. Half maximum needs \(g^2 = \gamma^2\), i.e. \(|\omega_0^2-\omega^2| = \gamma\omega\), i.e. \(\omega^2 \pm \gamma\omega - \omega_0^2 = 0\). The positive roots are \(\omega_\mp = \mp\tfrac\gamma2 + \sqrt{\omega_0^2+\gamma^2/4}\), so \(\Delta\omega = \omega_+-\omega_- = \gamma\) exactly, and \(\omega_0/\Delta\omega = \omega_0/\gamma = Q\).

  4. \(\gamma = 0.4\): \(\omega_0 = 8.00000\), \(\omega_\mathrm{d} = \sqrt{64-0.04} = 7.99750\), \(\omega_{\text{peak}} = \sqrt{64-0.08} = 7.99500\). Half-power points \(\mp0.2+\sqrt{64.04} = 7.8025\) and \(8.2025\), difference \(0.4000\).

    A physicist saying "the resonant frequency" means \(\omega_0\), and is right to: it is where the system absorbs power, it is where the phase is exactly \(\pi/2\), and both statements are exact for every damping. \(\omega_{\text{peak}}\) is a fact about the amplitude curve, and \(\omega_\mathrm{d}\) about the free ring-down; neither is what an absorption measurement returns.

Check. Dimensions: all four frequencies are \(\mathrm{T^{-1}}\), and \(\gamma^2/2\) is too ✓. Limit \(\gamma \to 0\): all three collapse to \(\omega_0\) and \(|\chi|_{\max} \to \infty\) — undamped resonance ✓. Rung 1

Part 1 is a product of two sines with an explicit prefactor; part 2 is one function; part 3 is an argument about \(\ker L\); part 4 is two times and a detuning.

Rung 2

Rung 4 — the first line

Worked solution

Idea. Superpose the steady state with the free solution that cancels it at \(t = 0\); the result is a product of sines, and resonance is its limit.

Working.

  1. The envelope's maximum is \(2F_0/|\omega_0^2-\omega^2|\) (a ceiling \(|x|\) never exceeds).
  2. Fix \(t\) and let \(\omega \to \omega_0\). Both numerator and denominator vanish; differentiating each with respect to \(\omega\), \(\partial_\omega[F_0(\cos\omega t-\cos\omega_0t)] = -F_0t\sin\omega t\) and \(\partial_\omega[\omega_0^2-\omega^2] = -2\omega\), so by l'Hôpital \[x(t) = \frac{F_0\,t\sin\omega_0t}{2\omega_0}.\] Verify: \(\dot x = \frac{F_0}{2\omega_0}(\sin\omega_0t + \omega_0t\cos\omega_0t)\), \(\ddot x = \frac{F_0}{2\omega_0}(2\omega_0\cos\omega_0t - \omega_0^2t\sin\omega_0t)\), so \(\ddot x + \omega_0^2x = F_0\cos\omega_0t\) ✓, and \(x(0) = \dot x(0) = 0\) ✓.

The detuning is \(\Delta = 0.01602\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\), a fractional detuning of \(0.20\%\); the first full-scale excursion arrives at \(\pi/\Delta = 196\) s and they recur every \(392\) s.

Check. Dimensions: \([F_0t/\omega_0] = [x]\) ✓. Limit: as \(\Delta \to 0\) at fixed \(t\), \(2F_0\sin(\Delta t/2)/(2\omega_0\Delta) \to F_0t/2\omega_0\), reproducing part 2 ✓. Number: \(2F_0/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)\) at \(\omega = 7.98398\) is \(0.4/0.256 = 1.5625\) rad, full scale ✓.

For part 2, you need \(\dot G(\cdot-t_0) \in \ker L\); the reason is that \(L\) has constant coefficients and so commutes with \(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t\). Then you need three values at \(t_0^+\): \(G = 0\), \(\dot G = 1\), and \(\ddot G\), which comes from the equation \(LG = 0\) itself.

Rung 4 — the first line

For distinct roots, \(W(s) = (\lambda_--\lambda_+)e^{(\lambda_++\lambda_-)s}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Two computations and one uniqueness argument.

Working.

  1. Distinct roots. Take \(x_1 = e^{\lambda_+t}\), \(x_2 = e^{\lambda_-t}\). Then \(W(s) = e^{\lambda_+s}\lambda_-e^{\lambda_-s} - e^{\lambda_-s}\lambda_+ e^{\lambda_+s} = (\lambda_--\lambda_+)e^{(\lambda_++\lambda_-)s}\), and \[K(t,s) = \frac{e^{\lambda_+s}e^{\lambda_-t}-e^{\lambda_-s}e^{\lambda_+t}} {(\lambda_--\lambda_+)e^{(\lambda_++\lambda_-)s}} = \frac{e^{\lambda_-(t-s)}-e^{\lambda_+(t-s)}}{\lambda_--\lambda_+} = \frac{e^{\lambda_+(t-s)}-e^{\lambda_-(t-s)}}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-} = G(t-s).\]

    Repeated root \(\lambda = -\omega_0\). Take \(x_1 = e^{\lambda t}\), \(x_2 = te^{\lambda t}\); then \(W(s) = e^{\lambda s}(1+\lambda s)e^{\lambda s} - se^{\lambda s}\lambda e^{\lambda s} = e^{2\lambda s}\), and \(K(t,s) = \big(e^{\lambda s}te^{\lambda t}-se^{\lambda s}e^{\lambda t}\big)/ e^{2\lambda s} = (t-s)e^{\lambda(t-s)} = G(t-s)\).

    Independence of basis. If \((y_1,y_2) = (x_1,x_2)M\) with \(M\) an invertible constant matrix, then the numerator of \(K\) is \(\det\begin{pmatrix}y_1(s)&y_2(s)\\y_1(t)&y_2(t)\end{pmatrix}\), which picks up a factor \(\det M\); and \(W_y = \det M\cdot W_x\) likewise. The factors cancel, so \(K\) is unchanged. (It had to be: \(K\) is determined by the equation and the data, and no basis was mentioned in the statement of the problem \(Lx = f\), \(x(t_0) = \dot x(t_0) = 0\).)

  2. Put \(x_h(t) = x_0\big(\dot G(t-t_0)+\gamma G(t-t_0)\big) + v_0G(t-t_0)\). Each of \(G(\cdot-t_0)\) and \(\dot G(\cdot-t_0)\) is in \(\ker L\) on \(t > t_0\): the first because \(LG = 0\) there, the second because \(L\) has constant coefficients and therefore commutes with \(\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t\) (\(L\dot G = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}LG = 0\)). So \(x_h \in \ker L\). At \(t = t_0^+\): \(G = 0\), \(\dot G = 1\), and \(\ddot G = -\gamma\dot G-\omega_0^2G = -\gamma\), so \[x_h(t_0) = x_0(1+0) + 0 = x_0, \qquad \dot x_h(t_0) = x_0(-\gamma+\gamma) + v_0 = v_0 .\] Meanwhile \((G*f)(t_0) = 0\) and its derivative there is \(G(0)f(t_0) + \int_{t_0}^{t_0}\dot G\,f = 0\). So \(x = x_h + G*f\) satisfies \(Lx = f\) with the stated data, and by unit 01's uniqueness theorem it is the solution. \(\square\)
  3. If two systems have the same \(G\) then, by part 2, they produce the same \(x\) for every \(f\) and every \((x_0,v_0)\) — the formula contains no other information about the system. The two numbers hidden in \(G\) are \(\gamma\) and \(\omega_0\). From a measured \(G\): read the ringing period to get \(\omega_\mathrm{d} = 2\pi/T_\mathrm{d}\), read the logarithmic decrement of successive maxima to get \(\gamma = 2\delta/T_\mathrm{d}\), and then \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{\omega_\mathrm{d}^2+\gamma^2/4}\). Two readings off one ring-down and the system is known completely.

Check. Dimensions: \([\dot G] = 1\) and \([\gamma G] = 1\), so \(x_0\) multiplies a pure number ✓. Limit \(f = 0\), \(x_0 = 0\): \(x = v_0G\), which is the definition of the impulse response ✓. Number: for the galvanometer at \(\gamma = 0.4\), \(T_\mathrm{d} = 0.78564\) s and \(\delta = 0.15713\) give \(\gamma = 2\times0.15713/0.78564 = 0.4000\) and \(\omega_0 = \sqrt{7.99750^2+0.04} = 8.0000\) ✓.

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\dot E = \dot x\ddot x+\omega_0^2x\dot x = \dot x\big(\ddot x+\omega_0^2x\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Differentiate \(E\) once; then note that the energy is an exponential times something periodic, which makes the "per cycle" statement exact.

Working.

  1. \(\dot E = \dot x\ddot x + \omega_0^2x\dot x = \dot x(\ddot x + \omega_0^2x) = \dot x(-\gamma\dot x) = -\gamma\dot x^2 \le 0\), with equality only where \(\dot x = 0\). This is unit 02's gradient-like flow: \(E\) is a Lyapunov function with \(\dot E \le 0\), non-strict, which is exactly the situation LaSalle's invariance principle was built for — and it gives asymptotic stability of the origin without any linearisation.
  2. Write \(x = Ae^{-\gamma t/2}\cos(\omega_\mathrm{d}t-\phi)\); then both \(x\) and \(\dot x\) are \(e^{-\gamma t/2}\) times a \(T_\mathrm{d}\)-periodic function, so \(E(t) = e^{-\gamma t}P(t)\) with \(P(t+T_\mathrm{d}) = P(t)\). Hence \[\int_{t+T_\mathrm{d}}^{t+2T_\mathrm{d}}\!\!E = \int_{t}^{t+T_\mathrm{d}}\!\! e^{-\gamma(s+T_\mathrm{d})}P(s+T_\mathrm{d})\,\mathrm{d}s = e^{-\gamma T_\mathrm{d}}\int_{t}^{t+T_\mathrm{d}}\!\! E,\] substituting \(s \mapsto s+T_\mathrm{d}\). The period-average therefore falls by exactly \(e^{-\gamma T_\mathrm{d}}\) each cycle — no small-\(\gamma\) assumption.
  3. Successive maxima of \(x\) are \(T_\mathrm{d}\) apart (the stationarity condition \(\tan(\omega_\mathrm{d}t-\phi) = -\gamma/2\omega_\mathrm{d}\) has solutions spaced by \(\pi/\omega_\mathrm{d}\), alternating maxima and minima), and at each the cosine factor repeats; so the ratio is the envelope's, \(\delta = \gamma T_\mathrm{d}/2 = \pi\gamma/\omega_\mathrm{d}\). Then \[\frac{\pi}{\delta} = \frac{\omega_\mathrm{d}}{\gamma} = \frac{\omega_0}{\gamma}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{4Q^2}} = Q\Big(1 - \frac{1}{8Q^2}+O(Q^{-4})\Big),\] a relative error \(1/8Q^2\).
  4. \(\gamma = 0.4\), \(\omega_\mathrm{d} = 7.997500\): \(T_\mathrm{d} = 2\pi/\omega_\mathrm{d} = 0.785644\) s; \(\delta = \pi\gamma/\omega_\mathrm{d} = 0.157129\); successive-maximum ratio \(e^{-\delta} = 0.854594\); energy lost per cycle \(1-e^{-\gamma T_\mathrm{d}} = 0.269669\); \(2\pi/Q = 0.314159\). The last two disagree by \((0.314159-0.269669)/0.269669 = \mathbf{16.5\%}\). The definition used in this course is \(Q = \omega_0/\gamma\); the "energy-per-cycle" reading is a consequence of it, valid to \(O(1/Q)\), and it is the one to distrust.

P12 Causality, and where the poles are hard

  2. Prove that, in the underdamped case \(\gamma < 2\omega_0\), the same integral converges for every complex \(\omega\) with \(\operatorname{Im}\omega < \gamma/2\), and identify precisely which property of \(G\) makes one half-plane special rather than both.
  3. Locate the two zeros of \(p(i\omega)\) in the complex \(\omega\) plane, and interpret their real and imaginary parts physically. What happens to them as \(\gamma \to 0\), and what does that say about an undamped oscillator?

Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an identity between an integral and a rational function; part 2 is an inequality defining a half-plane; part 3 is two complex numbers with an interpretation; part 4 is a list of missing ingredients and one unit number.

Rung 2

\(G\) is a difference of two exponentials, so the integral is two copies of \(\int_0^\infty e^{c\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau\) with \(\operatorname{Re}c < 0\). Nothing harder than that appears, and no contour is needed anywhere in this problem.

Rung 3

For part 2, all that matters is \(|e^{-i\omega\tau}| = e^{\tau\operatorname{Im}\omega}\) against the decay of \(|G|\); then ask what would change if the integral ran from \(-\infty\). For part 3, solve \(p(i\omega) = 0\) as a quadratic in \(\omega\), not in \(i\omega\).

Rung 4 — the first line

\(\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{(\lambda-i\omega)\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau = \frac{-1}{\lambda-i\omega}\) whenever \(\operatorname{Re}(\lambda-i\omega) < 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. \(G\) is a sum of two exponentials, so the integral is elementary; the lower limit \(0\) rather than \(-\infty\) is what makes one half-plane special.

Working.

  1. Proof. \(G(\tau) = \big(e^{\lambda_+\tau}-e^{\lambda_-\tau}\big)/ (\lambda_+-\lambda_-)\) for \(\tau \ge 0\), with \(\operatorname{Re}\lambda_\pm < 0\). For real \(\omega\) and either root, \[\int_0^\infty e^{(\lambda-i\omega)\tau}\,\mathrm{d}\tau = \Big[\frac{e^{(\lambda-i\omega)\tau}}{\lambda-i\omega}\Big]_0^\infty = \frac{-1}{\lambda-i\omega},\] the upper limit vanishing because \(|e^{(\lambda-i\omega)\tau}| = e^{\operatorname{Re}\lambda\,\tau} \to 0\). Hence \[\hat G(\omega) = \frac{1}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-} \Big[\frac{-1}{\lambda_+-i\omega}+\frac{1}{\lambda_--i\omega}\Big] = \frac{1}{\lambda_+-\lambda_-}\cdot\frac{\lambda_+-\lambda_-} {(\lambda_+-i\omega)(\lambda_--i\omega)} = \frac{1}{(i\omega-\lambda_+)(i\omega-\lambda_-)},\] the last step using \((-1)(-1) = 1\) on both factors. Since \(p(\mu) = (\mu-\lambda_+)(\mu-\lambda_-)\), this is \(1/p(i\omega) = \chi(\omega)\). \(\square\) (The repeated-root case follows by continuity, or by the same integral with \(G = \tau e^{\lambda\tau}\), which gives \(1/(i\omega-\lambda)^2\).)

  2. Proof. Let \(\omega = \omega_r + i\omega_i\). Then \(|e^{-i\omega\tau}| = e^{\omega_i\tau}\) and \(|G(\tau)| \le e^{-\gamma\tau/2}/\omega_\mathrm{d}\), so the integrand is bounded by \(e^{(\omega_i-\gamma/2)\tau}/\omega_\mathrm{d}\), which is integrable on \([0,\infty)\) whenever \(\omega_i < \gamma/2\). \(\square\)

    The hypothesis matters. Overdamped, \(0 \le G(\tau) \le e^{\lambda_+\tau}/(\lambda_+-\lambda_-)\) and the same argument gives only \(\omega_i < |\lambda_+|\), which is smaller than \(\gamma/2\) because \(\lambda_+ > -\gamma/2\); and it cannot do better, since \(\chi\) has a pole at \(\omega = i|\lambda_+|\). Critically damped, \(G = \tau e^{-\omega_0\tau}\) gives \(\omega_i < \omega_0 = \gamma/2\). In every regime the closed lower half-plane is inside.

    The property responsible is \(G(\tau) = 0\) for \(\tau < 0\). Had \(G\) been non-zero on the negative axis, the integral would have run over all of \(\mathbb{R}\), and the factor \(e^{\omega_i\tau}\) would have blown up at one end or the other for every \(\omega_i \ne 0\): no half-plane would be distinguished. One half-plane, not both, is the mathematical shadow of "no response before the kick".

  3. \(p(i\omega) = -\omega^2+i\gamma\omega+\omega_0^2 = 0\) gives \(\omega^2 - i\gamma\omega - \omega_0^2 = 0\), so \[\omega = \frac{i\gamma \pm \sqrt{4\omega_0^2-\gamma^2}}{2} = \pm\omega_\mathrm{d} + \frac{i\gamma}{2}.\] For the galvanometer at \(\gamma = 0.4\): \(\omega = \pm7.9975 + 0.2\,i\). The real part is the ringing frequency, the imaginary part the decay rate of the envelope: a pole of the response is a free mode of the system, written as one complex number. They sit in the upper half-plane, at height \(\gamma/2\) — on the far side from the region where \(\hat G\) converges, exactly as causality requires.

    As \(\gamma \to 0\) the poles descend to the real axis at \(\pm\omega_0\). A response function with a singularity at a real frequency has no bounded steady state there, which is undamped resonance seen from the complex plane; and the "no singularity in the lower half-plane" statement becomes marginal, which is the analytic face of the fact that an undamped oscillator never forgets its initial conditions.

  4. Still to be proved: (i) that an integral of this form defines a holomorphic function of \(\omega\) on the open half-plane, not merely a convergent one — Morera's theorem, or differentiation under the integral sign; (ii) Cauchy's theorem, to say that the contour integral of \(\chi(\omega')/(\omega'-\omega)\) round a closed path in that half-plane vanishes; (iii) an estimate showing the large semicircle contributes nothing, which needs \(\chi(\omega) \to 0\) as \(|\omega|\to\infty\) — true here, since \(\chi \sim -1/\omega^2\); and (iv) the Sokhotski–Plemelj limit, which is where the principal value and the factor \(1/\pi\) come from. All four are unit 20. Attempting them here would be using what has not arrived.

Check. Dimensions: \([\hat G] = \mathrm{T}\times\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{T^2} = [\chi]\) ✓. Limit: at \(\omega = 0\), \(\hat G(0) = \int_0^\infty G = 1/\omega_0^2 = \chi(0)\) ✓ — and that integral is the step response at \(t = \infty\), the static deflection, which is the same statement in the time domain. Number: at \(\gamma = 0.4\), \(\omega_0 = 8\), \(\omega = 5\), numerical integration of \(\int_0^\infty G(\tau)e^{-5i\tau}\mathrm{d}\tau\) gives \(0.0255738 - 0.0013115\,i\), and \(1/(39+2i) = 0.0255738 - 0.0013115\,i\) ✓.
