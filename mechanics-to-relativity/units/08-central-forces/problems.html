Identities and the two-body reduction

P1 The unit radial vector, differentiated routine

exercises: BAC–CAB, Lagrange's identity, \(\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r} = r\dot r\), a proof

Let \(\vec r(t)\) be any \(C^1\) curve in \(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{\vec 0\}\), with \(r = \lVert\vec r\rVert\) and \(\hat r = \vec r/r\). No force law is assumed until part 4.

  1. Prove \(\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r} = r\dot r\).
  2. Prove \[ \frac{\mathrm d\hat r}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{(\vec r\times\dot{\vec r})\times\vec r}{r^3} . \]
  3. Prove, for any motion, \(\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2 = \dot r^2 + \lVert\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2/r^2\). Deduce that for a particle of mass \(\mu\) with angular momentum \(\vec L\) about the origin, \(\tfrac12\mu\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2 = \tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 + L^2/2\mu r^2\) — the effective potential's extra term, obtained without polar coordinates or even planarity.
  4. Now suppose \(\vec L = \vec 0\). Use part 2 to prove that \(\hat r\) is constant, so the motion is along a fixed ray.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

All four parts are identities between vector-valued functions of \(t\), proved by differentiating and then applying one of the cross-product identities from the identities slide of the deck.

Rung 2

For part 1 differentiate \(r^2\) two ways. For part 2 differentiate the quotient \(\vec r/r\), then recognise a BAC–CAB expansion in what you get. Part 3 is Lagrange's identity with two pairs of equal vectors.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\dfrac{\vec r}{r} = \dfrac{\dot{\vec r}}{r} - \dfrac{\dot r}{r^2}\vec r = \dfrac{r^2\dot{\vec r} - (\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r})\vec r}{r^3}\), using part 1. Now compare with \(\vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c)\) for a suitable choice of \(\vec a,\vec b,\vec c\).

Worked solution

Idea. Differentiate, then let the identities do the rearranging. Everything here is pure kinematics: it is true of any curve, which is why it can be used for any force.

1. \(r^2 = \vec r\cdot\vec r\). Differentiating, with the product rule for the dot product, \(2r\dot r = 2\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r}\). Divide by 2. \(\square\)

2. By the quotient rule and part 1, \[ \frac{\mathrm d\hat r}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{\dot{\vec r}}{r} - \frac{\dot r\,\vec r}{r^2} = \frac{(\vec r\cdot\vec r)\,\dot{\vec r} - (\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r})\,\vec r}{r^3} . \] BAC–CAB with \(\vec a = \vec r\), \(\vec b = \dot{\vec r}\), \(\vec c = \vec r\) reads \(\vec r\times(\dot{\vec r}\times\vec r) = \dot{\vec r}(\vec r\cdot\vec r) - \vec r(\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r})\), which is the numerator. Finally \(\vec r\times(\dot{\vec r}\times\vec r) = -\vec r\times(\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}) = (\vec r\times\dot{\vec r})\times\vec r\) by antisymmetry, twice. \(\square\)

3. Lagrange's identity with \(\vec a = \vec c = \vec r\) and \(\vec b = \vec d = \dot{\vec r}\): \(\lVert\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2 = r^2\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2 - (\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r})^2 = r^2\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2 - r^2\dot r^2\). Divide by \(r^2\) and rearrange. With \(\vec L = \mu\,\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}\), multiply by \(\tfrac12\mu\): \(\tfrac12\mu\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2 = \tfrac12\mu\dot r^2 + \lVert\vec L\rVert^2/2\mu r^2\). \(\square\)

4. By part 2, \(\mathrm d\hat r/\mathrm dt = (\vec L/\mu)\times\vec r/r^3 = \vec 0\), so \(\hat r\) is constant on every interval where \(\vec r\ne\vec 0\), and \(\vec r = r(t)\hat r_0\). \(\square\)

Check. Dimensions in part 2: \(\mathsf{L\cdot LT^{-1}\cdot L}/\mathsf{L^3} = \mathsf{T^{-1}}\), the dimension of the derivative of a unit vector ✓. Magnitude: \(\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}\perp\vec r\), so \(\lVert\mathrm d\hat r/\mathrm dt\rVert = \lVert\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}\rVert\,r/r^3 = L/\mu r^2\), which in the plane is \(|\dot\theta|\) — matching \(\mathrm d\hat r/\mathrm dt = \dot\theta\,\hat\theta\) from the polar-coordinates slide ✓. Special case: uniform circular motion at radius \(R\) and speed \(v\) gives \(\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2 = 0 + (Rv)^2/R^2 = v^2\) in part 3 ✓.

Another way

Part 3 is the Pythagorean theorem: \(\dot{\vec r}\) splits into its component \(\dot r\) along \(\hat r\) and a component perpendicular to \(\hat r\) of length \(\lVert\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}\rVert/r\) (the area corollary: \(\lVert\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}\rVert = r\,\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert\sin\varphi\)).

P2 The Earth–Moon pair routine

exercises: the two-body reduction, reduced mass, Kepler's third law with the total mass, a number

Treat the Earth and the Moon as an isolated two-body system with mean separation \(a = 3.84399\times10^{8}\) m, and take the masses in the ratio \(m_{\text{Moon}}/m_\oplus = GM_{\text{Moon}}/GM_\oplus\).

  1. Find \(\mu/m_{\text{Moon}}\).
  2. Where is the centre of mass? Give its distance from the Earth's centre, and say whether it lies inside the Earth (mean radius 6371 km).
  3. Compute the period of the relative orbit (take it circular) first with \(GM_\oplus\) alone, then with \(G(m_\oplus + m_{\text{Moon}})\). Compare both with the observed sidereal month, 27.3217 days, and say what is left over.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three numbers: a dimensionless ratio, a length, and two times. Everything comes from the two-body slide and the third-law slide; no orbit has to be solved.

Rung 2

Write \(x = m_{\text{Moon}}/m_\oplus\). Then \(\mu/m_{\text{Moon}} = 1/(1+x)\), and the centre of mass divides the separation in the inverse ratio of the masses. For the period, the relative orbit is governed by \(k/\mu = G(m_\oplus + m_{\text{Moon}}) = GM_\oplus(1+x)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(x = 4.9028695\times10^{12}/3.986004418\times10^{14} = 0.0123002\), and the centre of mass is at distance \(a\,x/(1+x)\) from the Earth's centre.

Worked solution

Idea. The relative coordinate obeys a one-body law with mass \(\mu\) in a field of strength \(k = Gm_\oplus m_{\text{Moon}}\), so the combination that sets the period is \(k/\mu = G(m_\oplus+m_{\text{Moon}})\), not \(Gm_\oplus\).

1. \(x = 0.0123002\) (the Moon is \(1/81.30\) of the Earth), and \(\mu/m_{\text{Moon}} = m_\oplus/(m_\oplus+m_{\text{Moon}}) = 1/(1+x) = 0.98785\). A \(1.2\%\) correction: far larger than for any planet about the Sun.

2. From \(\vec r_1 = \vec R + (m_2/M)\vec r\), the Earth's centre is at distance \((m_{\text{Moon}}/M)\,a = a\,x/(1+x) = 3.84399\times10^{8}\times0.0123002/1.0123002 = 4.6707\times10^{6}\) m from the centre of mass: \(4671\) km, which is \(1700\) km below the Earth's surface. The Earth wobbles about a point inside itself once a month.

3. Circular orbit, \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/(k/\mu)}\).

  • With \(GM_\oplus\) alone: \(a^3 = 5.6799\times10^{25}\ \mathrm{m^3}\), \(a^3/GM_\oplus = 1.42498\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{s^2}\), \(T = 2\pi\times3.77489\times10^{5}\) s \(= 2.37183\times10^{6}\) s \(= 27.452\) d.
  • With \(GM_\oplus(1+x)\): divide by \(\sqrt{1.0123002} = 1.006131\): \(T = 27.284\) d.

Observed: \(27.3217\) d. The naive value is \(0.48\%\) long; including the Moon's mass overshoots, to \(0.14\%\) short. What is left is not a two-body effect: it is the Sun. To first order in \(r/R\) (unit 04's Taylor expansion of the Sun's field about the Earth, at distance \(R\)), the Sun's pull on the Moon minus its pull on the Earth is the tidal acceleration \((GM_\odot/R^3)\big[3(\vec r\cdot\hat R)\hat R - \vec r\big]\), whose radial part is \(n'^2r(3\cos^2\varphi - 1)\), with \(n'^2 = GM_\odot/R^3 = (2\pi/T_{\text{year}})^2\) and \(\varphi\) the angle between the directions of the Moon and the Sun. Over a month \(\varphi\) runs through every value, \(\cos^2\varphi\) averages to \(\tfrac12\), and the radial part averages to \(+\tfrac12n'^2r\), outwards. Against the Earth–Moon attraction \(n^2r\), \(n = 2\pi/T_{\text{month}}\), that is a weakening by the fraction \(\tfrac12(T_{\text{month}}/T_{\text{year}})^2 = 0.28\%\), which at fixed \(a\) lengthens the period by half that, \(0.14\%\) — the size of the residual. The step not proved here is replacing the tidal force by its monthly average; justifying it is unit 10's secular-perturbation argument.

Check. Dimensions: \(\sqrt{\mathrm{m^3}/(\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}})} = \mathrm s\) ✓. Limit: as \(x\to0\) both periods agree and \(\mu\to m_{\text{Moon}}\) ✓. Size: the fractional change in \(T\) is \(\approx x/2 = 0.62\%\), and \(27.452\times(1-0.0061) = 27.284\) ✓.

Effective potentials, stability and closure

P3 The other closed family: the harmonic force routine

exercises: the effective potential, reading a graph, unit 06's oscillator, the apsidal angle

A particle of mass \(\mu\) moves under \(\vec F = -\kappa\vec r\), \(\kappa > 0\): the potential \(V = \tfrac12\kappa r^2\).

  1. Write down \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\) for angular momentum \(L\ne0\). Sketch it, and show that every orbit is bounded whatever its energy.
  2. Find the radius \(r_c\) of the circular orbit and its angular frequency \(\omega_\theta\), and the frequency \(\omega_r\) of small radial oscillations about it. What is \(\omega_r/\omega_\theta\)?
  3. Solve the equation of motion in Cartesian coordinates and prove that every orbit with \(L\ne0\) is an ellipse centred on the force centre (a circle counting as an ellipse).
  4. Deduce the apsidal angle, and reconcile it with part 2.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 are the effective-potential slides applied to a new \(V\). Part 3 is unit 06 applied to each coordinate separately. Part 4 compares the two pictures.

Rung 2

Circular orbits sit at the minimum of \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\), and \(\mu\omega_r^2 = V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_c)\). For part 3, the force separates: \(\mu\ddot x = -\kappa x\), \(\mu\ddot y = -\kappa y\), in the plane of the orbit. The image of the unit circle under an invertible linear map of \(\mathbb R^2\) is an ellipse centred at the origin — prove it with the singular value decomposition, or the spectral theorem applied to \(MM^{\!\top}\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: with \(\omega = \sqrt{\kappa/\mu}\), \((x,y)(t) = \vec\alpha\cos\omega t + \vec\beta\sin\omega t = M(\cos\omega t,\sin\omega t)^{\!\top}\) where \(M = [\vec\alpha\ \vec\beta]\), and \(L\ne0\) says \(\det M\ne0\).

Worked solution

Idea. The same orbit can be read two ways: through \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\) in the radial variable, or through the linear equation in Cartesian coordinates. The second gives the shape outright, and the first tells you how \(r\) oscillates along it.

1. \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) = \tfrac12\kappa r^2 + L^2/2\mu r^2\). It tends to \(+\infty\) at both ends, as \(r\to0\) (barrier) and as \(r\to\infty\) (spring). So \(\{V_{\mathrm{eff}}\le E\}\) is a bounded interval \([r_-,r_+]\) for every \(E\) above the minimum, and there are no unbounded orbits at all — in contrast to the inverse square, whose \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\to0\) at infinity.

2. \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}' = \kappa r - L^2/\mu r^3 = 0\) at \(r_c^4 = L^2/\mu\kappa\). There \(\omega_\theta = \dot\theta = L/\mu r_c^2 = \sqrt{\kappa/\mu}\). And \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_c) = \kappa + 3L^2/\mu r_c^4 = \kappa + 3\kappa = 4\kappa\), so \(\omega_r = \sqrt{4\kappa/\mu} = 2\omega_\theta\).

3. The motion is planar (the force is central); in that plane \(\mu\ddot x = -\kappa x\) and \(\mu\ddot y = -\kappa y\). By unit 06 each has solution space spanned by \(\cos\omega t,\sin\omega t\), \(\omega = \sqrt{\kappa/\mu}\), so \((x,y)^{\!\top} = M\,(\cos\omega t,\ \sin\omega t)^{\!\top}\) with \(M = [\vec\alpha\ \vec\beta]\), where \(\vec\alpha = \vec r(0)\) and \(\vec\beta = \dot{\vec r}(0)/\omega\). Then \(L = \mu(x\dot y - y\dot x) = \mu\omega\det M\), so \(L\ne0\) iff \(M\) is invertible. Write the singular value decomposition \(M = U\Sigma W^{\!\top}\) with \(U,W\) orthogonal and \(\Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(\sigma_1,\sigma_2)\), \(\sigma_i > 0\). \(W^{\!\top}\) maps the unit circle to itself, \(\Sigma\) maps it to \(\{X^2/\sigma_1^2 + Y^2/\sigma_2^2 = 1\}\), and \(U\) rotates (or reflects) that set about the origin. The image is an ellipse with semi-axes \(\sigma_1,\sigma_2\), centred at the origin. \(\square\)

4. On a centred ellipse \(r\) is largest at the two ends of the major axis and smallest at the two ends of the minor axis, which are \(90^\circ\) apart. So the apsidal angle is \(\Delta\theta = \pi/2\), and \(r\) runs through two full oscillations per revolution — exactly \(\omega_r = 2\omega_\theta\) from part 2, now for every orbit and not just the near-circular ones.

Check. Dimensions: \([\kappa] = \mathsf{MT^{-2}}\), so \(\sqrt{\kappa/\mu}\) is a frequency ✓; \(r_c^4 = L^2/\mu\kappa\) has dimension \(\mathsf{M^2L^4T^{-2}}/\mathsf{M^2T^{-2}} = \mathsf{L^4}\) ✓. Special case: \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2\) gives a circle of radius \(\sigma_1\), and then \(r_c = \sigma_1\) ✓. The period \(2\pi/\omega\) is the same for every orbit — the harmonic oscillator's isochronism (unit 00), in two dimensions.

P4 Which power laws have stable circles medium

exercises: effective potentials, linearisation about a circular orbit, stability, the apsidal angle, a number

Let \(\vec F = -c\,r^n\hat r\) with \(c > 0\) and \(n\) real.

  1. Show that for every \(r_0 > 0\) there is a circular orbit of radius \(r_0\), and find its \(L^2\).
  2. Prove that this circular orbit is a strict minimum of \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\) if and only if \(n > -3\).
  3. For \(n > -3\), show that small radial oscillations about it have angular frequency \(\omega_r = \omega_\theta\sqrt{3+n}\), where \(\omega_\theta\) is the circular orbit's angular velocity. Deduce that the apsidal angle of a near-circular orbit is \(\pi/\sqrt{3+n}\).
  4. A disc galaxy with a flat rotation curve has, in its outer parts, \(n = -1\). Through what angle, in degrees, does the radius turn from one pericentre of a near-circular stellar orbit to the next apocentre? Does the orbit close?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each part is a statement about the one-variable function \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\) near its critical point; the answer to part 4 is a single angle.

Rung 2

A circular orbit is a critical point of \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\) with \(L\) fixed. Stability is the sign of \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}''\) there, and near it the radial equation \(\mu\ddot r = -V_{\mathrm{eff}}'(r)\) is unit 06's oscillator with \(\mu\omega_r^2 = V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)\). The apsidal angle is how far \(\theta\) turns in half a radial period.

Rung 3

First line: \(V' = -f = c\,r^n\), so \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}'(r) = c\,r^n - L^2/\mu r^3\), which vanishes at \(r_0\) exactly when \(L^2 = \mu c\,r_0^{\,n+3}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Fix \(L\) so that \(r_0\) is a critical point, then compute the second derivative there. The ratio \(\omega_r/\omega_\theta\) turns out independent of \(r_0\) and \(c\), which is mechanical similarity at work: a power law has no preferred length.

1. \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}'(r) = c\,r^n - L^2/\mu r^3\). Choosing \(L^2 = \mu c\,r_0^{\,n+3}\) makes \(r_0\) a critical point, so \(\dot r\equiv0\), \(r\equiv r_0\) solves the radial equation. Any \(r_0 > 0\) will do.

2. \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r) = c\,n\,r^{n-1} + 3L^2/\mu r^4\). At \(r_0\), with the \(L^2\) of part 1, \[ V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0) = c\,n\,r_0^{\,n-1} + 3c\,r_0^{\,n-1} = c\,(n+3)\,r_0^{\,n-1} , \] positive iff \(n > -3\). A critical point with positive second derivative is a strict local minimum; for \(n < -3\) it is a strict local maximum. At \(n = -3\) exactly, \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\) is \((L^2/\mu - c)/2r^2\), identically zero for this \(L\): every radius is neutral, and the circle is not a strict minimum. \(\square\)

3. Put \(r = r_0 + \xi\). Taylor's theorem gives \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}'(r_0+\xi) = V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)\,\xi + O(\xi^2)\), so to first order \(\mu\ddot\xi = -V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)\,\xi\): unit 06's oscillator with \(\omega_r^2 = c(n+3)r_0^{\,n-1}/\mu\). The circular angular velocity is \(\omega_\theta = L/\mu r_0^2\), so \(\omega_\theta^2 = \mu c\,r_0^{\,n+3}/\mu^2r_0^4 = c\,r_0^{\,n-1}/\mu\). Dividing, \(\omega_r^2/\omega_\theta^2 = n + 3\). From pericentre to apocentre is half a radial period, \(\pi/\omega_r\), during which \(\theta\) advances by \(\omega_\theta\,\pi/\omega_r = \pi/\sqrt{3+n}\) (to leading order in the amplitude, since \(\dot\theta = L/\mu r^2\) differs from \(\omega_\theta\) by \(O(\xi)\)).

4. \(n = -1\): \(\Delta\theta = \pi/\sqrt2 = 127.28^\circ\). From one pericentre to the next is \(254.56^\circ\), short of a full turn, so the pericentre regresses by about \(105.44^\circ\) each radial period. Whether it closes depends on the exact apsidal angle, which is only approximately \(\pi/\sqrt2\) and varies continuously with the amplitude (P5): at the energies where it is a rational multiple of \(\pi\) the orbit closes, but those are exceptions, and a typical near-circular star traces a rosette filling an annulus.

Check. Dimensions: \([c\,r^{n-1}/\mu] = \mathsf{MLT^{-2}L^{-n}}\cdot\mathsf{L^{n-1}}/\mathsf M = \mathsf{T^{-2}}\) ✓ (since \([c] = \mathsf{MLT^{-2}}\mathsf{L^{-n}}\)). Limits: \(n = -2\) gives \(\omega_r = \omega_\theta\), \(\Delta\theta = \pi\), the Kepler ellipse ✓; \(n = 1\) gives \(\omega_r = 2\omega_\theta\), \(\Delta\theta = \pi/2\), the centred ellipse of P3 ✓; \(n\to-3^+\) gives \(\omega_r\to0\), marginal ✓.

P5 Proof: Bertrand's theorem for power laws hard

exercises: the apsidal-angle quadrature, a change of variable, limits of an integral, a proof

Let \(V(r) = a\,r^\alpha\) with \(\alpha > -2\), \(\alpha\ne0\), and \(a\alpha > 0\) (so the force \(-V'\) is attractive; this is P4's law with \(n = \alpha - 1\)). Fix \(L\ne0\) and write \(\Phi(E)\) for the apsidal angle of the bounded orbit of energy \(E\).

  1. Show that the substitution \(x = L/r\) turns the apsidal integral into \[ \Phi(E) = \int_{x_1}^{x_2}\frac{\mathrm dx}{\sqrt{2\mu\big(E - W(x)\big)}},\qquad W(x) = \frac{x^2}{2\mu} + V(L/x), \] with \(x_1 < x_2\) the roots of \(W = E\).
  2. For \(\alpha > 0\) show that \(\Phi(E)\to\pi/2\) as \(E\to\infty\).
  3. For \(-2 < \alpha < 0\) show that \(\Phi(E)\to\pi/(2+\alpha)\) as \(E\to0^-\).
  4. Suppose every bounded orbit closes. Using P4 and the lemma below, prove that \(\alpha = 2\) or \(\alpha = -1\): the harmonic force or the inverse square.

Lemma (cited, Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, §8): \(\Phi\) is a continuous function of \(E\) on the interval of energies with bounded orbits, and its limits at the ends of that interval may be computed by passing to the limit under the integral sign after the rescaling in parts 2 and 3.

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 4 is a pair of exponents, reached by equating three expressions for one continuous function: its near-circular value from P4 and its value at the far end of the energy range from part 2 or 3.

Rung 2

In parts 2 and 3, rescale \(x = x_2\,y\) so that the limits become \(0\) and \(1\), and see which term of \(W\) survives. For part 4: a closed orbit has \(\Phi/\pi\) rational, a continuous function taking only rational multiples of \(\pi\) on an interval is constant (intermediate value theorem), and near the circular orbit \(\Phi\to\pi/\sqrt{\alpha+2}\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: with \(b = -a > 0\) and \(\beta = -\alpha\in(0,2)\), \(V(L/x) = -b\,L^{-\beta}x^{\beta}\), so at \(E = 0\), \(2\mu(0 - W) = x_2^{\,2-\beta}x^\beta - x^2\) with \(x_2^{\,2-\beta} = 2\mu b L^{-\beta}\). Now put \(x = x_2 y\).

Worked solution

Idea. If every bounded orbit closes, the apsidal angle can take only rational multiples of \(\pi\); being continuous in \(E\), it must then be constant. So its value near the circular orbit (P4) must equal its value at the other end of the energy range, and each end can be computed in closed form because one term of the potential dominates there. This is Arnold's argument.

1. From the quadrature slide, \(\Phi = \int_{r_-}^{r_+}L\,\mathrm dr\big/r^2\sqrt{2\mu(E - V(r)) - L^2/r^2}\). With \(x = L/r\), \(\mathrm dx = -L\,\mathrm dr/r^2\), and \(L^2/r^2 = x^2\); reversing the limits absorbs the sign, and \(2\mu(E - V) - x^2 = 2\mu(E - W(x))\). The turning points \(r_\pm\) become \(x_1 = L/r_+\), \(x_2 = L/r_-\). \(\square\)

2. \(\alpha > 0\), \(a > 0\): \(W(x) = x^2/2\mu + aL^\alpha x^{-\alpha}\). As \(E\to\infty\), the large root has \(x_2^2/2\mu = E - aL^\alpha x_2^{-\alpha}\), so \(x_2^2/2\mu E\to1\), and the small root \(x_1\to0\). Put \(x = x_2y\): \[ \Phi = \int_{x_1/x_2}^{1}\frac{x_2\,\mathrm dy}{\sqrt{2\mu E - x_2^2y^2 - 2\mu aL^\alpha x_2^{-\alpha}y^{-\alpha}}} . \] Divide numerator and denominator by \(x_2\): the denominator becomes \(\sqrt{2\mu E/x_2^2 - y^2 - \varepsilon_E\,y^{-\alpha}}\) with \(\varepsilon_E = 2\mu aL^\alpha x_2^{-\alpha-2}\to0\). By the lemma the limit is \(\int_0^1\mathrm dy/\sqrt{1-y^2} = \pi/2\). \(\square\)

3. \(-2 < \alpha < 0\): write \(a = -b\), \(b > 0\), \(\beta = -\alpha\in(0,2)\). Then \(V(L/x) = -bL^{-\beta}x^\beta\) and bound orbits have \(E < 0\). By the lemma, \(\lim_{E\to0^-}\Phi(E)\) is the integral at \(E = 0\): \(2\mu(0 - W(x)) = 2\mu bL^{-\beta}x^\beta - x^2 = x_2^{\,2-\beta}x^\beta - x^2\) with roots \(0\) and \(x_2\). With \(x = x_2y\), \[ \Phi(0) = \int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dy}{\sqrt{y^\beta - y^2}} = \int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dy}{y^{\beta/2}\sqrt{1 - y^{2-\beta}}} . \] Now \(z = y^{(2-\beta)/2}\), \(\mathrm dz = \tfrac{2-\beta}{2}\,y^{-\beta/2}\mathrm dy\), maps \([0,1]\) to \([0,1]\): \[ \Phi(0) = \frac{2}{2-\beta}\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dz}{\sqrt{1-z^2}} = \frac{2}{2-\beta}\cdot\frac\pi2 = \frac{\pi}{2+\alpha} . \qquad\square \]

4. After \(m\) radial periods the radius has turned through \(2m\Phi\); the orbit closes iff \(2m\Phi = 2\pi j\) for some integers, i.e. iff \(\Phi/\pi\in\mathbb Q\). If every bounded orbit closes, \(\Phi\) is continuous (lemma) with values in the countable set \(\pi\mathbb Q\); a continuous non-constant function on an interval takes every value between two of its values, uncountably many, so \(\Phi\) is constant. Near the bottom of the well P4 gives \(\Phi\to\pi/\sqrt{3+n} = \pi/\sqrt{\alpha+2}\).

  • \(\alpha > 0\): equate with part 2, \(\sqrt{\alpha+2} = 2\), so \(\alpha = 2\): \(V = \tfrac12\kappa r^2\).
  • \(-2 < \alpha < 0\): equate with part 3, \(\sqrt{\alpha+2} = \alpha + 2\), so \(\alpha+2 = 1\), \(\alpha = -1\): \(V = -k/r\).

Conversely both do close: P3 for the harmonic force, the Binet slides for the inverse square. \(\square\)

Check. The two limit formulas agree with each other and with P4 at the survivors: \(\alpha = 2\) gives \(\pi/\sqrt4 = \pi/2\) ✓, \(\alpha = -1\) gives \(\pi/\sqrt1 = \pi/(2-1) = \pi\) ✓. A number for a non-survivor, \(\alpha = -\tfrac12\) (\(n = -\tfrac32\)): near-circular \(\Phi = 180^\circ/\sqrt{1.5} = 146.97^\circ\), at \(E\to0^-\) \(\Phi = 180^\circ/1.5 = 120^\circ\); evaluating the integral numerically at half the well's depth gives \(137.0^\circ\), in between, as a continuous non-constant \(\Phi\) must.

What is not proved here

The restriction to power laws. For a general \(V\) the near-circular condition only forces \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\) to look like a power law near every circular orbit, and upgrading that to the global statement needs an expansion to third order in the amplitude — Goldstein, Classical Mechanics, §3.6 (Appendix A of the 2nd edition).

The exponents left out

The statement excludes \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\alpha\le-2\). For \(\alpha\le-2\) (P4's \(n\le-3\)) the circular orbit is not a strict minimum of \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\) (P4, part 2) and there are no bounded orbits around it to close. The case \(\alpha = 0\) is the logarithmic potential \(V = c\ln r\), \(c > 0\), P4's \(n = -1\). Part 2's rescaling works for it unchanged: with \(x = x_2y\) and the root condition \(2\mu E = x_2^2 + 2\mu c\ln(L/x_2)\), the radicand divided by \(x_2^2\) is \(1 - y^2 + (2\mu c/x_2^2)\ln y\), and \(2\mu c/x_2^2\to0\) as \(E\to\infty\), so by the same lemma \(\Phi\to\pi/2\). Near the circle P4 gives \(\pi/\sqrt2\ne\pi/2\), so not every orbit closes. (Numerically, with \(\mu = L = c = 1\), \(\Phi\) falls from \(127.3^\circ\) near the circle to \(94.4^\circ\) at \(E = 10\) and \(90.4^\circ\) at \(E = 100\).) So among all power laws only \(n = -2\) and \(n = 1\) survive.

P6 Proof: the Kepler problem, without looking medium

exercises: the Binet substitution, unit 06's solution space, energy at a point, areal velocity, the checkpoint

A particle of mass \(\mu\) moves in the potential \(V(r) = -k/r\), \(k > 0\), with energy \(E < 0\) and angular momentum \(\vec L\ne\vec 0\). Without the slides open:

  1. Prove the motion is planar and that \(\theta\) is a strictly monotone function of \(t\).
  2. Derive the Binet equation for this force and solve it, getting \(r(\theta) = p/(1+e\cos\theta)\) with \(p = L^2/\mu k\). Say why the solution space of the homogeneous equation is two-dimensional.
  3. Prove \(e = \sqrt{1 + 2EL^2/\mu k^2}\), and that \(e < 1\).
  4. Prove Kepler's second law, and then the third in the form \(T^2 = 4\pi^2\mu a^3/k\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A chain of four results, each a few lines, each using the one before: a conserved vector, a change of variable, one energy evaluation, and an area divided by a rate.

Rung 2

Part 1: \(\vec r\cdot\vec L = 0\) and \(L = \mu r^2\dot\theta\). Part 2: \(\dot r = -(L/\mu)u'\) is the key line; then the radial equation. Part 3: evaluate \(E\) where \(\dot r = 0\). Part 4: area of an ellipse over \(L/2\mu\), and eliminate \(L\) with \(b^2 = ap\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: with \(u = 1/r\) and \(\dot\theta = Lu^2/\mu\), \(\dot r = \dfrac{\mathrm dr}{\mathrm d\theta}\dot\theta = -\dfrac{u'}{u^2}\cdot\dfrac{Lu^2}{\mu} = -\dfrac L\mu u'\).

Worked solution

Idea. Angular momentum makes the problem planar and supplies the change of variable \(t\mapsto\theta\); \(u = 1/r\) makes the orbit equation linear; energy fixes the one remaining constant; areal velocity turns the shape into a period.

1. The force \(-k\hat r/r^2\) is central, so \(\dot{\vec L} = \vec r\times\vec F = \vec 0\). Then \(\vec L\cdot\vec r = (\vec r\times\vec p)\cdot\vec r = 0\) for all \(t\), and \(\vec L\) is a fixed nonzero vector, so \(\vec r(t)\in\vec L^\perp\), a plane. In polar coordinates in that plane \(\vec L = \mu r^2\dot\theta\,\hat z\), so \(\dot\theta = L/\mu r^2\) has the fixed sign of \(L\ne0\) and never vanishes: \(\theta\) is strictly monotone in \(t\), hence a legitimate independent variable.

2. Radial equation (polar acceleration, radial component): \(\mu(\ddot r - r\dot\theta^2) = -k/r^2\), i.e. \(\mu\ddot r = -k/r^2 + L^2/\mu r^3\). With \(u = 1/r\): \(\dot r = -(L/\mu)u'\) as in rung 3, and \(\ddot r = -(L/\mu)u''\dot\theta = -(L^2/\mu^2)u^2u''\). Substituting, \(-(L^2/\mu)u^2u'' = -ku^2 + (L^2/\mu)u^3\); dividing by \(-(L^2/\mu)u^2\), \[ u'' + u = \frac{\mu k}{L^2} = \frac1p . \] The operator \(\mathcal D u = u'' + u\) has characteristic polynomial \(\lambda^2+1\), so \(\cos\theta,\sin\theta\in\ker\mathcal D\); they are independent, and \(\dim\ker\mathcal D = 2\) because by unit 01's uniqueness theorem a solution is fixed by \((u(0),u'(0))\in\mathbb R^2\) (unit 06's evaluation map is an isomorphism). The constant \(1/p\) is a particular solution, so \(u = 1/p + C\cos(\theta-\theta_0)\). Choose the axis so \(\theta_0 = 0\) and \(C\ge0\), and set \(e = Cp\): \(r = p/(1+e\cos\theta)\). \(\square\)

3. At \(\theta = 0\), \(r = p/(1+e)\) is a minimum of \(r\) so \(\dot r = 0\), and \(E = L^2/2\mu r^2 - k/r\). Using \(L^2 = \mu kp\): \[ E = \frac{k(1+e)^2}{2p} - \frac{k(1+e)}{p} = \frac{k(1+e)}{2p}(e - 1) = \frac{k(e^2-1)}{2p} = \frac{\mu k^2(e^2-1)}{2L^2} . \] So \(e^2 = 1 + 2EL^2/\mu k^2\), and \(e\ge0\) takes the positive root. \(E < 0\) gives \(e^2 < 1\). \(\square\)

4. Second law. The area swept between \(t\) and \(t+\Delta t\) is the triangle \(\tfrac12\lVert\vec r(t)\times\vec r(t+\Delta t)\rVert\) up to \(O(\Delta t^3)\); expanding \(\vec r(t+\Delta t) = \vec r + \Delta t\,\dot{\vec r} + O(\Delta t^2)\) and using \(\vec r\times\vec r = \vec 0\) gives \(\dot A = \tfrac12\lVert\vec r\times\dot{\vec r}\rVert = L/2\mu\), constant. (Or: \(\dot A = \tfrac12r^2\dot\theta\) from \(A = \int\tfrac12r^2\,\mathrm d\theta\).) Third law. For \(e < 1\) the orbit is an ellipse with \(a = p/(1-e^2)\), \(b = a\sqrt{1-e^2}\), so \(b^2 = ap\), and area \(\pi ab\). One period sweeps it at rate \(L/2\mu\): \(T = 2\pi\mu ab/L\). From \(L^2 = \mu kp = \mu kb^2/a\), \(T = 2\pi\mu ab\sqrt{a}/(b\sqrt{\mu k}) = 2\pi\sqrt{\mu a^3/k}\). \(\square\)

Check. Dimensions: \(p = L^2/\mu k\) is \(\mathsf{M^2L^4T^{-2}}/\mathsf{M\cdot ML^3T^{-2}} = \mathsf L\) ✓; \(\mu a^3/k\) is \(\mathsf{T^2}\) ✓. Limits: \(E = -\mu k^2/2L^2\) (bottom of the well) gives \(e = 0\), a circle of radius \(p\) ✓; \(E\to0^-\) gives \(e\to1\) and \(a = -k/2E\to\infty\) ✓. Number: for Halley's comet, the deck's running example, \(a = 17.8325\) AU gives \(T = 1.000019\times17.8325^{3/2} = 75.305\) yr ✓.

Idea. The energy fixes \(a\), and \(a\) fixes the speed at every radius. Each burn is the difference between the speed the next orbit needs at that radius and the speed the current one has.

1. Energy per unit mass on the orbit is \(\tfrac12v^2 - GM_\oplus/r\), and the first-law slide gives \(E/\mu = -GM_\oplus/2a\). Equate: \(v^2 = GM_\oplus(2/r - 1/a)\). \(\square\)

2. \(a = 2.44675\times10^{7}\) m, \(e = (r_2 - r_1)/(r_2 + r_1) = 0.72327\). Circular speeds \(v_1 = \sqrt{GM_\oplus/r_1} = 7672.6\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(v_2 = 3074.7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). On the ellipse by vis-viva: at perigee \(v_p = 10\,072.1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), at apogee \(v_a = 1617.4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). So \(\Delta v_1 = 2399.5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(\Delta v_2 = 1457.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), total \(3856.7\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Transfer time \(\pi\sqrt{a^3/GM_\oplus} = 1.9044\times10^{4}\) s \(= 5.290\) h, about 5 h 17 min.

3. Kinetic energy added: \(\tfrac12(1200)\big[(v_p^2 - v_1^2) + (v_2^2 - v_a^2)\big] = 2.9649\times10^{10}\) J. The burns are instantaneous, so each changes \(T\) and not \(V\), and the total equals \(E_2 - E_1 = GM_\oplus m(1/2r_1 - 1/2r_2)\) — unit 04's \(2.965\times10^{10}\) J ✓.

Check. Apse condition: \(r_1v_p = 6.8198\times10^{10} = r_2v_a\ \mathrm{m^2s^{-1}}\) ✓ (Kepler's second law). Dimensions of vis-viva: \(\mathrm{m^3s^{-2}\cdot m^{-1}} = \mathrm{m^2s^{-2}}\) ✓. Limits: \(r_2\to r_1\) gives \(a\to r_1\), \(v_p\to v_1\), both burns \(\to0\) ✓; \(r = a\) in vis-viva gives \(v^2 = GM/a\), circular ✓.

P11 A small extra attraction, and a rotating ellipse medium

exercises: the Binet equation for a modified force, unit 06, the apsidal angle, a number

Let \(f(r) = -k/r^2 - c/r^3\) with \(k > 0\) and \(0 < \mu c < L^2\).

  1. Write the Binet equation and solve it. Show the orbit is \(r = p'/(1 + e\cos\gamma\theta)\) with \(\gamma^2 = 1 - \mu c/L^2\), and find \(p'\).
  2. Show that successive perihelia are \(2\pi/\gamma\) apart in \(\theta\), so the perihelion advances by \(\delta = 2\pi(1/\gamma - 1)\) per orbit, and that \(\delta\approx\pi\mu c/L^2\) when \(\mu c\ll L^2\).
  3. Mercury's perihelion has an anomalous advance of \(43''\) per century. Its orbit has \(a = 5.7909\times10^{10}\) m, \(e = 0.20563\) and period \(87.969\) days. If the whole anomaly were due to such a term, what would \(c/k\) be, in metres? Compare with \(6GM_\odot/c_{\text{light}}^2\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The extra term is proportional to \(u^3\) in the force, so after Binet's division by \(u^2\) it is linear in \(u\): the equation stays in unit 06's class, with a changed frequency.

Rung 2

\(-(\mu/L^2u^2)f(1/u) = \mu k/L^2 + (\mu c/L^2)u\). Move the \(u\) term to the left. Perihelion is where \(\cos\gamma\theta = 1\). For part 3, \(L^2 \approx \mu kp\) with \(p = a(1-e^2)\), so \(\mu c/L^2\approx(c/k)/p\), and \(\delta\) per orbit is \(43''\) divided by the number of orbits per century.

Rung 3

First line: \(u'' + \big(1 - \mu c/L^2\big)u = \mu k/L^2\), whose particular solution is the constant \(\mu k/\gamma^2L^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. A \(1/r^3\) force is invisible to the Binet equation's structure except that it shifts the natural frequency in \(\theta\) away from 1. Frequency not equal to 1 means the ellipse does not come back to itself: it turns.

1. \(f(1/u) = -ku^2 - cu^3\), so the Binet right side is \((\mu/L^2u^2)(ku^2 + cu^3) = \mu k/L^2 + (\mu c/L^2)u\), and \(u'' + \gamma^2u = \mu k/L^2\) with \(\gamma^2 = 1 - \mu c/L^2 \in (0,1)\). By unit 06 the homogeneous solutions are \(\cos\gamma\theta,\sin\gamma\theta\); a particular solution is the constant \(\mu k/\gamma^2L^2\). Aligning the axis, \(u = (1 + e\cos\gamma\theta)/p'\) with \(p' = \gamma^2L^2/\mu k\).

2. \(r\) is least where \(\cos\gamma\theta = 1\), at \(\theta = 2\pi j/\gamma\). Consecutive perihelia are \(2\pi/\gamma > 2\pi\) apart: one full turn plus \(\delta = 2\pi(1/\gamma - 1)\), an advance. With \(\varepsilon = \mu c/L^2\) small, \(1/\gamma = (1-\varepsilon)^{-1/2} = 1 + \varepsilon/2 + O(\varepsilon^2)\), so \(\delta = \pi\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2) = \pi\mu c/L^2\).

3. Orbits per century: \(36\,525/87.969 = 415.20\). So \(\delta = 43''/415.20 = 0.10356''\) per orbit \(= 5.021\times10^{-7}\) rad. Then \(\mu c/L^2 = \delta/\pi\), and with \(L^2 = \mu kp\) (to leading order), \(c/k = p\,\delta/\pi\). Here \(p = a(1-e^2) = 5.5460\times10^{10}\) m, so \(c/k = 8.86\times10^{3}\) m. And \(6GM_\odot/c_{\text{light}}^2 = 6\times1476.6 = 8.860\times10^{3}\) m: the same, to the accuracy of the \(43''\). General relativity's correction, which unit 37 derives, advances the perihelion — to first order — exactly as this extra attraction does with \(c/k = 6GM_\odot/c_{\text{light}}^2\).

Check. Dimensions: \([c] = \mathsf{MLT^{-2}\cdot L^3}\), so \([\mu c/L^2] = \mathsf{M\cdot ML^4T^{-2}}/\mathsf{M^2L^4T^{-2}} = 1\) ✓, and \([c/k] = \mathsf L\) ✓. Limit: \(c\to0\) gives \(\gamma\to1\), \(\delta\to0\), \(p'\to p\), the closed Kepler ellipse ✓. Sign: an extra attraction gives an advance; a repulsive \(c < 0\) gives \(\gamma > 1\) and a regression ✓.

P12 How long a comet stays inside the Earth's orbit hard

exercises: quadrature for \(t(\theta)\) from the areal velocity, a trigonometric substitution, optimisation, a number

Model a long-period comet as moving on a parabola (\(e = 1\)) with perihelion distance \(q\), about the Sun.

  1. Show \(r = 2q/(1+\cos\theta)\) and \(h := L/\mu = \sqrt{2GM_\odot q}\).
  2. Using Kepler's second law, prove that the time from perihelion to angle \(\theta\) is \[ t(\theta) = \sqrt{\frac{2q^3}{GM_\odot}}\Big(\tau + \frac{\tau^3}{3}\Big),\qquad \tau = \tan\frac\theta2 . \]
  3. For \(q < R\), prove that the time the comet spends at \(r \le R\) is \[ t_{\text{in}} = \frac{2\sqrt2}{3\sqrt{GM_\odot}}\,(R + 2q)\sqrt{R - q} , \] and with \(R = 1\) AU express it in years as a function of \(q\) in AU.
  4. Which \(q\) maximises \(t_{\text{in}}\), and what is the maximum in days? Evaluate \(t_{\text{in}}\) for Halley's \(q = 0.586\) AU, and compare with the true value, \(78.1\) days, for its actual ellipse.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

This is the "timetable" quadrature, but done in \(\theta\) rather than in \(r\): the areal velocity turns \(\mathrm dt\) into \(r^2\,\mathrm d\theta/h\), and for a parabola the integral is elementary.

Rung 2

Use \(1+\cos\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)\), then substitute \(\tau = \tan(\theta/2)\), with \(\mathrm d\tau = \mathrm d\theta/2\cos^2(\theta/2)\). The radius \(R\) is reached where \(\cos^2(\theta/2) = q/R\), i.e. \(\tau^2 = R/q - 1\). By symmetry the time inside is twice the time from perihelion to that point.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\dfrac{\mathrm dt}{\mathrm d\theta} = \dfrac{r^2}{h} = \dfrac{4q^2}{h(1+\cos\theta)^2} = \dfrac{q^2}{h\cos^4(\theta/2)}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The areal velocity turns time into an integral over angle. For the ellipse that integral leads to Kepler's equation, which cannot be inverted in closed form (unit 09); for the parabola it is a cubic polynomial in \(\tan(\theta/2)\). And here only \(t\) at a known \(\theta\) is wanted, so no inversion is needed at all.

1. \(e = 1\) in \(r = p/(1+e\cos\theta)\), and perihelion \(q = p/2\), so \(p = 2q\). Dividing \(p = L^2/\mu k\) by \(\mu\) twice: \(p = h^2/GM_\odot\), so \(h = \sqrt{2GM_\odot q}\).

2. Kepler's second law in the form \(r^2\dot\theta = h\) gives \(\mathrm dt = r^2\,\mathrm d\theta/h\). With rung 3, \(\mathrm dt = (q^2/h)\sec^4(\theta/2)\,\mathrm d\theta\). Put \(\tau = \tan(\theta/2)\): \(\mathrm d\theta = 2\cos^2(\theta/2)\,\mathrm d\tau\) and \(\sec^2(\theta/2) = 1+\tau^2\), so \(\mathrm dt = (2q^2/h)(1+\tau^2)\,\mathrm d\tau\). Integrating from perihelion (\(\tau = 0\)), \[ t = \frac{2q^2}{h}\Big(\tau + \frac{\tau^3}{3}\Big) = \frac{2q^2}{\sqrt{2GM_\odot q}}\Big(\tau + \frac{\tau^3}{3}\Big) = \sqrt{\frac{2q^3}{GM_\odot}}\Big(\tau + \frac{\tau^3}{3}\Big) . \qquad\square \]

3. \(r = R\) where \(1 + \cos\theta = 2q/R\), i.e. \(\cos^2(\theta/2) = q/R\), so \(\tau_R^2 = \sec^2 - 1 = (R-q)/q\). The radial motion is symmetric about perihelion (the quadrature slide's reflection argument), so \[ t_{\text{in}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{2q^3}{GM_\odot}}\,\tau_R\Big(1 + \frac{\tau_R^2}{3}\Big) = 2\sqrt{\frac{2q^3}{GM_\odot}}\sqrt{\frac{R-q}{q}}\cdot\frac{2q + R}{3q} = \frac{2\sqrt2}{3\sqrt{GM_\odot}}(R+2q)\sqrt{R-q} . \] With \(R = 1\) AU and \(q\) in AU, \(\sqrt{\mathrm{AU}^3/GM_\odot} = T_\oplus/2\pi\) where \(T_\oplus = 1.000019\) yr is the period of a 1 AU orbit (the third-law example), so \(t_{\text{in}} = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{3\pi}(1+2q)\sqrt{1-q}\) yr, to the \(2\times10^{-5}\) of that factor. \(\square\)

4. \(g(q) = (1+2q)\sqrt{1-q}\): \(g'(q) = 2\sqrt{1-q} - (1+2q)/2\sqrt{1-q} = 0\) iff \(4(1-q) = 1 + 2q\), \(q = \tfrac12\), and \(g\) rises before and falls after, so this is the maximum: \(g(\tfrac12) = 2\sqrt{1/2} = \sqrt2\), \(t_{\text{in}} = 2/3\pi\) yr \(= 0.21221\) yr \(= 77.5\) days. No comet on a parabola spends more than about eleven weeks inside the Earth's orbit. For Halley, \(g(0.586025) = 2.17205\times0.64341 = 1.39752\), \(t_{\text{in}} = 0.20970\) yr \(= 76.6\) days, against \(78.1\) days for the true ellipse (computed with unit 09's Kepler equation): the parabolic model is \(1.9\%\) short, because a bound comet near perihelion moves slightly slower than a parabolic one at the same \(r\).

Check. Dimensions: \(\sqrt{\mathrm{m^3}/\mathrm{m^3s^{-2}}} = \mathrm s\) ✓. Limits: \(q\to R\) gives \(t_{\text{in}}\to0\), the comet just touches the circle ✓; \(q\to0\) gives \(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{3\pi}\) yr \(= 54.8\) days, a comet plunging straight at the Sun — less than the maximum, because it rushes through the inner region ✓. Small \(\theta\): \(t\approx\sqrt{2q^3/GM}\,\theta/2\), and directly \(\dot\theta = h/q^2\) at perihelion gives \(t\approx q^2\theta/h = \theta\sqrt{q^3/2GM}\), the same ✓.
