The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: Mars, \(a = 1.523679\) AU, \(e = 0.0934\), \(T = 686.980\)
days, 120 days after perihelion. The mean anomaly is \(M = 1.097532\), and
Kepler's equation gives \(E = 1.1840325\), true anomaly \(72.8930^\circ\),
distance \(r = 1.470000\) AU. Three Bessel terms give \(E = 1.184058\), within
the \(2.8\times10^{-5}\) the tail bound promised.

* The timetable

- true anomaly :: The angle \(\theta\) from perihelion to the planet, seen from
  the Sun: where the planet actually is. Unit 08's \(\theta\) in
  \(r = p/(1+e\cos\theta)\); astronomers write \(\nu\) or \(f\).
  /Slide: Where unit 08 left the clock./
- mean motion :: \(n = 2\pi/T\), the average angular rate over an orbit. For
  Mars \(0.524033^\circ\) per day.
- mean anomaly :: \(M = n(t - \tau)\), with \(\tau\) the time of perihelion: a
  clock reading in radians, the angle a body moving uniformly would have swept.
  It is what Kepler's equation takes as input.
- auxiliary circle :: The circle of radius \(a\) about the ellipse's centre. The
  ellipse is its image under the squash \(Y \mapsto (b/a)Y\), which multiplies
  every area by \(b/a\). /Slide: The eccentric anomaly./
- eccentric anomaly :: The angle \(E\), at the ellipse's centre, of the point on
  the auxiliary circle directly above the planet. Then \(r = a(1 - e\cos E)\) and
  \(\tan\frac\theta2 = \sqrt{(1+e)/(1-e)}\,\tan\frac E2\).
- Kepler's equation :: \(E - e\sin E = M\): the timetable of the bound Kepler
  orbit. Kepler's second law applied to the auxiliary circle, or unit 08's
  quadrature integral evaluated by \(r = a(1 - e\cos E)\). It has exactly one
  solution for each \(M\) when \(0 \le e < 1\), and no closed-form inverse.
  /Slide: Kepler's equation./
- Newton's method :: \(E_{k+1} = E_k - f(E_k)/f'(E_k)\): solve the tangent line
  instead of the curve. For Kepler \(f(E) = E - e\sin E - M\).
  /Slide: Newton's method, and why it doubles the digits./
- quadratic convergence :: \(|E_{k+1} - E| \le C|E_k - E|^2\). Proved from
  Taylor's theorem with \(C = \max|f''|/2\min|f'|\), which is \(e/2(1-e)\) for
  Kepler: \(0.0515\) for Mars. The number of correct digits roughly doubles each
  step.

* A series in the eccentricity

- series in a parameter :: A solution written as a power series in a small
  quantity of the problem, here \(e\). Each truncation is right to its order;
  whether the whole series converges is a separate question.
  /Slide: Each iteration is correct to one more order./
- Lagrange inversion :: For \(E = M + e\varphi(E)\), the \(e^k\) Taylor
  coefficient of \(F(E)\) is
  \(\frac1{k!}\frac{\mathrm d^{k-1}}{\mathrm dM^{k-1}}[\varphi(M)^kF'(M)]\).
  Proved from \(\partial_eE = \varphi(E)\,\partial_ME\) by induction. It gives the
  coefficients, not convergence. /Slide: Lagrange inversion: every coefficient at once./

* Fourier coefficients and Bessel's integral

- Fourier sine coefficients :: \(b_n = \frac2\pi\int_0^\pi g(M)\sin nM\,\mathrm dM\)
  for an odd \(2\pi\)-periodic \(g\). That a \(C^1\) such \(g\) equals
  \(\sum b_n\sin nM\) is cited from unit 19.
  /Slide: Just enough Fourier: sine coefficients./
- orthogonality :: \(\int_0^\pi\sin mM\sin nM\,\mathrm dM = \frac\pi2\delta_{mn}\).
  It is what isolates one coefficient from a series.
- Fourier–Bessel expansion :: \(E = M + \sum_{n\ge1}\frac2nJ_n(ne)\sin nM\), the
  harmonics of Kepler's equation. Obtained by integrating by parts and changing
  variable from \(M\) to \(E\). Converges for every \(e < 1\).
  /Slide: Worked: the harmonics of Kepler's equation./
- Bessel's integral :: \(J_n(x) = \frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\cos(n\vartheta - x\sin\vartheta)\,\mathrm d\vartheta\),
  the form in which Bessel met the functions on Kepler's problem. Proved equal to
  the power-series definition.

* Bessel functions

- Bessel function of the first kind :: For integer \(n \ge 0\),
  \(J_n(x) = \sum_k\frac{(-1)^k}{k!(n+k)!}(x/2)^{n+2k}\), converging for every
  \(x\); \(J_{-n} = (-1)^nJ_n\). Bounded by
  \(\frac{(|x|/2)^n}{n!}\exp\frac{x^2}{4(n+1)}\). /Slide: The definition, and a bound./
- generating function :: \(\exp\big(\frac x2(t - \frac1t)\big) = \sum_nJ_n(x)t^n\).
  One function of two variables encoding the whole family; differentiating it and
  comparing coefficients proves identities. /Slide: The generating function./
- Jacobi–Anger expansion :: \(\mathrm e^{ix\sin\vartheta} = \sum_nJ_n(x)\mathrm e^{in\vartheta}\):
  the generating function at \(t = \mathrm e^{i\vartheta}\). The spectrum of a
  phase-modulated wave. /Slide: The series and the integral are the same function./
- recurrence relations :: \(J_{n-1} + J_{n+1} = \frac{2n}xJ_n\) and
  \(J_{n-1} - J_{n+1} = 2J_n'\); equivalently \((x^nJ_n)' = x^nJ_{n-1}\) and
  \((x^{-n}J_n)' = -x^{-n}J_{n+1}\). /Slide: Recurrences./
- Bessel's equation :: \(x^2y'' + xy' + (x^2 - n^2)y = 0\). \(J_n\) solves it;
  on \((0,\infty)\) its solutions form a two-dimensional space, and every
  solution independent of \(J_n\) is unbounded at \(0\).
  /Slide: Bessel's differential equation./

* Frobenius

- regular singular point :: A point, here \(x = 0\), where the equation can be
  written \(x^2y'' + xp(x)y' + q(x)y = 0\) with \(p, q\) power series. Solutions
  behave like powers of \(x\), possibly times \(\ln x\).
  /Slide: Regular singular points./
- Frobenius method :: Try \(y = x^r\sum_ka_kx^k\) with \(a_0 \ne 0\), and solve
  for \(r\) and the \(a_k\) by comparing powers of \(x\).
- indicial equation :: The lowest-order coefficient condition,
  \(r(r-1) + p(0)r + q(0) = 0\); for Bessel \(r^2 = n^2\). When its roots differ
  by an integer the recursion may hit \(0\cdot a_k = \text{nonzero}\) at
  \(k = r_1 - r_2\). /Slide: The indicial equation, and the first solution recovered./
- logarithmic solution :: The second solution when Frobenius series run out.
  For \(n = 0\), \(J_0\ln x + \sum_{j\ge1}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}H_j}{(j!)^2}(x/2)^{2j}\),
  found by differentiating the \(r\)-dependent series in \(r\).
  /Slide: The double root, and where the logarithm comes from./
- harmonic number :: \(H_j = 1 + \frac12 + \cdots + \frac1j\). Appears as
  \(-a_{2j}'(0)/a_{2j}(0)\) in the logarithmic solution.
- Liouville substitution :: \(y = u/\sqrt x\), which removes the first-derivative
  term from Bessel's equation: \(u'' + (1 - (n^2 - \frac14)/x^2)u = 0\).
  /Slide: Far from the origin: a decaying cosine./
- large-\(x\) form :: \(J_n(x) = \sqrt{2/\pi x}\cos(x - n\pi/2 - \pi/4) + O(x^{-3/2})\).
  The form \(Ax^{-1/2}\cos(x - \varphi)\) is proved here; the constants are cited
  from unit 20.

* Mars, and where the series stop

- Laplace limit :: \(\lambda = 0.6627434\ldots\), with \(\lambda = \sqrt{x^2 - 1}\)
  and \(x = \coth x\). Lagrange's power series in \(e\) converges for every \(M\)
  when \(e < \lambda\) and diverges at \(M = \pi/2\) when \(e > \lambda\).
  Proved here for \(e < 0.6599\); sharpness and the reason owed to unit 20. The Fourier–Bessel series has no such limit.
  /Slide: The Laplace limit./
