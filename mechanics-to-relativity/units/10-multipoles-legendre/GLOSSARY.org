The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: the oblate Earth — flattening \(f = 1/298.257\), equatorial
radius \(R = 6378.137\) km, \(J_2 = 1.08263\times10^{-3}\) — and a satellite in a
circular orbit \(800\) km above it. Asking that the satellite's orbital plane turn
once a year fixes its inclination at \(98.60^\circ\).

* The generating function

- generating function :: \(g(x,t) = (1-2xt+t^2)^{-1/2}\) for \(x\in[-1,1]\),
  \(|t|<1\). It is \(r/|\vec r - \vec r\,{}'|\) with \(x = \cos\psi\) and
  \(t = r'/r\), which is why it matters. /Slide: The definition, and that it is legitimate./
- Legendre polynomials :: The Taylor coefficients \(P_n(x)\) of the generating
  function in \(t\): \(g = \sum_n P_n(x)t^n\). \(P_n\) has degree exactly \(n\),
  the parity of \(n\), \(P_n(1) = 1\) and \(|P_n| \le 1\) on \([-1,1]\).
  /Slide: The definition, and that it is legitimate./
- Bonnet's recurrence :: \((n+1)P_{n+1} = (2n+1)xP_n - nP_{n-1}\), read off from
  \((1-2xt+t^2)\partial_tg = (x-t)g\). Generates every \(P_n\) from \(P_0 = 1\),
  \(P_1 = x\). /Slide: Everything from one derivative./
- Legendre's equation :: \([(1-x^2)y']' + n(n+1)y = 0\). \(P_n\) solves it and is
  its only polynomial solution up to a scalar; the second solution \(Q_n\) is
  logarithmically singular at \(x = \pm1\). /Slide: Legendre's equation./
- Sturm–Liouville form :: An equation written as \([p\,y']' + q\,y = -\lambda w\,y\).
  Legendre's equation is the case \(p = 1-x^2\), \(q = 0\), \(w = 1\),
  \(\lambda = n(n+1)\); the form is what makes orthogonality a matter of two
  integrations by parts. Unit 19 gives the general theory.
  /Slide: Legendre's equation./
- Rodrigues' formula :: \(P_n(x) = \frac{1}{2^nn!}\frac{\mathrm d^n}{\mathrm dx^n}(x^2-1)^n\).
  Proved by showing the right side solves Legendre's equation and equals \(1\) at
  \(x = 1\). /Slide: Rodrigues' formula./
- orthogonality :: \(\int_{-1}^1P_mP_n\,\mathrm dx = \frac{2}{2n+1}\delta_{mn}\):
  the \(P_n\) are an orthogonal, not orthonormal, family in \(L^2[-1,1]\). It is
  what lets a single coefficient be extracted from a measured field.
  /Slide: Orthogonality, and the norm./

* The multipole expansion

- multipole expansion :: \(\Phi(\vec r) = -\frac Gr\sum_n r^{-n}\int\rho\,r'^{\,n}P_n(\cos\psi)\,\mathrm d^3r'\)
  outside a bounded body: the potential as a series in \(r'/r\), each term one
  power of \(r\) weaker than the last. /Slide: The expansion, and the first three terms./
- monopole :: The \(n = 0\) term, \(-GM/r\). Sees only the total mass.
  /Slide: The expansion, and the first three terms./
- dipole :: The \(n = 1\) term, \(-G\,\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\vec d/r^2\) with
  \(\vec d = M\vec R_{\mathrm{cm}}\). Zero when the origin is the centre of mass,
  which for gravity it always can be. /Slide: The expansion, and the first three terms./
- quadrupole :: The \(n = 2\) term, \(-G\,Q(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r})/2r^3\): the
  first term that sees the body's shape and cannot be removed by moving the
  origin. /Slide: The expansion, and the first three terms./
- quadrupole moment :: The symmetric, trace-free bilinear form
  \(Q(\vec u,\vec v) = \int\rho\,[3(\vec u\cdot\vec r\,{}')(\vec v\cdot\vec r\,{}') - r'^2\,\vec u\cdot\vec v]\)
  on \(\mathbb R^3\). Its matrix \(Q_{ij}\) transforms as \(R^\top QR\) under a
  change of orthonormal basis — a theorem about the components, not the
  definition. Equal to \(-3I + (\operatorname{tr}I)\mathbb 1\).
  /Slide: What the quadrupole is./
- principal axes :: An orthonormal basis diagonalising \(Q\) (equivalently the
  moment-of-inertia integrals \(I\)), which exists because \(Q\) is symmetric.
  The principal moments are written \(A \le B \le C\). /Slide: What the quadrupole is./
- zonal harmonic coefficients :: The \(J_n\) in
  \(\Phi = -\frac{GM}{r}[1 - \sum_{n\ge2}J_n(R/r)^nP_n(\cos\theta)]\) for an
  axially symmetric body. \(J_2 = (C-A)/MR^2\), positive for an oblate body:
  \(1.08263\times10^{-3}\) for the Earth. /Slide: An axially symmetric body: the J_n./
- the addition theorem :: The identity expressing \(P_n(\cos\psi)\) through
  spherical harmonics of the two directions separately. Cited here, to unit 19, for
  the fact that an axisymmetric body's \(n\)-th term is a multiple of
  \(P_n(\cos\theta)\) for every \(n\); only \(n = 2\) is derived and used.
  /Slide: An axially symmetric body: the J_n./
- Clairaut's flattening formula :: \(f = \tfrac32J_2 + \tfrac12q\) with
  \(q = \omega^2R^3/GM\), to first order, for a rotating body whose surface is a
  fluid level surface. For the Earth it predicts \(1/298.09\) against the measured
  \(1/298.257\). Not the same as unit 11's Clairaut relation for geodesics.
  /Slide: A check that has no free parameters./

* Orbital elements, and how to perturb them

- orbital elements :: \((a,e,i,\Omega,\omega,M)\): semi-major axis, eccentricity,
  inclination, longitude of the ascending node, argument of perigee, mean anomaly.
  Coordinates on phase space in which the Kepler flow is five constants and one
  uniformly advancing angle. /Slide: Six numbers, as a change of coordinates./
- osculating elements :: The elements of the Kepler orbit through the actual
  position and velocity at time \(t\) — the orbit the body would follow if the
  perturbation were switched off at that instant. Exact, not approximate.
  /Slide: Osculating elements, and the one lemma./
- variation of parameters :: For any \(c(\vec r,\vec v)\) conserved by the Kepler
  flow, \(\dot c = \nabla_{\vec v}c\cdot\vec a_{\mathrm p}\) along the perturbed
  motion. For \(c = \vec L\) it gives \(\dot{\vec L} = \vec r\times\vec a_{\mathrm p}\).
  /Slide: Osculating elements, and the one lemma./
- secular :: Of a perturbation: the part that accumulates, growing like
  \(\varepsilon t\). It comes from the orbit average \(\bar F\) of the right-hand
  side. /Slide: Secular versus periodic, and averaging./
- periodic :: Of a perturbation: the part \(\widetilde F = F - \bar F\) of zero
  average, which integrates to a bounded wobble of size \(\varepsilon\).
  /Slide: Secular versus periodic, and averaging./
- method of averaging :: Replace \(\dot c = \varepsilon F(c,u)\) by
  \(\dot{\bar c} = \varepsilon\bar F(\bar c)\); the error is \(O(\varepsilon)\) for
  times up to \(O(1/\varepsilon)\). Cited; the average must be taken in time, with
  the weight \(\mathrm dt = (r^2/L)\,\mathrm du\). /Slide: Secular versus periodic, and averaging./

* What J_2 does to an orbit

- argument of latitude :: \(u\), the angle in the orbit plane from the ascending
  node to the body: \(\vec r = r(\cos u\,\hat{\mathbf n} + \sin u\,\hat{\mathbf m})\),
  \(z = r\sin i\sin u\). /Slide: The torque, and what it moves./
- nodal precession :: The secular turning of the orbit plane about the Earth's
  axis, \(\dot\Omega = -\tfrac32J_2(R/a)^2n\cos i/(1-e^2)^2\); westward for
  prograde orbits, eastward for retrograde ones, zero for polar ones.
  /Slide: Averaging, and the result./
- sun-synchronous orbit :: One whose nodal precession is \(360^\circ\) per
  tropical year, so its plane keeps a fixed angle to the Sun. At \(800\) km,
  circular, it needs \(i = 98.60^\circ\). /Slide: The sun-synchronous inclination./
- apsidal precession :: The secular turning of the perigee within the orbit plane,
  \(\dot\omega = \tfrac34J_2(R/a)^2n(5\cos^2i - 1)/(1-e^2)^2\). Like the nodal
  precession, it comes from the \(P_2\) term alone. /Slide: The other precession: the apse line./
- critical inclination :: \(i = \arccos(1/\sqrt5) = 63.43^\circ\) (or
  \(116.57^\circ\)), where the apsidal precession vanishes; the inclination of
  every Molniya orbit. /Slide: The critical inclination./

* Tides, as the l = 2 term

- tidal tensor :: The Hessian \(\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\) of an external potential
  across a body; for a point mass at distance \(d\) along \(\hat{\mathbf n}\) it is
  \((GM/d^3)(\delta_{ij} - 3n_in_j)\), trace-free, eigenvalues \((-2,1,1)GM/d^3\).
  It is the Hessian of the \(\ell = 2\) term. /Slide: Two bulges, because P_2 is even./
- tidal locking :: The state in which a body's rotation period equals its orbital
  period, so it keeps one face to its partner. The long axis then sits at
  \(\gamma = 0\), the minimum of the orientation energy
  \(-\tfrac{3GM}{2d^3}(B-A)\cos^2\gamma\), and dissipation is what brings it there.
  /Slide: Why the Moon keeps one face towards us./
- free libration :: The small oscillation of a locked body about exact alignment,
  at \(\omega_{\mathrm{lib}} = n\sqrt{3(B-A)/C}\); about \(2.9\) years for the Moon.
  /Slide: Why the Moon keeps one face towards us./
