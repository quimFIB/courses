Real planets are not points: multipoles, Legendre, and secular perturbation

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. From \(J_2\) alone you predict the sun-synchronous inclination at a stated altitude to within a degree of the published value, and you can say which Legendre term produces each of the two precessions. You can derive \(P_n\) from the generating function, prove orthogonality on \([-1,1]\), and identify \(J_2\) with \((C-A)/MR^2\).

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Legendre polynomials

P1 What the generating function gives away routine

exercises: Bonnet's recurrence, reading values off the generating function, Legendre's equation at an endpoint

  1. From \(P_3 = \tfrac12(5x^3-3x)\) and \(P_4 = \tfrac18(35x^4-30x^2+3)\), compute \(P_5\) by Bonnet's recurrence. Check \(P_5(1) = 1\) and the parity.
  2. Put \(x = 0\) in the generating function and find \(P_n(0)\) for every \(n\). Check against \(P_2\) and \(P_4\), and give \(P_6(0)\).
  3. Show that \(P_n'(1) = \tfrac12n(n+1)\), using Legendre's equation and nothing else. Check it on \(P_5\).
  4. For the \(J_2\) term \(\Phi_2 = \frac{GMJ_2R^2}{r^3}P_2(\cos\theta)\), what is the ratio of its value over the pole to its value over the equator, at the same \(r\)? Which of the two places is more strongly bound than it would be round a spherical Earth?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A polynomial, then a whole sequence of numbers, then a one-line identity, then one ratio and a sign. Every part is a substitution into something the deck already proved: no new analysis is needed.

Rung 2

For part 2, \(g(0,t) = (1+t^2)^{-1/2}\), which is the binomial series in \(-t^2\): only even powers of \(t\) appear. For part 3, evaluate the equation at the one point where its leading coefficient \(1-x^2\) vanishes.

Rung 3

First lines: \(5P_5 = 9x\,P_4 - 4P_3\). And \((1+t^2)^{-1/2} = \sum_m c_m(-t^2)^m\) with \(c_m = \binom{2m}{m}4^{-m}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The recurrence builds the polynomials; the generating function at a special \(x\) collapses to an elementary function whose Taylor series is known; Legendre's equation degenerates at \(x = 1\) into an equation for the first derivative.

1. Bonnet at \(n = 4\): \(5P_5 = 9xP_4 - 4P_3\), so

\[ 5P_5 = \tfrac98(35x^5 - 30x^3 + 3x) - 2(5x^3 - 3x) = \tfrac18\big(315x^5 - 270x^3 + 27x - 80x^3 + 48x\big) = \tfrac18(315x^5 - 350x^3 + 75x), \] \[ P_5(x) = \tfrac18\big(63x^5 - 70x^3 + 15x\big). \]

\(P_5(1) = (63 - 70 + 15)/8 = 1\) ✓, and only odd powers appear ✓. The leading coefficient \(63/8\) equals \(\binom{10}{5}2^{-5} = 252/32 = 63/8\) ✓.

2. \(g(0,t) = (1+t^2)^{-1/2} = \sum_m c_m(-1)^mt^{2m}\), so

\[ P_{2m}(0) = (-1)^m\binom{2m}{m}4^{-m},\qquad P_{2m+1}(0) = 0 . \]

\(m = 1\): \(-2/4 = -\tfrac12\), and \(P_2(0) = \tfrac12(0-1) = -\tfrac12\) ✓. \(m = 2\): \(6/16 = \tfrac38\), and \(P_4(0) = \tfrac38\) ✓. \(m = 3\): \(P_6(0) = -20/64 = -\tfrac5{16}\).

3. \(P_n\) is a polynomial, so \(P_n''(1)\) is finite and \((1-x^2)P_n''\) vanishes at \(x = 1\). The equation \((1-x^2)P_n'' - 2xP_n' + n(n+1)P_n = 0\) at \(x = 1\) reads \(-2P_n'(1) + n(n+1)P_n(1) = 0\), and \(P_n(1) = 1\), so \(P_n'(1) = \tfrac12n(n+1)\). On \(P_5\): \(P_5' = \tfrac18(315x^4 - 210x^2 + 15)\), so \(P_5'(1) = 120/8 = 15 = \tfrac12\cdot5\cdot6\) ✓.

4. Over the pole \(\cos\theta = 1\) and \(P_2 = 1\); over the equator \(\cos\theta = 0\) and \(P_2 = -\tfrac12\). The ratio is \(-2\). Since \(J_2 \gt 0\), \(\Phi_2\) is positive over the pole and negative over the equator: the equator is the more strongly bound — the potential well is deepened where the bulge is.

Check. The values \(|P_{2m}(0)| = \tfrac12, \tfrac38, \tfrac5{16}\) are all at most \(1\), as the bound \(|P_n| \le 1\) requires, and they decrease, as they must if \(\sum P_{2m}(0)t^{2m}\) is to converge at every \(|t| \lt 1\). Part 3 on \(P_2\): \(P_2' = 3x\), \(P_2'(1) = 3 = \tfrac12\cdot2\cdot3\) ✓.

P2 Expanding in Legendre polynomials routine

exercises: the \(P_n\) as a basis of polynomials, extracting a coefficient by orthogonality

  1. Prove that \(\int_{-1}^1 q(x)P_n(x)\,\mathrm dx = 0\) for every polynomial \(q\) of degree \(\lt n\).
  2. Write \(x^2\), \(x^3\) and \(x^4\) as combinations of Legendre polynomials. Check one coefficient of \(x^4\) with \(a_n = \frac{2n+1}{2}\int_{-1}^1 f\,P_n\,\mathrm dx\).
  3. To first order in \(f\), the Earth's surface is \(r(\theta) = R(1 - f\cos^2\theta)\). Write it as \(r = R_0 + R_2P_2(\cos\theta)\) and give \(R_0\) and \(R_2\) in km. What is \(R_0\) geometrically?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a statement about bases; parts 2 and 3 are finite linear algebra — each answer is a short list of rational coefficients, and in part 3 two lengths.

Rung 2

\(P_0,\dots,P_{n-1}\) have degrees exactly \(0,\dots,n-1\). For part 2, peel from the top: subtract the multiple of \(P_k\) that kills the leading term of what is left, and repeat.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(P_2 = \tfrac12(3x^2 - 1)\) gives \(x^2 = \tfrac23P_2 + \tfrac13P_0\). Part 3 then needs only \(\cos^2\theta = \tfrac23P_2(\cos\theta) + \tfrac13\).

Worked solution

Idea. Polynomials of degree \(\lt n\) form an \(n\)-dimensional space, and \(P_0,\dots,P_{n-1}\) are \(n\) elements of it with distinct degrees, hence a basis. Orthogonality then does the rest.

1. The matrix expressing \(P_0,\dots,P_{n-1}\) in the monomial basis \(1,\dots,x^{n-1}\) is triangular with nonzero diagonal (the leading coefficients), hence invertible. So \(q = \sum_{k \lt n}b_kP_k\) and \(\int qP_n = \sum_{k\lt n}b_k\int P_kP_n = 0\) by orthogonality. \(\square\)

2. \(x^2 = \tfrac13P_0 + \tfrac23P_2\). From \(P_3 = \tfrac12(5x^3 - 3x)\), \(x^3 = \tfrac25P_3 + \tfrac35x = \tfrac35P_1 + \tfrac25P_3\). From \(P_4 = \tfrac18(35x^4 - 30x^2 + 3)\),

\[ x^4 = \tfrac8{35}P_4 + \tfrac67x^2 - \tfrac3{35} = \tfrac8{35}P_4 + \tfrac47P_2 + \big(\tfrac27 - \tfrac3{35}\big)P_0 = \tfrac15P_0 + \tfrac47P_2 + \tfrac8{35}P_4 . \]

Check the \(P_0\) coefficient by orthogonality: \(a_0 = \tfrac12\int_{-1}^1x^4\,\mathrm dx = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac25 = \tfrac15\) ✓. Only polynomials of the parity of \(x^k\) appear, as they must.

3. \(\cos^2\theta = \tfrac13 + \tfrac23P_2(\cos\theta)\), so

\[ r = R\big(1 - \tfrac f3\big) - \tfrac{2f}{3}R\,P_2(\cos\theta), \qquad R_0 = R\big(1-\tfrac f3\big) = 6371.009\ \mathrm{km},\quad R_2 = -\tfrac{2f}3R = -14.256\ \mathrm{km}. \]

\(R_0\) is the average of \(r\) over the sphere of directions, because \(\int_{-1}^1P_2 = 0\) kills the second term under the average in \(\cos\theta\) (and \(\mathrm d\Omega = \sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm d\phi\) is uniform in \(\cos\theta\), unit 05). It is \((2R + c)/3\) with \(c = R(1-f)\): the geodesists' mean radius, published as \(6371.0088\) km ✓.

Check. At \(x = 1\) every expansion must give \(1\), since \(P_n(1) = 1\): \(\tfrac15 + \tfrac47 + \tfrac8{35} = \tfrac{7+20+8}{35} = 1\) ✓, and \(\tfrac35 + \tfrac25 = 1\) ✓. In part 3, at the pole \(r = R_0 + R_2 = 6356.752\) km \(= R(1-f)\) ✓, and at the equator \(r = R_0 - \tfrac12R_2 = 6378.137\) km ✓.

P3 Proof: orthogonality from Rodrigues medium

exercises: Rodrigues' formula, repeated integration by parts, a Wallis-type recursion

Let \(v_n = (x^2-1)^n\) and \(D = \mathrm d/\mathrm dx\), so \(P_n = D^nv_n/(2^nn!)\). Without using Legendre's equation:

  1. Show that \(D^kv_n\) vanishes at \(x = \pm1\) for \(0 \le k \lt n\).
  2. Prove \(\int_{-1}^1q\,D^nv_n\,\mathrm dx = 0\) for every polynomial \(q\) of degree \(\lt n\), and deduce \(\int P_mP_n = 0\) for \(m \ne n\).
  3. Prove \(\int_{-1}^1P_n^2 = \frac{2}{2n+1}\). You will need \(I_n = \int_{-1}^1(1-x^2)^n\,\mathrm dx\); show \(I_n = \frac{2n}{2n+1}I_{n-1}\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three proofs, all by integration by parts, in which every boundary term dies because of part 1. The answer to part 3 is the deck's norm, reached by a different road.

Rung 2

Part 1: \(v_n = (x-1)^n(x+1)^n\) has a zero of order \(n\) at each end. Part 2: move all \(n\) derivatives off \(v_n\) and onto \(q\), one at a time. Part 3: \(P_n^2\) is \(P_n\) times its own leading term plus lower-degree terms, and part 2 kills the lower ones.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\int q\,D^nv_n = \big[q\,D^{n-1}v_n\big]_{-1}^1 - \int q'\,D^{n-1}v_n = -\int q'\,D^{n-1}v_n\).

Worked solution

Idea. Rodrigues' formula puts \(P_n\) in the form "\(n\)-th derivative of a function vanishing to order \(n\) at both ends", and that is precisely what integration by parts \(n\) times needs to transfer all the derivatives to the other factor with no boundary terms.

1. By Leibniz, \(D^k[(x-1)^n(x+1)^n] = \sum_j\binom kj D^j(x-1)^n\,D^{k-j}(x+1)^n\). With \(k \lt n\), each \(D^j(x-1)^n\) is a multiple of \((x-1)^{n-j}\) with \(n - j \ge 1\), so every term vanishes at \(x = 1\). The same with the roles of the factors swapped at \(x = -1\).

2. Integrate by parts \(k\) times, for \(k = 1,\dots,n\):

\[ \int_{-1}^1q\,D^nv_n = (-1)^k\int_{-1}^1(D^kq)\,D^{n-k}v_n , \]

each boundary term \(\big[D^{j}q\;D^{n-1-j}v_n\big]_{-1}^{1}\) vanishing by part 1 since \(n-1-j \lt n\). At \(k = n\), \(D^nq = 0\) because \(\deg q \lt n\). For \(m \lt n\), take \(q = P_m\). \(\square\)

3. \(P_n = \frac{(2n)!}{2^n(n!)^2}x^n + (\text{degree} \lt n)\) — the leading coefficient of \(D^n x^{2n}/(2^nn!)\). By part 2 only the top term of the first factor survives:

\[ \int P_n^2 = \frac{(2n)!}{2^n(n!)^2}\cdot\frac1{2^nn!}\int x^n\,D^nv_n = \frac{(2n)!}{4^n(n!)^3}(-1)^n\int (D^nx^n)\,v_n = \frac{(2n)!}{4^n(n!)^2}\,I_n , \]

using \(D^nx^n = n!\) and \((-1)^nv_n = (1-x^2)^n\). For \(I_n\), integrate by parts with \(u = (1-x^2)^n\), \(\mathrm dv = \mathrm dx\):

\[ I_n = \big[x(1-x^2)^n\big]_{-1}^1 + 2n\int x^2(1-x^2)^{n-1} = 2n\int\big(1 - (1-x^2)\big)(1-x^2)^{n-1} = 2n(I_{n-1} - I_n), \]

so \(I_n = \frac{2n}{2n+1}I_{n-1}\) with \(I_0 = 2\), giving \(I_n = 2\cdot\frac{2\cdot4\cdots(2n)}{3\cdot5\cdots(2n+1)} = \frac{2\cdot4^n(n!)^2}{(2n+1)!}\). Therefore

\[ \int_{-1}^1P_n^2 = \frac{(2n)!}{4^n(n!)^2}\cdot\frac{2\cdot4^n(n!)^2}{(2n+1)!} = \frac{2}{2n+1}. \qquad\square \]

Check. \(n = 1\): \(\int x^2 = \tfrac23\) ✓. \(n = 2\): \(\tfrac14\int(9x^4 - 6x^2 + 1) = \tfrac14\big(\tfrac{18}5 - 4 + 2\big) = \tfrac25\) ✓. And \(I_1 = \int(1-x^2) = \tfrac43 = \tfrac23I_0\) ✓.

Another way

The deck's route: Legendre's equation in self-adjoint form and two integrations by parts give orthogonality, and squaring the generating function gives the norm. The two proofs use disjoint facts — the equation there, the explicit formula here — which is a genuine check that the three definitions of \(P_n\) agree.

Multipoles

P4 The quadrupole of a pair medium

exercises: dipole and quadrupole moments from their definitions, the centre of mass, eigenvalues of a bilinear form

Two point masses \(m_1\), \(m_2\) are a distance \(d\) apart along the unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf e}\) (from 1 to 2). Put \(M = m_1 + m_2\) and \(\mu = m_1m_2/M\).

  1. With the origin at the centre of mass, show that the dipole vanishes and that \(Q(\vec u,\vec v) = \mu d^2\big[3(\vec u\cdot\hat{\mathbf e})(\vec v\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}) - \vec u\cdot\vec v\big]\).
  2. Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(Q\), and check its trace.
  3. Write the far-field quadrupole potential in terms of \(P_2\).
  4. For the Earth–Moon pair (\(m_{\mathrm{Moon}} = 7.342\times10^{22}\) kg, \(d = 384400\) km), compare \(\mu d^2\) with the Earth's own \(C - A = J_2M_\oplus R^2\). Seen from far away, which dominates the quadrupole of the Earth–Moon system?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

For point masses the integral in the definition of \(Q\) is a sum of two terms. The answer to part 2 is three numbers with eigenvectors; part 4 is one ratio.

Rung 2

The centre of mass at the origin forces \(\vec r_1 = -\frac{m_2}{M}d\,\hat{\mathbf e}\) and \(\vec r_2 = \frac{m_1}{M}d\,\hat{\mathbf e}\). Both are multiples of \(\hat{\mathbf e}\), so both terms of \(Q\) have the same shape.

Rung 3

First line: \(Q(\vec u,\vec v) = \sum_km_k\big[3(\vec u\cdot\vec r_k)(\vec v\cdot\vec r_k) - r_k^2\,\vec u\cdot\vec v\big]\) and \(m_1\frac{m_2^2}{M^2} + m_2\frac{m_1^2}{M^2} = \frac{m_1m_2}{M}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Everything is along one line, so \(Q\) is a multiple of the single trace-free form built from \(\hat{\mathbf e}\); the multiple is a moment of inertia, and it is the reduced mass that appears, exactly as in unit 08's two-body reduction.

1. Dipole: \(m_1\vec r_1 + m_2\vec r_2 = \big(-\frac{m_1m_2}M + \frac{m_2m_1}M\big)d\,\hat{\mathbf e} = 0\). Quadrupole: with \(\vec r_k = s_kd\,\hat{\mathbf e}\), \(s_1 = -m_2/M\), \(s_2 = m_1/M\),

\[ Q(\vec u,\vec v) = \Big(\sum_km_ks_k^2\Big)d^2\big[3(\vec u\cdot\hat{\mathbf e})(\vec v\cdot\hat{\mathbf e}) - \vec u\cdot\vec v\big], \qquad \sum_km_ks_k^2 = \frac{m_1m_2^2 + m_2m_1^2}{M^2} = \mu . \]

2. \(Q(\hat{\mathbf e},\cdot)\): the matrix is \(\mu d^2(3\hat{\mathbf e}\hat{\mathbf e}^\top - \mathbb 1)\). Eigenvector \(\hat{\mathbf e}\), eigenvalue \(2\mu d^2\); any vector perpendicular to \(\hat{\mathbf e}\), eigenvalue \(-\mu d^2\), twice. Trace \(2 - 1 - 1 = 0\) ✓.

3. \(Q(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r}) = \mu d^2(3\cos^2\psi - 1) = 2\mu d^2P_2(\cos\psi)\), \(\psi\) the angle from \(\hat{\mathbf e}\), so

\[ \Phi_2 = -\frac{G\,Q(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r})}{2r^3} = -\frac{G\mu d^2}{r^3}P_2(\cos\psi). \]

It is most negative along the line of the pair — a prolate distribution, the opposite sign from an oblate planet's \(J_2\) term.

4. \(\mu = \frac{5.9722\times10^{24}\cdot7.342\times10^{22}}{6.0456\times10^{24}} = 7.253\times10^{22}\) kg, so \(\mu d^2 = 7.253\times10^{22}\times(3.844\times10^8)^2 = 1.072\times10^{40}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). The Earth's \(C - A = 1.08263\times10^{-3}\times5.9722\times10^{24}\times(6.378137\times10^6)^2 = 2.630\times10^{35}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). The pair's is larger by a factor \(4.07\times10^4\): from far away the Earth's bulge is invisible beside the Moon's orbit.

Check. Dimensions: \(\mu d^2\) is \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\), like \(C - A\) ✓. Limit \(m_2\to0\): \(\mu\to0\) and a lone point mass at the origin has no quadrupole ✓. Equal masses: \(\mu = M/4\), and each mass at distance \(d/2\) contributes \(\frac M2\cdot\frac{d^2}4\), total \(Md^2/4\) ✓.

P5 Proof: what moving the origin does medium

exercises: origin dependence of multipole moments, why the centre of mass is the right origin

A body has mass \(M\), dipole \(\vec d\) and quadrupole \(Q\) about an origin \(O\). Move the origin to \(O + \vec c\), so every source point is described by \(\vec r\,{}'' = \vec r\,{}' - \vec c\).

  1. Show the new dipole is \(\vec d - M\vec c\).
  2. Show the new quadrupole is \(Q''(\vec u,\vec v) = Q(\vec u,\vec v) - 3\big[(\vec u\cdot\vec d)(\vec v\cdot\vec c) + (\vec u\cdot\vec c)(\vec v\cdot\vec d)\big] + 2(\vec c\cdot\vec d)(\vec u\cdot\vec v) + M\big[3(\vec u\cdot\vec c)(\vec v\cdot\vec c) - c^2\,\vec u\cdot\vec v\big]\), and check that it is still trace-free.
  3. Prove: the lowest non-vanishing member of the list \((M, \vec d, Q)\) does not depend on the origin. For \(M \ne 0\), prove there is exactly one origin with zero dipole.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 are the definitions with \(\vec r\,{}' - \vec c\) substituted; part 3 is a case analysis on which of \(M\), \(\vec d\) vanish, read off from parts 1 and 2.

Rung 2

Expand \(\big(\vec u\cdot(\vec r\,{}'-\vec c)\big)\big(\vec v\cdot(\vec r\,{}'-\vec c)\big)\) and \(|\vec r\,{}'-\vec c|^2\), and integrate term by term against \(\rho\), using \(\int\rho = M\) and \(\int\rho\,\vec r\,{}' = \vec d\).

Rung 3

First line: \(|\vec r\,{}'-\vec c|^2 = r'^2 - 2\vec r\,{}'\cdot\vec c + c^2\), whose integral against \(\rho\) is \(\int\rho\,r'^2 - 2\vec c\cdot\vec d + Mc^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. Each moment is an integral of a polynomial in \(\vec r\,{}'\); shifting \(\vec r\,{}'\) mixes in the lower moments, and only the lowest one has nothing below it to mix in.

1. \(\int\rho\,(\vec r\,{}' - \vec c) = \vec d - M\vec c\).

2. Expanding,

\[ 3\big(\vec u\cdot\vec r\,{}' - \vec u\cdot\vec c\big)\big(\vec v\cdot\vec r\,{}' - \vec v\cdot\vec c\big) = 3(\vec u\cdot\vec r\,{}')(\vec v\cdot\vec r\,{}') - 3(\vec u\cdot\vec r\,{}')(\vec v\cdot\vec c) - 3(\vec u\cdot\vec c)(\vec v\cdot\vec r\,{}') + 3(\vec u\cdot\vec c)(\vec v\cdot\vec c). \]

Integrating against \(\rho\) and subtracting \(\big(\int\rho r'^2 - 2\vec c\cdot\vec d + Mc^2\big)\vec u\cdot\vec v\) gives the stated formula term by term. Trace: put \(\vec u = \vec v = \hat e_i\) and sum over \(i\). The first term gives \(0\); the bracket gives \(-3\cdot2(\vec c\cdot\vec d)\); the next \(+2\cdot3(\vec c\cdot\vec d)\); the last \(M(3c^2 - 3c^2)\). Total \(0\) ✓.

3. If \(M \ne 0\), \(M\) is lowest and trivially origin-independent; the new dipole \(\vec d - M\vec c\) vanishes if and only if \(\vec c = \vec d/M\), the centre of mass — exactly one origin. If \(M = 0\) and \(\vec d \ne 0\), part 1 gives \(\vec d'' = \vec d\): the dipole is the lowest moment and does not move. If \(M = 0\) and \(\vec d = 0\), every correction term in part 2 carries a factor \(\vec d\) or \(M\), so \(Q'' = Q\). \(\square\)

Check. Take the origin at the centre of mass (\(\vec d = 0\)): the formula reduces to the parallel-axis shift \(Q'' = Q + M(3\vec c\,\vec c^{\,\top} - c^2\mathbb 1)\). For a single point mass \(M\) at \(O\) (so \(Q = 0\)), seen from \(O + \vec c\), it sits at \(-\vec c\) and the definition gives directly \(M[3(\vec u\cdot\vec c)(\vec v\cdot\vec c) - c^2\,\vec u\cdot\vec v]\) ✓. Dimensions: every term is mass times length squared ✓. This is why gravity's expansion always starts monopole-then-quadrupole: mass is positive, so the dipole can always be removed, and it is also why P4's answer did not depend on anything but \(\mu\) and \(d\).

P6 Quadrupole of an ellipsoid medium

exercises: change of variables and the Jacobian, principal moments, \(J_2\) from the flattening, inferring an interior

  1. A uniform solid ellipsoid \(E\) has semi-axes \(a_1, a_2, a_3\) and mass \(M\). Using the map \(x_k = a_ku_k\) from the unit ball, show \(\int_E x_3^2\,\mathrm dV = \frac{4\pi}{15}a_1a_2a_3\,a_3^2\), and hence that the moment integral \(I_{11} = \int\rho(x_2^2+x_3^2) = \frac M5(a_2^2 + a_3^2)\).
  2. For the oblate spheroid \(a_1 = a_2 = R\), \(a_3 = R(1-f)\), show \(J_2 = (2f - f^2)/5\), evaluate it for the Earth's \(f\), and compare with the measured \(J_2\).
  3. Now let the body be that same uniform spheroid, of mass \(M_{\mathrm s}\), plus an extra mass \(M_{\mathrm c}\) distributed with spherical symmetry about the centre — a crude dense core. Keeping \(R\) as reference radius, find \(J_2\), and the fraction \(M_{\mathrm c}/M\) that reproduces the measured value.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an integral done by a linear change of variables; part 2 is a ratio of two moments and one number; part 3 is one fraction. The spherical core contributes to one of \(M\), \(C - A\) and not to the other.

Rung 2

The Jacobian of \(x_k = a_ku_k\) is \(a_1a_2a_3\), and by symmetry \(\int_Bu_3^2 = \frac13\int_B|u|^2\), which is an integral in spherical coordinates. For part 3, a spherically symmetric mass has \(A = B = C\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\int_Bu_3^2\,\mathrm dV = \frac13\int_0^1r^2\cdot4\pi r^2\,\mathrm dr = \frac{4\pi}{15}\).

Worked solution

Idea. A linear map carries the ball to the ellipsoid and multiplies volumes by its determinant, so every second moment of the ellipsoid is a second moment of the ball, scaled. \(J_2\) then measures only the bulge, and anything spherical dilutes it.

1. With \(x = Au\), \(A = \operatorname{diag}(a_1,a_2,a_3)\), \(\mathrm dV_x = a_1a_2a_3\,\mathrm dV_u\) (unit 05):

\[ \int_Ex_3^2\,\mathrm dV = a_1a_2a_3\,a_3^2\int_Bu_3^2\,\mathrm dV = a_1a_2a_3\,a_3^2\cdot\tfrac13\int_0^1 r^2\,4\pi r^2\,\mathrm dr = \tfrac{4\pi}{15}a_1a_2a_3\,a_3^2 , \]

using \(\int_Bu_1^2 = \int_Bu_2^2 = \int_Bu_3^2\) by symmetry of the ball. With \(\rho = M/(\tfrac43\pi a_1a_2a_3)\), \(\rho\int_Ex_3^2 = \tfrac M5a_3^2\), and likewise for \(x_2\), so \(I_{11} = \tfrac M5(a_2^2 + a_3^2)\), and cyclically.

2. \(C = I_{33} = \tfrac25MR^2\), \(A = I_{11} = \tfrac15M(R^2 + c^2)\) with \(c = R(1-f)\):

\[ J_2 = \frac{C - A}{MR^2} = \frac{R^2 - c^2}{5R^2} = \frac{1 - (1-f)^2}{5} = \frac{2f - f^2}{5} = 1.33888\times10^{-3} \]

for \(f = 1/298.257223563\). The measured \(1.08263\times10^{-3}\) is smaller by a factor \(0.8086\): a uniform Earth would have \(23.7\%\) too much quadrupole.

3. The spherical part adds \(M_{\mathrm c}\) to the mass and equal amounts to \(A\) and \(C\), so \(C - A = \tfrac15M_{\mathrm s}(R^2 - c^2)\) is unchanged while \(M = M_{\mathrm s} + M_{\mathrm c}\):

\[ J_2 = \frac{M_{\mathrm s}}{M}\cdot\frac{2f - f^2}{5}, \qquad \frac{M_{\mathrm s}}{M} = \frac{1.08263}{1.33888} = 0.8086,\qquad \frac{M_{\mathrm c}}{M} = 0.191 . \]

Check. Dimensions: \(J_2\) is a ratio of squared lengths ✓. Limits: \(f \to 0\) gives \(J_2 \to 0\), a sphere ✓; \(M_{\mathrm c}\to0\) recovers part 2 ✓; \(M_{\mathrm c}\to\infty\) gives \(J_2\to0\), a point mass with a massless envelope, whose bulge weighs nothing ✓. A number to compare. \(M_{\mathrm c}\) is the core's mass in excess of the surrounding density. For the real Earth — core radius \(3485\) km, core mass \(1.94\times10^{24}\) kg, so mean core density \(10.9\) and mean mantle density \(4.45\ \mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}\) — that excess is \((10.94 - 4.45)\times10^3\times\frac43\pi(3.485\times10^6)^3 / M_\oplus = 0.193\) of the mass. Agreement to a percent is partly luck: the model ignores the density rising continuously through the mantle and the core's own flattening. But it shows how \(J_2\) and \(f\) together weigh an interior nobody can visit.

Idea. \(\vec A\) is conserved by the Kepler flow, so the deck's lemma gives its rate of change from the perturbation alone. Its component across itself turns the ellipse; its component along itself changes the eccentricity. Averaging with the correct weight keeps the first and kills the second.

1. The deck's \(\vec a_{\mathrm p} = \frac{3GMJ_2R^2}{2r^5}\big[(5z^2/r^2 - 1)\vec r - 2z\hat{\mathbf z}\big]\) at \(z = 0\) is \(-\frac{3GMJ_2R^2}{2r^5}\vec r = -\frac{3GMJ_2R^2}{2r^4}\hat{\mathbf r}\).

2. \(\vec A = \vec v\times(\vec r\times\vec v) - GM\hat{\mathbf r}\). 3.   • \(i = 63.4^\circ\): \(5\cos^2i - 1 = 0.002441\), \(\dot\omega = 4.03\times10^{-4}\,{}^\circ\)/day, one degree in \(2480\) days, \(6.8\) years.

Check. Dimensions: \(J_2(R/p)^2\) is dimensionless, so \(\dot\varpi\) has those of \(n\) ✓. \(J_2\to0\): no precession ✓. \(e\to0\): the rate stays finite, \(\tfrac32nJ_2(R/a)^2\), agreeing with the deck's near-circular effective-potential calculation at \(i = 0\) ✓. Another way

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The functions in question are Taylor coefficients of a function known to be harmonic. The proof is: Laplacian and Taylor coefficient commute.

Rung 2

For part 1, \(F(\vec r,s) = 1/|\vec r - s\hat{\mathbf z}|\) is harmonic in \(\vec r\) for each small \(s\), and the generating function with \(t = s/r\) expands it in powers of \(s\). For part 2, expand \(w/|w\vec r - \hat{\mathbf z}|\) in powers of \(w\) instead.

Rung 3

First line: for \(|s| \lt r\), \(\frac1{|\vec r - s\hat{\mathbf z}|} = \frac1r\Big(1 - 2\frac sr\cos\theta + \frac{s^2}{r^2}\Big)^{-1/2} = \sum_n s^n\,r^{-n-1}P_n(\cos\theta)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Differentiating a harmonic family with respect to a parameter gives harmonic functions, because the \(\vec r\)-Laplacian and the parameter derivative commute. The generating function supplies the family.

1. Fix \(\vec r \ne 0\). On the set \(\{(\vec r,s) : |s| \lt |\vec r|\}\) the function \(F = 1/|\vec r - s\hat{\mathbf z}|\) is smooth (its denominator is \(\ge r - |s| \gt 0\)) and \(\nabla_{\vec r}^2F = 0\), since \(\vec r \ne s\hat{\mathbf z}\). By the law of cosines and the generating function with \(x = \cos\theta\), \(t = s/r\), \(F = \sum_ns^n\,r^{-n-1}P_n(\cos\theta)\), so \(r^{-n-1}P_n(\cos\theta) = \frac1{n!}\partial_s^nF\big|_{s=0}\). Mixed partial derivatives of a smooth function commute (Clairaut, unit 03), so

\[ \nabla^2\big(r^{-n-1}P_n(\cos\theta)\big) = \frac1{n!}\,\partial_s^n\big(\nabla_{\vec r}^2F\big)\Big|_{s=0} = 0 .\qquad\square \]

2. Polynomial: \(P_n\) has the parity of \(n\), so \(r^nP_n(z/r) = \sum_ja_j\,z^{n-2j}r^{2j}\), and \(r^{2j} = (x^2+y^2+z^2)^j\) is a polynomial. Harmonic: let \(G(\vec r,w) = w/|w\vec r - \hat{\mathbf z}|\) on \(\{|w|\,|\vec r| \lt 1\}\), where the denominator is \(\ge 1 - |w||\vec r| \gt 0\), so \(G\) is smooth. By the chain rule \(\nabla_{\vec r}^2\big[1/|w\vec r - \hat{\mathbf z}|\big] = w^2\,(\nabla^2h)(w\vec r)\) with \(h(\vec y) = 1/|\vec y - \hat{\mathbf z}|\), and \(w\vec r \ne \hat{\mathbf z}\), so \(G\) is harmonic in \(\vec r\). Expanding with \(t = wr\): \(G = w\sum_n(wr)^nP_n(\cos\theta) = \sum_nw^{n+1}r^nP_n(\cos\theta)\). Taking \(\frac1{(n+1)!}\partial_w^{n+1}\) at \(w = 0\) and commuting as in part 1 gives \(\nabla^2(r^nP_n) = 0\). \(\square\)

3. \(r^2P_2 = \tfrac12(3z^2 - r^2) = z^2 - \tfrac12(x^2+y^2)\), with Laplacian \(2 - 1 - 1 = 0\) ✓. For \(\phi = 3z^2/r^5 - 1/r^3\) the deck gives \(\partial_x\phi = 3x/r^5 - 15z^2x/r^7\) and \(\partial_z\phi = 9z/r^5 - 15z^3/r^7\). Differentiating again with \(\partial_jr = x_j/r\):

\[ \partial_x^2\phi = \frac3{r^5} - \frac{15x^2}{r^7} - \frac{15z^2}{r^7} + \frac{105x^2z^2}{r^9},\qquad \partial_z^2\phi = \frac9{r^5} - \frac{90z^2}{r^7} + \frac{105z^4}{r^9}, \]

and \(\partial_y^2\phi\) as \(\partial_x^2\phi\) with \(y\) for \(x\). Summing: \(\frac{15}{r^5} - \frac{15(x^2+y^2)}{r^7} - \frac{120z^2}{r^7} + \frac{105z^2(x^2+y^2+z^2)}{r^9} = \frac{15}{r^5} - \frac{15(x^2+y^2+z^2)}{r^7} = 0\) ✓.

4. The tidal potential inside a body is, at order \(\ell = 2\), \(-\frac{GM}{d^3}r'^2P_2(\cos\psi)\), a multiple of \(r'^2P_2\), which is harmonic by part 2 (with the axis along \(\hat{\mathbf n}\)). The tidal tensor is its Hessian, and the trace of the Hessian is the Laplacian (unit 05): zero. \(\square\)

Check. \(n = 0\): part 1 says \(1/r\) is harmonic, which was the input ✓. \(n = 1\): \(z/r^3\) and \(z\) are harmonic — the field of a dipole, and a linear function ✓. Parts 1 and 2 are related by \(\vec r \mapsto \vec r/r^2\) with a factor \(1/r\): \(r^{-n-1}P_n = r^{-1}\cdot(r^nP_n)(\vec r/r^2)\), a map called the Kelvin transform. That it carries every harmonic function to a harmonic function is a general theorem, not proved here and not needed: parts 1 and 2 were proved separately.

P12 Sun against Moon routine

exercises: scaling of the \(\ell = 2\) term, successive multipoles as a small-parameter series, the equilibrium tide

A body of mass \(M_{\mathrm b}\) at distance \(d\) produces, inside the Earth, the tidal potential \(\Phi^{(2)} = -\frac{GM_{\mathrm b}}{d^3}r'^2P_2(\cos\psi)\) as its leading non-removable term. Take \(GM_{\mathrm{Moon}} = 4.9028695\times10^{12}\), \(GM_\odot = 1.32712440018\times10^{20}\) (both \(\mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\)), \(d_{\mathrm{Moon}} = 384400\) km, \(d_\odot = 1.495978707\times10^{11}\) m.

  1. Find the ratio of the Sun's tidal term to the Moon's.
  2. At new and full Moon the two add; at quarter Moon the Sun's bulge sits across the Moon's. Estimate the ratio of spring to neap tidal range.
  3. How large is the \(\ell = 3\) term relative to the \(\ell = 2\) term at the Earth's surface, for each body?
  4. The ocean surface is a level surface of the total potential. Show that the tide raises it by \(h = \frac{M_{\mathrm b}}{M_\oplus}\Big(\frac Rd\Big)^3R\,P_2(\cos\psi)\), and give the range between \(\psi = 0\) and \(\psi = 90^\circ\) for the Moon.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four dimensionless numbers and one length. Each part is a ratio in which most constants cancel.

Rung 2

The tidal strength is \(GM_{\mathrm b}/d^3\). Successive terms of the expansion differ by a factor \(r'/d\). For part 4, a potential change \(\delta\Phi\) moves a level surface by \(-\delta\Phi/g\), with \(g = GM_\oplus/R^2\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\frac{GM_\odot/d_\odot^3}{GM_{\mathrm{Moon}}/d_{\mathrm{Moon}}^3} = \frac{3.964\times10^{-14}}{8.632\times10^{-14}}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}/\mathrm{s^{-2}}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Tides go as mass over distance cubed, not squared, because the uniform \(\ell = 1\) part of the field falls with the Earth and only its variation across the Earth remains.

1. \(GM_{\mathrm{Moon}}/d^3 = 8.632\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\) and \(GM_\odot/d_\odot^3 = 3.964\times10^{-14}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\): the Sun's tide is \(0.459\) of the Moon's, although the Sun's pull on the Earth is \(179\) times the Moon's.

2. Take a point on the equator with both bodies in the equatorial plane, and \(\alpha\) the angle from the point to a body. Then \(P_2(\cos\alpha) = \tfrac12(3\cos^2\alpha - 1) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac34\cos2\alpha\): as the Earth turns, each body contributes a wave in \(2\alpha\), twice a day, of amplitude proportional to its tidal strength. At new or full Moon the two bodies are at \(\alpha\) differing by \(0\) or \(\pi\), so the waves are in phase and add: \(1 + 0.459\). At quarter Moon they differ by \(\pi/2\), so the waves differ in phase by \(\pi\) and subtract: \(1 - 0.459\). The ratio of ranges is \(1.459/0.541 = 2.70\).

3. Consecutive terms carry an extra \(r'/d\) (times a ratio of \(P_\ell\) values of order one): \(R/d_{\mathrm{Moon}} = 0.0166\) and \(R/d_\odot = 4.3\times10^{-5}\). The lunar \(\ell = 3\) tide is a percent-level correction; the solar one is negligible.

4. On the undisturbed surface \(\Phi_\oplus = -GM_\oplus/R\). Raising the surface by \(h\) changes \(\Phi_\oplus\) by \(gh\), \(g = GM_\oplus/R^2\). A level surface of \(\Phi_\oplus + \Phi^{(2)}\) therefore has \(gh + \Phi^{(2)}(R) = \text{const}\), and choosing the constant so that \(h\) has zero average over the sphere,

\[ h = -\frac{\Phi^{(2)}}{g} = \frac{GM_{\mathrm b}R^2/d^3}{GM_\oplus/R^2}P_2(\cos\psi) = \frac{M_{\mathrm b}}{M_\oplus}\Big(\frac Rd\Big)^3R\,P_2(\cos\psi). \]

For the Moon the prefactor is \(\frac{4.9029\times10^{12}}{3.9860\times10^{14}}(0.016592)^3(6.378\times10^6\ \mathrm m) = 0.358\) m; with \(P_2\) ranging from \(1\) to \(-\tfrac12\), the range is \(1.5\times0.358 = 0.54\) m.

Check. Dimensions: \((M_{\mathrm b}/M_\oplus)(R/d)^3\) is dimensionless, so \(h\) is a length ✓. Limit \(d\to\infty\): \(h\to0\) ✓. The number: real open-ocean tides are of order half a metre to a metre, with larger values at coasts where resonance amplifies them ✓. The bulge is at \(\psi = 0\) and \(\psi = \pi\), because \(P_2\) is even — the deck's two bulges again.
