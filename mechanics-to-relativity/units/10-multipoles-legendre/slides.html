Real planets are not points: multipoles, Legendre, and secular perturbation

One family of polynomials, one expansion of 1/|r − r′|, and the first perturbation calculation in the course — which predicts the inclination of a real satellite.

needs 05, 08~2½ h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./mr problems 10

Unit 05 gave a ball its field, and unit 08 gave a point mass its orbits. Between them sits the fact that neither idealisation is true: the Earth is flattened by $1$ part in $298$, and the orbit of anything close to it is not a closed ellipse. This unit is the repair, and the repair needs two pieces of mathematics that arrive together.

The first is the Legendre polynomials. They are not introduced as "solutions of Legendre's equation" — that is a theorem about them, proved in section 1. They are introduced as the Taylor coefficients of one explicit function, $(1 - 2xt + t^2)^{-1/2}$, because that function is $r/|\vec r - \vec r\,{}'|$ in disguise, and everything else about them falls out of that.

The second is perturbation theory for ODEs: osculating elements, and averaging. It is the idea that unit 21 develops properly with asymptotic series, and that unit 37 spends on Mercury. Here it is done once, concretely, on one problem whose answer is checkable against a satellite catalogue.

One flattened planet, five sections

The Earth is not a ball. It is flattened by $f = 1/298.257$, and the departure of its potential from $-GM/r$ is one part in a thousand.

Ignorable? No: it turns a satellite's orbital plane through $360^\circ$ a year. Small and persistent beats large and oscillating.

The plan. 1 the generating function and the $P_n$ · 2 the multipole expansion, and $J_2$ · 3 orbital elements, allowed to vary · 4 the precession, and the inclination · 5 tides as the $\ell = 2$ term, and the Moon's locked face.

from the bulge to the inclination · four steps

1. The bulge adds to $-GM/r$ a term $\Phi_2 = \frac{GMJ_2R^2}{r^3}P_2(\cos\theta)$: $R$ the equatorial radius, $\theta$ the angle from the pole, $P_2(x) = \tfrac12(3x^2-1)$.

2. Its force pulls an orbiting satellite towards the equatorial plane, so it exerts a torque that tips the orbit's angular momentum $\vec L$ sideways.

3. So $\vec L$, and with it the orbital plane, turns about the polar axis: $\Omega$, the longitude at which the orbit crosses the equator northwards, drifts.

4. Averaging over one orbit — semi-major axis $a$, eccentricity $e$, tilt $i$ to the equator, mean angular rate $n$ — keeps the drift and discards the wobble:

$\dot\Omega = -\tfrac32 J_2 (R/a)^2\, n\cos i/(1-e^2)^2$.

One turn a year at $800$ km asks for $\cos i = -0.1496$: $i = 98.60^\circ$.

Where each step is done. Step 1 is sections 1 and 2: the $P_n$ are built from a generating function, the potential is expanded in them on The expansion, and the first three terms, and An axially symmetric body: the $J_n$ isolates the $J_2$ term. Step 2 is The perturbing acceleration (the gradient, with its pull towards the equator drawn on the slide after it) and The torque, and what it moves, which uses section 3's lemma $\dot{\vec L} = \vec r\times\vec a_{\mathrm p}$. Step 3 is The torque, drawn: the torque lies along the node line, so it moves the tip of $\vec L$ round a cone about the axis. Step 4 is Averaging, and the result, and the number is The sun-synchronous inclination. Section 3 exists to make step 4 honest: it says what an orbit's "elements" are once they are allowed to move, and what averaging throws away.

The target is a single number and it is worth stating now, so that every step can be judged by whether it is getting closer to it. A satellite in a circular orbit $800\ \mathrm{km}$ up, in a plane inclined at $98.60^\circ$ to the equator, has an orbital plane that turns eastward at exactly $0.9856^\circ$ per day. That is $360^\circ$ in a year, which means the plane keeps a fixed angle to the direction of the Sun, which means the satellite crosses the equator at the same local solar time on every pass, for years. Every imaging and weather satellite that needs consistent lighting flies there. The number comes out of $J_2$ and nothing else.

Why "small and persistent beats large and oscillating" is the whole moral of sections 3 and 4: the $J_2$ perturbation makes the node wobble back and forth with amplitude $0.0055^\circ$, twice per orbit, and it also makes it drift by $0.069^\circ$ per orbit, always the same way. After a hundred orbits the wobble is still $0.0055^\circ$ and the drift is $6.9^\circ$. Averaging is the technique for throwing away the first and keeping the second, and the point of section 3 is to say precisely what is being thrown away.

10 · generating function

Where the series has to come from

The potential of an extended body is unit 05's integral

$$\Phi(\vec r) = -G\int_B \frac{\rho(\vec r\,{}')}{|\vec r - \vec r\,{}'|}\,\mathrm{d}^3r' ,$$

and for a body that is not a sphere, symmetry does not evaluate it. So expand instead. Write $r' = |\vec r\,{}'|$, $r = |\vec r\,|$, and let $\psi$ be the angle between them. The law of cosines gives

$$|\vec r - \vec r\,{}'|^2 = r^2 - 2rr'\cos\psi + r'^2 ,$$ $$\frac{1}{|\vec r - \vec r\,{}'|} = \frac1r\big(1 - 2xt + t^2\big)^{-1/2},$$

with $x = \cos\psi \in [-1,1]$ and $t = r'/r$. For a field point outside a ball about the origin that encloses the body, $t < 1$: a small parameter, handed to us by geometry.

$\vec r\,{}'$ runs over the body, $\vec r$ is fixed outside it. Only the ratio $t = r'/r$ and the angle $\psi$ appear.

Two things are worth noticing before any mathematics happens. First, the integrand depends on $\vec r$ and $\vec r\,{}'$ only through $r$, $r'$ and the angle between them — that is rotational invariance of the Newtonian kernel, and it is why a one-variable family of polynomials can carry the whole angular dependence.

Second, $t = r'/r$ is genuinely small in every application in this unit. For a satellite at $a = 7178\ \mathrm{km}$ and a source point inside the Earth, $t \le 6378/7178 = 0.889$; for the Moon seen from the Earth's interior, $t \le 6378/384400 = 0.0166$. The expansion converges in both cases, but it is only useful in the second: the first needs the $n$-th term to be suppressed by something other than $t^n$, and it is — by the smallness of the body's own asphericity, which is what $J_2 \sim 10^{-3}$ measures.

The law of cosines here is just $|\vec r - \vec r\,{}'|^2 = (\vec r - \vec r\,{}')\cdot(\vec r - \vec r\,{}') = r^2 - 2\vec r\cdot\vec r\,{}' + r'^2$ with $\vec r\cdot\vec r\,{}' = rr'\cos\psi$, which is unit 04's definition of the angle between two vectors, not a separate fact.

10 · generating function

The definition, and that it is legitimate

definition · the generating function

For $x \in [-1,1]$ and $|t| < 1$, set $\;g(x,t) = (1 - 2xt + t^2)^{-1/2}$, the positive square root. The Legendre polynomials $P_n$ are defined by

$$g(x,t) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} P_n(x)\,t^n .$$
lemma · the series exists

For $|t| < 1$ the series converges to $g$, and $|P_n(x)| \le 1$ on $[-1,1]$. The key is the factorisation, with $x = \cos\varphi$,

$1 - 2xt + t^2 = (1 - te^{i\varphi})(1 - te^{-i\varphi}).$

proof in the notes (N)

lemma · $P_n$ is a polynomial

$P_n$ is a polynomial of degree exactly $n$, with leading coefficient $\binom{2n}{n}2^{-n}$, and it has the parity of $n$: $P_n(-x) = (-1)^nP_n(x)$.

both proved in the notes (N)

A definition by generating function names $P_n$ as a Taylor coefficient, which always exists. The first lemma is what makes the series equal to $g$ out to $|t| < 1$, which the physics needs.

First, what the definition means. For real $t\in(-1,1)$ and $x\in[-1,1]$ the quadratic $1-2xt+t^2 \ge 1 - 2|t| + t^2 = (1-|t|)^2 > 0$, so $g$ is a smooth function of $t$ there, and $P_n(x) := \frac1{n!}\partial_t^ng(x,0)$ is its $n$-th Taylor coefficient. What has to be proved is that the Taylor series actually sums to $g$ on the whole interval $|t| < 1$ — the multipole expansion needs $t$ as large as $0.889$ — and that $|P_n| \le 1$.

Full proof: convergence on $|t| < 1$ and the bound

Let $c_k = \binom{2k}{k}4^{-k}$, the coefficients of the binomial series $(1-s)^{-1/2} = \sum_k c_ks^k$ for real $|s| < 1$ (single-variable real analysis). Squaring that series and comparing with $(1-s)^{-1} = \sum s^k$ gives the coefficient identity $\sum_{j+k=n}c_jc_k = 1$ for every $n$, since two power series that agree on an interval have equal coefficients.

Now let $w$ be complex with $|w| < 1$ — complex numbers purely as algebra, in unit 06's sense. The series $h(w) = \sum_kc_kw^k$ converges absolutely, because $\sum c_k|w|^k$ does. Absolutely convergent series may be multiplied term by term (Cauchy product), so the coefficient identity gives $h(w)^2 = \sum_n w^n = 1/(1-w)$.

Put $w = te^{i\varphi}$ with $t\in(-1,1)$. The $c_k$ are real, so $h(te^{-i\varphi}) = \overline{h(te^{i\varphi})}$ and the product $h(te^{i\varphi})h(te^{-i\varphi}) = |h(te^{i\varphi})|^2$ is real and $\ge 0$. Its square is $\frac{1}{(1-te^{i\varphi})(1-te^{-i\varphi})} = \frac1{1-2t\cos\varphi+t^2}$, by the factorisation, which is checked by multiplying out: $1 - t(e^{i\varphi}+e^{-i\varphi}) + t^2 = 1 - 2t\cos\varphi + t^2$. A non-negative real number whose square is $1/(1-2xt+t^2)$ is $g(x,t)$. So, multiplying the two absolutely convergent series,

$g(\cos\varphi,t) = \sum_n t^n\,p_n(\varphi),\qquad p_n(\varphi) = \sum_{j+k=n}c_jc_k\,e^{i(j-k)\varphi} = \sum_{j+k=n}c_jc_k\cos\big((j-k)\varphi\big)$,

valid for every $|t| < 1$. A convergent power series is the Taylor series of its sum, so $p_n(\varphi) = P_n(\cos\varphi)$. That proves convergence on the whole interval, and it proves the bound at once: $|P_n(\cos\varphi)| \le \sum_{j+k=n}c_jc_k = 1$.

Full proof: degree and parity

The binomial series $(1-u)^{-1/2} = \sum_{k\ge0}c_ku^k$ with $c_k = \binom{2k}{k}4^{-k} > 0$ converges for $|u| < 1$. Put $u = t(2x - t)$, which is small when $t$ is: $$g = \sum_k c_k\,t^k(2x-t)^k = \sum_k c_k \sum_{j=0}^k \binom kj (2x)^{k-j}(-1)^j t^{k+j}.$$ The term $t^{k+j}x^{k-j}$ contributes to $t^n$ when $k+j = n$, and then the power of $x$ is $k - j = n - 2j$. So $P_n$ is a sum of powers $x^{n}, x^{n-2},\dots$, which gives both the degree bound and the parity. The top power comes from $j = 0$, $k = n$, with coefficient $c_n2^n = \binom{2n}{n}2^{-n} \ne 0$, so the degree is exactly $n$.

The rearrangement of the double sum is legitimate because for $|t|$ small enough all the terms are dominated by $\sum_k c_k(|t|(2+|t|))^k$, which converges — absolute convergence permits reordering.

The bound $|P_n| \le 1$ is what makes term-by-term integration of the multipole series legitimate in section 2: it turns every series in $P_n(x)t^n$ into one dominated by the geometric series $\sum|t|^n$, whatever $x$ is.

Everything from one derivative

Bonnet's recurrence

$(n+1)P_{n+1}(x) = (2n+1)\,x\,P_n(x) - n\,P_{n-1}(x)$, with $P_0 = 1$, $P_1 = x$.

proof

$\partial_t g = (x-t)(1-2xt+t^2)^{-3/2}$, so $(1-2xt+t^2)\,\partial_t g = (x-t)\,g$. Put in the series and read off the coefficient of $t^n$:

$(n{+}1)P_{n+1} - 2xnP_n + (n{-}1)P_{n-1} = xP_n - P_{n-1}$,

which rearranges to the claim. $\square$

two values, free

$x = 1$: $\;g = (1-t)^{-1} = \sum t^n$, so $P_n(1) = 1$ for every $n$.

$x = -1$: $\;g = (1+t)^{-1}$, so $P_n(-1) = (-1)^n$.

These fix the normalisation once and for all, and they are the reason $P_n(1) = 1$ rather than some other convention.

$P_0$ and $P_1$ come from expanding $g$ by hand to first order; the recurrence supplies the rest with no further analysis.

The coefficient extraction, in full. $\partial_t g = \sum_n nP_nt^{n-1}$, so

$(1-2xt+t^2)\sum_n nP_nt^{n-1} = \sum_n nP_nt^{n-1} - 2x\sum_n nP_nt^{n} + \sum_n nP_nt^{n+1}$,

whose $t^n$ coefficient is $(n+1)P_{n+1} - 2xnP_n + (n-1)P_{n-1}$. On the right, $(x-t)\sum_nP_nt^n$ has $t^n$ coefficient $xP_n - P_{n-1}$. Equate, and collect the $xP_n$ terms: $(n+1)P_{n+1} = (2n+1)xP_n - nP_{n-1}$.

Getting $P_0$ and $P_1$. $(1-2xt+t^2)^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12(2xt - t^2) + O(t^2) = 1 + xt + O(t^2)$, so $P_0 = 1$ and $P_1 = x$. The recurrence at $n=1$ then gives $2P_2 = 3x\cdot x - 1$, and so on.

Recurrences of this shape — three consecutive members, coefficients linear in the index — are not an accident of this family. Unit 19 will show that every family of polynomials orthogonal with respect to a weight on an interval satisfies one, for a reason with one line of proof: $xP_n$ has degree $n+1$, so it is a combination of $P_0,\dots,P_{n+1}$, and orthogonality kills all but three of the coefficients.

The first five, and three checks

worked example 1 · running the recurrence

$P_0 = 1$, $P_1 = x$. Then, term by term:

$n=1$: $2P_2 = 3x\,P_1 - 1\,P_0 = 3x^2 - 1$, so $P_2 = \tfrac12(3x^2-1)$.

$n=2$: $3P_3 = 5x\,P_2 - 2P_1 = \tfrac52(3x^3 - x) - 2x = \tfrac{15x^3 - 5x - 4x}{2}$, so $P_3 = \tfrac12(5x^3 - 3x)$.

$n=3$: $4P_4 = 7x\,P_3 - 3P_2 = \tfrac72(5x^4-3x^2) - \tfrac32(3x^2-1) = \tfrac{35x^4 - 21x^2 - 9x^2 + 3}{2}$, so $P_4 = \tfrac18(35x^4 - 30x^2 + 3)$.

three checks

Value at 1. $P_2(1) = \tfrac12(3-1) = 1$; $P_3(1) = \tfrac12(5-3)=1$; $P_4(1) = \tfrac18(35-30+3) = 1$.

Parity. $P_2, P_4$ even; $P_1, P_3$ odd — as the lemma requires, and so $P_3(0) = 0$, $P_2(0) = -\tfrac12$, $P_4(0) = \tfrac38$.

Leading coefficient. $\binom{2n}{n}2^{-n}$ gives $6/4 = \tfrac32$ for $n=2$, $20/8 = \tfrac52$ for $n=3$, $70/16 = \tfrac{35}{8}$ for $n=4$. All three match.

Worth doing by hand once: get $P_2$ a second way, by expanding the generating function directly to order $t^2$. With $u = t(2x-t)$, $(1-u)^{-1/2} = 1 + \tfrac12u + \tfrac38u^2 + O(u^3)$, and $u = 2xt - t^2$, $u^2 = 4x^2t^2 + O(t^3)$. So $g = 1 + xt - \tfrac12t^2 + \tfrac32x^2t^2 + O(t^3)$, and the coefficient of $t^2$ is $\tfrac12(3x^2-1)$. It agrees, as it must.

A numerical sanity check of the whole construction: at $x = \tfrac12$ the first six values are $1, \tfrac12, -\tfrac18, -\tfrac7{16}, -\tfrac{37}{128}, \tfrac{23}{256}$, and $\sum P_n(\tfrac12)t^n$ at $t = \tfrac12$ should equal $(1 - 2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac14)^{-1/2} = (3/4)^{-1/2} = 1.1547$. The six terms give $1 + 0.25 - 0.03125 - 0.05469 - 0.01807 + 0.00281 = 1.1488$, and the tail is still arriving: the ratio $t = \tfrac12$ makes convergence unhurried.

Legendre's equation

theorem

$P_n$ satisfies Legendre's equation

$$\big[(1-x^2)\,y'\big]' + n(n+1)\,y = 0,$$

equivalently $(1-x^2)y'' - 2xy' + n(n+1)y = 0$, and up to a scalar it is the only polynomial solution.

proof

Existence. Differentiating $g$ directly (notes) gives the identity $(1-x^2)\,\partial_x^2g - 2x\,\partial_xg + t\,\partial_t^2(tg) = 0$. The $t^n$ coefficient of $t\,\partial_t^2(t\cdot t^n)$ is $n(n+1)$, so the $t^n$ coefficient of the identity is Legendre's equation for $P_n$.

Uniqueness. If a polynomial solution has degree $m$, its $x^m$ coefficient forces $m(m+1) = n(n+1)$, so $m = n$; two such differ by a multiple that has degree $< n$, hence is $0$. $\square$

the other solution is not polynomial

On $(-1,1)$ the solution space is two-dimensional (unit 06's argument; notes). The second solution $Q_n$ blows up logarithmically at $x = \pm1$ — at the poles of the sphere. Physics discards it by regularity, not by fiat.

$Q_0(x) = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}$ is the whole story in miniature.

The form $[(1-x^2)y']' = -\lambda y$ is what makes orthogonality a two-line computation, and it is the course's first Sturm–Liouville problem.

Full proof of the identity for $g$

Write $s = 1 - 2xt + t^2$, so $g = s^{-1/2}$, $\partial_xs = -2t$, $\partial_ts = 2(t-x)$. Then

$\partial_xg = t\,s^{-3/2}$, $\quad\partial_x^2g = 3t^2s^{-5/2}$, $\quad\partial_tg = (x-t)s^{-3/2}$, $\quad\partial_t^2g = -s^{-3/2} + 3(x-t)^2s^{-5/2}$,

and $\partial_t^2(tg) = 2\partial_tg + t\,\partial_t^2g$. Multiply the whole left-hand side of the identity by $s^{5/2}$:

$3t^2(1-x^2) - 2xt\,s + 2t(x-t)\,s - t^2s + 3t^2(x-t)^2 = 3t^2\big[1 - x^2 + (x-t)^2\big] - 3t^2s$.

The bracket is $1 - 2xt + t^2 = s$, so the total is $0$. The identity is a smooth function of $(x,t)$ that vanishes identically, so all its $t$-derivatives at $t = 0$ vanish. Since $P_n = \frac1{n!}\partial_t^ng|_{t=0}$ and $g$ is smooth, $x$- and $t$-derivatives commute (Clairaut, unit 03), so the $n$-th Taylor coefficient of $\partial_x^2g$ is $P_n''$ and that of $\partial_xg$ is $P_n'$; and the $n$-th coefficient of $t\,\partial_t^2(tg)$ is $n(n+1)P_n$, because $t\,\partial_t^2(t^{n+1}) = (n+1)n\,t^n$.

The polynomial solutions, found directly

Try $y = \sum_ka_kx^k$. The coefficient of $x^k$ in $(1-x^2)y'' - 2xy' + n(n+1)y$ is $(k+2)(k+1)a_{k+2} - k(k-1)a_k - 2ka_k + n(n+1)a_k$, so

$a_{k+2} = \dfrac{k(k+1) - n(n+1)}{(k+1)(k+2)}\,a_k .$

Even and odd coefficients decouple, which is the parity again. The numerator vanishes at $k = n$, so the series with the parity of $n$ stops at $x^n$: that is $P_n$, up to the scalar fixed by $P_n(1) = 1$. The other parity never stops (its numerators are never zero), and $a_{k+2}/a_k \to 1$, so it converges for $|x| < 1$ only; that it genuinely diverges at $x = \pm1$ is the logarithm below.

Why $Q_n$ is singular at the ends

First, why there are exactly two independent solutions. Unit 06 proved that the solutions of a constant-coefficient second-order linear equation form a two-dimensional space, using only Picard–Lindelöf (unit 01): the map from a solution to its data $(y(x_0), y'(x_0))$ is linear, onto by existence and injective by uniqueness. On any closed subinterval of $(-1,1)$ Legendre's equation, divided by $1-x^2$, has continuous coefficients, so the right-hand side is Lipschitz in $(y,y')$ and the same argument applies word for word.

For two solutions $y_1, y_2$ of $[(1-x^2)y']' + \lambda y = 0$, the Wronskian $W = y_1y_2' - y_2y_1'$ satisfies $\big[(1-x^2)W\big]' = y_1[(1-x^2)y_2']' - y_2[(1-x^2)y_1']' = -\lambda y_1y_2 + \lambda y_2y_1 = 0$ — Abel's identity from unit 06. So $W = C/(1-x^2)$, and $C \ne 0$ for an independent pair. Since $(y_2/y_1)' = W/y_1^2$, with $y_1 = P_n$ one gets $Q_n = P_n\int^x \frac{C\,\mathrm ds}{(1-s^2)P_n(s)^2}$ on any interval where $P_n \ne 0$. Near $s = 1$ the integrand behaves like $C/(2(1-s))$ since $P_n(1) = 1$, so the integral diverges like $-\tfrac C2\ln(1-x)$. For $n = 0$ this is exactly $Q_0 = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1+x}{1-x}$.

This is where "regularity at the poles" enters: the potential of a bounded body is finite everywhere off the body, in particular on the axis, so $Q_n$ cannot appear. Unit 19 meets the same argument for the general Sturm–Liouville problem, where the singular endpoints are what quantise $\lambda$ to $n(n+1)$.

Rodrigues' formula

theorem (Rodrigues' formula) $$P_n(x) = \frac{1}{2^n\,n!}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}^n}{\mathrm{d}x^n}\big(x^2-1\big)^n .$$

Call the right-hand side $w_n$. It is a polynomial of degree $n$ (differentiate a degree-$2n$ polynomial $n$ times), and two facts finish the proof:

  • $w_n$ solves Legendre's equation — Leibniz's rule, in the notes;
  • $w_n(1) = 1$ — alongside.

By the uniqueness half of the previous theorem $w_n = cP_n$, and evaluating at $x = 1$ gives $c = 1$.

proof of $w_n(1) = 1$

$(x^2-1)^n = (x-1)^n(x+1)^n$. Differentiating $n$ times by Leibniz, every term keeps a factor of $(x-1)$ except the one where all $n$ derivatives fall on $(x-1)^n$. At $x = 1$ only that term survives:

$\dfrac{\mathrm{d}^n}{\mathrm{d}x^n}(x^2-1)^n\Big|_{x=1} = n!\,(x+1)^n\big|_{x=1} = n!\,2^n$. $\square$

Full proof that $v^{(n)}$ solves Legendre's equation, $v = (x^2-1)^n$

$v' = 2nx(x^2-1)^{n-1}$, so $(x^2-1)\,v' = 2nx\,v$. Differentiate both sides $n+1$ times by Leibniz. On the left only three terms survive, because $(x^2-1)$ has vanishing third derivative:

$(x^2-1)v^{(n+2)} + (n+1)(2x)v^{(n+1)} + \tbinom{n+1}{2}\,2\,v^{(n)}$,

and on the right only two: $2n\big[xv^{(n+1)} + (n+1)v^{(n)}\big]$. Write $w = v^{(n)}$ and subtract:

$(x^2-1)w'' + 2(n+1)xw' + n(n+1)w - 2nxw' - 2n(n+1)w = 0$,

that is $(x^2-1)w'' + 2xw' - n(n+1)w = 0$, which is Legendre's equation times $-1$. Nothing but Leibniz's rule has been used. Check at $n = 2$: $\frac{1}{8}\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm dx^2}(x^4 - 2x^2 + 1) = \frac18(12x^2 - 4) = \tfrac12(3x^2-1)$, which is $P_2$.

Why the Leibniz argument for $w_n(1)$ works. $\frac{\mathrm d^n}{\mathrm dx^n}(fh) = \sum_k\binom nk f^{(k)}h^{(n-k)}$. With $f = (x-1)^n$, $f^{(k)}$ is a multiple of $(x-1)^{n-k}$, which vanishes at $x = 1$ unless $k = n$. The surviving term is $f^{(n)}h = n!\,(x+1)^n$.

Rodrigues' formula is the cheapest route to several things that are awkward from the generating function: an orthogonality proof by repeated integration by parts (problem P3), and the explicit coefficient formula. It is also the form that generalises: Hermite, Laguerre and Chebyshev polynomials each have one, with $(x^2-1)^n$ replaced by the appropriate weight.

Olinde Rodrigues published it in 1816; Ivory and Jacobi each rediscovered it within the next dozen years. Legendre had introduced the polynomials in 1782, in exactly the problem of this unit: the attraction of a spheroid.

Orthogonality, and the norm

theorem $$\int_{-1}^{1}P_m(x)P_n(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{2}{2n+1}\,\delta_{mn}.$$
proof of orthogonality

Using the self-adjoint form and integrating by parts twice,

$\displaystyle\big[n(n{+}1) - m(m{+}1)\big]\!\!\int_{-1}^{1}\!\!P_mP_n = \Big[(1-x^2)\big(P_mP_n' - P_nP_m'\big)\Big]_{-1}^{1} = 0,$

because $(1-x^2)$ vanishes at both endpoints. For $m \ne n$ the bracket on the left is nonzero, so the integral is zero. $\square$

proof of the norm

Square the generating function and integrate term by term:

$\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1 g^2\,\mathrm dx = \sum_{m,n}t^{m+n}\!\!\int_{-1}^1\!\!P_mP_n = \sum_n t^{2n}\!\!\int_{-1}^1\!\!P_n^2 .$

But the left side is elementary:

$\displaystyle\int_{-1}^1\frac{\mathrm dx}{1-2xt+t^2} = \frac1t\ln\frac{1+t}{1-t} = 2\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{t^{2n}}{2n+1}.$

Match coefficients. $\square$

The $P_n$ are therefore an orthogonal — not orthonormal — family in $L^2[-1,1]$. That they are complete is unit 19's theorem, and it is not needed here.

The two integrations by parts, written out. $\int_{-1}^1 P_n\big[(1-x^2)P_m'\big]'\,\mathrm dx = \big[P_n(1-x^2)P_m'\big]_{-1}^1 - \int_{-1}^1(1-x^2)P_m'P_n'\,\mathrm dx$. The boundary term vanishes. The remaining integral is symmetric in $m$ and $n$, so subtracting the same identity with $m$ and $n$ swapped gives $\int P_n[(1-x^2)P_m']' - \int P_m[(1-x^2)P_n']' = 0$. Substituting the equation turns this into $[-m(m+1) + n(n+1)]\int P_mP_n = 0$.

The elementary integral. Substitute $s = 1-2xt+t^2$, $\mathrm ds = -2t\,\mathrm dx$: $\int_{-1}^1\frac{\mathrm dx}{1-2xt+t^2} = -\frac1{2t}\big[\ln s\big]_{s=(1+t)^2}^{s=(1-t)^2} = \frac1{2t}\ln\frac{(1+t)^2}{(1-t)^2} = \frac1t\ln\frac{1+t}{1-t}$. And $\ln\frac{1+t}{1-t} = 2(t + t^3/3 + t^5/5 + \cdots)$, so dividing by $t$ gives $2\sum t^{2n}/(2n+1)$. Comparing with $\sum t^{2n}\int P_n^2$ gives the norm.

Term-by-term integration of $g^2$ is justified by uniform convergence of $\sum P_n(x)t^n$ on $x\in[-1,1]$ for fixed $|t|<1$: the bound $|P_n| \le 1$ makes $\sum|P_n(x)t^n| \le \sum|t|^n$, a convergent geometric series independent of $x$, so the Weierstrass M-test applies.

Orthogonality is what makes the coefficients of a multipole expansion measurable: $J_n$ can be extracted from a measured field by integrating against $P_n$ over the sphere, one coefficient at a time, with no interference from the others. That is how the $J_n$ of the Earth are determined from satellite tracking.

What a body's field looks like from far away — and what each term means without reference to any coordinate system.

10 · multipoles

The expansion, and the first three terms

theorem · the multipole expansion

For a bounded body $B$ contained in the ball $r' \le b$, and any $r > b$,

$$\Phi(\vec r) = -\frac{G}{r}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{n}}\int_B \rho(\vec r\,{}')\,r'^{\,n}P_n(\cos\psi)\,\mathrm{d}^3r' ,$$

with $\psi$ the angle between $\vec r$ and $\vec r\,{}'$. The series converges absolutely, and term-by-term integration is legitimate.

$n = 0$ · monopole

$-GM/r$, with $M = \int_B\rho$. What unit 08 used.

$n = 1$ · dipole

$-G(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\vec d\,)/r^2$ with $\vec d = \int_B\rho\,\vec r\,{}' = M\vec R_{\mathrm{cm}}$.

$n = 2$ · quadrupole

$-G\,Q(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r})/2r^3$ with $Q(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r}) = \int_B\rho\,\big[3(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\vec r\,{}')^2 - r'^2\big]$ (next slide). The first term that sees the body's shape.

Proof. Substitute the generating function identity of section 1 with $t = r'/r \le b/r < 1$ and $x = \cos\psi$ into Newton's integral. Interchanging sum and integral is justified by the bound $|P_n|\le1$ proved there: for fixed $\vec r$, the $n$-th term of the integrand is at most $\rho(\vec r\,{}')(b/r)^n/r$ in modulus at every source point (the Weierstrass M-test bound), so the tail after $N$ terms integrates to at most $\frac Mr(b/r)^N/(1 - b/r)$, which tends to $0$: the integral of the sum is the sum of the integrals. The same bound shows the series of integrals converges absolutely, geometrically in $b/r$.

The dipole term. $r'\cos\psi = \hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\vec r\,{}'$, so $\int\rho\,r'P_1(\cos\psi) = \hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\int\rho\,\vec r\,{}' = \hat{\mathbf r}\cdot M\vec R_{\mathrm{cm}}$, where $\vec R_{\mathrm{cm}}$ is the centre of mass. Choosing the origin at the centre of mass kills this term exactly, and that is what everybody does. Note what this means physically: to the accuracy of the first two terms, a body attracts as a point mass at its centre of mass. The quadrupole is the first correction, and there is no way to make it go away by moving the origin.

The quadrupole term. $r'^2P_2(\cos\psi) = \tfrac12\big(3(\hat{\mathbf r}\cdot\vec r\,{}')^2 - r'^2\big)$, which is $\tfrac12 Q(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r})$ for the bilinear form defined on the next slide. The factor of $\tfrac12$ is carried by the definition of $P_2$, and the various conventions in the literature differ exactly by where that $\tfrac12$ is put — check it before using anyone's formula.

Why the expansion is useful and not merely true: each term falls off one power of $r$ faster than the last, so at large distances the series is also an asymptotic ordering by importance. Unit 21 gives the language for that distinction; here the convergent series and the asymptotic ordering happen to coincide.

10 · multipoles

What the quadrupole is

definition · the quadrupole moment

The map $Q : \mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R^3 \to \mathbb R$,

$$Q(\vec u, \vec v) = \int_B \rho(\vec r\,{}')\Big[3(\vec u\cdot\vec r\,{}')(\vec v\cdot\vec r\,{}') - r'^2\,(\vec u\cdot\vec v)\Big]\mathrm{d}^3r' ,$$

a symmetric bilinear form on $\mathbb R^3$. Bilinear and symmetric by inspection; trace-free because $\sum_iQ(\hat e_i,\hat e_i) = \int\rho(3r'^2 - 3r'^2) = 0$.

theorem · the components transform

In an orthonormal basis, $Q_{ij} = Q(\hat e_i,\hat e_j) = \int\rho\,(3x_i'x_j' - r'^2\delta_{ij})$. Under a change of orthonormal basis with matrix $R$, $\;Q' = R^{\!\top}QR$.

That is the change-of-basis rule for a bilinear form, proved in linear algebra. It is a theorem about the components, not the definition of $Q$.

Being real symmetric, $Q$ is diagonalised by an orthonormal basis: the body's principal axes. Its three eigenvalues sum to zero.

in terms of moments of inertia

With $I_{ij} = \int\rho(r'^2\delta_{ij} - x_i'x_j')$ and principal moments $A \le B \le C$,

$Q = -3I + (\operatorname{tr}I)\,\mathbb 1$, so $\;Q_{11} = B+C-2A$, $Q_{22} = C+A-2B$, $Q_{33} = A+B-2C$.

This is the habit the whole course keeps, and it is worth seeing it early on an object simple enough to check by hand. The quadrupole moment is a multilinear map — here, a symmetric bilinear form on $\mathbb R^3$, an element of $\mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb R^3)^*$. Its matrix $Q_{ij}$ is what you get after choosing a basis, and the rule $Q' = R^\top QR$ is what bilinearity says happens to that matrix when the basis changes. Many books define the quadrupole moment by that rule, which inverts the logic: it makes a computation into a definition and leaves "what is it" unanswered. Unit 16 gives the general account, of which this is the case $(p,q) = (0,2)$ on $\mathbb R^3$.

The relation to $I$, checked. $-3I_{ij} + (\operatorname{tr}I)\delta_{ij} = \int\rho\big[-3r'^2\delta_{ij} + 3x_i'x_j'\big] + \delta_{ij}\int\rho\,2r'^2 = \int\rho\big[3x_i'x_j' - r'^2\delta_{ij}\big] = Q_{ij}$, using $\operatorname{tr}I = \int\rho(3r'^2 - r'^2) = 2\int\rho r'^2$. In principal axes, $I = \operatorname{diag}(A,B,C)$ and $\operatorname{tr}I = A+B+C$, giving the three entries on the slide. Their sum is zero, as it must be.

$I_{ij}$ here is only an integral over the body; no rigid-body dynamics is being used or implied. Unit 16 will show it is the inertia tensor and that $\vec L = I\vec\omega$, which is not needed until then.

Origin dependence. $\vec d$ and $Q$ both depend on where the origin is put. Moving the origin by $\vec c$ changes $\vec d$ to $\vec d - M\vec c$ and changes $Q$ by terms involving $\vec d$ and $\vec c$. If $\vec d = 0$ — origin at the centre of mass — the change in $Q$ is the parallel-axis shift $+M(3c_ic_j - c^2\delta_{ij})$, so $Q$ is origin-independent only for a body of zero total mass and zero dipole. The lowest non-vanishing moment is always the origin-independent one. Problem P5 makes this precise.

An axially symmetric body: the $J_n$

Take the $z$-axis along the symmetry axis and the origin at the centre of mass, and let $\theta$ be the colatitude of the field point. Then $A = B$, so $Q = (C-A)\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-2)$, and

$$Q(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r}) = (C-A)(1 - 3\cos^2\theta) = -2(C-A)P_2(\cos\theta).$$
so the quadrupole term is $$\Phi_2 = -\frac{G\,Q(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r})}{2r^3} = \frac{G(C-A)}{r^3}P_2(\cos\theta) ,$$

and writing it in the standard form $\Phi_2 = \dfrac{GM}{r}J_2\Big(\dfrac Rr\Big)^{2}P_2(\cos\theta)$ defines

$$\boxed{\;J_2 = \frac{C - A}{MR^2}\;}$$
the zonal harmonic coefficients

$\displaystyle\Phi(r,\theta) = -\frac{GM}{r}\Big[1 - \sum_{n\ge2}J_n\Big(\frac Rr\Big)^{n}P_n(\cos\theta)\Big]$

with $R$ any agreed reference radius — for the Earth, the equatorial radius.

That the $n$-th term of an axially symmetric body's expansion is a multiple of $P_n(\cos\theta)$ for every $n$ is cited: it follows from the addition theorem for Legendre polynomials, proved with the spherical harmonics of unit 19. Only $n = 2$ is derived here, and only $n = 2$ is used.

The trigonometric step. $\hat{\mathbf r} = (\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \cos\theta)$, and $Q = (C-A)\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-2)$, so $Q(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r}) = (C-A)(\sin^2\theta\cos^2\phi + \sin^2\theta\sin^2\phi - 2\cos^2\theta) = (C-A)(\sin^2\theta - 2\cos^2\theta)$. Then $\sin^2\theta - 2\cos^2\theta = 1 - 3\cos^2\theta = -2P_2(\cos\theta)$. The $\phi$-dependence cancels, which is the axial symmetry showing up.

Dimensions. $[C - A] = \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, $[MR^2] = \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$, so $J_2$ is dimensionless, as a coefficient in a bracket beside $1$ must be. And $[G(C-A)/r^3] = \mathrm{m^3kg^{-1}s^{-2}\cdot kg\,m^2/m^3} = \mathrm{m^2s^{-2}}$, the dimensions of a potential.

The Earth's numbers. $J_2 = 1.08263\times10^{-3}$, so $C - A = J_2MR^2 = 2.63\times10^{35}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}$. With $C \approx 0.3307\,MR^2$ that is $(C-A)/C = 3.27\times10^{-3}$: the Earth's polar and equatorial moments of inertia differ by a third of a percent. $J_3 = -2.53\times10^{-6}$ and $J_4 = -1.62\times10^{-6}$ are smaller by factors of $430$ and $670$, which is why this unit can stop at $n = 2$ and still predict an inclination to a tenth of a degree.

$\Phi_2 = \frac{GMJ_2R^2}{r^3}P_2(\cos\theta)$ carries this pattern at every distance, shrinking like $r^{-3}$.

With $J_2 > 0$, the correction is positive over the poles: the potential there is raised, the well shallower than a sphere's. It is negative over the equator, half as large: the well is deeper where the bulge is.

A satellite on a tilted orbit passes alternately over both regions. That alternation is what section 4 turns into a torque.

Sign convention: the minus inside the bracket of the $J_n$ expansion makes $J_2 > 0$ for an oblate body. Check it before trusting a formula.

Signs, twice over. $\Phi_2 = -GQ(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf r})/2r^3 = -G\cdot(-2(C-A)P_2)/2r^3 = +G(C-A)P_2/r^3$. Matching to $+\frac{GM}{r}J_2(R/r)^2P_2$ gives $GMJ_2R^2 = G(C-A)$, hence $J_2 = (C-A)/MR^2$. For an oblate body $C > A$ and $J_2 > 0$; for a prolate one $J_2 < 0$. At the pole $P_2 = 1$ and $\Phi_2 > 0$: at a given $r$ the potential is raised over the pole relative to a sphere of the same mass, and lowered over the equator, where $P_2 = -\tfrac12$. That is right: the bulge's extra mass lies near the equatorial plane, so a point over the equator is nearer to it than a point over the pole at the same distance from the centre.

The quadrupole of a flattened planet

worked example 2 · a uniform oblate spheroid

Semi-axes $R$ (equatorial) and $c = R(1-f)$ (polar), uniform density, mass $M$. Its principal moments are $C = \tfrac25MR^2$ and $A = \tfrac15M(R^2+c^2)$, so

$C - A = \tfrac15M(R^2 - c^2)$, and

$J_2 = \dfrac{C-A}{MR^2} = \dfrac{R^2 - c^2}{5R^2} = \dfrac{1 - (1-f)^2}{5} = \dfrac{2f - f^2}{5}.$

For the Earth's $f = 1/298.257$: $\;J_2^{\text{unif}} = 1.3389\times10^{-3}$.

that is 23.7% too big

The measured value is $1.08263\times10^{-3}$. The model is not wrong about the shape — $f$ was measured — so it is wrong about the interior.

A uniform body puts as much mass in the bulge as anywhere. The real Earth is centrally condensed: a dense core contributes to $M$ but almost nothing to $C - A$. Concentrating mass towards the centre lowers $J_2$ at fixed $f$, and $23.7\%$ is a measure of how much.

Check the same ratio through $C$: for a uniform ball $C = 0.4MR^2$, and for the Earth $C = 0.3307MR^2$ — smaller, in the same direction and by a comparable amount.

Where the moments come from. For a uniform solid ellipsoid with semi-axes $(a_1,a_2,a_3)$, $I_{11} = \tfrac15M(a_2^2+a_3^2)$ and cyclically. This follows from the linear change of variables that carries the unit ball onto the ellipsoid, which is unit 05's Jacobian argument: $\int_E x_2^2 = a_1a_2a_3\cdot a_2^2\int_{B_1}u_2^2$ and $\int_{B_1}u_2^2 = \tfrac13\int_{B_1}r^2 = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac{4\pi}{5} = \tfrac{4\pi}{15}$. Problem P6 asks for it in full.

With $a_1 = a_2 = R$ and $a_3 = c$: $C = I_{33} = \tfrac25MR^2$, $A = I_{11} = \tfrac15M(R^2+c^2)$, and $C - A = \tfrac15M(2R^2 - R^2 - c^2) = \tfrac15M(R^2-c^2)$.

Dimension check. $J_2$ came out as a ratio of squared lengths: dimensionless. Limit check. $f \to 0$ gives $J_2 \to 0$ — a sphere has no quadrupole, which is unit 05's shell theorem seen from the other side. Small $f$ gives $J_2 \approx 2f/5$, so a $1/298$ flattening would give $1.34\times10^{-3}$: quadrupole and flattening are the same order of smallness, not different ones.

The number. $(2f - f^2)/5$ with $f = 1/298.257223563$ is $(6.70562\times10^{-3} - 1.12413\times10^{-5})/5 = 1.338876\times10^{-3}$. Dividing by the measured $1.08263\times10^{-3}$ gives $1.23669$, hence $23.7\%$.

This overshoot is the standard route to inferring the interior of a planet you cannot drill into. For Jupiter the ratio is far more extreme, and for the Moon almost absent. The formal version is the Radau–Darwin relation, which converts the measured pair $(f, J_2)$ into an estimate of $C/MR^2$, the single most useful number about a planet's radial density profile.

A check that has no free parameters

If the Earth were a rotating fluid, its surface would be a level set of the potential in the rotating frame — gravity plus the centrifugal term, with $\omega_{\mathrm s}$ the spin rate (subscript s for spin: plain $\omega$ is kept for section 3). Take the surface as $r(\theta) = R(1 - f\cos^2\theta)$ and work to first order in $f$, $J_2$ and $q = \omega_{\mathrm s}^2R^3/GM$:

$$U = -\frac{GM}{r} + \frac{GMJ_2R^2}{r^3}P_2(\cos\theta) - \tfrac12\omega_{\mathrm s}^2r^2\sin^2\theta .$$

Substituting $1/r = (1+f\cos^2\theta)/R$ — and $r = R$ in the two terms that are already small — every term is a constant plus a multiple of $\cos^2\theta$. A level surface has $U$ independent of $\theta$, so the coefficient of $\cos^2\theta$ must vanish:

$$-\frac{GM}{R}f + \frac{3GMJ_2}{2R} + \tfrac12\omega_{\mathrm s}^2R^2 = 0 .$$
Clairaut's flattening formula $$f = \tfrac32 J_2 + \tfrac12 q .$$

Three independently measured numbers on the right; the shape on the left.

Full derivation

Write $\mu = \cos\theta$ and keep only first-order terms. On the surface $r = R(1 - f\mu^2)$, so $1/r = R^{-1}(1 + f\mu^2) + O(f^2)$, while in the two terms that already carry a small factor we may set $r = R$. Then

$U = -\dfrac{GM}{R}(1+f\mu^2) + \dfrac{GMJ_2}{R}\cdot\dfrac{3\mu^2-1}{2} - \tfrac12\omega_{\mathrm s}^2R^2(1-\mu^2)$.

The coefficient of $\mu^2$ is $-\dfrac{GM}{R}f + \dfrac{3GMJ_2}{2R} + \tfrac12\omega_{\mathrm s}^2R^2$, and a level surface requires it to vanish:

$f = \tfrac32J_2 + \dfrac{\omega_{\mathrm s}^2R^3}{2GM} = \tfrac32J_2 + \tfrac12q$. $\square$

Dimension check. $q = \omega_{\mathrm s}^2R^3/GM$ has $\mathrm{s^{-2}\,m^3}/(\mathrm{m^3s^{-2}}) = 1$: dimensionless, as $f$ and $J_2$ are. Limit checks. $\omega_{\mathrm s} \to 0$: for a non-rotating self-gravitating fluid the only consistent state is a sphere, $f = J_2 = 0$, and the formula is satisfied by it. $J_2 \to 0$ with $\omega_{\mathrm s} \ne 0$ gives $f = q/2$: a fluid envelope round a point mass, which has no quadrupole of its own (Roche's model). A uniform fluid, at the other extreme, has $J_2 = \tfrac25f$ (worked example 2), and substituting gives $f = \tfrac54q$, which is Maclaurin's result for a slowly rotating homogeneous fluid. The Earth, with $f = 0.97\,q$, sits between the two, as a centrally condensed body should.

The name: unit 11 has a "Clairaut relation" for geodesics on a surface of revolution. Same Clairaut, different theorem.

The level surface, drawn, and the number

A meridian section, flattening exaggerated ($f = 0.28$). At the marked point gravity pulls towards the centre and the centrifugal force away from the axis; their sum is perpendicular to the surface, so it has no component along it to make the fluid flow.

the number

$\omega_{\mathrm s} = 7.292115\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, $R = 6378.137\ \mathrm{km}$, $GM = 3.986004418\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$:

$q = \omega_{\mathrm s}^2R^3/GM = 3.46139\times10^{-3}$.

$f = \tfrac32(1.08263\times10^{-3}) + \tfrac12(3.46139\times10^{-3}) = 3.35464\times10^{-3} = 1/298.09.$

Measured: $1/298.257$. The prediction is high by $0.055\%$.

Nothing was fitted. Rotation rate, mass, radius and $J_2$ are independent measurements, and the shape follows.

Why a level set. A fluid at rest in the rotating frame cannot sustain a force along its free surface: any tangential component of the net force per unit mass would make the surface fluid flow. So $-\nabla U$ is normal to the surface everywhere, which is the statement that the surface is a level set of $U$. The centrifugal potential $-\tfrac12\omega_{\mathrm s}^2\rho^2$, $\rho$ the distance from the axis, is the potential of unit 03's centrifugal force: $-\nabla(-\tfrac12\omega_{\mathrm s}^2\rho^2) = \omega_{\mathrm s}^2\rho\,\hat{\boldsymbol\rho} = -\vec\omega_{\mathrm s}\times(\vec\omega_{\mathrm s}\times\vec r\,)$.

Reading the figure. The arrows are drawn so that the sum is exactly normal at the marked point of the exaggerated shape; it is a schematic of the condition, not a to-scale Earth. Two things to see. The net force does not point at the centre: it is steeper than the radial line, and extended it would cross the axis beyond the centre. And the flattening is what lets it be normal: on a sphere the normal is radial, and the centrifugal push would have a component along the surface, towards the equator, so fluid would flow equatorwards until the surface had tilted to cancel it. For the real Earth the centrifugal force is $q \approx 1/289$ of gravity at the equator, and the angle between the net force and the radial direction is at most about $0.19^\circ$, at latitude $45^\circ$.

The $0.055\%$ residual is arithmetic, not physics: it is the second-order terms in $f$, $J_2$ and $q$ that this calculation threw away. The exact theory of a level ellipsoid (Pizzetti in 1894, completed by Somigliana in 1929) keeps them all, and with the same $R$, $GM$, $\omega_{\mathrm s}$ and $f = 1/298.257$ it returns $J_2 = 1.082630\times10^{-3}$ — the measured value to every digit quoted. (Clairaut's interior theory, a differential equation for the flattening $f(r)$ of the level surfaces inside the body, does not help here: at first order it leaves the surface relation exactly as above, whatever the density profile.) The point is that the first-order relation binds three independently measured numbers into one equation, and they agree.

Note also what the relation says about causation: a planet does not have a quadrupole "because it spins"; it has one because it is a fluid that spins, and a rigid planet can have any $J_2$ its history gave it. The Moon is the standard counterexample: its $J_2$ is far larger than its present rotation would support, a frozen record of when it was closer to the Earth and spinning faster.

What kind of object this is. The map $(\vec r,\vec v\,)\mapsto(a,e,i,\Omega,\omega,\mathcal M)$, on the open subset of $\mathbb R^6$ where $E < 0$, $\vec L \ne 0$, $e \ne 0$ and $i \ne 0$, is a change of coordinates on phase space, in unit 03's sense and no more mysterious than polar coordinates. It is injective because a bound Kepler orbit is fixed by its plane ($i,\Omega$), its ellipse in the plane ($a,e,\omega$, via unit 08's $r = p/(1+e\cos\nu)$) and the body's place on it ($\mathcal M$); writing the inverse out needs Kepler's equation (unit 09) and nothing in this unit uses it. Only the forward map is ever differentiated here, and only through the variation-of-parameters lemma. The excluded sets are where the construction degenerates — a circular orbit has no perigee to measure $\omega$ from, an equatorial orbit has no node to measure $\Omega$ from. Practitioners patch with alternative element sets there, exactly as one patches the polar chart at the origin.

Why this chart and not another. In these coordinates the unperturbed Kepler flow is $\dot a = \dot e = \dot i = \dot\Omega = \dot\omega = 0$, $\dot{\mathcal M} = n(a)$. Five coordinates are constant and the sixth advances uniformly. No other coordinate system on this phase space is anything like as simple, and a perturbation calculation done in these coordinates starts from a solved problem rather than from a hard one. That is the entire strategic idea of section 3, and it is the same idea as the interaction picture, the variation of constants, and action–angle variables (unit 17).

The node vector. $\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf H}$ lies in the equatorial plane (perpendicular to $\hat{\mathbf z}$) and in the orbit plane (perpendicular to $\hat{\mathbf H}$), so it lies along the intersection of the two planes. Its length is $\sin i$. Normalising gives $\hat{\mathbf n}$, pointing at the ascending node, the crossing where the body moves from south to north — that choice is what makes the frame right-handed with $\hat{\mathbf m} = \hat{\mathbf H}\times\hat{\mathbf n}$.

The sixth element. $\tau$, the time of perigee passage, is the one element with the dimensions of time. Unit 09's Kepler equation is what converts $\mathcal M$ into an actual position; none of that is needed here, because everything in this unit is averaged over one orbit and the phase drops out.

The orbit frame, and the argument of latitude

The orbit plane seen from above, $\hat{\mathbf H}$ towards you. The node line is where it crosses the equator; the shaded half, $0 < u < \pi$, lies north of it. Eccentricity $0.3$, $\omega = 120^\circ$.

definition · the argument of latitude

$u$, the angle in the orbit plane from $\hat{\mathbf n}$ to the body:

$\vec r = r(\cos u\,\hat{\mathbf n} + \sin u\,\hat{\mathbf m})$, and so $\;z = r\sin i\,\sin u$,

since $\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\hat{\mathbf z} = 0$ and $\hat{\mathbf m}\cdot\hat{\mathbf z} = \sin i$.

$u = \omega + \nu$, with $\nu$ the true anomaly, the angle from perigee. Over one orbit $u$ runs once through $2\pi$: it is the fast angle that Secular versus periodic, and averaging averages over.

Why $\hat{\mathbf m}\cdot\hat{\mathbf z} = \sin i$. With $\hat{\mathbf n} = (\cos\Omega,\sin\Omega,0)$ and $\hat{\mathbf H} = (\sin i\sin\Omega,\,-\sin i\cos\Omega,\,\cos i)$ — the unit vector at angle $i$ from $\hat{\mathbf z}$ and perpendicular to the node — the cross product is $\hat{\mathbf m} = \hat{\mathbf H}\times\hat{\mathbf n} = (-\cos i\sin\Omega,\ \cos i\cos\Omega,\ \sin i)$. Its third component is $\sin i$. So $\hat{\mathbf m}$ is the direction in the orbit plane that climbs most steeply out of the equator, and a body at $u = 90^\circ$ is at the orbit's northernmost point, at height $r\sin i$.

Why $u$ and not the true anomaly. The $J_2$ force depends on where the body is relative to the equator — through $z$ — and not on where it is relative to the perigee. $u$ measures exactly that. The true anomaly $\nu$ enters only through the distance, $1/r = (1 + e\cos\nu)/p$, which is why the averaging integral on Averaging, and the result carries $\cos(u - \omega)$.

The frame moves. $\hat{\mathbf n}$, $\hat{\mathbf m}$ and $\hat{\mathbf H}$ are built from $\vec L$, so once $\vec L$ changes the whole frame turns with it — slowly, at the rate the perturbation sets. That turning is the precession section 4 computes, and the correction it forces on $\omega$ is the warning box on The other precession: the apse line.

Osculating elements, and the one lemma

definition · osculating elements

Let $\ddot{\vec r} = -\dfrac{GM}{r^3}\vec r + \vec a_{\mathrm p}(\vec r,\vec v,t)$. At each instant, apply the same map to the actual state $(\vec r(t),\vec v(t))$. The resulting $a(t),\dots,M(t)$ are the elements of the Kepler orbit the body would follow if $\vec a_{\mathrm p}$ were switched off now: its osculating orbit.

lemma · variation of parameters

If $c(\vec r,\vec v\,)$ is constant along the Kepler flow, then along the perturbed motion

$\dfrac{\mathrm dc}{\mathrm dt} = \nabla_{\!\vec v}\,c\cdot\vec a_{\mathrm p}.$

Proof. The chain rule gives $\dot c = \nabla_{\!\vec r}c\cdot\vec v + \nabla_{\!\vec v}c\cdot\ddot{\vec r}$. Split $\ddot{\vec r}$ into the Kepler part and $\vec a_{\mathrm p}$; the Kepler part reassembles the derivative of $c$ along the Kepler flow, which is zero. $\square$

Only the perturbing acceleration appears. The whole of Kepler's problem has been absorbed into the choice of coordinates, and what is left is first order in the perturbation and therefore small.

Applied to $c = \vec L$: since $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec v$, $\nabla_{\!\vec v}\vec L\cdot\vec a_{\mathrm p} = \vec r\times\vec a_{\mathrm p}$, the torque. Section 4 uses exactly this.

Nothing has been approximated at this point: the osculating elements are exact functions of the exact solution, and the lemma is an identity. The next slide draws the picture.

Why the lemma is worth boxing. Without it, one would have to differentiate, say, $\Omega$ as an explicit function of six state variables and substitute the equations of motion — a page of algebra, repeated for each element. The lemma says that the entire Kepler part cancels identically for any conserved quantity, so only a gradient in $\vec v$ contracted with $\vec a_{\mathrm p}$ survives. For the elements built from $\vec L$ the gradient is immediate; for those built from $\vec A$ it is a two-line computation (problem P9).

The full set of resulting equations — six ODEs for six elements, driven by the perturbation — are Lagrange's planetary equations when $\vec a_{\mathrm p}$ comes from a potential, and Gauss's form when it does not (drag, thrust, radiation pressure). They are not written out here; the two the running example needs are derived directly in section 4, which is shorter and shows where every term comes from.

The same manoeuvre is unit 06's variation of parameters — let the constants of the unperturbed solution move — now applied to a nonlinear problem; quantum mechanics calls it time-dependent perturbation theory. Euler first used it in the 1740s on precisely this problem — the mutual perturbations of the planets, Jupiter and Saturn first — and Lagrange made it a general method in his memoirs of 1808–1810; the question it was sharpened to settle was whether the solar system is stable.

"Osculating" is Latin for kissing: the osculating orbit touches the true trajectory to first order in time — same position, same velocity — and then parts company from it. Secular versus periodic, and averaging

The element equations have the form $\dot c = \varepsilon\,F(c, u)$, $F$ periodic in a fast angle $u$ — for an orbit, the argument of latitude — that advances uniformly in time, $\varepsilon \sim J_2$. Split $F$ into its mean and the rest:

$$\bar F(c) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint F(c,u)\,\mathrm{d}u, \qquad F = \bar F + \widetilde F .$$
the two kinds of term

$\bar F$ produces secular change: it accumulates, $\Delta c \sim \varepsilon t$.

$\widetilde F$ produces periodic change: it integrates to a bounded wobble of size $\varepsilon$, forever.

average over the right variable

$\oint\,\cdot\,\mathrm dt$ and $\oint\,\cdot\,\mathrm du$ are different averages. Time is the physical one, so the weight $\mathrm dt = (r^2/L)\,\mathrm du$ must be carried. Dropping it is the commonest error in this calculation.

"Advances uniformly" is exact for a circular orbit and true on average otherwise; the weight in the warning is what handles the difference.

Why "secular". From saeculum, an age: eighteenth-century astronomers divided the perturbations of the planets into inégalités périodiques, which come back, and inégalités séculaires, which do not within a human lifetime. Whether the secular terms of the solar system are genuinely growing or merely the start of a very long period was the great open problem of the era; Laplace and Lagrange proved that the semi-major axes have no secular terms at first order, which was taken for a century as a proof of stability, and is not one.

The warning, made concrete. The node rate will come out proportional to $\oint \frac{\sin^2u}{r}\,\mathrm du$, and the weight matters: $1/r$ varies by a factor $(1+e)/(1-e)$ around an eccentric orbit, so for a Molniya orbit with $e = 0.74$ it varies by a factor of nearly seven. That is where the $(1-e^2)^{-2}$ in the final formula comes from — it is entirely a consequence of the averaging weight, and it is not small.

One more distinction, which unit 21 makes properly: the averaged system is not the first term of a convergent series. It is the first term of an asymptotic expansion, and pushing it to higher order eventually makes things worse rather than better. That is the subject of unit 21 and the reason it exists.

Let $\bar c$ solve $\dot{\bar c} = \varepsilon\bar F(\bar c\,)$ with $\bar c(0) = c(0)$. Under standard smoothness and boundedness hypotheses,

$|c(t) - \bar c(t)| = O(\varepsilon)$ uniformly for $0 \le t \le K/\varepsilon$.

Where it is proved: in the notes.

That is a guarantee, not a forecast: the theorem says nothing about longer times, and the reason the secular rates are nonetheless trusted for years is that they are checked against tracking.

The perturbing acceleration

Write the $J_2$ term in Cartesian form, with $k = \tfrac12 GMJ_2R^2$ and $z = r\cos\theta$:

$$\Phi_2 = \frac{GMJ_2R^2}{r^3}\,\frac{3\cos^2\theta - 1}{2} = k\Big(\frac{3z^2}{r^5} - \frac{1}{r^3}\Big).$$

Differentiate, using $\partial_j r = x_j/r$:

$\partial_x\Phi_2 = k\big(3x/r^5 - 15z^2x/r^7\big)$, the same in $y$, and $\partial_z\Phi_2 = k\big(9z/r^5 - 15z^3/r^7\big)$.

collecting $$\vec a_{\mathrm p} = \frac{3GMJ_2R^2}{2r^5}\Big[\Big(\frac{5z^2}{r^2} - 1\Big)\vec r - 2z\,\hat{\mathbf z}\Big]$$

$= -\nabla\Phi_2$.

The $z$-component, in full. $\partial_z\big(3z^2r^{-5} - r^{-3}\big) = 6zr^{-5} + 3z^2(-5r^{-6})(z/r) + 3r^{-4}(z/r) = 9z/r^5 - 15z^3/r^7$. Collecting all three components, $\nabla\Phi_2 = k\big[(3r^{-5} - 15z^2r^{-7})\vec r + 6z\,r^{-5}\hat{\mathbf z}\big]$; the $\hat{\mathbf z}$ piece supplies the difference between $9z/r^5$ and $3z/r^5$ in the third slot. Negating and pulling out $3k/r^5 = \tfrac32GMJ_2R^2/r^5$ gives the boxed form.

Dimension check. $[GMJ_2R^2/r^5]\cdot[\vec r] = \mathrm{m^3s^{-2}\cdot m^2/m^5}\cdot\mathrm m = \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. An acceleration.

Only the second piece can exert a torque about the centre. That pull towards the bulge is the whole mechanism of the next three slides.

size, at the satellite

$|\vec a_{\mathrm p}| \sim \tfrac32 GMJ_2R^2/a^4 = 9.9\ \mathrm{mm\,s^{-2}}$ against a central $GM/a^2 = 7.74\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$: a ratio of $1.28\times10^{-3}$.

Small. But it points the same way relative to the orbit on every pass, and that is what makes it accumulate.

What to look for in the figure. At the equator the arrow points straight in, at the poles straight out, and the magnitude there is twice the equatorial one. In between, the component along the meridian, towards the equator, is $\tfrac32GMJ_2R^2\cdot 2\sin\theta\cos\theta/r^4$, largest at latitude $45^\circ$ and zero at the equator and the poles. A satellite on an inclined orbit therefore feels a sideways tug towards the equatorial plane in both its northern and southern halves.

Limit checks. On the equator, $z = 0$, the acceleration is $-\tfrac32GMJ_2R^2\vec r/r^5$: purely radial and inward, the extra pull of the bulge. On the axis, $\vec r = \pm r\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $z = \pm r$, the bracket is $4(\pm r\hat{\mathbf z}) - 2(\pm r)\hat{\mathbf z} = \pm2r\hat{\mathbf z} = 2\vec r$, so the acceleration is $3GMJ_2R^2\,\vec r/r^5$: radially outward along the axis, of magnitude $3GMJ_2R^2/r^4$. Outward is right: over the pole, the equatorial bulge is entirely "below and to the side", and its net pull is weaker than a sphere's, which shows up as a positive correction to the outward direction. And $J_2 \to 0$ gives zero, as it must.

The torque, and what it moves

By the lemma, $\dot{\vec L} = \vec r\times\vec a_{\mathrm p}$. The first term of $\vec a_{\mathrm p}$ is along $\vec r$ and contributes nothing:

$$\dot{\vec L} = \frac{3GMJ_2R^2}{r^5}\,z\,\big(\hat{\mathbf z}\times\vec r\,\big).$$

Now write $\vec L = L\hat{\mathbf H}$ in the orbit frame $\{\hat{\mathbf n}, \hat{\mathbf m}, \hat{\mathbf H}\}$. Differentiating $\hat{\mathbf H} = (\sin i\sin\Omega,\,-\sin i\cos\Omega,\,\cos i)$ gives $\partial_\Omega\hat{\mathbf H} = \sin i\,\hat{\mathbf n}$ and $\partial_i\hat{\mathbf H} = -\hat{\mathbf m}$, so

$$\dot{\vec L} = \dot L\,\hat{\mathbf H} + L\sin i\,\dot\Omega\,\hat{\mathbf n} - L\,\dot i\,\hat{\mathbf m}.$$
three scalar equations

$L\sin i\,\dot\Omega = \dot{\vec L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$

$-L\,\dot i = \dot{\vec L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf m}$

$\dot L = \dot{\vec L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf H}$

With $u$ the argument of latitude, $\vec r = r(\cos u\,\hat{\mathbf n} + \sin u\,\hat{\mathbf m})$ and $z = r\sin i\sin u$, the triple products are one line each:

$\dot{\vec L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} = -\dfrac{3GMJ_2R^2}{r^3}\sin i\cos i\,\sin^2u$,

$\dot{\vec L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf m} = +\dfrac{3GMJ_2R^2}{r^3}\sin i\cos i\,\sin u\cos u$,

$\dot{\vec L}\cdot\hat{\mathbf H} = -\dfrac{3GMJ_2R^2}{r^3}\sin^2 i\,\sin u\cos u$.

The triple products. Since $\{\hat{\mathbf n},\hat{\mathbf m},\hat{\mathbf H}\}$ is right-handed, $\hat{\mathbf n}\times\hat{\mathbf m} = \hat{\mathbf H}$, $\hat{\mathbf m}\times\hat{\mathbf H} = \hat{\mathbf n}$, $\hat{\mathbf H}\times\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf m}$. Then $(\hat{\mathbf z}\times\vec r\,)\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} = \hat{\mathbf z}\cdot(\vec r\times\hat{\mathbf n})$ by the cyclic property of the scalar triple product (unit 08), and $\vec r\times\hat{\mathbf n} = r\sin u\,(\hat{\mathbf m}\times\hat{\mathbf n}) = -r\sin u\,\hat{\mathbf H}$, so the product is $-r\sin u\cos i$. Multiplying by $3GMJ_2R^2z/r^5$ with $z = r\sin i\sin u$ gives the first line. The other two are the same computation with $\hat{\mathbf m}$ and $\hat{\mathbf H}$ in place of $\hat{\mathbf n}$: $\vec r\times\hat{\mathbf m} = r\cos u\,\hat{\mathbf H}$ and $\vec r\times\hat{\mathbf H} = r(\sin u\,\hat{\mathbf n} - \cos u\,\hat{\mathbf m})$.

Where the derivatives of $\hat{\mathbf H}$ come from. $\hat{\mathbf H}$ is a unit vector parametrised by $(i,\Omega)$ exactly as a point on a sphere is parametrised by colatitude and longitude, with $i$ the colatitude measured from $\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\Omega + 90^\circ$ the longitude. The two partial derivatives are the coordinate basis vectors of that chart, of lengths $1$ and $\sin i$ — which is unit 05's spherical Jacobian in another costume, and why $\sin i$ appears in the $\Omega$ equation and not in the $i$ equation.

A torque along $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is perpendicular to $\vec L$ and to $\hat{\mathbf z}$, so it changes neither $|\vec L|$ nor $L_z$. It swings $\vec L$ round a cone about the axis: the plane turns, the tilt stays.

the direction

Prograde, $\cos i > 0$: the node moves west, $\dot\Omega < 0$. For the satellite, $\cos i < 0$: the torque reverses and the node moves east — the direction the Sun moves.

The geometry of the figure. The page is the plane containing $\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\hat{\mathbf m}$, and $\hat{\mathbf n}$ points out of it. The orbit plane contains $\hat{\mathbf n}$, so it is seen edge on, as the line at angle $i$ to the equator; its northernmost point $u = 90^\circ$ is at $r\hat{\mathbf m}$ and its southernmost at $-r\hat{\mathbf m}$. The arrows at the two ends are the part of $\vec a_{\mathrm p}$ along $\hat{\mathbf H}$, the only part that can tip the plane, computed from the boxed formula on The perturbing acceleration: at $u = 90^\circ$ it is $-\tfrac32GMJ_2R^2r^{-4}\cdot2\sin i\cos i$ along $\hat{\mathbf H}$, and the same with the opposite sign at $u = 270^\circ$. Then $\vec r\times\vec a_{\mathrm p} = (r\hat{\mathbf m})\times(-c\,\hat{\mathbf H}) = -rc\,\hat{\mathbf n}$ with $c > 0$ at the top, and $(-r\hat{\mathbf m})\times(+c\,\hat{\mathbf H})$ is the same vector at the bottom.

Why the tip of $\vec L$ moves round a cone. $\partial_\Omega\hat{\mathbf H} = \sin i\,\hat{\mathbf n}$ (The torque, and what it moves), so a change of $\vec L$ along $-\hat{\mathbf n}$ is a decrease of $\Omega$ at fixed $i$: the tip of $\vec L$ goes round the circle of colatitude $i$ about $\hat{\mathbf z}$, and the node line, which is perpendicular to both $\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\vec L$, turns with it. A gyroscope precesses the same way under gravity, for the same reason: the torque is always perpendicular to the angular momentum and to the vertical.

Read the three equations before averaging. All three right-hand sides are $O(J_2)$, so all three elements wobble. But two of them carry $\sin u\cos u = \tfrac12\sin2u$, which averages to zero over an orbit, while the first carries $\sin^2u$, whose average is $\tfrac12$ and is not zero. That single observation is the whole result: to first order $J_2$ does not change the orbit's size, shape or tilt — it only turns the orbit plane about the Earth's axis.

Why $i$ and $L$ are untouched is worth a physical sentence: the perturbing potential is axially symmetric, so it cannot exert a torque about the axis $\hat{\mathbf z}$; the component $L_z = L\cos i$ is therefore exactly conserved, not merely on average. With $L$ also conserved on average, $i$ follows.

Averaging, and the result

From the first equation, with $L^2 = GMp$ and $p = a(1-e^2)$,

$$\dot\Omega = -\frac{3GMJ_2R^2}{L\,r^3}\cos i\,\sin^2 u .$$

Average over one orbit in time, using $\mathrm dt = (r^2/L)\,\mathrm du$ and $1/r = (1 + e\cos(u - \omega))/p$:

$$\langle\dot\Omega\rangle = -\frac{3GMJ_2R^2\cos i}{T L^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\sin^2u}{r}\,\mathrm du ,$$

and the integral is $\pi/p$, since $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2u\,\mathrm du = \pi$ and $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2u\cos(u-\omega)\,\mathrm du = 0$.

the nodal precession rate $$\boxed{\;\dot\Omega = -\frac32\,J_2\left(\frac Ra\right)^{\!2}\frac{n\cos i}{(1-e^2)^2}\;}$$

with $n = \sqrt{GM/a^3}$, and $\dot i = \dot a = \dot e = 0$ at this order.

checks

Dimensions. $J_2$, $(R/a)^2$, $\cos i$ and $(1-e^2)^{-2}$ are all dimensionless, so $\dot\Omega$ has the dimensions of $n$: $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

Limits. $J_2 \to 0$: no precession. $i \to 90^\circ$: no precession — a polar orbit passes over the bulge symmetrically and is torqued equally either way. $i > 90^\circ$: the sign flips.

Full derivation of the averaging step

$\langle\dot\Omega\rangle = \frac1T\int_0^T\dot\Omega\,\mathrm dt$. Substituting $\mathrm dt = (r^2/L)\mathrm du$ — which is Kepler's second law, $r^2\dot u = L$, from unit 08 — turns $\int \dot\Omega\,\mathrm dt$ into $-\frac{3GMJ_2R^2\cos i}{L^2}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\sin^2u}{r}\mathrm du$. With $1/r = (1+e\cos\nu)/p$ and $\nu = u - \omega$ the true anomaly,

$\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\sin^2u}{r}\mathrm du = \frac1p\Big[\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2u\,\mathrm du + e\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2u\cos(u-\omega)\,\mathrm du\Big] = \frac{\pi}{p}$,

because $\sin^2u = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos2u$ contains only the frequencies $0$ and $2$, and $\cos(u-\omega)$ only the frequency $1$: every product integrates to zero over a full period. (To first order $\omega$ is a constant during the averaging; treating a slowly varying quantity as constant over one fast period is exactly what the averaging theorem licenses.)

Then $T = 2\pi/n$, $L^2 = GMp$ and $p = a(1-e^2)$:

$\langle\dot\Omega\rangle = -\frac{3GMJ_2R^2\cos i\,\pi\,n}{2\pi\,GM\,p\cdot p} = -\frac{3J_2R^2n\cos i}{2a^2(1-e^2)^2}$, which is the boxed formula.

The periodic part, for comparison

Writing $\sin^2u = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos2u$ and integrating the second piece for a circular orbit gives an oscillation of the node of amplitude $\tfrac34J_2(R/a)^2|\cos i|$. For the running example that is $9.6\times10^{-5}$ radians, or $0.0055^\circ$ — a wobble that never grows, beside a drift of $0.0690^\circ$ per orbit that never stops.

A check against the equations themselves. Integrating $\ddot{\vec r} = -GM\vec r/r^3 + \vec a_{\mathrm p}$ numerically for three days from a circular state at the satellite's $a$ and $i = 98.603^\circ$, and fitting a straight line to the osculating $\Omega(t)$, gives $0.990^\circ$ per day against the formula's $0.9856^\circ$. The $0.4\%$ gap is second order: the initial osculating $a$ is not the orbit's mean $a$, and at the level of $J_2^2$ the distinction matters. Absorbing it would move the predicted inclination by about $0.04^\circ$.

A third check, against a real orbit. The International Space Station has $a \approx 6798\ \mathrm{km}$ and $i = 51.6^\circ$. The formula gives $\dot\Omega = -4.95^\circ$ per day, so its orbital plane makes a full turn in about $73$ days. That is the published value, and it is the reason its ground track and its visible-pass seasons repeat on roughly that cycle.

The sun-synchronous inclination

worked example 3 · solving for $i$

Demand. A sun-synchronous orbit keeps its plane at a fixed angle to the Earth–Sun line, so the plane must turn eastward once per year:

$\dot\Omega = \dfrac{2\pi}{365.2422\times86400\ \mathrm s} = 1.99106\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} = 0.98565^\circ/\mathrm{day}$.

Orbit. $h = 800$ km, so $a = 6378.137 + 800 = 7178.137$ km, $e = 0$, and

$n = \sqrt{GM/a^3} = 1.038129\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ (period $100.87$ min).

Invert. $\cos i = -\dfrac{2\dot\Omega\,a^2}{3J_2R^2\,n} = -\dfrac{2(1.99106{\times}10^{-7})(7178.137)^2}{3(1.08263{\times}10^{-3})(6378.137)^2(1.038129{\times}10^{-3})}$

$= -0.149589$, so $\;i = 98.603^\circ$.

it is the right number

Every sun-synchronous satellite at $800$ km flies at $98.6^\circ$. The same formula gives $97.79^\circ$ at $600$ km and $99.48^\circ$ at $1000$ km, which is also what they fly at.

why it must be retrograde

$\dot\Omega > 0$ forces $\cos i < 0$. A sun-synchronous orbit is necessarily inclined past $90^\circ$ — it goes round the Earth against the Earth's own rotation, and it does so because of the sign of a cosine.

There is a ceiling: $|\cos i| \le 1$ caps the altitude at about $5974$ km, beyond which no inclination precesses fast enough.

The arithmetic, carefully. Numerator: $2\times1.99106\times10^{-7}\times(7.178137\times10^6)^2 = 2\times1.99106\times10^{-7}\times5.15256\times10^{13} = 2.05179\times10^{7}$. Denominator: $3\times1.08263\times10^{-3}\times(6.378137\times10^6)^2\times1.038129\times10^{-3} = 3\times1.08263\times10^{-3}\times4.06806\times10^{13}\times1.038129\times10^{-3} = 1.37161\times10^{8}$. The ratio is $0.149589$, and with the minus sign $i = \arccos(-0.149589) = 98.6031^\circ$.

Which year. The relevant period is the one after which the Earth–Sun direction repeats: the tropical year, $365.2422$ days. Using the sidereal year ($365.2564$ days) instead changes $\dot\Omega$ in the fifth digit and the inclination by about $0.0003^\circ$ — below the accuracy at which $J_2$ alone is meaningful anyway.

Which radius. "Altitude" here is measured above the equatorial radius, which is also the reference radius in $J_2$. Using the mean radius instead moves $a$ by $7\ \mathrm{km}$ and $i$ by about $0.03^\circ$. The checkpoint asks for a degree, so the ambiguity does not matter; it is worth knowing that it exists.

The ceiling. Setting $\cos i = -1$ and $e = 0$ gives $\dot\Omega = \tfrac32J_2R^2\sqrt{GM}\,a^{-7/2}$, so $a_{\max} = \big(\tfrac32J_2R^2\sqrt{GM}/\dot\Omega\big)^{2/7} = 12352\ \mathrm{km}$, an altitude of $5974\ \mathrm{km}$. Above that the orbit is simply too slow and too far for the bulge to turn it once a year, whatever its inclination.

What is really wanted. Strictly, sun-synchronicity is about the local solar time of the equator crossing staying fixed, which is the condition that the node keeps pace with the mean Sun. Real mission design adds the higher $J_n$, the lunisolar perturbations and atmospheric drag, and then adjusts the inclination by a few hundredths of a degree and maintains it with thrusters. The first three digits come from this slide.

The other precession: the apse line

Step 1 — average the potential. On a nearly circular orbit, $\cos\theta = z/r = \sin i\sin u$, so with $\langle\sin^2u\rangle = \tfrac12$,

$$\langle\Phi_2\rangle = \frac{GMJ_2R^2}{r^3}\Big\langle\frac{3\sin^2i\sin^2u - 1}{2}\Big\rangle = \frac{c}{r^3},$$

with $c = \tfrac14GMJ_2R^2\big(1 - 3\cos^2 i\big)$.

Step 2 — a central-force problem. Unit 08's machinery applies verbatim to $V_{\mathrm{eff}} = -\frac{GM}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2r^2} + \frac{c}{r^3}$. Equating $V_{\mathrm{eff}}'(r_0) = 0$ and computing $\omega_r^2 = V_{\mathrm{eff}}''(r_0)$ against $\omega_u^2 = L^2/r_0^4$:

$$\omega_r^2 = n^2 + \frac{3c}{r_0^5},\qquad \omega_u^2 = n^2 - \frac{3c}{r_0^5},$$

so the apse line turns, in inertial space, at $\;\omega_u - \omega_r = -3c/(r_0^5n) = \tfrac34 J_2(R/a)^2 n\,(3\cos^2i - 1)$.

but $\omega$ is measured from the node

And the node is moving. $\hat{\mathbf n}$ advances within the orbit plane at rate $\dot\Omega\cos i$, so

$\dot\omega = (\omega_u - \omega_r) - \dot\Omega\cos i$.

the apsidal precession rate $$\dot\omega = \frac34\,J_2\left(\frac Ra\right)^{\!2}\frac{n\,(5\cos^2 i - 1)}{(1-e^2)^2}$$

since $(3\cos^2i - 1) + 2\cos^2 i = 5\cos^2 i - 1$. The factor $(1-e^2)^{-2}$ is derived at $i = 0$ in problem P9 and cited in general.

Forgetting the second term gives $3\cos^2i - 1$ and a critical inclination of $54.7^\circ$, which is wrong. The moving frame is not optional.

Why step 1 is allowed

Averaging the potential over $u$ at fixed $r$ throws away the part of $\Phi_2$ that varies as $\cos2u$. Its in-plane force drives the radial motion at frequency $2n$, far from the radial frequency $\omega_r \approx n$, so it produces a bounded forced wobble in $r$ (unit 06's non-resonant response) and no secular turning of the apse line; that is the averaging theorem's licence, applied to this term. Its out-of-plane part turns the plane, which is the $\dot\Omega$ already found and is accounted for by the moving-node correction below.

Full derivation of step 2

$V_{\mathrm{eff}}' = GM/r^2 - L^2/r^3 - 3c/r^4 = 0$ at $r_0$ gives $L^2 = GMr_0 - 3c/r_0$. Then $V_{\mathrm{eff}}'' = -2GM/r^3 + 3L^2/r^4 + 12c/r^5$, and substituting $L^2$:

$\omega_r^2 = -\frac{2GM}{r_0^3} + \frac{3GM}{r_0^3} - \frac{9c}{r_0^5} + \frac{12c}{r_0^5} = \frac{GM}{r_0^3} + \frac{3c}{r_0^5}$, and $\omega_u^2 = L^2/r_0^4 = \frac{GM}{r_0^3} - \frac{3c}{r_0^5}$.

With $n^2 = GM/r_0^3$ and $3c/r_0^5 \ll n^2$, expanding the square roots to first order gives $\omega_r \approx n(1 + \tfrac{3c}{2n^2r_0^5})$ and $\omega_u \approx n(1 - \tfrac{3c}{2n^2r_0^5})$, hence $\omega_u - \omega_r = -3c/(n r_0^5)$. Substituting $c = \tfrac14GMJ_2R^2(1-3\cos^2i)$ and $GM = n^2r_0^3$: $-\tfrac34 n J_2(R/r_0)^2(1 - 3\cos^2i)$, which is the slide's expression.

Full derivation of the node's in-plane motion

$\hat{\mathbf n} = (\cos\Omega,\sin\Omega,0)$, so $\dot{\hat{\mathbf n}} = \dot\Omega(-\sin\Omega,\cos\Omega,0)$. Project onto $\hat{\mathbf m} = (-\cos i\sin\Omega,\ \cos i\cos\Omega,\ \sin i)$: $\dot{\hat{\mathbf n}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf m} = \dot\Omega\cos i(\sin^2\Omega + \cos^2\Omega) = \dot\Omega\cos i$. Since $\hat{\mathbf m}$ is $90^\circ$ ahead of $\hat{\mathbf n}$ in the direction of motion, the node advances along the orbit at angular rate $\dot\Omega\cos i$, and an angle measured from the node decreases by that much. Substituting $\dot\Omega\cos i = -\tfrac32J_2(R/a)^2n\cos^2i$ and subtracting supplies the extra $+2\cos^2i$.

Consistency check at $i = 0$. There the node is undefined and only the sum $\varpi = \Omega + \omega$ is meaningful. The formulas give $\dot\varpi = \tfrac34J_2(R/a)^2n(5-1) - \tfrac32J_2(R/a)^2n = \tfrac32J_2(R/a)^2n$, which is exactly what step 2 gives directly at $\cos^2i = 1$. The two routes agree, which is a genuine check on the kinematic correction.

What is derived and what is cited. The derivation above assumed a nearly circular orbit, so it proves the formula at $e \to 0$. At general $e$ the tool is the lemma of section 3 applied to the Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector $\vec A$: problem P9 does it for an equatorial orbit, where $\vec a_{\mathrm p}$ is purely radial, and recovers the $(1-e^2)^{-2}$. The general-inclination, general-$e$ case is the same computation with the non-radial part of $\vec a_{\mathrm p}$ carried along; it is cited from Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications, the chapter on general perturbations, and was checked here against a direct numerical integration (next slide's notes).

The critical inclination

$\dot\omega = 0$ when $\;5\cos^2i = 1$: the critical inclination

$$i = \arccos\frac{1}{\sqrt5} = 63.4349^\circ \quad\text{(or }116.5651^\circ\text{)} .$$

At that inclination the perigee does not move, so an orbit designed to loiter over one hemisphere keeps loitering there indefinitely rather than drifting to the other.

a Molniya orbit

Half a sidereal day: $a = 26562\ \mathrm{km}$, $e = 0.74$, $i = 63.4349^\circ$. Perigee altitude $528$ km, apogee $39839$ km; with perigee in the far south it spends $11.1$ hours of each twelve north of the equator.

At $i = 60^\circ$ instead, $\dot\omega = 0.0413^\circ$ per day: perigee swings through a right angle in six years, and apogee wanders off the northern hemisphere within the satellite's working life.

and the node still moves

At the critical inclination $\dot\Omega = -0.1477^\circ$ per day. Nothing cancels it, and nothing needs to: a Molniya orbit cares where its apogee sits relative to the ground, which is governed by $\omega$, not by $\Omega$.

one Legendre term, two precessions

Both rates came from the single $P_2$ term of $\Phi$. The $\ell = 2$ term is responsible for both precessions; they differ because $\dot\Omega$ comes from the part of the torque along $\hat{\mathbf n}$ and $\dot\omega$ from the radial average of the same potential. $\ell = 0$ gives Kepler; $\ell = 1$ vanishes at the centre of mass; $\ell \ge 3$ contributes at the part-per-million level.

The critical inclination is exact in the sense that it is where the first-order secular rate vanishes; at that inclination the second-order and higher terms take over, and the true behaviour near $63.43^\circ$ is a slow libration of $\omega$ rather than a strict standstill. That structure — a resonance where the leading secular term vanishes — is the first hint of what unit 17 calls a resonance island, and the "critical inclination problem" was a well-known headache in the 1960s for exactly that reason.

The Molniya numbers. A period of half a sidereal day is $T = 43082.05\ \mathrm s$, so $a = (GM(T/2\pi)^2)^{1/3} = 26561.8\ \mathrm{km}$ and $n = 1.45842\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. With $e = 0.74$: perigee radius $a(1-e) = 6906\ \mathrm{km}$, altitude $528\ \mathrm{km}$; apogee radius $a(1+e) = 46218\ \mathrm{km}$, altitude $39839\ \mathrm{km}$, which is above the geostationary altitude. With $\omega = 270^\circ$ the body is north of the equator while the true anomaly runs from $90^\circ$ to $270^\circ$: on the apogee side of the chord through the Earth perpendicular to the major axis. By Kepler's second law (unit 08) the fraction of the period spent there is the fraction of the ellipse's area on that side, half the ellipse plus the strip between centre and focus: $\tfrac12 + \frac{2}{\pi ab}\int_0^{ae}b\sqrt{1 - x^2/a^2}\,\mathrm dx = \tfrac12 + \frac1\pi\big(e\sqrt{1-e^2} + \arcsin e\big) = 0.924$, or $11.1$ hours of the twelve. The factor $(1-e^2)^{-2} = (1 - 0.5476)^{-2} = 4.886$ multiplies both rates — the eccentricity almost quintuples the precession, which is the averaging weight of section 3 making itself felt.

$\dot\Omega$ at the critical inclination: $-\tfrac32(1.08263\times10^{-3}) (6378.137/26561.8)^2(1.45842\times10^{-4})(0.44721)(4.886) = -2.984\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, which is $-0.1477^\circ$ per day. At $i = 60^\circ$, $5\cos^2i - 1 = 0.25$ and $\dot\omega = 8.34\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} = 0.0413^\circ$ per day; $90/0.0413 = 2180$ days, or $6.0$ years, to move perigee through a right angle, and $23.9$ years for a full circuit. A Molniya satellite's working life is several years, so a $60^\circ$ orbit would lose its northern apogee in service.

Checked against a direct integration. Integrating the $J_2$-perturbed equations of motion numerically for $20$ days from a Molniya state at $i = 63.4349^\circ$ gives a perigee drift below $0.001^\circ$ per day — zero to within the second-order effects the formula leaves out — against $0.455^\circ$ per day at $i = 30^\circ$ from the integration and $0.454^\circ$ from the formula. The node rate from the integration is $-0.146^\circ$ per day against the formula's $-0.148^\circ$, a $1\%$ gap of the same osculating-versus-mean kind as for the satellite.

10 · tides

Two bulges, because $P_2$ is even

Expand the potential of a distant mass $M_{\mathrm e}$ at distance $d$ inside the body feeling it, with $\vec r\,{}'$ measured from the body's centre, $\hat{\mathbf s}$ the unit vector towards $M_{\mathrm e}$ (s for source; $\hat{\mathbf n}$ stays the node) and $\psi$ the angle between $\vec r\,{}'$ and $\hat{\mathbf s}$:

$$\Phi_{\mathrm{ext}}(\vec r\,{}') = -\frac{GM_{\mathrm e}}{d}\sum_{n\ge0}\Big(\frac{r'}{d}\Big)^{n}P_n(\cos\psi).$$
  • $n=0$: a constant. No force.
  • $n=1$: $-\frac{GM_{\mathrm e}}{d^2}r'\cos\psi$, a uniform field — it accelerates the whole body equally and vanishes in the free-falling frame.
  • $n=2$: $-\frac{GM_{\mathrm e}}{d^3}r'^2P_2(\cos\psi)$. The first term that stretches.
the Hessian is unit 05's tidal tensor

$-\frac{GM_{\mathrm e}}{2d^3}\big(3(\hat{\mathbf s}\cdot\vec r\,{}')^2 - r'^2\big)$ has second derivatives — the tidal tensor

$\partial_i\partial_j\Phi^{(2)}_{\mathrm{ext}} = \frac{GM_{\mathrm e}}{d^3}\big(\delta_{ij} - 3s_is_j\big)$,

eigenvalues $(-2,1,1)GM_{\mathrm e}/d^3$: stretch along $\hat{\mathbf s}$, squeeze across it, trace zero.

Unit 05 got the two bulges by computing $\partial_i\partial_j\Phi$ for a point mass and finding eigenvalues $(-2,1,1)$. That computation and this one are the same computation: the tidal tensor is the Hessian of the $\ell = 2$ term, and its eigenvalue structure is the statement that $P_2(\cos\psi) = \tfrac12(3\cos^2\psi - 1)$ is $+1$ at $\psi = 0$ and $\psi = \pi$ and $-\tfrac12$ at $\psi = \pi/2$. The ratio $-2 : 1 : 1$ is the ratio $1 : -\tfrac12 : -\tfrac12$ with the overall sign of the potential, and its trace is zero because $r'^2P_2(\cos\psi)$ is a harmonic polynomial — the external mass is elsewhere, so its potential satisfies Laplace's equation inside the body, term by term (problem P11).

Checking the Hessian. $\partial_i\partial_j\big[3(\hat{\mathbf s}\cdot\vec r\,{}')^2 - r'^2\big] = 6s_is_j - 2\delta_{ij}$, so multiplying by $-GM_{\mathrm e}/2d^3$ gives $(GM_{\mathrm e}/d^3)(\delta_{ij} - 3s_is_j)$. Its trace is $(GM_{\mathrm e}/d^3)(3 - 3) = 0$. Its eigenvectors are $\hat{\mathbf s}$ with eigenvalue $(1-3) = -2$, and anything perpendicular with eigenvalue $1$.

Why the $n=1$ term is the one that leaves. It gives a force per unit mass $-\nabla(-GM_{\mathrm e}r'\cos\psi/d^2) = (GM_{\mathrm e}/d^2)\hat{\mathbf s}$, the same vector at every point of the body. In a frame falling freely with the body's centre, that acceleration is subtracted off everywhere. Unit 30 makes this the equivalence principle: the $n=1$ term is exactly what a change of frame can remove, and the $n \ge 2$ terms are exactly what it cannot. The tidal tensor is what survives, and unit 33 will identify it with the Riemann curvature tensor's Newtonian limit.

why two, in one line

The arrows are symmetric under reflection through the plane perpendicular to $\hat{\mathbf s}$: the far side is pushed away from $M_{\mathrm e}$ exactly as hard as the near side is pulled towards it. Nothing in the picture says which side the mass is on, and that is the evenness of $P_2$, drawn. The naive picture — "the Moon pulls the near ocean away from the Earth and the Earth away from the far ocean" — is correct but is not an explanation; it is a restatement. The explanation is that the first non-removable term of the expansion is even in $\cos\psi$, so it cannot distinguish the two ends.

The energy of a quadrupole in a tidal field

Turn it around: a body of mass $m$ with a quadrupole $Q$, and a distant point mass $M_{\mathrm e}$ at $d\,\hat{\mathbf s}$. The mutual energy is $M_{\mathrm e}$ times the body's potential at $d\,\hat{\mathbf s}$:

$$U = -\frac{GM_{\mathrm e}m}{d} - \frac{GM_{\mathrm e}}{2d^3}\,Q(\hat{\mathbf s},\hat{\mathbf s}) - \cdots$$

The second term depends on the body's orientation, so there is a torque.

the torque

For a body with principal moments $A < B$ about axes in the orbit plane, and $\gamma$ the angle by which the long axis is turned ahead of $\hat{\mathbf s}$,

$$N = -\frac{\partial U}{\partial\gamma} = -\frac{3GM_{\mathrm e}}{2d^3}(B - A)\sin 2\gamma .$$
this is not the tide raising the bulge

$Q$ here is a permanent quadrupole. The tide-raised part is a separate, induced one, and dissipation in it is what drove the body to $\gamma = 0$.

Full derivation of the torque

In principal axes the long axis is $\hat e_1$, and with the long axis turned by $\gamma$ ahead of $\hat{\mathbf s}$ the body sees $\hat{\mathbf s} = (\cos\gamma,-\sin\gamma,0)$ in its $1$–$2$ plane, so turning the body by $\delta\gamma$ about its polar axis increases $\gamma$ by $\delta\gamma$. Then $Q(\hat{\mathbf s},\hat{\mathbf s}) = Q_{11}\cos^2\gamma + Q_{22}\sin^2\gamma$ with $Q_{11} = B + C - 2A$ and $Q_{22} = C + A - 2B$ from section 2. Differentiating,

$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\gamma}Q(\hat{\mathbf s},\hat{\mathbf s}) = \sin2\gamma\,(Q_{22} - Q_{11}) = \sin2\gamma\,\big[(C+A-2B) - (B+C-2A)\big] = -3(B-A)\sin2\gamma$.

Hence $\partial U/\partial\gamma = -\frac{GM_{\mathrm e}}{2d^3}\cdot(-3(B-A)\sin2\gamma) = \frac{3GM_{\mathrm e}}{2d^3}(B-A)\sin2\gamma$, and $N = -\partial U/\partial\gamma$ is the slide's expression. It is the torque on the body about its polar axis because a rotation by $\delta\gamma$ moves each element by $\delta\gamma\,\hat{\mathbf z}\times\vec r\,{}'$, and the work the forces do in that displacement, $N\,\delta\gamma$, is minus the change in $U$ (unit 04). Note that $C$ drops out entirely: only the asymmetry within the orbit plane produces a torque about the spin axis.

Dimension check. $[GM_{\mathrm e}(B-A)/d^3] = \mathrm{m^3s^{-2}\cdot kg\,m^2/m^3} = \mathrm{kg\,m^2s^{-2}}$: an energy, and a torque has the same dimensions. Limit check. $B = A$ gives $N = 0$: an axially symmetric body feels no torque about its symmetry axis, which is the same statement that made $\dot L_z = 0$ exact in section 4.

The expansion of $|\vec d - \vec r\,{}'|^{-1}$ used here is the multipole expansion with the roles of the two points swapped, which is legitimate because $r' < d$: the body is small compared with its distance from $M_{\mathrm e}$. For the Moon, $r'/d \le 1737/384400 = 0.0045$, so the quadrupole term is smaller than the monopole by $(r'/d)^2 = 2\times10^{-5}$, and each further term by another factor $0.0045$.

How the arrows were drawn. At each end of the long axis, $\vec r\,{}' = \pm\ell(\cos\gamma,\sin\gamma)$ in a plane where $M_{\mathrm e}$ lies along $-\hat{\mathbf x}$. The tidal acceleration $\frac{GM_{\mathrm e}}{d^3}\big(3(\hat{\mathbf s}\cdot\vec r\,{}')\hat{\mathbf s} - \vec r\,{}'\big)$ there is $\pm\frac{GM_{\mathrm e}\ell}{d^3}(2\cos\gamma,\,-\sin\gamma)$, and its component across the long axis is $\mp\frac{3GM_{\mathrm e}\ell}{2d^3}\sin2\gamma$: opposite at the two ends, at opposite ends — so the two contributions to the torque have the same sign, clockwise in the figure, towards $\gamma = 0$. Integrated over the whole body with its mass distribution, this is the $N$ on The energy of a quadrupole in a tidal field, and the integral is where $B - A$ comes from.

A round body has as much mass across the axis as along it, and the torques on the two sets cancel: $B = A$, $N = 0$. Only the excess along the long axis is pulled round.

10 · tides · example

Why the Moon keeps one face towards us

worked example 4 · the libration period

Set up. Let $\gamma$ be the angle by which the Moon's long axis is turned ahead of the Earth direction. In a frame rotating with the mean motion $n$, rotation about the polar axis obeys $C\ddot\gamma = N$ with $N$ from The energy of a quadrupole in a tidal field:

$C\ddot\gamma = -\dfrac{3GM_{\oplus}}{2d^3}(B-A)\sin2\gamma$.

Linearise for small $\gamma$, using $\sin2\gamma \approx 2\gamma$ and $GM_{\oplus}/d^3 \approx n^2$:

$\ddot\gamma = -3\,\dfrac{B-A}{C}\,n^2\,\gamma \;\Longrightarrow\; \omega_{\mathrm{lib}} = n\sqrt{3\dfrac{B-A}{C}}$.

Numbers. $(B-A)/C = 2.278\times10^{-4}$, so $\sqrt{3(B-A)/C} = 0.026142$, and with $n = 2\pi/27.3216\ \mathrm{d}$:

$T_{\mathrm{lib}} = 27.3216/0.026142 = 1045\ \mathrm{days} = 2.86$ years.

checks

Dimensions. $(B-A)/C$ is dimensionless, so $\omega_{\mathrm{lib}}$ has the dimensions of $n$.

Limit. $B \to A$ gives $\omega_{\mathrm{lib}} \to 0$: a round Moon has no preferred face and does not librate.

Magnitude. $\omega_{\mathrm{lib}} \ll n$, so the Moon rocks slowly about a rotation that is already locked — and the observed free libration in longitude does have a period close to three years.

Tidal locking is this pendulum plus friction. Dissipation in the induced tide drains rotational energy at nearly fixed angular momentum until $\gamma$ settles at the bottom of the well.

Where $(B-A)/C$ comes from. It is measured — from the Moon's physical librations, tracked by laser ranging to the retroreflectors left there. The standard symbol for it is $\gamma_{\mathrm M} = (B-A)/C = 2.278\times10^{-4}$; the companion $\beta = (C-A)/B = 6.31\times10^{-4}$. Both are far larger than the present tidal field could raise, which is the evidence that the Moon's shape is a frozen relic of a time when it was closer.

The approximation $GM_\oplus/d^3 \approx n^2$. Exactly, $n^2 = G(M_\oplus + M_{\mathrm M})/d^3$, so using $GM_\oplus$ alone understates $n^2$ by about the mass ratio $1/81$ (the Sun's pull on the lunar orbit shifts it a little further). Numerically $GM_\oplus/d^3 = 7.018\times10^{-12}$ against the observed $n^2 = 7.085\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}$: agreement to $0.95\%$, and the libration period shifts by half that. Well inside the accuracy of a linearised rigid-body model of a body with a fluid core.

Why $C\ddot\gamma = N$ is legitimate here. Rotation about a fixed principal axis is the one case of rigid-body dynamics that needs no machinery: for rotation at rate $\Omega_z$ about the $z$-axis each mass element has $\dot x = -\Omega_zy$, $\dot y = \Omega_zx$, so $L_z = \int\rho\,(x\dot y - y\dot x) = \Omega_z\int\rho\,(x^2+y^2) = C\Omega_z$. Here $\Omega_z = n + \dot\gamma$ and $n$ is constant, and unit 08's $\dot{\vec L} = \vec N$, summed over the mass elements — the internal forces are central and cancel in pairs, their torques with them — applied to that component gives the equation. Unit 16 does the general case, where the axis is not fixed and the inertia tensor has to be carried around.

Why locking is the endpoint. At fixed total angular momentum, synchronous rotation minimises the energy — spinning faster or slower than the orbit costs rotational kinetic energy with no compensating gain. Dissipation removes energy monotonically, so the system slides to that minimum and stays. The same argument explains Mercury's $3:2$ spin–orbit resonance, which is a different minimum reached because Mercury's eccentricity makes $\gamma = 0$ not the only stable well.

The technique, in one sentence: expand in a small parameter, average over the fast angle, and keep what does not cancel. Mercury's $43''$ per century in unit 37 is that sentence used once more.

The historical thread is worth seeing whole. Legendre introduced the polynomials in 1782 to expand the attraction of a spheroid — the problem of this unit, in its original form. Laplace turned them into the theory of spherical harmonics within a decade, for the figure of the Earth and the shape of Saturn's rings. Clairaut's flattening formula is from 1743, forty years earlier, in his Théorie de la figure de la terre — written after he had himself been on the Lapland expedition of 1736–37, one of the two (the other went to Peru) sent to settle whether the Earth is flattened at the poles or drawn out there. The perturbation machinery is Lagrange's and Laplace's, developed to explain the "great inequality" of Jupiter and Saturn, which turned out to be a near-resonance with an $880$-year period.

And the unit's own punchline is twentieth century: the sun-synchronous orbit was first proposed in the 1950s and first flown in the 1960s, and it works because somebody noticed that a nuisance — the node drift that ruined every orbit's predictability — is exactly what is needed if you want the Sun angle to stay put. The whole of section 4 is the arithmetic behind that observation.

Mercury is the one to look forward to. Unit 08's Binet equation $u'' + u = GM/L^2$ becomes $u'' + u = GM/L^2 + 3GMu^2/c^2$ in general relativity, and the extra term is a $1/r^3$ perturbation of exactly the kind treated on the apsidal precession slide. The oblateness of the Sun contributes to the same number, which is why measuring the solar $J_2$ mattered to the test.
