Checkpoint. You derive the cycloid from scratch — the descent-time functional, the Beltrami identity, the integration, both constants from the endpoints — and you produce a functional whose Euler–Lagrange solution is stationary but not a minimum, together with the test that detects it: a conjugate point inside the interval, or an explicit variation making the second variation negative.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Eleven problems, about six hours. Work them on paper first. Notation as in the deck: \(J[y] = \int_a^b F(x,y,y')\,\mathrm dx\), subscripts are partial derivatives, and the Euler–Lagrange equation is \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}F_{y'} = F_y\). For the bead, \(y\) is measured downwards; for chains, strings and beams it is stated each time.

Constants: \(g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\); Young's modulus of steel \(E = 200\ \mathrm{GPa}\), density of steel \(7850\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\).

Deriving Euler–Lagrange equations, and what they miss

P1 Four functionals, and one the equation cannot see medium

exercises: Euler–Lagrange and Beltrami by hand, null Lagrangians, extremals that fail to exist

  1. \(J[y] = \int_0^1\big(y'^2 + k^2y^2\big)\,\mathrm dx\), \(y(0) = 0\), \(y(1) = 1\), with \(k \gt 0\) a constant. Find the extremal, evaluate \(y(\tfrac12)\) at \(k = 2\), and show that \(k\to0\) gives the straight line.
  2. \(J[y] = \int\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,/\,y\;\mathrm dx\) on curves with \(y \gt 0\). Show that the extremals are arcs of circles centred on the \(x\)-axis. Which extremals can a graph \(y(x)\) not represent?
  3. \(J[y] = \int_a^b y\,y'\,\mathrm dx\) with fixed ends. Show that every curve satisfies the Euler–Lagrange equation, and explain why.
  4. A soap film spans two coaxial rings of radius \(R\) at \(x = \pm d\); its area is \(2\pi\int_{-d}^{d}y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,\mathrm dx\) with \(y(\pm d) = R\). Show that the extremals are \(y = c\cosh(x/c)\), and that one meets the rings only if \(d/R \le 0.66274\). For \(d/R = 0.5\) find both extremals and compare their areas with \(2\pi R^2\), the area of two flat discs. For \(d/R = 1\), what happens?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1's Euler–Lagrange equation has the shape of unit 00's third case, force a function of position, so energy-style quadrature solves it; parts 2 and 4 have no explicit \(x\), so the Beltrami identity gives a first-order equation at once; part 3 is a statement that an expression vanishes identically. Part 4's existence question is about whether a transcendental equation for \(c\) has a root.

Rung 2

For part 3, write \(yy'\) as the derivative of something. For part 4, the endpoint condition is \(R = c\cosh(d/c)\). Put \(t = d/c\): it becomes \(R/d = \cosh t/t\), so ask for the minimum value of \(\cosh t/t\) on \(t \gt 0\).

Rung 3

Part 2's first line: \(F - y'F_{y'} = \dfrac{1}{y\sqrt{1+y'^2}}\), so \(y^2(1+y'^2) = C^2\), and \(y^2 + (yy')^2 = C^2\) is the equation of a circle once you notice \(yy' = \tfrac12(y^2)'\).

Worked solution

Idea. Each part is the same two moves — compute \(F_y\) and \(F_{y'}\), or the Beltrami combination when \(x\) is absent — and then a question about what the equation does not say.

1. \(F_y = 2k^2y\), \(F_{y'} = 2y'\), so Euler–Lagrange is \(y'' = k^2y\) (and \(F_{y'y'} = 2 \ne 0\), so the extremal is \(C^2\) by P3). Multiply by \(y'\) and integrate, as in unit 00: \(y'^2 = k^2y^2 + E\) for a constant \(E\), and at \(x = 0\) this says \(E = y'(0)^2 \ge 0\). Try \(E = k^2b^2\) with \(b \gt 0\); then \(y'\) never vanishes, and is positive since \(y(1) \gt y(0)\). Separate and substitute \(y = b\sinh s\): then \(\mathrm dy/\sqrt{k^2y^2 + k^2b^2} = \mathrm ds/k = \mathrm dx\), so \(s = kx\) (as \(s = 0\) at \(x = 0\)), and \(y(1) = 1\) fixes \(b = 1/\sinh k\): \[ y(x) = \frac{\sinh kx}{\sinh k}. \] This is the only extremal. If \(y_1\), \(y_2\) are two, \(w = y_1 - y_2\) has \(w'' = k^2w\) and \(w(0) = w(1) = 0\); integrating \(w'^2\) by parts, \(\int_0^1(w'^2 + k^2w^2)\,\mathrm dx = [ww']_0^1 - \int_0^1 w(w'' - k^2w)\,\mathrm dx = 0\), so \(w \equiv 0\). So the other choice of constant, \(E = 0\), needs no separate treatment: whatever it would give, it cannot be a second extremal. At \(k = 2\): \(y(\tfrac12) = \sinh 1/\sinh 2 = 1.17520/3.62686 = 0.32403\), below the straight line's \(0.5\): the \(k^2y^2\) term penalises height, so the curve sags towards zero. As \(k\to0\), \(\sinh kx/\sinh k = (kx + O(k^3))/(k + O(k^3)) \to x\): the straight line, the extremal of \(\int y'^2\).

2. \(F = \sqrt{1+p^2}/y\) has no \(x\). \(F_{p} = p/(y\sqrt{1+p^2})\), and \(F - pF_p = \big((1+p^2) - p^2\big)/(y\sqrt{1+p^2}) = 1/(y\sqrt{1+p^2})\). Setting it equal to \(1/C\) with \(C \gt 0\): \(y^2(1 + y'^2) = C^2\), that is \(y^2 + (yy')^2 = C^2\). Since \(y^2 \le C^2\), write \(yy' = \pm\sqrt{C^2 - y^2}\); then \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\sqrt{C^2-y^2} = -yy'/\sqrt{C^2-y^2} = \mp1\), so \(\sqrt{C^2 - y^2} = \mp(x - x_0)\), and squaring, \[ (x - x_0)^2 + y^2 = C^2 : \] a circle of radius \(C\) centred at \((x_0, 0)\) on the axis. Check: differentiating, \(yy' = -(x-x_0)\), and \(y^2(1 + y'^2) = y^2 + (x-x_0)^2 = C^2\) ✓. The graph formulation misses the vertical lines \(x = \text{const}\), which are extremals too — the limit \(C\to\infty\) of the circles. Each upper semicircle is itself a graph over \((x_0 - C, x_0 + C)\), its vertical tangents occurring only where it meets the axis, outside the region \(y \gt 0\). (This is the upper half-plane model of hyperbolic geometry; the circles are its geodesics.)

3. \(F = yy'\): \(F_y = y'\) and \(F_{y'} = y\), so \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}F_{y'} - F_y = y' - y' = 0\) for every curve. The reason: \(yy' = \big(\tfrac12y^2\big)'\), so \(J[y] = \tfrac12\big(y(b)^2 - y(a)^2\big)\) depends only on the fixed endpoint values, and its first variation vanishes in every direction. An integrand that is a total derivative is a null Lagrangian; adding one to \(F\) changes no Euler–Lagrange equation. Unit 12 uses exactly this freedom.

4. Up to the constant \(2\pi\), \(F = y\sqrt{1+p^2}\) has no \(x\), and \(F - pF_p = y/\sqrt{1+p^2} = c\). This is the deck's catenary computation with \(\mu = 0\) and \(\rho g = 1\), so \(y = c\cosh((x-x_0)/c)\), and \(x_0 = 0\) by the symmetry of the rings. The endpoint condition is \(R = c\cosh(d/c)\). With \(t = d/c\), this reads \(R/d = \cosh t/t =: m(t)\). Now \(m(t)\to\infty\) as \(t\to0\) and as \(t\to\infty\), and \(m'(t) = (t\sinh t - \cosh t)/t^2\) vanishes where \(t\tanh t = 1\), which has the single positive root \(t_* = 1.19968\) (the left side increases from \(0\) to \(\infty\)). There \(m(t_*) = 1.50888\). So a root exists if and only if \(R/d \ge 1.50888\), i.e. \(d/R \le 1/1.50888 = 0.66274\).

At \(d/R = 0.5\) (take \(R = 1\)), \(1 = c\cosh(0.5/c)\) has two roots: \(c = 0.84834\) and \(c = 0.23509\). Their areas, \(2\pi\int_{-0.5}^{0.5}c\cosh^2(x/c)\,\mathrm dx\), are \(5.99180\) and \(6.84566\) (in units of \(R^2\)), against \(2\pi = 6.28319\) for the two discs. The shallow catenary beats the discs; the deep one is an extremal that loses even to them — a first sign, before section 5 of the deck, that extremal does not mean minimal.

At \(d/R = 1\) there is no extremal at all. Yet the area can be made as close to \(2\pi R^2\) as desired: take a curve that drops almost vertically from \(R\) to nearly \(0\) near each ring and runs along close to the axis in between. The limit — two discs joined by a segment of the axis, the Goldschmidt curve — is not a graph with \(y \gt 0\), so the Euler–Lagrange equation, which only sees admissible graphs, cannot find it. Physically the film pops and leaves two discs.

Check. Dimensions in part 4: \(c\) is a length, \(d/c\) is a number ✓. Limit in part 1: \(k\to0\) recovers the line ✓. Limit in part 4: as \(d\to0\) the shallow root has \(c\to R\), the film becomes a short cylinder of area \(\approx 2\pi R\cdot 2d \to 0\), far below the discs' \(2\pi R^2\) ✓.

P2 The cycloid from scratch routine

exercises: setting up a functional from physics, the Beltrami identity, the half-angle substitution, fixing constants, a number

A bead slides without friction, from rest, from \(A = (0,0)\) to \(B = (1\ \mathrm m, 1\ \mathrm m)\), with \(y\) measured downwards.

  1. Write down the descent-time functional \(T[y]\), saying where each factor comes from.
  2. Use the Beltrami identity to reduce the Euler–Lagrange equation to \(y(1+y'^2) = 2a\).
  3. Integrate it to the cycloid \(x = a(\theta - \sin\theta)\), \(y = a(1-\cos\theta)\).
  4. Find \(\theta_B\), \(a\) and the descent time, and compare with the straight wire. Does this cycloid dip below \(B\)?
  5. Free fall through \(1\) m takes less time than the cycloid. Why is that no contradiction?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a curve given parametrically, two constants, and three times in seconds. Everything follows the deck's section 3; the point is to do it without looking.

Rung 2

\(\mathrm dt = \mathrm ds/v\), and energy conservation gives \(v\) as a function of \(y\) alone. For part 3 separate variables and substitute \(y = 2a\sin^2(\theta/2)\). For part 4, the ratio \(x/y\) at \(B\) does not involve \(a\).

Rung 3

First line of part 4: solve \((\theta - \sin\theta)/(1 - \cos\theta) = 1\) for \(\theta\in(0,2\pi)\) — the root is a little above \(2.4\).

Worked solution

Idea. Energy gives the speed as a function of depth, the functional has no \(x\), Beltrami gives a first-order equation, and a half-angle substitution integrates it.

1. The wire's reaction is normal to the velocity and does no work, so \(\tfrac12mv^2 = mgy\) and \(v = \sqrt{2gy}\). Arc length along a graph is \(\mathrm ds = \sqrt{1+y'^2}\,\mathrm dx\). So \[ T[y] = \int_0^{1}\sqrt{\frac{1+y'^2}{2gy}}\;\mathrm dx,\qquad y(0) = 0,\ y(1) = 1 . \]

2. \(F = \sqrt{1+p^2}/\sqrt{2gy}\) has no \(x\). \(F_p = p/(\sqrt{1+p^2}\sqrt{2gy})\) and \(F - pF_p = 1/(\sqrt{1+p^2}\sqrt{2gy})\), a positive constant, which we call \(1/\sqrt{4ga}\). Squaring and inverting: \(2gy(1+y'^2) = 4ga\), i.e. \(y(1+y'^2) = 2a\).

3. \(y'^2 = (2a-y)/y\); on the descending part \(\mathrm dx = \sqrt{y/(2a-y)}\,\mathrm dy\). With \(y = 2a\sin^2(\theta/2) = a(1-\cos\theta)\): \(\sqrt{y/(2a-y)} = \tan(\theta/2)\) and \(\mathrm dy = 2a\sin(\theta/2)\cos(\theta/2)\,\mathrm d\theta\), so \(\mathrm dx = 2a\sin^2(\theta/2)\,\mathrm d\theta = a(1-\cos\theta)\,\mathrm d\theta\). Integrating from \(A\), where \(\theta = 0\): \(x = a(\theta - \sin\theta)\).

4. At \(B\), \(x/y = 1\): \((\theta_B - \sin\theta_B)/(1-\cos\theta_B) = 1\), whose unique root in \((0, 2\pi)\) (the left side is strictly increasing — the deck's constants slide) is \(\theta_B = 2.41201\), i.e. \(138.20^\circ\). Then \(a = 1/(1-\cos\theta_B) = 0.57292\) m. Along the cycloid \(\mathrm dt = \sqrt{a/g}\,\mathrm d\theta\), so \[ T = \theta_B\sqrt{a/g} = 2.41201\times0.24167 = 0.58290\ \mathrm s . \] The straight wire has length \(\sqrt2\) m and constant acceleration \(g/\sqrt2\), so \(T = \sqrt{2\cdot 2/(g\cdot1)} = 0.63855\) s: the cycloid is \(8.7\%\) quicker. Since \(\theta_B \lt \pi\), the bead is still descending at \(B\): this cycloid does not dip below \(B\) (its lowest point, \(2a = 1.14583\) m deep, lies beyond).

5. Free fall through \(1\) m takes \(\sqrt{2/g} = 0.45152\) s, but it ends at \((0, 1)\), not at \(B\). It is not an admissible curve, so it is not a competitor. It is the limit \(X/Y\to0\) of the brachistochrone, checked on the deck.

Check. Dimensions: \(a\) in metres, \(\sqrt{a/g}\) in seconds ✓. Back-substitution: \(y' = \cot(\theta/2)\) and \(y(1+y'^2) = a(1-\cos\theta)/\sin^2(\theta/2) = 2a\) ✓. Ordering: \(0.45152 \lt 0.58290 \lt 0.63855\) s, free fall fastest, line slowest ✓.

Another way

Because \(\theta_B \lt \pi\), the curve is also a graph \(x(y)\), and \(T = \int_0^1\sqrt{(1+x'^2)/(2gy)}\,\mathrm dy\) has no \(x\) at all. So the Euler–Lagrange equation says at once that \(F_{x'} = x'/\sqrt{2gy(1+x'^2)}\) is constant — the same first integral, with no Beltrami needed. Better: \(F\) is strictly convex in \(x'\) (\(F_{x'x'} = (2gy)^{-1/2}(1+x'^2)^{-3/2} \gt 0\)) and does not depend on \(x\), so for any admissible \(\eta\) convexity gives \(F(y, x'+\eta') \ge F(y,x') + F_{x'}\eta'\) pointwise, and integrating, \(T[x+\eta] \ge T[x] + \delta T[x;\eta] = T[x]\). The extremal is the global minimum among curves that are graphs over \(y\) — a complete proof, available because the endpoint is before the bottom of the arch.

P3 Proof: du Bois-Reymond, and when \(y''\) comes back medium

exercises: the fundamental lemmas, the implicit function theorem, regularity of extremals

  1. Let \(M, N\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) with \(\int_a^b(M\eta + N\eta')\,\mathrm dx = 0\) for every \(\eta\in C^1_0[a,b]\). Prove that \(N\) is \(C^1\) and \(N' = M\).
  2. (Hilbert.) Let \(F\) be \(C^2\), let \(y\in C^1\) be an extremal, and suppose \(F_{y'y'}\big(x_0, y(x_0), y'(x_0)\big)\ne0\). Prove that \(y\) is \(C^2\) near \(x_0\).
  3. Show that \(y = 0\) for \(x\le 0\), \(y = x^2\) for \(x \gt 0\) minimises \(J[y] = \int_{-1}^{1}y^2(2x - y')^2\,\mathrm dx\) with \(y(-1) = 0\), \(y(1) = 1\), and is not \(C^2\). Which hypothesis of part 2 fails, and where?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 reduces to the du Bois-Reymond lemma of the deck; part 2 is one application of the implicit function theorem of unit 04 to an equation whose unknown is the slope; part 3 is a direct check plus a look at \(F_{y'y'}\).

Rung 2

Part 1: move the derivative off \(\eta\) the other way — integrate \(M\eta\) by parts using an antiderivative of \(M\), so that everything is multiplied by \(\eta'\). Part 2: by part 1, \(x\mapsto F_{y'}(x, y(x), y'(x))\) is \(C^1\); call it \(\Pi(x)\).

Rung 3

Part 2's first line: define \(G(x,p) = F_{y'}\big(x, y(x), p\big) - \Pi(x)\). It is \(C^1\) in \((x,p)\), vanishes at \(p = y'(x)\), and \(\partial_pG = F_{y'y'}\ne0\) at \((x_0, y'(x_0))\).

Worked solution

Idea. Integrate by parts in the direction that needs no unknown derivative; then the slope of the extremal is defined implicitly by a \(C^1\) equation, and the implicit function theorem makes it \(C^1\).

1. Let \(A(x) = \int_a^xM(t)\,\mathrm dt\), which is \(C^1\) with \(A' = M\). For \(\eta\in C^1_0\), \(\int_a^bM\eta\,\mathrm dx = [A\eta]_a^b - \int_a^bA\eta'\,\mathrm dx = -\int_a^bA\eta'\,\mathrm dx\), since \(\eta(a) = \eta(b) = 0\). The hypothesis becomes \(\int_a^b(N - A)\eta'\,\mathrm dx = 0\) for all \(\eta\in C^1_0\). \(N - A\) is continuous, so the du Bois-Reymond lemma makes it a constant \(k\). Hence \(N = A + k\) is \(C^1\) with \(N' = A' = M\). ∎

2. The first variation of an extremal vanishes: \(\int(F_y\eta + F_{y'}\eta') = 0\) for all \(\eta\in C^1_0\), with \(F_y\) and \(F_{y'}\) evaluated along \(y\) and so continuous in \(x\). By part 1, \(\Pi(x) = F_{y'}(x,y(x),y'(x))\) is \(C^1\). Define \(G(x,p) = F_{y'}(x, y(x), p) - \Pi(x)\) near \((x_0, p_0)\), \(p_0 = y'(x_0)\). Since \(F_{y'}\) is \(C^1\) (as \(F\) is \(C^2\)) and \(y\), \(\Pi\) are \(C^1\), \(G\) is \(C^1\). It satisfies \(G(x, y'(x)) = 0\) for every \(x\), and \(\partial_pG(x_0,p_0) = F_{y'y'} \ne 0\). The implicit function theorem gives neighbourhoods \(U\ni x_0\), \(V\ni p_0\) and a \(C^1\) function \(\pi : U\to V\) such that, for \(x\in U\) and \(p\in V\), \(G(x,p) = 0\) if and only if \(p = \pi(x)\). Because \(y'\) is continuous, \(y'(x)\in V\) for \(x\) near \(x_0\), and \(G(x,y'(x)) = 0\); by uniqueness \(y'(x) = \pi(x)\) there. So \(y'\) is \(C^1\), and \(y\) is \(C^2\) near \(x_0\). ∎

3. The integrand is a square times a square, so \(J \ge 0\) always. For the given curve: on \([-1,0]\), \(y = 0\) kills the integrand; on \((0,1]\), \(y' = 2x\) makes \(2x - y' = 0\). So \(J = 0\), the least possible value, and the curve is a global minimiser. It is \(C^1\): \(y'(0^-) = 0 = y'(0^+)\). It is not \(C^2\): \(y''\) jumps from \(0\) to \(2\) at \(x = 0\). Part 2's hypothesis fails exactly there: \(F_{y'y'} = 2y^2\), which is \(0\) on the whole of \([-1,0]\), including at the kink. Where \(y \gt 0\) the hypothesis holds and \(y\) is indeed smooth.

Check. Endpoints: \(y(-1) = 0\), \(y(1) = 1\) ✓. Consistency: along the minimiser, \(\Pi = F_{y'} = -2y^2(2x - y') = 0\) identically, which is \(C^1\) as part 1 says it must be, even though \(y\) is not \(C^2\) — so part 1's conclusion is about \(F_{y'}\), not about \(y'\).

Fermat, free ends and multipliers

P4 Snell from Fermat, and a mirage medium

exercises: Fermat's principle, the Beltrami identity as Snell's law, a catenary in optics, a number

Take Fermat's principle: a light ray makes the optical path \(\int n\,\mathrm ds\) stationary, \(n\) the refractive index.

  1. For two media of indices \(n_1\), \(n_2\) meeting on a plane, derive \(n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2\).
  2. For a medium stratified in height, \(n = n(y)\), show that along a ray \(n(y)\cos\alpha\) is constant, \(\alpha\) the angle of the ray above the horizontal, and explain why this is part 1 applied layer by layer.
  3. Over a hot road, \(n(y) = n_0(1 + \kappa y)\) for \(y \ge 0\) the height above the road, with \(\kappa \gt 0\). Solve for the rays.
  4. Take \(\kappa = 2.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm m^{-1}\) and an eye \(1.5\) m above the road. Find where the ray that just grazes the road touches it, and at what angle below the horizontal it reaches the eye. What does the driver see?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is ordinary calculus in one variable (the crossing point). Parts 2 and 3 are the Beltrami identity for \(F = n(y)\sqrt{1+y'^2}\), and the answer to part 3 is a curve you have met in the deck in quite another role. Part 4 is a distance in metres and an angle in degrees.

Rung 2

For a graph \(y(x)\), \(\cos\alpha = 1/\sqrt{1+y'^2}\). With \(u = 1+\kappa y\), the Beltrami equation becomes \(u = A\sqrt{1+y'^2}\) for a constant \(A\), and \(y' = u'/\kappa\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: \(u^2 = A^2\big(1 + u'^2/\kappa^2\big)\); try \(u = A\cosh s(x)\) and find \(s'\). For part 4, the grazing ray has its lowest point on the road, where it is horizontal, so \(A = 1\) there.

Worked solution

Idea. Stationary optical path, with no explicit \(x\), is exactly the Beltrami situation, and the Beltrami constant is Snell's invariant.

1. As on the deck's Snell slide: from \(P = (0, h_1)\) to \(Q = (d, -h_2)\) via \((x, 0)\), the optical path is \(n_1\sqrt{x^2+h_1^2} + n_2\sqrt{(d-x)^2+h_2^2}\); its derivative is \(n_1\sin\theta_1 - n_2\sin\theta_2\), angles measured from the normal. Setting it to zero gives Snell's law.

2. \(F = n(y)\sqrt{1+p^2}\) has no \(x\). \(F - pF_p = n(y)/\sqrt{1+p^2}\), which is therefore constant along a ray. With \(\tan\alpha = y'\), \(1/\sqrt{1+y'^2} = \cos\alpha\), so \(n\cos\alpha = \text{const}\). The angle from the vertical normal to the layers is \(\theta = \pi/2 - \alpha\), so this is \(n\sin\theta = \text{const}\): part 1 at every interface between thin layers, with the scaffolding removed — exactly Johann Bernoulli's argument for the bead, run in reverse.

3. \(n_0(1+\kappa y)/\sqrt{1+y'^2} = C\). Put \(u = 1 + \kappa y\) and \(A = C/n_0\): \(u = A\sqrt{1+y'^2}\), and since \(u' = \kappa y'\), \(u^2 = A^2(1 + u'^2/\kappa^2)\). Try \(u = A\cosh s\): then \(u' = A\sinh s\,s'\), and the equation becomes \(A^2\cosh^2 s = A^2 + A^4\sinh^2 s\,s'^2/\kappa^2\), i.e. \(s'^2 = \kappa^2/A^2\). So \(s = \kappa(x - x_0)/A\) and \[ 1 + \kappa y = A\cosh\!\Big(\frac{\kappa(x - x_0)}{A}\Big) : \] the rays are catenaries, bending upwards, towards the denser air. Check: \(y' = \sinh(\kappa(x-x_0)/A)\), \(\sqrt{1+y'^2} = \cosh(\cdot) = u/A\) ✓.

4. The grazing ray has its vertex on the road: \(y = 0\), \(y' = 0\) at \(x_0\), so \(A = 1\). Put \(x_0 = 0\): \(1 + \kappa y = \cosh(\kappa x)\). It reaches height \(h = 1.5\) m at \[ x = \frac{\operatorname{arcosh}(1 + \kappa h)}{\kappa} = \frac{\operatorname{arcosh}(1.00003)}{2.0\times10^{-5}} = 387.30\ \mathrm m, \] with slope \(\sinh(\kappa x) = 7.746\times10^{-3}\), i.e. the ray arrives travelling upwards at \(0.444^\circ\), so the eye receives it from \(0.444^\circ\) below the horizontal. Rays reaching the eye from steeper below than this left the road surface; rays from shallower below never touched the road — traced back, they bottom out above it and came from the sky. So the road looks like a sheet of reflected sky from about \(387\) m ahead: the mirage.

Check. Dimensions: \(\kappa x\) and \(\kappa h\) are pure numbers ✓. Small-angle limit: \(\cosh\kappa x\approx1 + \kappa^2x^2/2\) gives the parabola \(y = \kappa x^2/2\) and \(x = \sqrt{2h/\kappa} = 387.30\) m, agreeing to five figures ✓. Limit \(\kappa\to0\): \(x\to\infty\), no mirage in uniform air ✓.

P5 Free ends routine

exercises: natural boundary conditions, their reading as force balance, a number

  1. A string of length \(L\) under tension \(\tau\) carries a load \(f\) per unit length. Its left end is fixed at \(y(0) = 0\); its right end is a light ring free to slide on a frictionless vertical rod at \(x = L\). With \(y\) the downward deflection and small slopes, its energy is \(J[y] = \int_0^L\big(\tfrac12\tau y'^2 - fy\big)\,\mathrm dx\). Find the equilibrium shape and the ring's deflection. Evaluate for \(\tau = 50\) N, \(f = 2\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\), \(L = 1\) m.
  2. Explain the natural boundary condition as a statement about forces on the ring.
  3. The bead of P2 must now only reach the vertical line \(x = 1\) m, at any depth. Find the quickest wire, the depth at which it arrives, and the time; compare with P2.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each part is "Euler–Lagrange in the interior, plus \(F_{y'} = 0\) at the free end". Part 1 gives a parabola; part 3 a cycloid with one constant fixed by a condition on the slope.

Rung 2

For the string, \(F_{y'} = \tau y'\), so the ring end has \(y'(L) = 0\). For the bead, \(F_{y'} = y'/\sqrt{2gy(1+y'^2)}\), and its vanishing forces the slope to be zero where the wire meets the line.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: Euler–Lagrange reads \(\tau y'' = -f\). First line of part 3: \(y' = 0\) on the cycloid means \(\theta = \pi\), so \(1 = a\pi\).

Worked solution

Idea. A free end contributes one boundary condition that nobody imposed: the boundary term of the first variation must vanish by itself.

1. \(F = \tfrac12\tau y'^2 - fy\): \(F_y = -f\), \(F_{y'} = \tau y'\). Euler–Lagrange: \(\tau y'' = -f\). Natural condition at \(x = L\): \(\tau y'(L) = 0\). Integrating, \(\tau y' = f(L - x)\) (the constant chosen so that \(y'(L) = 0\)), and with \(y(0) = 0\), \[ y(x) = \frac{f}{\tau}\Big(Lx - \frac{x^2}{2}\Big),\qquad y(L) = \frac{fL^2}{2\tau} . \] With the numbers, \(y(L) = 2\times1/(2\times50) = 0.020\) m: the ring sits \(2.0\) cm down.

2. In the small-slope approximation the vertical component of the tension at the ring is \(\tau y'(L)\). The ring is massless and the rod frictionless, so the rod can push only horizontally: nothing can balance a vertical component, and it must vanish. The minimisation discovers the force balance on its own.

3. The quickest wire is still a cycloid from \(A\) (the Euler–Lagrange equation is unchanged), and the natural condition \(F_{y'} = 0\) at \(x = 1\) forces \(y' = 0\) there: the bead arrives horizontally, at the bottom of the arch, \(\theta = \pi\). So \(x = a\pi = 1\), \(a = 1/\pi = 0.31831\) m, arrival depth \(2a = 0.63662\) m, and \[ T = \pi\sqrt{a/g} = \sqrt{\pi\cdot 1/g} = 0.56590\ \mathrm s , \] against \(0.58290\) s for the fixed end \(B = (1,1)\) of P2. Freeing the end can only help, because the old answer is still a competitor; here it helps by \(0.0170\) s.

Check. Dimensions: \(fL^2/\tau\) is \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{m^2}/\mathrm N = \mathrm m\) ✓; \(\sqrt{L/g}\) is seconds ✓. Symmetry check on part 1: a string of length \(2L\) fixed at both ends under the same load sags as \(\frac{f}{2\tau}x(2L - x)\), whose slope is zero at the middle \(x = L\) and whose left half is exactly the answer — the ring on its rod is the mirror plane ✓. Limit: \(f\to0\) gives \(y\equiv0\) ✓.

P6 Dido's problem medium

exercises: the isoperimetric multiplier, the multiplier as a rate, a number

Among curves \(y(x) \ge 0\) from \((-d, 0)\) to \((d, 0)\) of fixed length \(\ell\), with \(2d \lt \ell \lt \pi d\), find the one enclosing the most area \(\int_{-d}^{d}y\,\mathrm dx\) with the \(x\)-axis.

  1. Show the extremal is an arc of a circle whose radius is the multiplier \(\lambda\).
  2. For \(d = 1\) m and \(\ell = 3\) m, find the arc's half-angle, its radius and the area.
  3. Show that \(\lambda = \mathrm dA^*/\mathrm d\ell\) is right geometrically: adding a sliver of length \(\delta\ell\) to the arc should add area \(R\,\delta\ell\). Why must \(\ell \lt \pi d\) be assumed?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a circle, two numbers from a transcendental equation, and a one-line geometric argument. The multiplier theorem of the deck applies to maxima as well as minima.

Rung 2

Extremise \(\int\big(y - \lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}\big)\,\mathrm dx\). It has no \(x\), so use Beltrami. For part 2, an arc of radius \(R\) and half-angle \(\alpha\) has chord \(2R\sin\alpha\) and length \(2R\alpha\).

Rung 3

First line: the Beltrami combination is \(y - \lambda/\sqrt{1+y'^2} = C\). Part 2 reduces to \(\sin\alpha/\alpha = 2d/\ell = 2/3\).

Worked solution

Idea. A multiplier turns a fixed-length problem into a free one; its value is the exchange rate between the constraint and the objective.

1. The arc is not a straight line (\(\ell \gt 2d\)), so it is not an extremal of length and the multiplier theorem applies: for some constant \(\lambda\), \(y\) is an extremal of \(\tilde F = y - \lambda\sqrt{1+p^2}\). With no \(x\), \(\tilde F - p\tilde F_p = y - \lambda\sqrt{1+p^2} + \lambda p^2/\sqrt{1+p^2} = y - \lambda/\sqrt{1+p^2} = C\). So \((y - C)^2(1 + y'^2) = \lambda^2\), which is P1 part 2's equation shifted by \(C\): its solutions are \((x - x_0)^2 + (y - C)^2 = \lambda^2\). The extremal is an arc of a circle of radius \(\lvert\lambda\rvert\), centre \((0, C)\) by symmetry; and \(y - C = \lambda/\sqrt{1+y'^2}\) is positive on the upper arc, so \(\lambda = R \gt 0\).

2. Chord \(2 = 2R\sin\alpha\) and length \(3 = 2R\alpha\) give \(\sin\alpha/\alpha = 2/3\), so \(\alpha = 1.49578\) rad \(= 85.70^\circ\), \(R = 1/\sin\alpha = 1.00282\) m, and the centre sits \(R\cos\alpha = 0.07516\) m below the chord. The area is the circular segment \(A^* = R^2(\alpha - \sin\alpha\cos\alpha) = 1.42907\ \mathrm m^2\).

3. Parametrise the optimal arcs by \(\alpha\), with \(R = d/\sin\alpha\): \(\ell = 2d\alpha/\sin\alpha\) and \(A^* = d^2(\alpha - \sin\alpha\cos\alpha)/\sin^2\alpha\). Differentiating, \(\mathrm d\ell/\mathrm d\alpha = 2d(\sin\alpha - \alpha\cos\alpha)/\sin^2\alpha\), and, using \(1 - \cos2\alpha = 2\sin^2\alpha\), \(\mathrm dA^*/\mathrm d\alpha = 2d^2\sin\alpha\,(\sin\alpha - \alpha\cos\alpha)/\sin^4\alpha\). The ratio is \[ \frac{\mathrm dA^*}{\mathrm d\ell} = \frac{d}{\sin\alpha} = R = \lambda, \] as the multiplier theorem promised; numerically, differentiating \(A^*(\ell)\) at \(\ell = 3\) gives \(1.00282\) m. Geometrically: lengthening the arc by \(\delta\ell\) while keeping it optimal is, to first order, pushing it outward, and a boundary of curvature radius \(R\) sweeps area \(R\) per unit of added length. The restriction \(\ell \lt \pi d\) keeps the arc less than a semicircle, so that it is a graph \(y(x)\) with no vertical tangent in \((-d, d)\); longer arcs are still circular, but only a parametric formulation can express them.

Check. Dimensions: \(\lambda\) is area per length, a length, and it is a radius ✓. Limit \(\ell\downarrow2d\): \(\sin\alpha/\alpha\to1\), \(\alpha\to0\), \(R\to\infty\), the arc flattens to the chord and the area to \(0\) ✓. Limit \(\ell\uparrow\pi d\): \(\alpha\to\pi/2\), \(R\to d\), a semicircle of area \(\pi d^2/2\) ✓.

exercises: the catenary with numbers, a transcendental equation, the multiplier as tension

A cable of mass \(1.5\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}\) and length \(12\) m hangs between two supports at the same height, \(10\) m apart. Measure \(y\) upwards from the supports.

  1. Find the catenary parameter \(c\), the sag, and the horizontal tension.
  2. Find the tension at the supports two ways: from the force balance, and from the multiplier \(\mu\) of the deck's worked example.
  3. Show in general that the tension at the supports equals \(\rho g\,(c + \text{sag})\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three forces in newtons and two lengths in metres. Everything follows from \(y = y_0 + c\cosh(x/c)\) with \(d = 5\) m.

Rung 2

The length condition is \(2c\sinh(d/c) = \ell\); solve it numerically (bisection is enough: the left side decreases in \(c\)). Horizontal tension is \(\rho gc\); the multiplier is \(\mu = -\rho gc\cosh(d/c)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(2c\sinh(5/c) = 12\) has its root between \(4.6\) and \(4.8\).

Worked solution

Idea. The shape is fixed by one transcendental equation; the tension then comes for free, and the multiplier is it.

1. \(2c\sinh(5/c) = 12\) gives \(c = 4.69542\) m. Then \(\cosh(5/c) = 1.62261\) and the sag is \(c(\cosh(d/c) - 1) = 2.92344\) m. The horizontal tension is \(H = \rho gc = 1.5\times9.81\times4.69542 = 69.093\) N.

2. Force balance: the vertical component at each support carries half the weight, \(\rho g\ell/2 = 88.29\) N, and the horizontal component is \(H\), so \(T = \sqrt{69.093^2 + 88.29^2} = 112.111\) N. Multiplier: \(\mu = -\rho gc\cosh(d/c) = -112.111\) N, and the deck's claim is \(T_{\text{support}} = -\mu\) ✓. (Differentiating the minimum energy \(U^*(\ell) = -327.203\) J numerically at \(\ell = 12\) m gives \(\mathrm dU^*/\mathrm d\ell = -112.111\) N too.)

3. Along the chain \(T = \rho g c\cosh(x/c)\) (the deck's tension slide), so at \(x = \pm d\), \(T = \rho g\,c\cosh(d/c) = \rho g\big(c + c(\cosh(d/c) - 1)\big) = \rho g(c + \text{sag})\). Numerically \(1.5\times9.81\times(4.69542 + 2.92344) = 112.111\) N ✓. The reading: the tension at any point equals the weight of a length \(c + (\text{height above the lowest point})\) of cable — the directrix of the catenary lies a distance \(c\) below its lowest point.

Check. Dimensions: \(\rho g c\) is \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,m\,s^{-2}\,m} = \mathrm N\) ✓. Two independent routes to \(112.111\) N agree ✓. Limit: a longer cable sags more and \(c\) falls, so \(H\) falls — slack cables pull less sideways ✓.

Geodesics, and stationary versus minimal

P8 Geodesics on a surface of revolution hard

exercises: arc length on a surface, a cyclic variable and its first integral, Clairaut's relation, a number

The surface is \(\big(r(z)\cos\phi,\ r(z)\sin\phi,\ z\big)\) with \(r \gt 0\) smooth. Describe a curve on it by \(\phi(z)\).

  1. Show that its length is \(\int\sqrt{1 + r'(z)^2 + r(z)^2\phi'(z)^2}\;\mathrm dz\).
  2. Derive a first integral of the geodesic equation, and show it is Clairaut's relation \(r\sin\psi = \text{const}\), where \(\psi\) is the angle between the curve and the meridian.
  3. Deduce that a geodesic with Clairaut constant \(c \gt 0\) never enters the region \(r \lt c\).
  4. On the cone \(r = z\tan\alpha\), a geodesic comes in from far away, turns at \(r = c\) and goes back out. Show that \(\phi\) advances by \(\pi/\sin\alpha\) in total. For \(\alpha = 20^\circ\), does the geodesic cross itself?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is unit 03's arc length; part 2 notices that \(\phi\) itself does not appear in the integrand; part 4 is one improper integral whose value is an angle.

Rung 2

When \(F\) has no \(\phi\), \(F_{\phi'}\) is constant. For the angle: the unit vector along the parallels is \((-\sin\phi, \cos\phi, 0)\); dot it with the unit tangent. For the cone, meridian length is \(\mathrm dm = \mathrm dr/\sin\alpha\), and along the curve \(r\,\mathrm d\phi = \sin\psi\,\mathrm ds\), \(\mathrm dm = \cos\psi\,\mathrm ds\).

Rung 3

First line of part 4: \(\dfrac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm dr} = \dfrac{\tan\psi}{r\sin\alpha} = \dfrac{c}{r\sin\alpha\sqrt{r^2 - c^2}}\), and \(\int_c^\infty\dfrac{c\,\mathrm dr}{r\sqrt{r^2-c^2}}\) yields to \(r = c\sec t\).

Worked solution

Idea. Rotational symmetry makes \(\phi\) absent from the length integrand, so its conjugate \(F_{\phi'}\) is conserved — and that conserved quantity is \(r\sin\psi\). Unit 13 will call this Noether's theorem for rotations; unit 36 re-derives it from a Killing vector.

1. \(\gamma(z) = (r\cos\phi, r\sin\phi, z)\) has \(\gamma' = (r'\cos\phi - r\phi'\sin\phi,\ r'\sin\phi + r\phi'\cos\phi,\ 1)\). Squaring and adding, the cross terms \(\mp2rr'\phi'\sin\phi\cos\phi\) cancel, leaving \(\lvert\gamma'\rvert^2 = r'^2 + r^2\phi'^2 + 1\). Unit 03's arc length gives the formula.

2. \(F = \sqrt{1 + r'^2 + r^2\phi'^2}\) depends on \(z\) and \(\phi'\) but not on \(\phi\), so Euler–Lagrange for \(\phi\) reads \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dz}F_{\phi'} = 0\): \[ F_{\phi'} = \frac{r^2\phi'}{\sqrt{1 + r'^2 + r^2\phi'^2}} = c . \] Now the unit tangent is \(\gamma'/\lvert\gamma'\rvert\) and \(e_\phi = (-\sin\phi, \cos\phi, 0)\) is the unit vector along the parallel, perpendicular to the meridian. Their dot product is \(r\phi'/\lvert\gamma'\rvert = \sin\psi\). So \(F_{\phi'} = r\cdot r\phi'/\lvert\gamma'\rvert = r\sin\psi\), and the first integral is \(r\sin\psi = c\).

3. \(\lvert\sin\psi\rvert\le1\) gives \(r \ge \lvert c\rvert\) all along the geodesic. At \(r = c\), \(\sin\psi = 1\): the geodesic runs along the parallel momentarily and turns back. (There \(\phi(z)\) has infinite slope, so the description by \(\phi(z)\) breaks down; the relation holds on each side and, by continuity along the curve, at the turning point.)

4. On the cone, \(z = r/\tan\alpha\), so the meridian element is \(\mathrm dm = \sqrt{\mathrm dr^2 + \mathrm dz^2} = \mathrm dr/\sin\alpha\). By part 1, \(\mathrm ds^2 = \mathrm dm^2 + r^2\mathrm d\phi^2\), and the angle \(\psi\) to the meridian splits the step as \(r\,\mathrm d\phi = \sin\psi\,\mathrm ds\), \(\mathrm dm = \cos\psi\,\mathrm ds\). Hence on the outgoing half \[ \frac{\mathrm d\phi}{\mathrm dr} = \frac{\tan\psi}{r\sin\alpha} = \frac{c}{r\sin\alpha\sqrt{r^2-c^2}},\qquad \sin\psi = c/r . \] With \(r = c\sec t\), \(t\in[0,\pi/2)\): \(\mathrm dr = c\sec t\tan t\,\mathrm dt\), \(\sqrt{r^2-c^2} = c\tan t\), so \(\int_c^\infty\frac{c\,\mathrm dr}{r\sqrt{r^2-c^2}} = \int_0^{\pi/2}\mathrm dt = \frac\pi2\). Incoming and outgoing halves each contribute \(\pi/(2\sin\alpha)\), so \[ \Delta\phi = \frac{\pi}{\sin\alpha} . \] At \(\alpha = 20^\circ\), \(\Delta\phi = 9.1854\) rad \(= 526.3^\circ \gt 360^\circ\): the geodesic wraps more than once round the cone and crosses itself. The threshold is \(\sin\alpha = \tfrac12\), \(\alpha = 30^\circ\), where \(\Delta\phi = 2\pi\) exactly.

Check. Limit \(\alpha\to\pi/2\): the cone flattens to a plane, \(\Delta\phi\to\pi\), and a straight line in the plane does turn through exactly \(\pi\) as seen from a point off it ✓. Independent check: cut the cone along a meridian and unroll it; it becomes a flat sector of angle \(2\pi\sin\alpha\) (circumference \(2\pi r\) laid out at slant distance \(r/\sin\alpha\)), a geodesic becomes a straight line turning through \(\pi\) in the plane, and a plane angle \(\beta\) corresponds to \(\phi = \beta/\sin\alpha\) — so \(\Delta\phi = \pi/\sin\alpha\) ✓. Dimensions: \(c\) is a length, \(\Delta\phi\) a pure number ✓.

P9 Stationary but not minimal: past the antipode hard

exercises: the second variation, Legendre, the accessory equation, conjugate points, a number

On the unit sphere use latitude \(\lambda\) and longitude \(\phi\), and describe curves as \(\lambda(\phi)\), \(\phi\in[0,\ell]\), with \(\lambda(0) = \lambda(\ell) = 0\).

  1. Show the length is \(L[\lambda] = \int_0^\ell\sqrt{\lambda'^2 + \cos^2\lambda}\;\mathrm d\phi\) and that the equator \(\lambda\equiv0\) is an extremal for every \(\ell\).
  2. Show that \(\delta^2L[0;\eta] = \int_0^\ell(\eta'^2 - \eta^2)\,\mathrm d\phi\), and that the Legendre condition holds.
  3. Solve the accessory equation, find the point conjugate to \(\phi = 0\), and identify it geometrically. Exhibit the family of extremals it comes from.
  4. Take \(\ell = 3\pi/2\). Find a variation with \(\delta^2L \lt 0\), and compute the actual length of \(\lambda_\varepsilon = \varepsilon\sin(2\phi/3)\) at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\).
  5. For \(\ell \lt \pi\), prove that \(\delta^2L[0;\eta] \gt 0\) for every non-zero \(\eta\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

This is the deck's \(\int(y'^2 - y^2)\) example living on a sphere. The conjugate point is a point on the sphere every great circle through the start passes through.

Rung 2

Expand \(F = \sqrt{p^2 + \cos^2\lambda}\) to second order about \((\lambda, p) = (0,0)\) — or compute \(F_{pp}\), \(F_{\lambda p}\), \(F_{\lambda\lambda}\) there. Great circles through \((1,0,0)\) tilted by \(\beta\) satisfy \(\tan\lambda = \tan\beta\sin\phi\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: at \(\lambda = 0\), \(p = 0\): \(F \approx 1 + \tfrac12(p^2 - \lambda^2)\), so \(P = F_{pp} = 1\), \(F_{\lambda p} = 0\), \(F_{\lambda\lambda} = -1\).

Worked solution

Idea. The equator past the antipode is a great circle, hence stationary; but the great circles from the start all re-meet at the antipode, and past a conjugate point the second variation can be made negative.

1. The point is \((\cos\lambda\cos\phi, \cos\lambda\sin\phi, \sin\lambda)\). Its \(\phi\)-derivative is \((-\lambda'\sin\lambda\cos\phi - \cos\lambda\sin\phi,\ -\lambda'\sin\lambda\sin\phi + \cos\lambda\cos\phi,\ \lambda'\cos\lambda)\); the squared norm is \(\lambda'^2(\sin^2\lambda + \cos^2\lambda) + \cos^2\lambda\), the cross terms cancelling. So \(F = \sqrt{p^2 + \cos^2\lambda}\). \(F_\lambda = -\sin\lambda\cos\lambda/F\) and \(F_p = p/F\) both vanish identically on \(\lambda\equiv0\), so Euler–Lagrange, \((F_p)' = F_\lambda\), holds: the equator is an extremal for every \(\ell\).

2. On \(\lambda = 0\), \(p = 0\): \(F = 1\), and \(F_{pp} = \cos^2\lambda/F^3 = 1\); \(F_{\lambda p} = \partial_\lambda(p/F) = -p\,F_\lambda/F^2 = 0\); \(F_{\lambda\lambda} = \partial_\lambda(-\sin\lambda\cos\lambda/F) = -\cos2\lambda/F + \sin\lambda\cos\lambda\,F_\lambda/F^2 = -1\). So \(\delta^2L = \int(F_{\lambda\lambda}\eta^2 + 2F_{\lambda p}\eta\eta' + F_{pp}\eta'^2) = \int(\eta'^2 - \eta^2)\). \(P = 1 \gt 0\): Legendre holds, strictly.

3. \(P = 1\), \(Q = -1\), so the accessory equation is \(\eta'' + \eta = 0\); with \(\eta(0) = 0\), \(\eta = \sin\phi\) is a solution, and it vanishes next at \(\phi = \pi\). No solution \(h \not\equiv 0\) vanishing at \(0\) can vanish at some \(c \in (0,\pi)\): multiplying \(h'' + h = 0\) by \(h\) and integrating by parts gives \(\int_0^c(h'^2 - h^2) = [hh']_0^c = 0\), which part 5's strict inequality on \([0,c]\) forbids (and \(h\) cannot vanish on all of \([0,c]\), since \(h'^2 + h^2\) is constant along a solution). So \(\pi\) is the first conjugate point: \((\cos\pi, \sin\pi, 0) = (-1,0,0)\), the antipode of the start. The family: the great circle through \((1,0,0)\) tilted by \(\beta\) about the \(x\)-axis lies in the plane \(z = \tan\beta\,y\); in coordinates \(\tan\lambda = \tan\beta\sin\phi\). Then \(\partial_\beta\lambda\big|_{\beta=0} = \sin\phi\) — the Jacobi field — and every member passes through the antipode, where \(\sin\phi = 0\).

4. \(\eta = \sin(2\phi/3)\) vanishes at \(0\) and \(3\pi/2\). Then \(\delta^2L = \int_0^{3\pi/2}\big(\tfrac49\cos^2\tfrac{2\phi}{3} - \sin^2\tfrac{2\phi}{3}\big)\mathrm d\phi = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}\big(\tfrac49 - 1\big) = -\tfrac{5\pi}{12} = -1.30900\). Taylor predicts \(L[\varepsilon\eta]\approx\tfrac{3\pi}{2} + \tfrac{\varepsilon^2}{2}\delta^2L\), i.e. shorter by \(0.0065450\) at \(\varepsilon = 0.1\). Quadrature of the exact integrand gives \(L = 4.705854\) against \(3\pi/2 = 4.712389\): shorter by \(0.0065354\). A curve within \(0.1\) rad of the equator, with slope within \(0.067\), is shorter: the \(270^\circ\) arc is stationary and not even a weak local minimum.

5. For \(\ell \lt \pi\), \(u(\phi) = \sin\big(\phi + (\pi-\ell)/2\big)\) solves \(u'' + u = 0\) and is positive on \([0,\ell]\), since its argument stays in \(\big[(\pi-\ell)/2, (\pi+\ell)/2\big]\subset(0,\pi)\). The Picone identity (deck, Jacobi's theorem) gives \(\int_0^\ell(\eta'^2 - \eta^2) = \int_0^\ell(\eta' - \eta u'/u)^2 \gt 0\) for \(\eta\ne0\). ∎

Check. The two ways of computing the shortening agree to \(0.15\%\), the difference being fourth order in \(\varepsilon\) ✓. Dimensions: all angles, all pure numbers ✓. Special case \(\ell = \pi\): \(\sin\phi\) itself gives \(\delta^2L = 0\) — the half great circle ties with its tilted neighbours, as it must, since all of them have length \(\pi\) ✓.

Higher derivatives, and existence

P10 A ruler over the edge of a table medium

exercises: the Euler–Poisson equation, natural boundary conditions with second derivatives, a number

A uniform beam of length \(L\), bending stiffness \(EI\) and weight \(w\) per unit length is clamped horizontally at \(x = 0\) and free at \(x = L\). With \(y\) measured upwards, its energy is \(J[y] = \int_0^L\big(\tfrac12EI\,y''^2 + wy\big)\,\mathrm dx\), with \(y(0) = y'(0) = 0\).

  1. Derive the Euler–Poisson equation and the two natural boundary conditions at the free end. Say what each condition means physically.
  2. Solve for \(y\) and show the tip deflection is \(wL^4/(8EI)\).
  3. A steel ruler \(25\) mm wide and \(1.0\) mm thick, lying flat, overhangs a table edge by \(30\) cm. How far does its tip droop? (For a rectangle, \(I = bt^3/12\).)
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A fourth-order linear ODE with four conditions: two imposed at the clamp, two natural at the tip. The answer is a quartic polynomial and a deflection in millimetres.

Rung 2

Allow variations with \(\eta(0) = \eta'(0) = 0\) only. Integrate \(EIy''\eta''\) by parts twice and keep the boundary terms at \(x = L\): they multiply \(\eta'(L)\) and \(\eta(L)\), which are free.

Rung 3

First line: \(\delta J = \int_0^L(EIy'''' + w)\eta\,\mathrm dx + EIy''(L)\eta'(L) - EIy'''(L)\eta(L)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Two integrations by parts leave an interior term, which gives the equation, and two boundary terms at the free end, which give one condition each.

1. \(\delta J = \int_0^L(EIy''\eta'' + w\eta)\,\mathrm dx\). Integrating by parts twice, \(\int_0^LEIy''\eta'' = [EIy''\eta']_0^L - [EIy'''\eta]_0^L + \int_0^LEIy''''\eta\), and the terms at \(0\) vanish. So \(\delta J = \int_0^L(EIy'''' + w)\eta\,\mathrm dx + EIy''(L)\eta'(L) - EIy'''(L)\eta(L)\). Variations vanishing near both ends give the Euler–Poisson equation \(EIy'''' = -w\) (fundamental lemma). That leaves \(\delta J = EIy''(L)\eta'(L) - EIy'''(L)\eta(L)\) for every admissible \(\eta\). Take \(\eta = x^2(x - L)\), which has \(\eta(0) = \eta'(0) = 0\), \(\eta(L) = 0\) and \(\eta'(L) = L^2\): it isolates the first term. Then \(\eta = x^2\), with \(\eta(L) = L^2\), isolates the second. So \[ EIy''(L) = 0,\qquad EIy'''(L) = 0 . \] \(EIy''\) is the bending moment and \(EIy'''\) the shear force: a free end carries neither.

2. Integrate \(y'''' = -w/EI\) four times, fixing one constant at each stage: \(y''' = -w(x - L)/EI\) (so \(y'''(L) = 0\)); \(y'' = -w(x-L)^2/(2EI)\) (so \(y''(L) = 0\)); \(y' = -w\big((x-L)^3 + L^3\big)/(6EI)\) (so \(y'(0) = 0\)); \(y = -w\big((x-L)^4 - L^4 + 4L^3x\big)/(24EI)\) (so \(y(0) = 0\)). Expanding \((x-L)^4\), \[ y(x) = -\frac{w\,x^2\,(x^2 - 4Lx + 6L^2)}{24EI},\qquad y(L) = -\frac{wL^4}{8EI} . \]

3. \(I = 0.025\times(0.001)^3/12 = 2.0833\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm m^4\); \(w = 7850\times0.025\times0.001\times9.81 = 1.9252\ \mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\). Then \(wL^4/(8EI) = 1.9252\times0.0081/(8\times2.0\times10^{11}\times2.0833\times10^{-12}) = 4.68\times10^{-3}\) m. The tip droops about \(4.7\) mm.

P11 Proof: a functional with no minimiser hard

exercises: minimising sequences, why convexity in \(y'\) matters, the Legendre condition as a test

Let \(J[y] = \int_0^1\big((y'^2 - 1)^2 + y^2\big)\,\mathrm dx\) on \(C^1\) curves with \(y(0) = y(1) = 0\) (Bolza's example).

  1. Prove \(J[y] \gt 0\) for every admissible \(y\).
  2. Prove \(\inf J = 0\). Start from a sawtooth with slopes \(\pm1\) and \(n\) teeth, compute its value, and then repair its corners.
  3. Show \(y\equiv0\) is an extremal, compute \(J[0]\), and use the Legendre condition to show it is not even a weak local minimum.
  4. Say which step of the direct method fails, and why.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The two terms want incompatible things: the first wants \(\lvert y'\rvert = 1\), the second wants \(y = 0\). The answer to part 2 is a sequence of curves and a bound tending to \(0\); part 4 is one sentence about convexity.

Rung 2

For part 1, suppose \(J[y] = 0\) and see what each term forces. For part 2, the sawtooth \(z_n\) has \(z_n'^2 = 1\) wherever defined, so only \(\int z_n^2\) survives; to round a corner, let the slope pass linearly from \(+1\) to \(-1\) over a short interval of width \(2\delta\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: each tooth has height \(1/(2n)\) and \(\int_0^{1/n}z_n^2\,\mathrm dx = 1/(12n^3)\), so \(J[z_n] = 1/(12n^2)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Curves can make both terms small by oscillating ever faster, but the limit of the oscillation is \(y = 0\), where the first term is at its worst. Non-convexity in \(y'\) is what lets the limit be worse than the sequence.

1. Both terms are non-negative and continuous, so \(J[y] = 0\) forces both to vanish identically (a continuous non-negative function with zero integral is zero). Then \(y\equiv0\), so \(y'\equiv0\), and the first term is \(1\), not \(0\). Contradiction; hence \(J[y] \gt 0\). ∎

2. Let \(z_n\) rise with slope \(1\) on \([k/n, k/n + 1/(2n)]\) and fall with slope \(-1\) on \([k/n + 1/(2n), (k+1)/n]\), for \(k = 0,\dots,n-1\). It vanishes at both ends and is piecewise \(C^1\) with \(2n - 1\) interior corners. Where differentiable, \((z_n'^2 - 1)^2 = 0\), and on one tooth \(\int z_n^2 = 2\int_0^{1/(2n)}t^2\,\mathrm dt = 1/(12n^3)\); so \(J[z_n] = 1/(12n^2)\), e.g. \(8.33\times10^{-4}\) at \(n = 10\).

Repair: at each corner \(x_k\), with slope \(s = \pm1\) before and \(-s\) after, replace the slope on \([x_k - \delta, x_k + \delta]\) by the linear function going from \(s\) to \(-s\). The new slope is continuous, so its integral \(y\) is \(C^1\); and since the linear slope, like the old one, has zero integral over the interval (it is odd about \(x_k\)), \(y\) agrees with \(z_n\) outside the repair intervals. Inside them \(\lvert y'\rvert\le1\) and \(\lvert y\rvert\le1/(2n)\), so the integrand is at most \(1 + 1/(4n^2)\le2\). Hence \(J[y]\le1/(12n^2) + (2n-1)\cdot2\delta\cdot2\). Choosing \(\delta = 1/(48n^3)\) (small enough that the intervals do not overlap) gives \(J[y]\le1/(12n^2) + 1/(6n^2) = 1/(4n^2)\to0\). With part 1, \(\inf J = 0\) and it is not attained. ∎

3. \(F = (p^2 - 1)^2 + y^2\): \(F_y = 2y\), \(F_p = 4p(p^2 - 1)\). On \(y\equiv0\) both vanish, so Euler–Lagrange holds, and \(J[0] = 1\). The Legendre coefficient is \(F_{pp} = 12p^2 - 4\), which at \(p = 0\) is \(-4 \lt 0\). By the Legendre condition, \(y\equiv0\) is not a weak local minimum: a squeezed bump lowers \(J\).

4. Step 3, lower semicontinuity. The minimising sequence converges uniformly to \(0\) (step 2 succeeds for positions), but \(J[0] = 1 \gt 0 = \lim J[y_n]\). The failure is caused by \(F\) not being convex in \(p\) — it has two wells at \(p = \pm1\) — so the slopes of the sequence can keep oscillating between \(+1\) and \(-1\), costing nothing, while their local averages — and hence the limit curve's slope — tend to \(0\), where the cost is highest. Tonelli's convexity hypothesis is exactly what excludes this.

Check. Numbers: \(J[z_n] = 1/(12n^2)\) is \(1/12 = 0.0833\) at \(n = 1\), and a single tooth of height \(\tfrac12\) has \(\int z^2 = 2\int_0^{1/2}t^2 = 1/12\) ✓. Dimensions: the example is dimensionless. Limit: the bound \(1/(4n^2)\to0\) as \(n\to\infty\) ✓.
