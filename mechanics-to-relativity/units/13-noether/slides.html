Noether: every conservation law you have met is one theorem

A one-parameter family of transformations that leaves the Lagrangian alone, or changes it only by a total time derivative, hands you a conserved quantity — and the proof is three lines.

needs 12~2 h slides + 4 h problemsproblems: ./mr problems 13

So far the course has proved three conservation laws by three unrelated tricks. Energy (unit 00): multiply $m\ddot x = F(x)$ by $\dot x$ and notice a derivative. Angular momentum (unit 08): $\mathrm d(\vec r\times\vec p)/\mathrm dt = \vec r\times\vec F$, which vanishes when $\vec F$ is central. Momentum: add the equations of motion and let Newton's third law cancel the internal forces. Each proof works, and none says why the quantity exists. This unit replaces all three with one theorem, and then uses it to find conserved quantities nobody would have guessed.

The language is unit 12's: a Lagrangian $L(q,\dot q,t)$, its Euler–Lagrange equations, and the fact that adding a total derivative $\mathrm d\Lambda/\mathrm dt$ to $L$ changes nothing. The only new mathematical object is a one-parameter group of transformations, which is unit 02's flow of a vector field looked at from a different angle.

Why this unit is on the spine: unit 29 proves the same theorem for fields and gets the energy–momentum tensor out of it, which is the right-hand side of Einstein's equations; unit 36 uses it on the Schwarzschild metric to get the conserved energy and angular momentum of an orbit around a black hole; unit 37 feeds those into unit 08's Binet equation to get the 43 arcseconds. Each is this unit's three lines in a new setting.

How to read this deck

Section 1 makes "symmetry" precise, section 2 proves the theorem, and sections 3 to 5 run it four times on the Sun–Jupiter Lagrangian — time translation, rotations, the Galilean boost, and a hidden symmetry that is not a transformation of space at all. Section 3 also settles a question usually glossed over: the conserved quantity from time translation is not always the energy $T + V$.

the theorem, and why it is true

Let $\delta q = \xi$ change $L$ by $\mathrm dF/\mathrm dt$ for every curve. With $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot q_i$, the charge $\;Q = p\cdot\xi - F\;$ is constant on every solution:

1. Product rule: $\dot Q = \dot p\cdot\xi + p\cdot\dot\xi - \dot F$.

2. On a solution, Euler–Lagrange says $\dot p = \partial L/\partial q$, so the first two terms are the change in $L$, $\ \delta L = \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial q}\cdot\xi + \tfrac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\cdot\dot\xi$.

3. The hypothesis says $\delta L = \dot F$. So $\dot Q = 0$.

In the table, $M = m_S + m_J$ is the total mass, $\vec R$ the centre of mass, $\vec P$ the total momentum.

13 · symmetry

Three laws, three unrelated proofs

energy · unit 00

$m\ddot x = -V'(x)$. Multiply by $\dot x$:

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Big(\tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + V\Big) = 0.$$

Works because nothing depends on $t$ explicitly.

momentum · Newton III

$\dot{\vec p}_S = \vec F_{JS}$, $\dot{\vec p}_J = \vec F_{SJ}$. Add, with $\vec F_{JS} = -\vec F_{SJ}$:

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\vec p_S + \vec p_J\big) = \vec 0.$$

Works because only $\vec r_S - \vec r_J$ appears.

angular momentum · unit 08

$\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\vec r\times\vec p) = \dot{\vec r}\times\vec p + \vec r\times\vec F$. The first term vanishes always, the second when $\vec F$ is central:

$$\dot{\vec L} = \vec 0.$$

Works because only $\lvert\vec r\rvert$ appears.

the pattern

Each "works because" names a change you could make to the system without it noticing: shift the clock (homogeneity of time), shift everything in space (homogeneity of space), turn everything (isotropy). The theorem says that is not a coincidence: a continuous symmetry is a conservation law.

Read each proof again and ask what it used. The energy proof used that $V$ has no explicit $t$ — shift the origin of time and the equation is unchanged. The momentum proof used the third law, which is assumed in Newton; section 4 will derive it from the fact that the potential depends only on the difference of positions. The angular-momentum proof used that the force is central, which is what a potential depending only on distance produces.

The word continuous matters and will be made precise on the next slide. A mirror reflection is a symmetry of the running example too, but it comes in no family: you cannot reflect "a little". Discrete symmetries give no conserved quantity in classical mechanics (in quantum mechanics they give conserved parity, a different kind of thing).

History. The link between invariance and conservation was used case by case by Lagrange and by Jacobi, who derived momentum and angular-momentum conservation from translation and rotation invariance of the Lagrangian in his lectures on dynamics of 1842–43. Emmy Noether's paper Invariante Variationsprobleme (Göttingen, 1918) proved the general theorem for any variational problem, fields included, and a second theorem about infinite-dimensional symmetry groups; she was answering a question of Hilbert and Klein about what energy conservation means in general relativity, the subject that ends this course. The extension to transformations that change $L$ by a total derivative is due to Bessel-Hagen (1921).

One-parameter groups of transformations

definition · one-parameter group

On an open configuration space $Q\subseteq\mathbb R^n$, a family of maps $\Phi_s : Q\times\mathbb R\to Q$, $(q,t)\mapsto\Phi_s(q,t)$, with $(s,q,t)\mapsto\Phi_s(q,t)$ of class $C^2$ and

$$\Phi_0 = \mathrm{id},\qquad \Phi_{s}\big(\Phi_{s'}(q,t),t\big) = \Phi_{s+s'}(q,t).$$
definition · infinitesimal generator $$\xi(q,t) := \frac{\partial}{\partial s}\Phi_s(q,t)\Big|_{s=0}.$$

The velocity with which the family starts moving $q$.

Each point is carried along its own curve, an orbit of the family. Moving by $s'$ and then by $s$ lands where moving by $s+s'$ does — the group law — and $\xi$ is the tangent arrow at the start. Schematic: $\Phi_s(x,y) = (x+s,\ y - \cos(x+s) + \cos x)$.

Reading the figure. The family drawn is $\Phi_s(x,y) = (x + s,\ y - \cos(x+s) + \cos x)$ on the plane, with $t$ playing no part. Check the group law: applying $\Phi_{s'}$ and then $\Phi_s$ gives first coordinate $x + s' + s$ and second $y - \cos(x+s') + \cos x - \cos(x+s'+s) + \cos(x+s')= y - \cos(x+s+s') + \cos x$, which is $\Phi_{s+s'}$. The quantity $y + \cos x$ is unchanged by every $\Phi_s$, so each point stays on its own curve $y = -\cos x + c$: those curves are the orbits. Differentiating at $s = 0$ gives $\xi(x,y) = (1, \sin x)$, the tangent to the orbit, drawn at the three marked points. It is longer where the orbit is steeper, because the family moves every point by exactly $s$ in $x$.

Why a group, and not just a family. A single symmetry is one map. Noether's theorem needs a derivative with respect to the size of the transformation, so it needs a family through the identity, $\Phi_0 = \mathrm{id}$. The group law is what makes the family the same at every point along it: moving by $s+h$ is moving by $s$ and then by the small amount $h$, so the derivative at any $s$ is the derivative at $0$, transported. The next slide turns that remark into a theorem.

The generator determines the group

the generator determines the group

For each fixed $(q, t)$, $s\mapsto\Phi_s(q,t)$ solves $\;\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\Phi_s = \xi(\Phi_s, t)$, $\Phi_0 = q$.

Proof. $\partial_s\Phi_s = \partial_h\Phi_{h}(\Phi_s)\big|_{h=0} = \xi(\Phi_s)$, by the group law $\Phi_{s+h} = \Phi_h\circ\Phi_s$. ∎

three generators

Translation $q\mapsto q + s\,c$: $\ \xi = c$.
Boost $\vec r\mapsto\vec r + s\,t\,\hat n$: $\ \xi = t\,\hat n$.
Scaling $q\mapsto e^{s}q$: $\ \xi = q$.

The three generators as arrows. A translation pushes every point the same way; a boost pushes harder the later it is; a scaling pushes each point away from the origin in proportion to its distance.

Reading the figure. Each arrow is $\xi$ at its base point: the first-order displacement per unit $s$. In the middle panel time runs to the right and the one space coordinate up, so the boost's generator $\xi = t\hat n$ is zero at $t = 0$ and grows linearly; the dashed lines are worldlines of bodies at rest, and the boost tilts each of them by the same slope $s$ — which is the statement that a boost adds the velocity $s\hat n$ to everything. In the right panel the arrows $\xi = q$ point straight out from the origin, and integrating them gives $\Phi_s(q) = e^sq$ by the theorem on this slide.

This is unit 02's flow, read backwards. There, a vector field was given and its flow $\varphi_s$ was the object built from it, with the group property $\varphi_{s+s'} = \varphi_s\circ\varphi_{s'}$ proved from uniqueness. Here the family is given and the vector field is extracted by differentiating at $s = 0$. The theorem on the slide says the two constructions are inverse: by unit 01's Picard–Lindelöf theorem (which applies because $\xi$ is $C^1$, hence locally Lipschitz), the initial-value problem $\mathrm d\Phi/\mathrm ds = \xi(\Phi)$, $\Phi_0 = q$, has exactly one solution, and $s\mapsto\Phi_s(q)$ is it. So a one-parameter group is known once its generator is known, and everything below is phrased in terms of $\xi$.

Full proof of the displayed step. Fix $q$ and $s$. By the group law, $\Phi_{s+h}(q) = \Phi_h(\Phi_s(q))$ for every $h$. Differentiate in $h$ at $h = 0$: the left side gives $\partial_s\Phi_s(q)$, the right side gives $\partial_h\Phi_h(p)|_{h=0}$ at the point $p = \Phi_s(q)$, which is $\xi(p)$ by definition. ∎

The role of $t$. It is a parameter carried along: the boost moves a point by an amount proportional to the time, so $\Phi_s$ is allowed to depend on $t$, and the group law holds at each fixed $t$. Transformations that move $t$ itself — time translation — are handled in section 3 by a different device.

Only the generator is used. Noether's theorem below needs nothing but $\xi$ and one identity it satisfies. The group structure is what makes "a symmetry" a meaningful object, and what makes the infinitesimal condition equivalent to the finite one; but the proof itself is first order in $s$.

Rotations, and why their generator is a cross product

rotation by $s$ about the unit vector $\hat n$ $$\begin{aligned}R_s\vec x = {}&\cos s\;\vec x + \sin s\;\hat n\times\vec x\\ &+ (1-\cos s)(\hat n\cdot\vec x)\,\hat n.\end{aligned}$$

In an orthonormal basis $(\vec e_1,\vec e_2,\hat n)$ it is the plane rotation by $s$ on the first two axes and fixes $\hat n$; the addition formulas for $\cos$ and $\sin$ give $R_sR_{s'} = R_{s+s'}$.

its generator

Differentiate at $s=0$: $\ \xi(\vec x) = \hat n\times\vec x$.

As $s$ grows, the tip of $\vec x$ runs round a circle about $\hat n$, of radius $\lvert\vec x\rvert\sin\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between $\hat n$ and $\vec x$. The generator is the tip's starting velocity: tangent to that circle, so perpendicular to both $\hat n$ and $\vec x$, with length $\lvert\vec x\rvert\sin\alpha$ — the cross product $\hat n\times\vec x$.

Reading the figure. Everything the cross product needs is visible. Its direction: the tip moves round a circle centred on the axis, so its velocity is perpendicular to the radius of that circle and to the axis — hence to $\hat n$ and, since $\vec x$ is the radius plus a multiple of $\hat n$, to $\vec x$ as well. Its length: one radian of rotation moves the tip one radius, $\lvert\vec x\rvert\sin\alpha = \lvert\hat n\times\vec x\rvert$. Its sense: counterclockwise seen from the tip of $\hat n$, the right-hand rule. A vector along the axis ($\alpha = 0$) does not move, and $\hat n\times\hat n = \vec 0$ agrees.

Full check of the formula. Take the orthonormal basis $(\vec e_1,\vec e_2,\hat n)$ with $\vec e_1\times\vec e_2 = \hat n$, so $\hat n\times\vec e_1 = \vec e_2$ and $\hat n\times\vec e_2 = -\vec e_1$. For $\vec x = \vec e_1$: $R_s\vec e_1 = \cos s\,\vec e_1 + \sin s\,\vec e_2$. For $\vec x = \vec e_2$: $R_s\vec e_2 = \cos s\,\vec e_2 - \sin s\,\vec e_1$. For $\vec x = \hat n$: $R_s\hat n = \cos s\,\hat n + 0 + (1-\cos s)\hat n = \hat n$. That is the plane rotation by angle $s$ on $\mathrm{span}(\vec e_1,\vec e_2)$, fixing $\hat n$. The composition $R_sR_{s'}$ is the plane rotation by $s$ after the one by $s'$; multiplying the $2\times2$ matrices and using $\cos(s+s') = \cos s\cos s' - \sin s\sin s'$ and $\sin(s+s') = \sin s\cos s' + \cos s\sin s'$ gives the plane rotation by $s+s'$. So this is a one-parameter group, and it is orthogonal with determinant $1$ for every $s$.

Every rotation family starts as a cross product

lemma

Any differentiable family of orthogonal matrices with $R_0 = I$ has $\dot R_0$ antisymmetric, and every antisymmetric $3\times3$ matrix acts as $\vec x\mapsto\vec\omega\times\vec x$ for exactly one $\vec\omega$.

So it is not special to the formula on the previous slide: every rotation family starts off as a cross product. That is why angular momentum is a cross product.

proof

Differentiate $R_s^{\top}R_s = I$ at $0$: $\dot R_0^{\top} + \dot R_0 = 0$. An antisymmetric matrix has three free entries; writing $\Omega_{32} = \omega_1$, $\Omega_{13} = \omega_2$, $\Omega_{21} = \omega_3$ gives $\Omega\vec x = \vec\omega\times\vec x$ entry by entry. ∎

The lemma's second half, written out. Antisymmetry forces zero diagonal and $\Omega_{ij} = -\Omega_{ji}$, so $\Omega = \begin{pmatrix}0&-\omega_3&\omega_2\\ \omega_3&0&-\omega_1\\ -\omega_2&\omega_1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\Omega\vec x = (\omega_2x_3 - \omega_3x_2,\ \omega_3x_1 - \omega_1x_3,\ \omega_1x_2 - \omega_2x_1) = \vec\omega\times\vec x$ by unit 08's component formula for the cross product. Uniqueness: two such $\vec\omega$ would give the same matrix, whose entries are the $\omega_i$.

Why this matters beyond this slide. Unit 03 met the same fact as the angular-velocity vector of a rotating frame. Unit 16 will say it properly: the antisymmetric matrices are the tangent space to the rotation group at the identity, and the cross product is a coincidence of three dimensions — in $n$ dimensions there are $n(n-1)/2$ independent rotation generators, and "angular momentum" is an antisymmetric matrix, not a vector. In four dimensions there are six, which is why section 5's hidden symmetry has room for $\vec L$ and $\vec A$ together.

Moving curves, and what the Lagrangian does

A transformation of $Q$ moves every curve: $q(t)\mapsto q_s(t) := \Phi_s\big(q(t),t\big)$. Its velocity comes from the chain rule (unit 03), the lift to velocities:

$$\dot q_s(t) = D_q\Phi_s\,\dot q(t) + \partial_t\Phi_s .$$
the variation and the induced variation of $L$ $$\delta q := \partial_s q_s\big|_{s=0} = \xi\big(q(t),t\big),$$ $$\delta L := \partial_s L(q_s,\dot q_s,t)\big|_{s=0} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\cdot\xi + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\cdot\dot\xi,$$

where $\dot\xi := \dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,\xi\big(q(t),t\big)$ along the curve. The second equality is the chain rule plus the lemma on the next slide.

The family pushes the whole curve, so each point moves by $s\,\xi$ to first order — including the two ends, which unit 11's variations kept fixed. Drawn for the scaling $q_s = e^{s}q$, $\ \xi = q$, with $s = 0.32$.

Reading the figure. The solid curve is $q(t)$ on $[t_0, t_1]$; the dashed one is the moved curve $q_s(t) = e^{s}q(t)$, and the arrows join $q(t)$ to $q_s(t)$ at the same time $t$. For this family each arrow has length $(e^{s}-1)q(t)\approx s\,q(t) = s\,\xi$: longer where $q$ is larger. Nothing pins the ends, and that is the difference from unit 11 that the whole theorem turns on.

The lift, in words. A map of positions induces a map of velocities, because a velocity is the derivative of a curve of positions and the chain rule says how the derivative transforms. For a rotation, $D_q R_s = R_s$ and $\partial_tR_s = 0$, so velocities are rotated by the same matrix. For the boost $\vec r\mapsto\vec r + st\hat n$, $D_q\Phi_s = I$ and $\partial_t\Phi_s = s\hat n$, so every velocity gains $s\hat n$ — which is what a boost is. Unit 31 will call the lifted map the tangent map of $\Phi_s$ and the space of $(q,\dot q)$ the tangent bundle; nothing here needs those words. In the figure the lift is the slope of the dashed curve at each $t$, which here is $e^{s}$ times the slope of the solid one.

Notation. $\delta$ is not a new kind of derivative; it is $\partial_s$ at $s = 0$. The symbol is kept because it is universal in physics and because unit 11 used it for exactly this. $D_q\Phi_s$ is the Jacobian matrix of $\Phi_s$ in the $q$ variables.

13 · symmetry

Varying the action along the family

$\delta$ commutes with $\mathrm d/\mathrm dt$

$\partial_s\dot q_s\big|_{0} = \dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\,\partial_s q_s\big|_{0} = \dot\xi$.

Why: $(s,t)\mapsto q_s(t)$ is $C^2$, so the mixed partials agree — Clairaut, unit 03.

That is the step the formula for $\delta L$ on the previous slide used: the change in the velocity is the velocity of the change.

Integrating over an interval gives the variation of the action that unit 11 differentiated:

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}S[q_s]\Big|_{s=0} = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\delta L\;\mathrm dt .$$

Unit 11 took variations vanishing at the ends. A symmetry moves the ends too, and the boundary term that unit 11 threw away is where the conserved quantity will come from.

Full proof of the formula for $\delta L$. $L$ is $C^2$ and $s\mapsto(q_s(t),\dot q_s(t))$ is $C^1$, so the chain rule gives $\partial_sL(q_s,\dot q_s,t) = \sum_i\big(\partial_{q_i}L\, \partial_sq_{s,i} + \partial_{\dot q_i}L\,\partial_s\dot q_{s,i}\big)$. At $s = 0$, $\partial_sq_s = \xi$ by definition and $\partial_s\dot q_s = \dot\xi$ by the commuting lemma. ∎

The regularity bookkeeping, once. Curves are $C^2$ (so the Euler–Lagrange equations make sense), $\Phi$ is $C^2$ in all its arguments, $L$ is $C^2$. Then $q_s(t)$ is $C^2$ in $(s,t)$ jointly and Clairaut applies. Nothing later needs more.

Differentiating under the integral. $S[q_s] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}L(q_s,\dot q_s,t)\,\mathrm dt$ over a fixed interval, and the integrand is $C^1$ in $(s,t)$ jointly on a compact set, so the $s$-derivative passes inside the integral (the rule unit 11 used for the same step).

Symmetry, and symmetry up to a total derivative

definition · symmetry

$\Phi_s$ is a symmetry of $L$ if, for every $C^2$ curve and every $s$,

$$L(q_s,\dot q_s,t) = L(q,\dot q,t).$$

Infinitesimally: $\delta L = 0$ for every curve.

definition · quasi-symmetry

$\Phi_s$ is a quasi-symmetry if there are $C^2$ functions $\Lambda_s(q,t)$, $\Lambda_0 = 0$, with

$$L(q_s,\dot q_s,t) = L(q,\dot q,t) + \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Lambda_s\big(q(t),t\big).$$

Infinitesimally: $\delta L = \dfrac{\mathrm dF}{\mathrm dt}$ with $F = \partial_s\Lambda_s|_{s=0}$.

why a total derivative is allowed

Unit 12: $L$ and $L + \mathrm d\Lambda/\mathrm dt$ have the same Euler–Lagrange equations, because $\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\dot\Lambda\,\mathrm dt = \Lambda(t_1) - \Lambda(t_0)$ does not change under variations fixed at the ends. So a quasi-symmetry maps solutions to solutions exactly as a symmetry does.

"for every curve", not "for every solution"

The identity must hold off-shell — on curves that do not solve the equations. On-shell, every $\xi$ satisfies $\delta L = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(p\cdot\xi)$, and the theorem would say nothing.

Why "for every curve" is the whole content. Along a solution, the Euler–Lagrange equations give $\partial L/\partial q = \dot p$ with $p = \partial L/\partial\dot q$, so $\delta L = \dot p\cdot\xi + p\cdot\dot\xi = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(p\cdot\xi)$ for any $\xi$ whatsoever. If we only asked for $\delta L = \dot F$ on solutions, $F = p\cdot\xi$ would always work and the "charge" $p\cdot\xi - F$ would be identically $0$. The theorem has content because $F$ is required to be a fixed function of $(q,\dot q,t)$ satisfying the identity on all curves, which is a checkable property of $L$ that knows nothing about solutions.

Why the finite version implies the infinitesimal one. Differentiate the defining identity in $s$ at $s = 0$. For the quasi-symmetry, $\partial_s\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Lambda_s = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\partial_s\Lambda_s$ by Clairaut again. The converse — infinitesimal symmetry at every point of the family implies finite symmetry — is true for groups and is proved by integrating in $s$ using the group law; nothing below needs it.

Terminology. Some books call quasi-symmetries divergence symmetries (the name from field theory, where the total derivative becomes a divergence, unit 29) and some say "$L$ is invariant up to a gauge term". The words matter less than the fact: the boost of section 4, the translation of a charge in a magnetic field (problem P2) and section 5's hidden symmetry are all quasi-symmetries and not symmetries, and each gives a conserved quantity that a strict-invariance version of the theorem would miss.

The finite version is exact too: $L_s - L = s\,\hat n\cdot M\dot{\vec R} + \frac12Ms^2 = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(sM\hat n\cdot\vec R + \tfrac12Ms^2t\big)$.
  • Scaling. $\xi_a = \vec r_a$, $\dot\xi_a = \dot{\vec r}_a$, so $\delta T = 2T$; $V$ is homogeneous of degree $-1$ in the positions, so $\delta V = -V$ (Euler's theorem, proved in section 3). Hence $\delta L = 2T - (-V) = 2T + V$.

Full proof that scaling is not a quasi-symmetry. Suppose $2T + V = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}F(q,t) = \partial_tF + \nabla_qF\cdot\dot q$ for every curve. The right side is affine in the velocities; the left side has the quadratic part $2T$. Take a curve through a fixed $(q,t)$ with velocity $\lambda v$: the left side grows like $\lambda^2$ and the right side like $\lambda$, a contradiction as $\lambda\to\infty$. ∎ (Section 5 extends exactly this argument to show that the Runge–Lenz vector comes from no transformation of configuration space.)

The scaling is not useless: with time rescaled as well, $t\mapsto\lambda^3 t$, $\vec r\mapsto\lambda^2\vec r$, it maps solutions to solutions, and problem P9 extracts Kepler's third law from it. It is a symmetry of the equations of motion but not of $L$, and that difference is the subject of the warning slide in section 2.

13 · theorem

Noether's theorem

theorem · Noether's theorem for point mechanics

Let $L(q,\dot q,t)$ be $C^2$, and let $\xi(q,\dot q,t)$ and $F(q,\dot q,t)$ be $C^1$ functions such that, for every $C^2$ curve $q(t)$,

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\cdot\xi + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\cdot\dot\xi \;=\; \frac{\mathrm dF}{\mathrm dt}.$$

Then along every solution of the Euler–Lagrange equations the Noether charge

$$Q \;:=\; \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}\,\xi_i \;-\; F \;=\; p\cdot\xi - F$$

is constant.

proof $$\frac{\mathrm dQ}{\mathrm dt} \;=\; \dot p\cdot\xi + p\cdot\dot\xi - \dot F \;=\; \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\cdot\xi + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\cdot\dot\xi - \dot F \;=\; \delta L - \dot F \;=\; 0 .\qquad\blacksquare$$

Product rule; the Euler–Lagrange equation $\dot p = \partial L/\partial q$; the hypothesis.

That is the whole theorem. Three equalities, each with one reason, and the second is the only place the equations of motion enter. The hypothesis is about $L$ alone and can be checked without solving anything; the conclusion is about solutions.

Why $\xi$ may depend on $\dot q$. Nothing in the three lines used that $\xi$ is a function of position only. If $\xi = \xi(q,\dot q,t)$, then $\dot\xi$ along a curve involves $\ddot q$, and the hypothesis is an identity in $(q,\dot q,\ddot q,t)$ that must hold for all curves; the proof goes through word for word. Such a $\xi$ is a generalised symmetry: it does not come from a transformation of configuration space, because it moves a point differently depending on how fast it is moving. Time translation (section 3) and the hidden symmetry (section 5) are of this kind. When $\xi = \xi(q,t)$, the hypothesis has no $\ddot q$ on the left, so $F$ can have no $\dot q$ dependence: $F = F(q,t)$, matching the quasi-symmetry definition.

Regularity. $Q$ is $C^1$ along a $C^2$ solution because $p = \partial L/\partial\dot q$ is $C^1$ in its arguments and $\xi$, $F$ are $C^1$. That is all the product rule needs.

Several coordinates, several symmetries. Each independent generator gives its own charge. Rotations about three independent axes give three charges, which section 4 assembles into a vector. Linearity helps: if $\xi_1$ and $\xi_2$ satisfy the hypothesis with $F_1$ and $F_2$, then $c_1\xi_1 + c_2\xi_2$ does with $c_1F_1 + c_2F_2$, and its charge is $c_1Q_1 + c_2Q_2$.

Uniqueness. $F$ is determined by $\xi$ only up to an additive constant, so $Q$ is too — harmless, since a constant is conserved anyway.

The same theorem, seen from the action

Let $q$ solve the Euler–Lagrange equations on $[t_0,t_1]$ and vary it by $\delta q = \xi$, not zero at the ends. Unit 11's integration by parts, keeping the boundary term:

$$\delta S = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Big(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q} - \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\Big)\cdot\xi\,\mathrm dt + \Big[p\cdot\xi\Big]_{t_0}^{t_1}.$$

On a solution the integral is $0$: $\ \delta S = [\,p\cdot\xi\,]_{t_0}^{t_1}$.

If $\xi$ is a (quasi-)symmetry, compute $\delta S$ a second way:

$$\delta S = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\frac{\mathrm dF}{\mathrm dt}\,\mathrm dt = \Big[F\Big]_{t_0}^{t_1}.$$
equate $$\big[\,p\cdot\xi - F\,\big]_{t_0}^{t_1} = 0\quad\text{for all } t_0 \lt t_1 .$$

The charge is the boundary term that unit 11 discarded.

Full proof of the boundary formula

$\delta S = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\big(\partial_qL\cdot\xi + \partial_{\dot q}L\cdot\dot\xi\big)\,\mathrm dt$ by the previous section. Integrate the second term by parts: $\int p\cdot\dot\xi\,\mathrm dt = [p\cdot\xi]_{t_0}^{t_1} - \int\dot p\cdot\xi\,\mathrm dt$. This is legitimate because $p(t) = \partial_{\dot q}L(q(t),\dot q(t),t)$ is $C^1$ along a $C^2$ curve. Collect terms. ∎ In unit 11 the variations vanished at $t_0$ and $t_1$, the bracket was zero, and the fundamental lemma turned "$\delta S = 0$ for all such $\xi$" into the Euler–Lagrange equation. Here the logic runs the other way: the equation is known, the integral vanishes, and the bracket is all that is left.

Why the two proofs are the same proof. The first slide differentiated $Q$; this one integrated $\dot Q$ over $[t_0,t_1]$. Equal values at every pair of times is constancy.

What the hypotheses are for

a symmetry of the equations is not enough

$L = e^{\gamma t}\big(\tfrac12m\dot q^2 - \tfrac12m\omega^2q^2\big)$ gives the damped oscillator $\ddot q + \gamma\dot q + \omega^2q = 0$ (unit 06), whose equation is unchanged by $t\mapsto t+s$. But $L$ becomes $e^{\gamma s}L$, and the change $(e^{\gamma s}-1)L$ is not a total derivative: the energy is not conserved. Problem P8 finds the symmetry this $L$ does have.

discrete symmetries give nothing

A reflection $\vec r\mapsto-\vec r$ leaves the running example's $L$ unchanged, but there is no $s$ to differentiate in, so no $\xi$ and no charge.

the charge is gauge-invariant

Replace $L$ by $L' = L + \dot\Lambda(q,t)$. Then $p' = p + \partial_q\Lambda$ and $F' = F + \partial_q\Lambda\cdot\xi$, so

$$Q' = p'\cdot\xi - F' = p\cdot\xi - F = Q .$$

Momenta are not gauge-invariant (unit 12); Noether charges are.

non-holonomic systems

Everything here assumes the equations of motion are Euler–Lagrange equations. For a rolling constraint they are not (unit 12), and the theorem can fail: a symmetric rolling body need not conserve the naive charge.

Full proof of gauge invariance

$p' = \partial_{\dot q}(L + \partial_t\Lambda + \partial_q\Lambda\cdot\dot q) = p + \partial_q\Lambda$. For $\delta$ of the extra term: $\delta\dot\Lambda = \partial_q(\dot\Lambda)\cdot\xi + \partial_{\dot q}(\dot\Lambda)\cdot\dot\xi = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\partial_q\Lambda)\cdot\xi + \partial_q\Lambda\cdot\dot\xi = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\partial_q\Lambda\cdot\xi)$, using $\partial_q\dot\Lambda = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\partial_q\Lambda$ (Clairaut). So $\delta L' = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(F + \partial_q\Lambda\cdot\xi)$, which is the $F'$ on the slide, and the two extra terms in $Q'$ cancel. ∎ Problem P4 asks for this with the physics attached.

The damped oscillator, a little more. With $p = me^{\gamma t}\dot q$, the Euler–Lagrange equation is $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(me^{\gamma t}\dot q) = -me^{\gamma t}\omega^2q$, i.e. $\ddot q + \gamma\dot q + \omega^2 q = 0$. The time shift sends $L$ to $e^{\gamma s}L$: a symmetry of the equations (multiplying $L$ by a constant never changes them) but not of the action, which it rescales. Section 3's energy function for this $L$ is $h = e^{\gamma t}(\frac12m\dot q^2 + \frac12m\omega^2q^2)$, and it is not conserved: $\dot h = -\partial_tL = -\gamma L$. Neither is the mechanical energy. The lesson: the hypothesis is invariance of $L$ up to $\dot F$, and invariance of the equations it produces is weaker.

Why the reflection gives nothing. Conserved quantities come from derivatives at the identity, and a discrete group has no elements near the identity other than the identity.

Non-holonomic constraints, for completeness: for velocity constraints that cannot be integrated to position constraints, the correct equations of motion come from d'Alembert's principle and are not the Euler–Lagrange equations of any constrained action (unit 12 says this honestly). Noether's theorem then does not apply, and there are standard examples — the rolling disc, the Chaplygin sleigh — where a symmetry does not give the charge the unconstrained formula predicts. Cited: Bloch, Nonholonomic Mechanics and Control, ch. 5.

Time translation as a variation

Shifting the clock sends a curve $q(t)$ to $q_s(t) = q(t+s)$. It moves $t$, not $q$, but its effect on curves is a variation:

$$\delta q = \partial_s q(t+s)\big|_{s=0} = \dot q .$$

A velocity-dependent $\xi$, which the theorem allows. Then

$$\delta L = \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\cdot\dot q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}\cdot\ddot q = \frac{\mathrm dL}{\mathrm dt} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}.$$

So when $L$ has no explicit $t$, $\delta L$ is a total derivative with $F = L$.

Sliding the graph left by $s$ changes the value at a fixed time by $s\,\dot q$, to first order — steep where the curve is steep. So the generator of a time shift is the velocity itself.

Reading the figure. The dashed curve is the solid one moved left by $s$: its value at time $t$ is the solid curve's value at $t + s$. At the marked time the vertical gap is $q(t+s) - q(t) = s\,\dot q(t) + O(s^2)$ by Taylor's theorem, and the dotted line is the tangent whose slope $\dot q$ sets it. Where the solid curve is flat — at its peaks — the two curves momentarily agree to first order and $\xi = \dot q = 0$ there.

Why not treat time translation as a transformation of $(q, t)$? One can, and the general theory does (it gives the charge $p\cdot\xi - h\,\tau - F$ for a transformation moving $t$ by $s\tau$ and $q$ by $s\xi$). For this unit the variation $\delta q = \dot q$ is cleaner: it says what time translation does to a curve, which is all the theorem needs, and it shows at once why velocity-dependent generators cannot be excluded — the most important conservation law of all comes from one.

The energy function, and when it is conserved

definition · energy function $$h(q,\dot q,t) := \sum_i\dot q_i\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i} - L .$$

It is the Noether charge $p\cdot\xi - F$ of the previous slide, with $\xi = \dot q$ and $F = L$.

theorem

Along every solution, $\ \dfrac{\mathrm dh}{\mathrm dt} = -\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial t}$. So if $L$ has no explicit $t$, $h$ is conserved.

Proof. If $\partial_tL = 0$, $\delta L = \dot L$: Noether with $\xi = \dot q$, $F = L$ gives $Q = p\cdot\dot q - L = h$. In general, $\dot h = \dot p\cdot\dot q + p\cdot\ddot q - \dot L$, and substituting $\dot L$ and $\dot p = \partial_qL$ leaves $-\partial_tL$. ∎

checks

Dimensions: $[\dot q\,\partial L/\partial\dot q] = [L]$, so $h$ is an energy ✓. One particle, $L = \tfrac12m\dot x^2 - V(x)$: $h = \tfrac12m\dot x^2 + V$, unit 00's energy ✓.

Full proof of the general formula. Along a solution, $\dot L = \partial_qL\cdot\dot q + \partial_{\dot q}L\cdot\ddot q + \partial_tL$ (chain rule) and $\dot p = \partial_qL$ (Euler–Lagrange). So $\dot h = \dot p\cdot\dot q + p\cdot\ddot q - \dot L = \partial_qL\cdot\dot q + p\cdot\ddot q - \partial_qL\cdot\dot q - p\cdot\ddot q - \partial_tL = -\partial_tL$. ∎

You have seen this before. Unit 11's Beltrami identity says that for an integrand $F(y,y')$ with no explicit $x$, the quantity $F - y'F_{y'}$ is constant on extremals. That is $-h$, with $x$ playing time. The brachistochrone was solved with the energy function of a variational problem, before the word "energy" was needed.

The one-particle check. $\partial L/\partial\dot x = m\dot x$, so $h = m\dot x^2 - \tfrac12m\dot x^2 + V = \tfrac12m\dot x^2 + V$. The next slide says exactly when this "$h = T + V$" survives in other coordinates.

When is $h$ the energy?

Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions

If $f(\lambda v) = \lambda^kf(v)$ for all $\lambda \gt 0$ and $f$ is $C^1$, then $v\cdot\nabla f(v) = k\,f(v)$.

Proof. Differentiate in $\lambda$ at $\lambda = 1$, by the chain rule. ∎

Split $L$ into pieces homogeneous of degree 2, 1, 0 in $\dot q$: $\ L = L_2 + L_1 + L_0$. Then $\dot q\cdot\partial_{\dot q}L = 2L_2 + L_1$, and

the energy function, decoded $$h = L_2 - L_0 .$$

For $L = T - V$ with $V = V(q,t)$, write the Cartesian positions as $\vec r_a = \vec r_a(q,t)$. Then

$$T = \underbrace{\tfrac12\dot q^{\top}M(q,t)\,\dot q}_{T_2} + \underbrace{b(q,t)\cdot\dot q}_{T_1} + \underbrace{T_0(q,t)}_{T_0},$$ $$h = T_2 - T_0 + V .$$
so $h = T + V$ only if

$T_1 = T_0 = 0$, which holds when $\partial_t\vec r_a = 0$: the coordinates are fixed in an inertial frame. With a moving constraint or moving coordinates, $h$ is not the energy — and whether it is conserved is the separate question of whether $\partial_tL = 0$.

Full proof of the decomposition

With $\vec r_a = \vec r_a(q,t)$, the chain rule gives $\dot{\vec r}_a = \sum_i\partial_{q_i}\vec r_a\,\dot q_i + \partial_t\vec r_a$. Then $T = \frac12\sum_am_a\lvert\dot{\vec r}_a\rvert^2$ expands into $T_2 = \frac12\sum_{a,i,j}m_a\,\partial_{q_i}\vec r_a\cdot\partial_{q_j}\vec r_a\,\dot q_i\dot q_j$, $T_1 = \sum_{a,i}m_a\,\partial_{q_i}\vec r_a\cdot\partial_t\vec r_a\,\dot q_i$, and $T_0 = \frac12\sum_am_a\lvert\partial_t\vec r_a\rvert^2$. So $L_2 = T_2$, $L_1 = T_1$ (plus any velocity-linear potential), $L_0 = T_0 - V$, and $h = L_2 - L_0 = T_2 - T_0 + V$. If $\partial_t\vec r_a = 0$ for all $a$, then $T_1 = T_0 = 0$, $T = T_2$ and $h = T + V$. ∎

Euler's theorem in full. Fix $v$ and set $g(\lambda) = f(\lambda v) = \lambda^kf(v)$. By the chain rule (unit 03), $g'(\lambda) = \nabla f(\lambda v)\cdot v$; also $g'(\lambda) = k\lambda^{k-1}f(v)$. At $\lambda = 1$: $v\cdot\nabla f(v) = kf(v)$. ∎ Here it is applied with $v = \dot q$ and $(q,t)$ held fixed.

The two conditions are separate. $h$ is conserved when $\partial_tL = 0$. $h = T + V$ when the coordinates are not moving. The bead on the rotating hoop, next, has the first and not the second; a pendulum whose length is being pulled in by a string has neither; a charge in a static magnetic field has both, with $L_1 = q\dot{\vec r}\cdot\vec A$ dropping out of $h$ entirely — the magnetic force does no work, and $h = \frac12m\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2 + q\phi$ (problem P5).

Full proof that the hoop's power is $\Omega\tau_z$

The bead's velocity is $R\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta + R\Omega\sin\theta\,\hat e_\phi$, where $\hat e_\theta$ is along the hoop and $\hat e_\phi$ is horizontal, perpendicular to the plane of the hoop. A frictionless hoop exerts a force $\vec N$ with no $\hat e_\theta$ component, so $\vec N = N_r\hat e_r + N_\phi\hat e_\phi$ with $\hat e_r$ pointing from the centre to the bead. The velocity has no $\hat e_r$ component (the bead stays at distance $R$), so the power is $\vec N\cdot\dot{\vec r} = N_\phi R\Omega\sin\theta$. The torque of $\vec N$ about the vertical axis is $\tau_z = N_\phi\cdot R\sin\theta$ (lever arm $R\sin\theta$); gravity is parallel to the axis and has no $z$-torque. So power $= \Omega\tau_z$, and $\tau_z = \mathrm dL_z/\mathrm dt$ by unit 08's torque law. By the work–energy theorem the power equals $\mathrm dE/\mathrm dt$. ∎

The identity $h = E - \Omega L_z$. $E - \Omega L_z = \frac12mR^2\dot\theta^2 + \frac12mR^2\Omega^2\sin^2\theta - mgR\cos\theta - mR^2\Omega^2\sin^2\theta$, which is the $h$ of the previous slide. It is not special to the hoop: whenever coordinates rotate uniformly at $\Omega$ about a fixed axis, $h = E - \Omega L_z$. That is Jacobi's integral, the conserved quantity of the restricted three-body problem in the frame rotating with the two primaries. The free-particle slide that follows — one particle, two coordinate systems — derives it in rotating coordinates; problem P7 does the translating analogue, where a uniformly moving frame gives $h = E - uP_x$.

Who drives whom. On the swing inwards, from $90^\circ$ to $11.19^\circ$, $L_z$ falls and the work done on the bead is negative: the bead drives the motor. On the swing back out the motor returns the $0.1925$ J. Over a full oscillation the books balance and $E$ returns to its starting value.

Which is "the" energy? Both are honest. $E$ is the bead's energy in the inertial frame and is not conserved because the bead is not isolated: a motor is holding $\Omega$ fixed. $h$ is what Noether's theorem attaches to the time-independence of the Lagrangian in these coordinates. $h$ belongs to the coordinates, not to the motion

one free particle, two coordinate systems

Inertial polar coordinates: $L = \frac12m(\dot\rho^2 + \rho^2\dot\varphi^2 + \dot z^2)$, and $h = E$.

Rotating ones, $\varphi = \varphi' + \Omega t$:

$$L' = \tfrac12m\big(\dot\rho^2 + \rho^2(\dot\varphi' + \Omega)^2 + \dot z^2\big),$$

still with no explicit $t$, and

$$h' = E - \Omega L_z .$$

Both conserved, and they differ. "The conserved quantity from time translation" depends on which time translation: at fixed $\varphi$ or at fixed $\varphi'$.

the running example

Cartesian coordinates in the centre-of-mass frame; $T$ is quadratic, so

$$\begin{aligned}h = T + V = E &= -\frac{Gm_Sm_J}{2a}\\ &= -1.618\times10^{35}\ \mathrm J .\end{aligned}$$

In coordinates co-rotating with Jupiter's mean motion, the same Noether argument gives $E - \Omega L_z$ instead — the form the three-body problem uses.

Every step of the rotating case. $\dot\varphi = \dot\varphi' + \Omega$, so $L' = \frac12m(\dot\rho^2 + \rho^2\dot\varphi'^2 + 2\Omega\rho^2\dot\varphi' + \Omega^2\rho^2 + \dot z^2)$. The pieces: $L_2 = \frac12m(\dot\rho^2 + \rho^2\dot\varphi'^2 + \dot z^2)$, $L_1 = m\Omega\rho^2\dot\varphi'$, $L_0 = \frac12m\Omega^2\rho^2$. Then $h' = L_2 - L_0 = \frac12m(\dot\rho^2 + \rho^2\dot\varphi'^2 + \dot z^2 - \Omega^2\rho^2)$. Substituting $\dot\varphi' = \dot\varphi - \Omega$: $\rho^2\dot\varphi'^2 - \Omega^2\rho^2 = \rho^2\dot\varphi^2 - 2\Omega\rho^2\dot\varphi$, so $h' = E - \Omega\,m\rho^2\dot\varphi = E - \Omega L_z$. ∎ Both $E$ and $L_z$ are conserved for a free particle, so $h'$ is too; the point is only that $h'\ne E$.

Why two different "time translations". In inertial coordinates, shifting the clock moves the curve $t\mapsto(\rho,\varphi,z)(t)$ to $(\rho,\varphi,z)(t+s)$. In rotating coordinates, shifting the clock at fixed $\varphi'$ is shifting the clock and rotating by $-\Omega s$, as seen from the inertial frame. So $h' = h + (-\Omega)\times(\text{rotation charge})$ by the linearity remark on the theorem slide: the charges add as the generators do.

The running-example number. $E = -k/2a$ with $k = 2.51905\times10^{47}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^3\,s^{-2}}$, $a = 7.78567\times10^{11}$ m, gives $-1.61775\times10^{35}$ J. The relation between energy and semi-major axis is unit 08's. In the frame where the centre of mass moves, $h$ gains the constant $\frac12M\lvert\dot{\vec R}\rvert^2$, also conserved.

The logic reversed. Newton postulated the third law and derived momentum conservation. Here the postulate is that the Lagrangian of an isolated system does not change when the whole system is moved — space has no preferred point — and both momentum conservation and the third law (for forces derived from a potential) are consequences. That is more economical and it is more general: it survives into field theory, where "action and reaction" between a charge and the field is not even well defined but momentum conservation is (unit 29).

Partial symmetry. If $L$ is invariant under translations in some directions only — a projectile in uniform gravity $V = Mgz$ is invariant under horizontal shifts — then only those components of $\vec P$ are conserved. For the vertical shift, $\delta L = -Mg$, which is $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(-Mgt)$: a quasi-symmetry after all, with charge $P_z + Mgt$. The checkpoint slide's cold try is this, and problem P1 has the same structure for a uniform electric field.

The cyclic-coordinate view. In coordinates $(\vec R, \vec r)$ — centre of mass and separation — $L = \frac12M\lvert\dot{\vec R}\rvert^2 + \frac12\mu\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2 - V(\vec r)$, and $\vec R$ does not appear: it is cyclic (unit 12), and its momentum $M\dot{\vec R} = \vec P$ is conserved. Noether's theorem is the coordinate-free version: no need to find the coordinates in which the symmetry is a shift of one of them.

Rotations: all three components of angular momentum

Rotate both bodies about $\hat n$: $\xi_a = \hat n\times\vec r_a$, $\delta L = 0$, $F = 0$. The charge, using the triple product $\vec p\cdot(\hat n\times\vec r) = \hat n\cdot(\vec r\times\vec p)$:

$$\begin{aligned}Q &= \sum_a\vec p_a\cdot(\hat n\times\vec r_a)\\ &= \hat n\cdot\sum_a\vec r_a\times\vec p_a = \hat n\cdot\vec L .\end{aligned}$$

Every axis $\hat n$ gives one component: the whole vector $\vec L$ is conserved, and the orbit is planar (unit 08).

the running example's value

The boost, where $L$ is not invariant

the Galilean boost on the running example

1. Family. $\vec r_a\mapsto\vec r_a + s\,t\,\hat n$, the view from a frame moving at velocity $-s\hat n$.

2. Generator. $\xi_a = t\hat n$, $\ \dot\xi_a = \hat n$.

3. $\delta L$. $V$ sees $\xi_S - \xi_J = 0$; $T$ gives $\sum_am_a\dot{\vec r}_a\cdot\hat n$:

$$\delta L = \hat n\cdot\big(m_S\dot{\vec r}_S + m_J\dot{\vec r}_J\big) = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(M\,\hat n\cdot\vec R\big).$$

4. So $F = M\hat n\cdot\vec R$ with $M\vec R = m_S\vec r_S + m_J\vec r_J$: a quasi-symmetry, not a symmetry.

5. Charge. $Q = \sum_a\vec p_a\cdot t\hat n - M\hat n\cdot\vec R = \hat n\cdot\big(\vec P\,t - M\vec R\big)$.

the boost charge $$\vec G = \vec P\,t - M\vec R\quad\text{is conserved.}$$

Since $\vec P$ is conserved too, $\vec R(t) = \vec R(0) + (\vec P/M)\,t$: the centre of mass moves uniformly.

Without $F$ the "charge" would be $\vec Pt$, which is not conserved. The extra term is the whole content.

Why the boost cannot be a strict symmetry. Kinetic energy is not Galilean invariant: a body at rest in one frame is moving in another. So $L$ must change. What Galilean relativity requires is only that the equations of motion be the same in both frames, and unit 12's gauge freedom says that is guaranteed exactly when $L$ changes by a total derivative. The finite version is on the example slide's notes: $L_s - L = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(sM\hat n\cdot\vec R + \frac12Ms^2t)$.

One body in a fixed potential. For unit 08's $L = \frac12\mu\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2 + k/r$ the boost $\vec r\mapsto\vec r + st\hat n$ changes $V$: $\delta V = \nabla V\cdot t\hat n \ne 0$, so it is not even a quasi-symmetry. The fixed centre is an external body that was not boosted. That is why the running example keeps both bodies.

No point symmetry produces $\vec A$

no point symmetry gives it

If $\xi = \xi(q,t)$ then $F = F(q,t)$, and $Q = \mu\dot{\vec r}\cdot\xi - F$ is affine in $\dot{\vec r}$. Time translation adds a multiple of $h$, whose velocity-quadratic part is a multiple of $\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2$. But

$$\begin{aligned}\hat e\cdot\vec A = {}&\mu^2\big(\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec r) - (\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r})(\hat e\cdot\dot{\vec r})\big)\\ &- \mu k\,\frac{\hat e\cdot\vec r}{r},\end{aligned}$$

whose quadratic part is not a multiple of $\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2$.

so the search space was too small

Configuration-space transformations, even with time shifts, cannot produce $\vec A$. The theorem, though, never asked $\xi$ to be a function of position: it allowed $\xi(q,\dot q,t)$.

the plan

Find a velocity-dependent $\xi$ whose $\delta L$ is a total derivative, and check that its charge is $\hat e\cdot\vec A$. It exists; the next two slides give it and verify it.

Full proof that no point symmetry gives $\vec A$

Step 1: $F$ has no velocities. If $\xi = \xi(\vec r,t)$ then $\dot\xi = D\xi\,\dot{\vec r} + \partial_t\xi$ has no $\ddot{\vec r}$, so $\delta L$ is a function of $(\vec r,\dot{\vec r},t)$ alone. If $F$ depended on $\dot{\vec r}$, $\dot F$ would contain $\partial_{\dot{\vec r}}F\cdot\ddot{\vec r}$, and since $\ddot{\vec r}$ can be chosen freely at any point of any curve, the identity $\delta L = \dot F$ forces $\partial_{\dot{\vec r}}F = 0$.

Step 2: the charge is affine. $Q = \mu\dot{\vec r}\cdot\xi(\vec r,t) - F(\vec r,t)$ is affine in $\dot{\vec r}$. Adding a multiple $c\,h$ of the time-translation charge (a combined generator $\xi + c\dot{\vec r}$) adds $c(\frac12\mu\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2 - k/r)$. So every such charge has velocity-quadratic part $\frac c2\mu\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2$, i.e. the quadratic form $\frac c2\mu\,I$.

Step 3: $\hat e\cdot\vec A$ is not of that form. By BAC–CAB, $\vec p\times(\vec r\times\vec p) = \vec r\lvert\vec p\rvert^2 - \vec p(\vec r\cdot\vec p)$, which gives the displayed formula. Its quadratic part in $\vec v = \dot{\vec r}$ is the form $\mu^2\big(\lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec r) - (\vec r\cdot\vec v)(\hat e\cdot\vec v)\big)$, with matrix $\mu^2\big((\hat e\cdot\vec r)I - \frac12(\vec r\hat e^{\top} + \hat e\vec r^{\top})\big)$. This is a multiple of $I$ only if $\vec r\hat e^{\top} + \hat e\vec r^{\top}$ is. But that matrix sends any vector perpendicular to both $\vec r$ and $\hat e$ to $0$, so it would have to be $0$, which forces $\vec r = 0$. At any $\vec r\ne0$ the forms differ. ∎

Even allowing time to be reparametrised by a function $\tau(\vec r,t)$ does not help: that adds $-\tau h$, whose quadratic part is still a multiple of $\lvert\vec v\rvert^2$ at each point. Problem P11 is this proof, set as an exercise with the phase-space rescue attached.

Letting the transformation see the velocity

a generalised symmetry

A variation $\delta q = \xi(q,\dot q,t)$ with $\delta L = \dot F$ for every curve, $F = F(q,\dot q,t)$. Noether's three lines apply unchanged.

For $L_{\mathrm{rel}}$ and a fixed unit vector $\hat e$, take

$$\xi = \mu\Big(2\,(\hat e\cdot\vec r)\,\dot{\vec r} - (\hat e\cdot\dot{\vec r})\,\vec r - (\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r})\,\hat e\Big),$$ $$F = \mu^2\Big(\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec r) - (\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r})(\hat e\cdot\dot{\vec r})\Big) + \mu k\,\frac{\hat e\cdot\vec r}{r}.$$
claim

$\delta L_{\mathrm{rel}} = \dot F$ for every curve, and the charge is

$$Q = \mu\dot{\vec r}\cdot\xi - F = \hat e\cdot\vec A .$$

Verified on the next slide.

what kind of object this is

$\xi$ moves a point $\vec r$ by an amount depending on $\dot{\vec r}$. So it is not a map of configuration space; it acts on states $(\vec r,\dot{\vec r})$. Its natural home is phase space, unit 14.

dimensions

$[\xi] = \mathsf{M\,L^2T^{-1}}$, so $[s] = \mathsf{L}/[\xi] = \mathsf{M^{-1}L^{-1}T}$ and $[Q] = \mathsf{ML^2T^{-1}}/[s] = \mathsf{M^2L^3T^{-2}} = [\mu k]$ ✓.

Where the formula comes from. Guess $\xi = \mu\big(\alpha(\hat e\cdot\vec r)\dot{\vec r} + \beta(\hat e\cdot\dot{\vec r})\vec r + \gamma(\vec r\cdot\dot{\vec r})\hat e\big)$ — the most general vector built from $\vec r$, $\dot{\vec r}$, $\hat e$ that is linear in $\hat e$, linear in $\dot{\vec r}$ and linear in $\vec r$. Then demand that $\delta L$ be a total derivative. Two conditions come out: the terms containing $\ddot{\vec r}$ must assemble into a derivative, which forces $\gamma = \beta$; and the leftover $\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\dot{\vec r})$ term must vanish, which forces $\beta = -\alpha/2$. With $\alpha = 2$ the potential terms then assemble into $\mu k\,\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\hat e\cdot\vec r/r)$ on their own — and that step works only for the $1/r$ potential. Problem P10 is this derivation.

A tidier form. Using BAC–CAB twice, $\xi = \vec L\times\hat e + \vec r\times(\vec p\times\hat e)$ with $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$. The second piece is $\vec r\times(\vec p\times\hat e) = (\hat e\times\vec p)\times\vec r$: a rotation, but about the state-dependent axis $\hat e\times\vec p$. The first, $\vec L\times\hat e$, does not depend on $\vec r$ at all — it is a translation by a state-dependent vector. Neither piece alone is a generalised symmetry.

History. The velocity-dependent generator of the Runge–Lenz vector was written down in this Lagrangian form by Lévy-Leblond, "Conservation laws for gauge-variant Lagrangians in classical mechanics", Am. J. Phys. 39 (1971) 502, whose point was precisely that quasi-invariance is needed. The underlying symmetry was found first in quantum mechanics: Pauli (1926) used the quantum Runge–Lenz vector to get the hydrogen spectrum before Schrödinger's equation was solved for it, and Fock (1935) showed the bound-state symmetry group is $SO(4)$.

The Runge–Lenz generator, checked

every step, off-shell

Compute $\delta L_{\mathrm{rel}} = -\dfrac{k}{r^3}\,\vec r\cdot\xi + \mu\,\vec v\cdot\dot\xi$ and compare it with $\dot F$, where $\vec v = \dot{\vec r}$ and $\vec a = \ddot{\vec r}$ are arbitrary: no equation of motion is used.

1. Differentiate $\xi$. $\dot\xi/\mu = 2(\hat e\cdot\vec v)\vec v + 2(\hat e\cdot\vec r)\vec a - (\hat e\cdot\vec a)\vec r - (\hat e\cdot\vec v)\vec v - (\lvert\vec v\rvert^2 + \vec r\cdot\vec a)\hat e$.

2. Kinetic part. Dot with $\mu\vec v$:

$$\begin{aligned}\mu\vec v\cdot\dot\xi = \mu^2\big[&2(\hat e\cdot\vec r)(\vec v\cdot\vec a) - (\vec r\cdot\vec v)(\hat e\cdot\vec a)\\ &- (\hat e\cdot\vec v)(\vec r\cdot\vec a)\big].\end{aligned}$$

(The $\lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec v)$ terms cancel: $2 - 1 - 1 = 0$.)

3. That is exactly $\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\mu^2\big[\lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec r) - (\vec r\cdot\vec v)(\hat e\cdot\vec v)\big]$ — expand the derivative and the $\lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec v)$ terms cancel again.

4. Potential part. $\vec r\cdot\xi = \mu\big[2(\hat e\cdot\vec r)(\vec r\cdot\vec v) - r^2(\hat e\cdot\vec v) - (\vec r\cdot\vec v)(\hat e\cdot\vec r)\big] = \mu\big[(\hat e\cdot\vec r)(\vec r\cdot\vec v) - r^2(\hat e\cdot\vec v)\big]$, so

$$\begin{aligned}-\frac{k}{r^3}\vec r\cdot\xi &= \mu k\Big[\frac{\hat e\cdot\vec v}{r} - \frac{(\hat e\cdot\vec r)(\vec r\cdot\vec v)}{r^3}\Big]\\ &= \mu k\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac{\hat e\cdot\vec r}{r}.\end{aligned}$$

5. Steps 3 and 4 add to $\dot F$ ✓. 6. Charge. $\mu\vec v\cdot\xi = \mu^2\big[2\lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec r) - 2(\hat e\cdot\vec v)(\vec r\cdot\vec v)\big]$, and subtracting $F$:

$$\begin{aligned}Q &= \mu^2\big[\lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec r) - (\vec r\cdot\vec v)(\hat e\cdot\vec v)\big] - \mu k\frac{\hat e\cdot\vec r}{r}\\ &= \hat e\cdot\vec A\ \checkmark\end{aligned}$$

Step 3 in full. $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big[\lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec r)\big] = 2(\vec v\cdot\vec a)(\hat e\cdot\vec r) + \lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec v)$ and $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big[(\vec r\cdot\vec v)(\hat e\cdot\vec v)\big] = (\lvert\vec v\rvert^2 + \vec r\cdot\vec a)(\hat e\cdot\vec v) + (\vec r\cdot\vec v)(\hat e\cdot\vec a)$. Subtracting, the $\lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec v)$ terms cancel and what is left is step 2's bracket. ✓

Step 4 in full. $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\frac{\hat e\cdot\vec r}{r} = \frac{\hat e\cdot\vec v}{r} - \frac{(\hat e\cdot\vec r)\dot r}{r^2}$ and $\dot r = \vec r\cdot\vec v/r$ (differentiate $r^2 = \vec r\cdot\vec r$), giving the bracket on the slide. This is the only step that uses the potential, and it uses $1/r$ exactly: for $V = -k/r^n$ the factor would be $r^{-n-2}$ and the bracket would not be a derivative.

Step 6 and unit 08. $\vec A = \vec p\times\vec L - \mu k\hat r$ with $\vec p\times\vec L = \mu^2\vec v\times(\vec r\times\vec v) = \mu^2\big(\vec r\lvert\vec v\rvert^2 - \vec v(\vec r\cdot\vec v)\big)$ by BAC–CAB, so $\hat e\cdot\vec A$ is the displayed $Q$. As $\hat e$ ranges over three independent directions, all three components of $\vec A$ are Noether charges.

An independent check on $Q$, in one line. Take a circular orbit of radius $r$, where $\vec r\cdot\vec v = 0$ and the force balance gives $\mu\lvert\vec v\rvert^2 = k/r$. Then $Q = \mu^2\lvert\vec v\rvert^2(\hat e\cdot\vec r) - \mu k(\hat e\cdot\vec r)/r = \mu(\hat e\cdot\vec r)\big(\mu\lvert\vec v\rvert^2 - k/r\big) = 0$ for every $\hat e$, so $\vec A = \vec 0$ — which is $\lvert\vec A\rvert = \mu ke$ with $e = 0$ ✓.

Where the hypothesis "every curve" was used. Steps 1 to 5 never used the equation of motion: $\vec a$ was arbitrary. Only when the theorem is applied does $\mu\vec a = -k\vec r/r^3$ enter, through $\dot p = \partial L/\partial q$ — which is unit 08's direct differentiation of $\vec A$, reorganised.

The hidden SO(4)

For a bound orbit, $E \lt 0$. Rescale $\vec A$ to have the dimensions of angular momentum:

$$\vec M := \frac{\vec A}{\sqrt{-2\mu E}} .$$
the energy knows only $L^2 + M^2$ $$\lvert\vec L\rvert^2 + \lvert\vec M\rvert^2 = -\frac{\mu k^2}{2E}.$$

Proof. Unit 08: $\lvert\vec A\rvert^2 = \mu^2k^2 + 2\mu E\lvert\vec L\rvert^2$. Divide by $-2\mu E$. ∎

checks

$[\vec A/\sqrt{\mu E}] = \mathsf{M^2L^3T^{-2}}/\mathsf{M\,L\,T^{-1}} = \mathsf{ML^2T^{-1}}$ ✓. Circle: $\vec M = 0$, $\lvert\vec L\rvert^2 = \mu ka$ ✓.

Jupiter

$\lvert\vec L\rvert = 1.9262\times10^{43}$, $\ \lvert\vec M\rvert = e\sqrt{\mu ka} = 9.430\times10^{41}$, and $\sqrt{L^2 + M^2} = \sqrt{\mu ka} = 1.9285\times10^{43}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}$: the angular momentum of the circular orbit with the same energy.

Six conserved components, $\vec L$ and $\vec M$, with equal dimensions; the next slide draws what they say.

Their Poisson brackets (defined in unit 14) close on the Lie algebra of $SO(4)$, rotations in four dimensions; the energy depends only on the combination $L^2 + M^2$ that such rotations preserve.

Cited, proved in unit 14: $\{L_1,L_2\} = L_3$, $\{M_1,L_2\} = M_3$, $\{M_1,M_2\} = L_3$, and the same under cycling $1\to2\to3\to1$.

The numbers. $\mu k a = 1.89631\times10^{27}\cdot2.51905\times10^{47}\cdot7.78567\times10^{11}$ gives $\sqrt{\mu ka} = 1.92851\times10^{43}$; $\lvert\vec M\rvert = \lvert\vec A\rvert/\sqrt{-2\mu E}$ with $\lvert\vec A\rvert = \mu ke = 2.33591\times10^{73}$ and $E = -1.61775\times10^{35}$ J is $9.43040\times10^{41}$, which equals $e\sqrt{\mu ka}$ since $-2\mu E = \mu k/a$. Then $\sqrt{L^2 + M^2} = 1.92851\times10^{43}$ agrees with $\sqrt{\mu k a}$ to all digits, and $-\mu k^2/2(L^2 + M^2)$ returns $E = -1.61775\times10^{35}$ J ✓.

Why this is $SO(4)$ — cited. The Poisson bracket, defined in unit 14, turns conserved quantities into generators of flows on phase space and measures how two such flows fail to commute. The three brackets on the slide are the commutation relations of the six rotation generators of $\mathbb R^4$ (rotations in the planes $x_ix_j$ and $x_ix_4$), which is the claim; unit 14 computes them. Equivalently $\vec J_\pm = \frac12(\vec L\pm\vec M)$ satisfy two independent copies of the angular-momentum relations, and $L^2 + M^2 = 2(J_+^2 + J_-^2)$. For $E \gt 0$ the sign under the root flips and the algebra becomes that of the Lorentz group $SO(3,1)$ — unit 26's group, appearing in Newtonian gravity for scattering orbits.

Why physicists care. In quantum mechanics this symmetry is why the hydrogen energy levels depend on the principal quantum number alone: $n^2$ states share each energy, the dimension of an irreducible representation of $SO(4)$ of the relevant kind. Pauli computed the spectrum this way in 1926. The classical statement on this slide — $E$ depends only on $L^2 + M^2$ — is the same fact before quantisation.

What "hidden" means. The group generated by $\vec L$ and $\vec M$ is bigger than the rotation group of space, $SO(3)$, and it does not act on space: its extra generators are the velocity-dependent $\xi$ of the previous slides. It mixes orbits of the same energy but different angular momentum and eccentricity — a circle can be continuously turned into a thin ellipse of the same semi-major axis — which is why $E$ depends on $a$ alone and not on $e$.

The converse is false, and what rescues it

the converse of Noether's theorem, for point symmetries

"Every conserved quantity is the charge of a transformation of configuration space" is false. $\vec A$ is a counterexample (the slide No point symmetry produces $\vec A$).

why it was never plausible

Conserved quantities are cheap. Any function of conserved quantities is conserved: $h^2$ is, and its generator is $\xi = 2h\,\dot q$, with $F = 2hL + h^2$ — velocity-dependent. And by unit 01 the initial state is a function of the state at time $t$, so every system has $2n$ independent time-dependent ones. Symmetries of configuration space are far scarcer.

the rescue: enlarge the space

Describe a state by $(q,p)$ instead of $(q,\dot q)$, and write the energy $h$ as a function $H(q,p)$ of them — unit 14's phase space and Hamiltonian. Then every function $G(q,p)$ generates a flow, the solutions of $\dot q = \partial G/\partial p$, $\dot p = -\partial G/\partial q$, and that flow leaves $H$ unchanged exactly when $G$ is conserved.

So on phase space the correspondence symmetry ⇔ charge is a bijection. Cited: proved in unit 14 with the Poisson bracket, where it takes one line.

The velocity-dependent $\xi$ for $\vec A$ is the shadow in $(q,\dot q)$ of the phase-space flow generated by $\vec A$.

Check the $h^2$ generator (for an autonomous $L$). With $\xi = 2h\dot q$, $\delta L = 2h(\partial_qL\cdot\dot q + \partial_{\dot q}L\cdot\ddot q) + 2\dot h\,p\cdot\dot q = 2h\dot L + 2\dot h\,p\cdot\dot q$, off-shell. And $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(2hL + h^2) = 2\dot hL + 2h\dot L + 2h\dot h$. The difference is $2\dot h(p\cdot\dot q - L - h) = 0$ by the definition of $h$. So $F = 2hL + h^2$ and $Q = 2h\,p\cdot\dot q - 2hL - h^2 = 2h^2 - h^2 = h^2$ ✓. The same trick makes any function $f(Q)$ of a charge into a charge, of the generator $f'(Q)\,\xi$ — and that generator depends on the velocities whenever $Q$ does.

The initial-condition functions, and a caution. For the oscillator $\ddot q = -\omega^2q$, $I = q\cos\omega t - (\dot q/\omega)\sin\omega t$ is the initial position as a function of the state at time $t$: $\dot I = \dot q\cos\omega t - \omega q\sin\omega t - (\ddot q/\omega)\sin\omega t - \dot q\cos\omega t = 0$ on-shell. It is not a counterexample to the converse: it is the charge of the time-dependent translation $\xi = -\sin(\omega t)/(m\omega)$, a quasi-symmetry with $F = m\dot\xi\,q$ (check: $\delta L - \dot F = -mq(\ddot\xi + \omega^2\xi) = 0$ for every curve). The Runge–Lenz vector is the honest counterexample, because it is quadratic in the velocities in a way the no-point-symmetry proof rules out.

The rescue, stated precisely (cited, unit 14). Given $H(q,p)$ and any $G(q,p)$, the flow of $\dot q = \partial G/\partial p$, $\dot p = -\partial G/\partial q$ changes $H$ at rate $\{H, G\}$, and $G$ changes along the true motion at rate $\{G,H\} = -\{H,G\}$. So "$G$ is conserved" and "$G$'s flow preserves $H$" are the same equation. This is Noether's theorem and its converse in one line — the reason unit 14 calls it "Noether's converse, now cheap". The price is that the symmetries are transformations of phase space that mix positions and momenta, which have no picture in configuration space.

What the theorem does and does not say, summarised. It says: continuous (quasi-)symmetry of $L$ ⇒ conserved charge, explicitly. It does not say: every conserved quantity comes from a symmetry of space; a symmetry of the equations of motion gives a charge; a discrete symmetry gives one; or anything at all when the equations are not Euler–Lagrange equations.

Done when

checkpoint

Given any Lagrangian $L(q,\dot q,t)$ and any one-parameter symmetry of it, you can, on paper and without the deck,

  • write down the conserved quantity: find the generator $\xi$, compute $\delta L$ for an arbitrary curve, identify the $F$ with $\delta L = \dot F$, and form $Q = p\cdot\xi - F$;
  • verify it is constant on-shell, by differentiating $Q$ and using the Euler–Lagrange equations; and
  • do both for at least one quasi-invariant case, where $L$ itself is not invariant and the charge acquires the extra term $-F$ — the Galilean boost, or a translation in a magnetic field.

Falsifiable: a charge that omits $F$ in a quasi-invariant case, or a $\delta L$ computed only on solutions, fails it. Record it with ./mr done 13.

Try it cold

A particle in uniform gravity, $L = \frac12m\lvert\dot{\vec r}\rvert^2 - mgz$, under the vertical shift $z\mapsto z + s$. Answer in the notes.

The cold try, worked. $\xi = \hat z$, $\dot\xi = 0$, so $\delta L = -mg$. That is not zero, but it is $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(-mgt)$: a quasi-symmetry with $F = -mgt$. The charge is $Q = m\dot z - (-mgt) = m\dot z + mgt$. On-shell, $\ddot z = -g$, so $\dot Q = m\ddot z + mg = 0$ ✓. Dimensions: $[mgt] = \mathsf{M\,LT^{-2}\,T} = \mathsf{MLT^{-1}}$, a momentum ✓. Limit $g\to0$: $Q$ is the vertical momentum of a free particle ✓. If you wrote $Q = m\dot z$ and then found it was not conserved, you have met the checkpoint's failure mode.

Computing $\delta L$ only along solutions (using the equation of motion while computing it) makes every $\xi$ look like a symmetry with charge $0$; go back to the off-shell warning. Forgetting the $F$ term gives $\vec Pt$ instead of $\vec G$ for the boost; go back to the boost example.
