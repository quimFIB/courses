The Legendre transform

P1 Three transforms by hand routine

exercises: the Legendre transform from the supremum, the value \(+\infty\), Young's inequality, involutivity

  1. Compute \(f^*\) for \(f(x) = e^x\) on \(\mathbb R\), for every \(p\in\mathbb R\) — including the values of \(p\) where the supremum is not attained or is infinite. Then compute \(f^{**}\) and check it is \(e^x\).
  2. A Lagrangian \(L(v) = \tfrac12 mv^2 + cv\) with a constant \(c\) (unit 12's charged particle has a term of this shape). Compute \(H = L^*\), the momentum \(p\) as a function of \(v\), and say why \(p\ne mv\).
  3. For \(a = 3\), verify Young's inequality \(px\le x^a/a + p^b/b\) numerically at \((x,p) = (2,3)\) and at \((x,p) = (2,4)\), and explain from the equality condition why one of them is an equality.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each answer is a function of \(p\) that may take the value \(+\infty\). Before differentiating anything, split by the sign of \(p\) and ask what \(px - f(x)\) does as \(x\to\pm\infty\).

Rung 2

Where the bracket \(px - f(x)\) is strictly concave with a critical point, that point is the maximum. Where there is no critical point the supremum is a limit. For part 3 you need \(b\) from \(\tfrac1a + \tfrac1b = 1\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1, for \(p > 0\): \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}(px - e^x) = p - e^x = 0\) at \(x = \ln p\). For part 3, \(b = 3/2\), and equality holds exactly when \(p = x^{a-1} = x^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. Use the definition, not the recipe: the recipe finds only the critical point, and two of the three regimes of part 1 have none.

1. \(p > 0\): \(px - e^x\) is strictly concave with its critical point at \(x = \ln p\), so \(f^*(p) = p\ln p - p\). \(p = 0\): \(\sup(-e^x) = 0\), approached as \(x\to-\infty\) and never attained; \(f^*(0) = 0\), which is also the limit of \(p\ln p - p\) as \(p\to0^+\). \(p < 0\): as \(x\to-\infty\), \(px\to+\infty\) and \(e^x\to0\), so \(f^*(p) = +\infty\). Geometrically: no line of negative slope lies below the exponential.

For \(f^{**}(x) = \sup_p\big(xp - f^*(p)\big)\), only \(p\ge0\) matter (the rest give \(-\infty\)). For \(p > 0\), \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dp}\big(xp - p\ln p + p\big) = x - \ln p = 0\) at \(p = e^x\), a maximum since the bracket is concave in \(p\) (second derivative \(-1/p\)). The value is \(xe^x - xe^x + e^x = e^x\) ✓, and \(p = 0\) gives only \(0 < e^x\).

2. \(pv - \tfrac12mv^2 - cv\) is maximal at \(v = (p-c)/m\), so \[ H(p) = \frac{(p-c)^2}{2m}, \qquad p = \frac{\partial L}{\partial v} = mv + c. \] The momentum conjugate to \(x\) is not \(mv\) because the term \(cv\) in \(L\) depends on the velocity: \(p\) is whatever \(\partial L/\partial\dot x\) is. For a charge in a magnetic field this is the distinction between canonical momentum \(m\vec v + q\vec A\) and kinetic momentum \(m\vec v\). The Hamiltonian in terms of \(v\) is \((mv)^2/2m = \tfrac12 mv^2\): the linear term carries no energy.

3. \(b = a/(a-1) = 3/2\). At \((2,3)\): \(px = 6\) and \(x^3/3 + p^{3/2}/(3/2) = 8/3 + \tfrac23\cdot 5.196152 = 2.666667 + 3.464102 = 6.130768 \gt 6\) ✓. At \((2,4)\): \(px = 8\) and \(8/3 + \tfrac23\cdot8 = 8\): equality, because \(p = x^{a-1} = x^2 = 4\) — \(p\) is exactly the slope of \(f = x^3/3\) at \(x = 2\).

Check. Dimensions in part 2: \(c\) must be a momentum for \(cv\) to be an energy, so \((p-c)^2/2m\) is an energy ✓. Limit \(c\to0\): \(H = p^2/2m\) ✓. And the smooth-duality theorem: \(H'(p) = (p-c)/m = v\) ✓, \(H'' = 1/m = 1/L''\) ✓.

P2 Degenerate, and what survives medium

exercises: the degenerate Legendre transform, constraints, a first-order Lagrangian, Hamilton's equations without a Legendre transform

  1. Let \(f(\vec v) = \lVert\vec v\rVert\) on \(\mathbb R^n\) (the arc-length integrand). Compute \(f^*\) and say what the recipe "\(H = p\cdot v - L\)" would have given.
  2. Let \(f(v) = \sqrt{1+v^2}\) on \(\mathbb R\) (unit 11's arc length of a graph). Show it is strictly convex and compute \(f^*\). Compare with the deck's third degenerate-case example.
  3. Let \(L = \tfrac12(x\dot y - y\dot x) - V(x,y)\). Compute the momenta \(p_x, p_y\); show \(L\) is degenerate; write the Euler–Lagrange equations; and show they are Hamilton's equations for the Hamiltonian \(V\) with \(y\) as the coordinate and \(x\) as its momentum. What does the naive recipe give, and why is it wrong?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1: a function of \(\vec p\) taking only the values \(0\) and \(+\infty\). Part 2: a finite function on a bounded set, \(+\infty\) outside. Part 3: two first-order equations, not four.

Rung 2

Part 1: Cauchy–Schwarz for \(\lVert\vec p\rVert\le1\), and the ray \(\vec v = t\vec p\) otherwise. Part 2: \(f'(v) = v/\sqrt{1+v^2}\) maps \(\mathbb R\) onto \((-1,1)\) only. Part 3: \(L\) is linear in the velocities, so its velocity Hessian is zero.

Rung 3

Part 3's first line: \(p_x = \partial L/\partial\dot x = -y/2\) and \(p_y = x/2\) — momenta that are functions of position alone. Then the \(x\)-equation is \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(-y/2) = \dot y/2 - \partial_xV\).

Worked solution

Idea. Degeneracy is a failure of the change of variables, not of the dynamics. The transform says precisely what goes wrong (a constraint on \(p\)); part 3 shows the dynamics can survive intact, in half the variables.

1. If \(\lVert\vec p\rVert\le1\), Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\vec p\cdot\vec v\le \lVert\vec p\rVert\lVert\vec v\rVert\le\lVert\vec v\rVert\), so the bracket is \(\le0\), with \(0\) at \(\vec v = 0\). If \(\lVert\vec p\rVert > 1\), \(\vec v = t\vec p\) gives \(t\lVert\vec p\rVert(\lVert\vec p\rVert - 1)\to\infty\). So \(f^* = 0\) on the closed unit ball and \(+\infty\) outside. The recipe: \(\vec p = \vec v/\lVert\vec v\rVert\) is a unit vector for every \(\vec v\ne0\) and cannot be solved for \(\vec v\) (every positive multiple gives the same \(\vec p\)); plugging in anyway gives \(\vec p\cdot\vec v - \lVert\vec v\rVert = 0\) identically. So the recipe's "\(H = 0\)" is right on the sphere \(\lVert\vec p\rVert = 1\), and what it misses is that \(\vec p\) is confined there — a constraint.

2. \(f''(v) = (1+v^2)^{-3/2} > 0\). For \(|p| < 1\) the critical point of \(pv - \sqrt{1+v^2}\) is \(v = p/\sqrt{1-p^2}\), with value \(p^2/\sqrt{1-p^2} - 1/\sqrt{1-p^2} = -\sqrt{1-p^2}\). At \(|p| = 1\) the supremum is \(\lim_{v\to\pm\infty}(|v| - \sqrt{1+v^2}) = 0\), not attained. For \(|p|>1\) it is \(+\infty\). So \[ f^*(p) = -\sqrt{1-p^2}\ \ (|p|\le1),\qquad +\infty\ \ (|p| > 1). \] This is the deck's example \(f(v) = -\sqrt{1-v^2}\) with the roles exchanged: there a bounded velocity had an unbounded momentum; here an unbounded velocity has a bounded momentum. Transforming twice returns \(\sqrt{1+v^2}\), as involutivity promises.

3. \(p_x = -y/2\), \(p_y = x/2\); the velocity Hessian is the zero matrix. Euler–Lagrange: \[ x:\ \ \tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(-\tfrac y2\big) = \tfrac{\dot y}2 - \partial_xV\ \Rightarrow\ \dot y = \partial_xV; \qquad y:\ \ \tfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big(\tfrac x2\big) = -\tfrac{\dot x}2 - \partial_yV\ \Rightarrow\ \dot x = -\partial_yV. \] With \(q := y\), \(p := x\), \(H := V\) these read \(\dot q = \partial H/\partial p\), \(\dot p = -\partial H/\partial q\): Hamilton's equations on the two-dimensional \((y,x)\) plane. The naive recipe gives \(H = p_x\dot x + p_y\dot y - L = V\) on the four-dimensional space, and Hamilton's equations there would say \(\dot x = \partial V/\partial p_x = 0\) — false. The recipe's \(H\) is right; its phase space is wrong: the constraints \(p_x = -y/2\), \(p_y = x/2\) cut the four dimensions to two, and on those two \(x\) and \(y\) are conjugate.

This \(L\) is the phase-space action of the deck with \(y\) and \(x\) as \(q\) and \(p\), up to the total derivative \(\tfrac12\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(xy)\). It is also the \(m\to0\) limit of unit 12's charge in a uniform magnetic field (with \(qB = 1\)), where it gives the drift of the guiding centre along the level curves of \(V\); and it is Kirchhoff's Lagrangian for a point vortex in a two-dimensional fluid (cited: Saffman, Vortex Dynamics, the chapter on line vortices in two dimensions).

Check. \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}V = \partial_xV\,\dot x + \partial_yV\,\dot y = -\partial_xV\partial_yV + \partial_yV\partial_xV = 0\): \(V\) is conserved, as the Hamiltonian must be ✓. Special case \(V = \tfrac12(x^2+y^2)\): \(\dot y = x\), \(\dot x = -y\), uniform circular motion ✓.

P3 Proof: the transform sees only the convex envelope medium

exercises: a proof, the Fenchel–Young inequality, involutivity, a non-convex example

Let \(f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) be any function with \(f^*\) finite somewhere.

  1. Prove that \(f^{**}\le f\) and that \(f^{**}\) is convex.
  2. Prove that if \(g\) is convex and differentiable with \(g\le f\), then \(g\le f^{**}\). Conclude that \(f^{**}\) is the largest convex differentiable function below \(f\) whenever that largest one exists and \(f^{**}\) is itself differentiable.
  3. For the double well \(f(x) = (x^2-1)^2\), find \(f^{**}\) explicitly, and prove your answer.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 are each two lines, using only the definition, the Fenchel–Young inequality and the involutivity theorem. Part 3's answer agrees with \(f\) outside an interval and is constant inside it.

Rung 2

The transform reverses order: \(g\le f\) implies \(g^*\ge f^*\). Apply it twice. For part 3, guess the candidate \(h\) — flatten the bump — and squeeze: show \(h\le f^{**}\) by part 2, and \(f^{**}\le h\) using convexity of \(f^{**}\) and part 1.

Rung 3

The candidate is \(h(x) = 0\) for \(|x|\le1\) and \(h(x) = (x^2-1)^2\) for \(|x| \gt 1\). First line of the squeeze: \(f^{**}(\pm1)\le f(\pm1) = 0\), so by convexity \(f^{**}\le0\) on \([-1,1]\).

Worked solution

Idea. \(f^{**}\) is a supremum of affine functions lying below \(f\) — the supporting lines — so it is convex and below \(f\); and any convex differentiable \(g\) below \(f\) is itself a supremum of such lines.

1. Fenchel–Young: \(px - f^*(p)\le f(x)\) for all \(p\); take the supremum over \(p\) to get \(f^{**}(x)\le f(x)\). Convexity: \(f^{**}\) is a supremum of the affine functions \(x\mapsto px - f^*(p)\) (those with \(f^*(p)\) finite), and a supremum of affine functions is convex.

2. If \(g\le f\) then \(px - g(x)\ge px - f(x)\) for every \(x\), so \(g^*(p)\ge f^*(p)\). Then \(px - g^*(p)\le px - f^*(p)\), so \(g^{**}\le f^{**}\). By the involutivity theorem, \(g^{**} = g\). Hence \(g\le f^{**}\). So every convex differentiable minorant lies below \(f^{**}\), and \(f^{**}\) is a convex minorant by part 1; if it is differentiable it is the largest such.

3. Let \(h\) be the candidate of rung 3. It is \(C^1\): on \(|x| \gt 1\), \(h' = 4x(x^2-1)\), which tends to \(0\) at \(x = \pm1\), matching \(h' = 0\) inside. It is convex: \(h'\) is \(0\) on \([-1,1]\) and on \(|x|\gt1\) has derivative \(12x^2 - 4 \gt 0\), and it is increasing through \(\pm1\), so \(h'\) is nondecreasing everywhere. And \(h\le f\), since \(f\ge0\). By part 2, \(h\le f^{**}\). Conversely \(f^{**}\le f = h\) outside \([-1,1]\); and \(f^{**}(\pm1)\le f(\pm1) = 0\), so for \(x = (1-\lambda)(-1) + \lambda\cdot1\) convexity gives \(f^{**}(x)\le0 = h(x)\). So \(f^{**} = h\). \(\square\)

What it means. The bump between the wells is invisible to the transform: \(f^*\) is unchanged if \(f\) is replaced by \(h\). In thermodynamics this is Maxwell's construction — the free energy of a system with two coexisting phases is the convex envelope of the single-phase one — and in mechanics it is why the Legendre transform to a Hamiltonian needs \(L\) convex in \(\dot q\): if it is not, the transform silently replaces it by its envelope, and the Hamiltonian describes different dynamics.

Check. Numerically, \(f^*(0) = \sup(-f) = 0\) and \(h^*(0) = 0\) ✓; at \(x = 0\), \(f(0) = 1\) while \(f^{**}(0) = 0\) ✓. Limit: far from the wells \(f\) is convex and \(f^{**} = f\), as involutivity says it must be locally where no line has to bridge a gap ✓.

P4 The Hamiltonian in a rotating frame routine

exercises: Legendre transform of a Lagrangian with a linear term, Hamilton's equations, \(H\) versus \(T+V\), unit 03's fictitious forces

In a frame rotating at constant \(\vec\Omega\) relative to an inertial one, a particle of mass \(m\) at rotating-frame position \(\vec r\) in a potential \(V(\vec r)\) has \(L = \tfrac12 m\lVert\dot{\vec r} + \vec\Omega\times\vec r\rVert^2 - V(\vec r)\).

  1. Find \(\vec p\) and show \(H = \lVert\vec p\rVert^2/2m - \vec\Omega\cdot(\vec r\times\vec p) + V\).
  2. Write Hamilton's equations and eliminate \(\vec p\) to recover unit 03's \(m\ddot{\vec r} = -\nabla V - 2m\vec\Omega\times\dot{\vec r} - m\vec\Omega\times(\vec\Omega\times\vec r)\).
  3. Express \(H\) in terms of \(\dot{\vec r}\), and say what it is in terms of the inertial energy and angular momentum. Is \(T + V\) (with \(T = \tfrac12 m\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2\)) conserved?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

\(L\) is quadratic in \(\dot{\vec r}\) plus a linear term plus a velocity-independent part: this is the deck's "general quadratic" case with an extra linear piece, like P1's part 2.

Rung 2

\(\vec p = m(\dot{\vec r} + \vec\Omega\times\vec r)\), the inertial momentum. For the bracket algebra in part 2 use \(\vec\Omega\cdot(\vec r\times\vec p) = \vec p\cdot(\vec\Omega\times\vec r) = \vec r\cdot(\vec p\times\vec\Omega)\).

Rung 3

First lines of part 2: \(\dot{\vec r} = \partial H/\partial\vec p = \vec p/m - \vec\Omega\times\vec r\) and \(\dot{\vec p} = -\partial H/\partial\vec r = \vec p\times\vec\Omega - \nabla V\).

Worked solution

Idea. The Legendre transform does the frame change's bookkeeping: the Coriolis and centrifugal forces come out of one term, \(-\vec\Omega\cdot\vec L\), in \(H\).

1. \(\partial L/\partial\dot{\vec r} = m(\dot{\vec r} + \vec\Omega\times\vec r) = \vec p\), so \(\dot{\vec r} = \vec p/m - \vec\Omega\times\vec r\). Then \[ H = \vec p\cdot\dot{\vec r} - L = \frac{\lVert\vec p\rVert^2}{m} - \vec p\cdot(\vec\Omega\times\vec r) - \frac{\lVert\vec p\rVert^2}{2m} + V = \frac{\lVert\vec p\rVert^2}{2m} - \vec\Omega\cdot(\vec r\times\vec p) + V, \] using \(\dot{\vec r} + \vec\Omega\times\vec r = \vec p/m\) inside \(L\) and the triple product.

2. From rung 3, \(m\ddot{\vec r} = \dot{\vec p} - m\vec\Omega\times\dot{\vec r}\) \(= -\vec\Omega\times\vec p - \nabla V - m\vec\Omega\times\dot{\vec r}\). Substitute \(\vec p = m\dot{\vec r} + m\vec\Omega\times\vec r\): \[ m\ddot{\vec r} = -\nabla V - 2m\vec\Omega\times\dot{\vec r} - m\vec\Omega\times(\vec\Omega\times\vec r). \] Coriolis and centrifugal, as in unit 03 (no Euler term, since \(\vec\Omega\) is constant) ✓.

3. With \(\vec p = m(\dot{\vec r} + \vec\Omega\times\vec r)\): \(\lVert\vec p\rVert^2/2m = \tfrac12m\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2 + m\dot{\vec r}\cdot(\vec\Omega\times\vec r) + \tfrac12m\lVert\vec\Omega\times\vec r\rVert^2\) and \(\vec\Omega\cdot(\vec r\times\vec p) = m\dot{\vec r}\cdot(\vec\Omega\times\vec r) + m\lVert\vec\Omega\times\vec r\rVert^2\). So \[ H = \tfrac12m\lVert\dot{\vec r}\rVert^2 - \tfrac12m\lVert\vec\Omega\times\vec r\rVert^2 + V = E_{\text{inertial}} - \vec\Omega\cdot\vec L_{\text{inertial}}, \] since \(\vec p\) is the inertial momentum and \(\lVert\vec p\rVert^2/2m + V\) the inertial energy. \(H\) is conserved (no explicit \(t\)); it is the Jacobi integral of the restricted three-body problem. \(T + V\) is not conserved in general: \(H = T + V - \tfrac12m\lVert\vec\Omega\times\vec r\rVert^2\), and the last term changes whenever the distance from the axis does.

Check. Dimensions: \([\Omega\,rp] = \mathrm{s^{-1}\cdot m\cdot kg\,m\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm J\) ✓. Limit \(\vec\Omega = 0\): \(H = p^2/2m + V\) ✓. Special case: the hoop of the deck is this with the motion constrained to a circle through the axis, and its \(-\tfrac12mR^2\Omega^2\sin^2\theta\) is exactly \(-\tfrac12m\lVert\vec\Omega\times\vec r\rVert^2\) ✓.

P5 A bracket toolkit, and the virial theorem for Halley routine

exercises: Leibniz and chain rules for brackets, the time-evolution theorem, a time average, a number

  1. Prove \(\{f,\phi(g)\} = \phi'(g)\{f,g\}\) for a \(C^1\) function \(\phi:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\).
  2. With \(D = \vec r\cdot\vec p\) in three dimensions, compute \(\{D, r^2\}\), \(\{D, p^2\}\) and \(\{D, 1/r\}\), and say in words what the flow generated by \(D\) does.
  3. For \(H = p^2/2\mu - k/r\), show \(\dot D = 2T + V\). For a bound orbit, average over one period to obtain \(\langle T\rangle = -E\) and \(\langle V\rangle = 2E\).
  4. Evaluate the root-mean-square speed of Halley's comet over its orbit, and compare it with its perihelion speed \(54.5698\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 2's three answers are each a multiple of the function bracketed with \(D\). Part 4 is a single number in \(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\).

Rung 2

\(\{D, f\} = \vec p\cdot\partial f/\partial\vec p - \vec r\cdot\partial f/\partial\vec r\). A time derivative averaged over a period is \(\big(D(T_{\mathrm{orb}}) - D(0)\big)/T_{\mathrm{orb}} = 0\). Mean-square speed is \(2\langle T\rangle/\mu\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: \(\dot D = \{D,H\} = \tfrac1{2\mu}\{D,p^2\} - k\{D,1/r\}\).

Worked solution

Idea. \(D\) generates scaling — positions out, momenta in — and \(\dot D\) measures how the Hamiltonian responds to it. For a bound orbit \(D\) is bounded, so its derivative averages to zero.

1. \(\nabla\phi(g) = \phi'(g)\nabla g\) by the chain rule, so \(\{f,\phi(g)\} = \nabla f^\top J\,\phi'(g)\nabla g = \phi'(g)\{f,g\}\). \(\square\)

2. \(\{D,f\} = \sum_i(\partial_{r_i}D\,\partial_{p_i}f - \partial_{p_i}D\,\partial_{r_i}f) = \vec p\cdot\partial_{\vec p}f - \vec r\cdot\partial_{\vec r}f\). Hence \(\{D, r^2\} = -2r^2\), \(\{D, p^2\} = 2p^2\), and, with part 1 and \(\{D,r^2\}\), \(\{D, 1/r\} = \{D,(r^2)^{-1/2}\} = -\tfrac12(r^2)^{-3/2}(-2r^2) = 1/r\). The flow of \(D\): \(\vec r' = \{\vec r, D\} = \vec r\), \(\vec p' = \{\vec p, D\} = -\vec p\), so \(\vec r\mapsto e^s\vec r\), \(\vec p\mapsto e^{-s}\vec p\): dilation, with the momentum scaled inversely — canonical, as it must be, since \(\operatorname{diag}(e^s, e^{-s})\) has determinant \(1\) in each \((r_i,p_i)\) plane.

3. \(\dot D = \{D,H\} = \frac{2p^2}{2\mu} - \frac kr = 2T + V\). Over one period \(T_{\mathrm{orb}}\) of a bound orbit, \(\langle\dot D\rangle = (D(T_{\mathrm{orb}}) - D(0))/T_{\mathrm{orb}} = 0\), so \(2\langle T\rangle + \langle V\rangle = 0\). With \(\langle T\rangle + \langle V\rangle = E\): \(\langle T\rangle = -E\), \(\langle V\rangle = 2E\).

4. Per unit mass, \(\langle v^2\rangle = 2\langle T\rangle/\mu = -2\varepsilon = 4.97480\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\), so \(v_{\mathrm{rms}} = 7053.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = 7.0532\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) — less than \(13\%\) of the perihelion speed. The comet spends most of its \(75.3\) years slowly, far out.

Check. Dimensions: \([\{D,H\}] = [D][H]/\text{action} = \mathrm{J}\) since \([D]\) is an action ✓. Special case, a circular orbit: \(v^2 = GM/r\) and \(\varepsilon = -GM/2r\), so \(\langle v^2\rangle = GM/r = -2\varepsilon\) exactly ✓. For a general homogeneous potential of degree \(\kappa\), the same computation gives \(2\langle T\rangle = \kappa\langle V\rangle\); the oscillator, \(\kappa = 2\), has \(\langle T\rangle = \langle V\rangle\) ✓.

P6 Is it canonical? Four small maps routine

exercises: the symplectic condition, fundamental brackets, generating functions, a map that is not canonical, two degrees of freedom

For each map decide whether it is canonical, by all three tests where they apply — \(M^\top JM = J\), the fundamental brackets, a generating function — and check the verdicts agree.

  1. \(Q = p/m\omega\), \(P = -m\omega q\). Also transform the oscillator \(H = p^2/2m + \tfrac12m\omega^2q^2\).
  2. \(Q = q\), \(P = p + f'(q)\) for a \(C^2\) function \(f\). Where have you seen this?
  3. \(Q = \lambda q\), \(P = \lambda p\) with \(\lambda^2\ne1\). Show nonetheless that Hamilton's equations for any \(H\) become Hamilton's equations in \((Q,P)\) for some Hamiltonian, and say which.
  4. Two degrees of freedom: \(Q_1 = q_1 + q_2\), \(Q_2 = q_1 - q_2\), \(P_1 = \tfrac12(p_1+p_2)\), \(P_2 = \tfrac12(p_1-p_2)\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three yes and one no. In one degree of freedom \(M^\top JM = (\det M)J\), and \(\{Q,P\} = \det M\) too — so there the first two tests are one computation, and a generating function is the independent route. In part 4 they are genuinely different computations.

Rung 2

Generating functions to try: \(F_1 = m\omega qQ\) for part 1; \(F_2 = qP - f(q)\) for part 2; \(F_2 = (q_1+q_2)P_1 + (q_1-q_2)P_2\) for part 4. For part 3, compute \(MJM^\top\): it is a multiple of \(J\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: \(\dot w = M\dot z = MJM^\top\nabla_wK = \lambda^2J\nabla_wK\) with \(K = H\circ\Phi^{-1}\).

Worked solution

Idea. A map is canonical iff its Jacobian is symplectic; the other tests are the same condition read differently. A map that only rescales \(J\) keeps the form of the equations at the price of rescaling the Hamiltonian, which is exactly what "canonical" forbids.

1. \(M = \begin{pmatrix}0&1/m\omega\\-m\omega&0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\det M = 1\), so \(M^\top JM = J\) ✓. \(\{Q,P\} = \partial_qQ\,\partial_pP - \partial_pQ\,\partial_qP = 0 - \tfrac1{m\omega}(-m\omega) = 1\) ✓. \(F_1 = m\omega qQ\): \(p = \partial_qF_1 = m\omega Q\) and \(P = -\partial_QF_1 = -m\omega q\) ✓. The oscillator: \(p = m\omega Q\), \(q = -P/m\omega\), so \(K = \tfrac12m\omega^2Q^2 + P^2/2m\) — the same Hamiltonian with position and momentum exchanged.

2. \(M = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\f''&1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\det = 1\) ✓. \(\{Q,P\} = 1\cdot1 - 0 = 1\) ✓. \(F_2(q,P) = qP - f(q)\): \(Q = \partial_PF_2 = q\), \(p = \partial_qF_2 = P - f'(q)\) ✓. This is unit 12's gauge freedom: \(L\to L + \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}f(q)\) adds \(f'(q)\) to \(\partial L/\partial\dot q\). The change is canonical, so the gauge freedom of the Lagrangian is a canonical transformation of the Hamiltonian picture.

3. \(M = \lambda I\), \(M^\top JM = \lambda^2J\ne J\): not canonical. \(\{Q,P\} = \lambda^2\ne1\), the same verdict. No generating function: \(p\,\mathrm dq - P\,\mathrm dQ = (1-\lambda^2)p\,\mathrm dq\), whose coefficient vector \(((1-\lambda^2)p, 0)\) has antisymmetric derivative \((1-\lambda^2)J\ne0\), so it is not a gradient. Yet by rung 3, \(\dot w = J\nabla_w(\lambda^2K)\): Hamilton's equations hold with the Hamiltonian \(\lambda^2H(Q/\lambda, P/\lambda)\) — for every \(H\). So the three-tests theorem's condition (iii) must be read with \(K = H\circ\Phi^{-1}\) exactly; this map passes a weaker test (it is "conformally symplectic"). For the oscillator, \(\lambda^2H(Q/\lambda,P/\lambda) = H(Q,P)\): quadratic Hamiltonians hide the failure entirely.

4. \(M = \operatorname{diag}(A, B)\) with \(A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\), \(B = \tfrac12A\). Then \(M^\top JM = \begin{pmatrix}0&A^\top B\\-B^\top A&0\end{pmatrix}\) and \(A^\top B = \tfrac12A^2 = \tfrac12\cdot2I = I\) ✓. Brackets, from the formulas: \(\{Q_1,P_1\} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1\), \(\{Q_1,P_2\} = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = 0\), \(\{Q_2,P_1\} = 0\), \(\{Q_2,P_2\} = 1\), and \(\{Q_i,Q_j\} = \{P_i,P_j\} = 0\) since each involves only positions or only momenta ✓. \(F_2\) of rung 2: \(Q_1 = q_1+q_2\), \(Q_2 = q_1-q_2\), \(p_1 = P_1+P_2\), \(p_2 = P_1 - P_2\), which inverts to the given \(P\)s ✓. It is a point transformation, and the rule "positions by \(A\), momenta by \(A^{-\top}\)" is what every point transformation does.

Check. Dimensions in part 1: \([p/m\omega] = \mathrm m\) and \([m\omega q] = \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\): \(Q\) is a length and \(P\) a momentum, so \(\{Q,P\}\) is dimensionless as it must be ✓. Limit in part 3: \(\lambda\to1\) is the identity, canonical ✓; \(\lambda = -1\) is canonical too (\(\lambda^2 = 1\)) ✓.

Rung 2

P11 Proof: Poincaré recurrence medium

exercises: a proof from volume preservation, pigeonhole for measures, when the hypotheses hold

Let \(H\) be \(C^2\) with \(\{H\le E\}\) bounded, so its flow \(\varphi_t\) is defined for all time and maps the bounded set \(B = \{H\le E\}\) into itself and preserves volume. Fix \(\tau \gt 0\) and put \(\phi = \varphi_\tau\).

  1. Prove: for every open \(U\subset B\) with \(\operatorname{vol}U \gt 0\) there are a point \(z\in U\) and an integer \(n\ge1\) with \(\phi^n(z)\in U\).
  2. Why does the proof fail for the damped pendulum, and for a free particle?
  3. For Kepler's reduced system in \((r,p_r)\) at fixed \(L\ne0\), check that \(\{H\le E\}\) is bounded for \(E \lt 0\). What does the theorem say about Halley's comet, and why is it much weaker than what you already know?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The proof is a pigeonhole argument: infinitely many sets of the same positive volume cannot all be disjoint inside a set of finite volume.

Rung 2

Look at \(U, \phi(U), \phi^2(U),\dots\). If two of them meet, pull the intersection back by the smaller power. \(\phi\) is a bijection of \(B\) because \(\phi^{-1} = \varphi_{-\tau}\) also maps \(B\) into itself.

Rung 3

First line: \(\operatorname{vol}\phi^k(U) = \operatorname{vol}U\) for all \(k\), so if the \(\phi^k(U)\) were pairwise disjoint then \(\operatorname{vol}B\ge N\operatorname{vol}U\) for every \(N\).

Worked solution

Idea. Volume preservation plus finite total volume forces returns. Nothing about the dynamics is used beyond those two facts.

1. Energy conservation gives \(\varphi_t(B)\subset B\) for all \(t\), positive and negative, so \(\phi\) is a bijection of \(B\). By Liouville, \(\operatorname{vol}\phi^k(U) = \operatorname{vol}U\) for every \(k\ge0\). If the sets \(\phi^k(U)\), \(k = 0,1,2,\dots\), were pairwise disjoint, then for every \(N\), \(\operatorname{vol}B\ge\operatorname{vol}\bigcup_{k \lt N}\phi^k(U) = N\operatorname{vol}U\), impossible since \(B\) is bounded. So \(\phi^i(U)\cap\phi^j(U)\ne\emptyset\) for some \(i \lt j\). Apply \(\phi^{-i}\): \(U\cap\phi^{j-i}(U)\ne\emptyset\). Take \(z\in U\) with \(\phi^{j-i}(z)\) in it... precisely: take \(w\in U\cap\phi^{n}(U)\), \(n = j - i\ge1\), and \(z = \phi^{-n}(w)\in U\); then \(\phi^n(z) = w\in U\). \(\square\)

2. Damped pendulum: the flow does not preserve volume (divergence \(-\gamma\)), so \(\operatorname{vol}\phi^k(U) = e^{-k\gamma\tau}\operatorname{vol}U\) and the pigeonhole fails; indeed almost every orbit spirals into the down position and never returns to a small set away from it. Free particle: the flow preserves volume but no energy shell is bounded (\(x\) is unrestricted), so the sets can march off disjointly for ever — and they do.

3. \(H = p_r^2/2\mu + V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)\) with \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\to+\infty\) as \(r\to0\) (because \(L\ne0\)) and \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}\to0\) as \(r\to\infty\). For \(E \lt 0\), \(H\le E\) forces \(V_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)\le E\), which confines \(r\) to \([r_-,r_+]\), and \(p_r^2\le2\mu(E - \min V_{\mathrm{eff}})\): bounded ✓. For Halley the theorem says that states near the present one recur — but you already know far more: every bound Kepler orbit is exactly periodic, with period \(75.3052\) yr. Recurrence is informative for systems too complicated to solve, such as a gas in a box, where Zermelo used it in 1896 against Boltzmann: the recurrence times are real but, for a macroscopic gas, vastly longer than the age of the universe.

Check. Special case, the harmonic oscillator: \(\{H\le E\}\) is an ellipse and \(\phi\) is a rotation in scaled coordinates; for \(\tau\) an irrational multiple of the period, points return arbitrarily close without ever repeating exactly — consistent with the theorem, which promises returns to \(U\), not to the point ✓.

For \(\dot q = p\), \(\dot p = -q\) (units with \(m = \omega = 1\)) and a step \(h \gt 0\):

  1. Euler's method \(q' = q + hp\), \(p' = p - hq\). Find the one-step matrix, show it multiplies \(q^2 + p^2\) by exactly \(1 + h^2\), and evaluate the energy growth per period and over \(100\) periods for \(h = 0.01\).
  2. Symplectic Euler \(p' = p - hq\), \(q' = q + hp'\). Find the one-step matrix \(M\), show \(M^\top JM = J\), and show \(C = q^2 - hqp + p^2\) is exactly conserved.
  3. Deduce that along the symplectic-Euler orbit the energy \(\tfrac12(q^2+p^2)\) never leaves the band \(\big[\tfrac{C}{2(1+h/2)},\ \tfrac{C}{2(1-h/2)}\big]\). Evaluate the ratio of the band's ends for \(h = 0.01\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each method is a linear map of the plane. Part 1 is a norm computation, part 2 a matrix identity, part 3 an inequality between \(qp\) and \(q^2+p^2\).

Rung 2

In the plane, \(M^\top JM = (\det M)J\). For the invariant, check \(M^\top SM = S\) with \(S = \begin{pmatrix}1&-h/2\\-h/2&1\end{pmatrix}\). For part 3, \(|qp|\le\tfrac12(q^2+p^2)\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: substituting, \(q' = (1-h^2)q + hp\), so \(M = \begin{pmatrix}1-h^2&h\\-h&1\end{pmatrix}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Same cost, same accuracy per step; one map is canonical and one is not. Liouville turns that difference into bounded versus exponentially growing energy error.

1. \(M_E = \begin{pmatrix}1&h\\-h&1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\det = 1 + h^2\). \((q+hp)^2 + (p-hq)^2 = (1+h^2)(q^2+p^2)\), the cross terms cancelling. One period is \(2\pi/h = 628.32\) steps; \((1.0001)^{628.32} = 1.06484\): energy up \(6.48\%\) per period. After \(100\) periods, \((1.0001)^{62832} = 535\).

2. \(\det M = (1-h^2) + h^2 = 1\), so \(M^\top JM = J\): symplectic Euler is a canonical map. For the invariant, write \(z' = Mz\) and compute \(C(z') = q'^2 - hq'p' + p'^2\) with \(q' = (1-h^2)q + hp\), \(p' = p - hq\): \(q'^2 = (1-h^2)^2q^2 + 2h(1-h^2)qp + h^2p^2\); \(-hq'p' = -h\big[(1-h^2)qp - h(1-h^2)q^2 + hp^2 - h^2qp\big]\); \(p'^2 = p^2 - 2hqp + h^2q^2\). The coefficient of \(q^2\) is \((1-h^2)^2 + h^2(1-h^2) + h^2 = 1\); of \(p^2\), \(h^2 - h^2 + 1 = 1\); of \(qp\), \(2h(1-h^2) - h(1-h^2) + h^3 - 2h = -h\). So \(C(z') = C(z)\) ✓.

3. From \(|qp|\le\tfrac12(q^2+p^2)\): \((q^2+p^2)(1 - h/2)\le C\le(q^2+p^2)(1+h/2)\), which rearranges to the band. Its ends differ by the ratio \((1+h/2)/(1-h/2) = 1.005/0.995 = 1.01005\): the energy wobbles by about \(1\%\) at \(h = 0.01\), and that stays true after any number of periods.

Check. Limit \(h\to0\): both matrices tend to the identity, \(C\to q^2+p^2\), and the band closes onto the true energy ✓. The explicit method's growth per period, \((1+h^2)^{2\pi/h}\approx e^{2\pi h}\), tends to \(1\) as \(h\to0\) — so it is convergent; its defect is only that its error compounds, which is precisely what a non-unit determinant does ✓.

P13 The oscillator's hidden symmetry hard

exercises: bracket algebra, conserved quantities closing on a Lie algebra, a Casimir, the geometry of orbit space

The two-dimensional isotropic oscillator, in units with \(m = \omega = 1\): \(H = \tfrac12(p_x^2 + p_y^2 + x^2 + y^2)\). Put \[ S_1 = \tfrac14(p_x^2 - p_y^2 + x^2 - y^2),\qquad S_2 = \tfrac12(xy + p_xp_y),\qquad S_3 = \tfrac12(xp_y - yp_x). \]

  1. Show each \(S_i\) is conserved.
  2. Show \(\{S_1,S_2\} = S_3\), \(\{S_2,S_3\} = S_1\), \(\{S_3,S_1\} = S_2\): the algebra \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\) of unit 03.
  3. Show \(S_1^2 + S_2^2 + S_3^2 = H^2/4\).
  4. Interpret: what are the orbits with \(\vec S\) at the poles of the sphere \(\lVert\vec S\rVert = E/2\), and on its equator? Compare with the Kepler problem, whose angular momentum alone does not explain why its orbits close.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every quantity is quadratic, so every bracket is quadratic too; you are matching coefficients. Write \(H_x = \tfrac12(p_x^2 + x^2)\), \(H_y\) likewise: then \(H = H_x + H_y\) and \(S_1 = \tfrac12(H_x - H_y)\).

Rung 2

\(H_x\) generates rotation in the \((x,p_x)\) plane: \(\{x, H_x\} = p_x\), \(\{p_x,H_x\} = -x\). Use Leibniz on the products \(xy\), \(p_xp_y\), \(xp_y\). For part 1, \(H_x\) and \(H_y\) are each conserved.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\{S_1, xy\} = y\{S_1,x\} + x\{S_1,y\}\) with \(\{S_1,x\} = -\partial S_1/\partial p_x = -p_x/2\) and \(\{S_1,y\} = -\partial S_1/\partial p_y = p_y/2\).

Worked solution

Idea. The oscillator separates into two identical one-dimensional oscillators rotating in step. Quadratic combinations that do not care about the common phase are conserved, and they close into \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\) — the analogue of Kepler's \(\mathfrak{so}(4)\), and the reason all orbits close.

1. \(\{H_x,H_y\} = 0\) (different variables), so \(H_x\) and \(H_y\) are separately conserved, and so is \(S_1\). For \(S_2\): \(\{xy, H\} = y\{x,H_x\} + x\{y,H_y\} = yp_x + xp_y\) and \(\{p_xp_y,H\} = p_y(-x) + p_x(-y)\); the sum is \(0\). For \(S_3\): it is \(\tfrac12L_z\), conserved because \(H\) is rotation-invariant (the lemma of the deck), or directly: \(\{xp_y - yp_x, H\} = p_xp_y + x(-y) - p_yp_x - y(-x) = 0\).

2. From rung 3, \(\{S_1,xy\} = \tfrac12(xp_y - yp_x)\). Similarly \(\{S_1,p_x\} = \partial S_1/\partial x = x/2\) and \(\{S_1,p_y\} = -y/2\), so \(\{S_1,p_xp_y\} = \tfrac12(xp_y - yp_x)\). Hence \(\{S_1,S_2\} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot2(xp_y - yp_x) = S_3\) ✓. Next \(\{S_2,S_3\}\): with \(\{S_3,x\} = -\partial S_3/\partial p_x = y/2\), \(\{S_3,y\} = -x/2\), \(\{S_3,p_x\} = p_y/2\), \(\{S_3,p_y\} = -p_x/2\), one gets \(\{S_3, xy\} = \tfrac12(y^2 - x^2)\) and \(\{S_3,p_xp_y\} = \tfrac12(p_y^2 - p_x^2)\), so \(\{S_3,S_2\} = -\tfrac14(x^2 - y^2 + p_x^2 - p_y^2) = -S_1\), i.e. \(\{S_2,S_3\} = S_1\) ✓. Finally \(\{S_3,S_1\} = \tfrac14\big(\{S_3,p_x^2 + x^2\} - \{S_3,p_y^2 + y^2\}\big) = \tfrac14\big(p_xp_y + xy + p_xp_y + xy\big) = S_2\) ✓, using \(\{S_3,p_x^2\} = 2p_x\cdot p_y/2\), \(\{S_3,x^2\} = 2x\cdot y/2\), \(\{S_3,p_y^2\} = -p_xp_y\), \(\{S_3,y^2\} = -xy\).

3. \(4(S_1^2 + S_2^2) = \tfrac14(a - b)^2 + (xy + p_xp_y)^2\) with \(a = p_x^2 + x^2\), \(b = p_y^2 + y^2\), and \(4S_3^2 = (xp_y - yp_x)^2\). Now \((xy + p_xp_y)^2 + (xp_y - yp_x)^2 = x^2y^2 + p_x^2p_y^2 + x^2p_y^2 + y^2p_x^2 = ab\) (the cross terms \(\pm2xyp_xp_y\) cancel). So \(4\lVert\vec S\rVert^2 = \tfrac14(a-b)^2 + ab = \tfrac14(a+b)^2 = H^2\) ✓.

4. At fixed energy \(E\), \(\vec S\) lives on the sphere of radius \(E/2\), and each point of it is one orbit (all orbits are ellipses centred at the origin, and \(\vec S\) fixes the ellipse). Poles: \(S_3 = \pm E/2\), \(S_1 = S_2 = 0\) — maximal \(|L_z| = E\), the two circular orbits, one each way round. Equator: \(S_3 = 0\), no angular momentum — the straight-line orbits through the origin, with direction set by \((S_1,S_2)\): the angle of \((S_1,S_2)\) is twice the angle of the line. Rotation-invariance alone gives only \(L_z\), which does not explain why orbits close; the extra conserved \(S_1, S_2\) do, exactly as \(\vec A\) does for Kepler.

Check. Numbers: the orbit \(x = \cos t\), \(y = 0\) has \(E = \tfrac12\), \(S_1 = \tfrac14\), \(S_2 = S_3 = 0\): on the equator, radius \(\tfrac14 = E/2\) ✓. The circle \(x = \cos t\), \(y = \sin t\): \(E = 1\), \(S_3 = \tfrac12\cdot1 = E/2\), \(S_1 = \tfrac14(\sin^2t - \cos^2t + \cos^2t - \sin^2t) = 0\), \(S_2 = \tfrac12(\cos t\sin t - \sin t\cos t) = 0\): a pole ✓.
