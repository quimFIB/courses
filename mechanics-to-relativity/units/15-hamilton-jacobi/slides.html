Hamilton–Jacobi: mechanics was optics all along, and free motion is already geodesic motion

One nonlinear PDE replaces the equations of motion; its solutions are wavefronts, its trajectories are rays, and a particle in a potential is a free particle in a rescaled geometry.

needs 14, 11~2½ h slides + 6 h problemsproblems: ./mr problems 15

Part B's thesis is that nothing in it says anything Newton did not, and that the change of language is worth having for what it makes obvious. This unit is the sharpest case. It contains no new physics at all. What it contains is three things that are invisible in $m\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla V$ and obvious here.

  • Frequencies without motion. The frequency of a bounded motion is the derivative of the energy with respect to an area in phase space. You can compute a period without ever finding $x(t)$, and you can compute how it shifts when the potential is perturbed.
  • Invariance under slow change. The same area is what survives when a parameter of the system is changed slowly — the fact Einstein used in 1911 and Bohr and Sommerfeld built the old quantum theory on.
  • Geometry. Trajectories at fixed energy are the rays of an optical medium, and the geodesics of a metric. Force-driven motion is free motion in the right geometry — in Newtonian mechanics, a century before general relativity made that the definition of gravity.

It is a branch: no later unit's proofs depend on it except unit 40's separation of Kerr geodesics. But unit 32 will define a geodesic, and unit 30 will argue that gravity is geometry, and both are easier to believe once you have seen, here, that a Kepler ellipse is already a geodesic.

15 · the action function

The action, as a function of its endpoint

definition

Fix a start $(q_0, t_0)$. Suppose that for every $(q, t)$ in an open set $U$ there is exactly one solution of the Euler–Lagrange equations running from $q_0$ at $t_0$ to $q$ at $t$, depending $C^2$ on $(q,t)$. Hamilton's principal function is the action along it,

$$S(q, t) = \int_{t_0}^{t} L\big(q(s), \dot q(s), s\big)\,\mathrm{d}s .$$
theorem $$\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i} = p_i, \qquad \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = -H\big(q, p, t\big), \qquad\text{i.e.}\quad \mathrm dS = p\cdot\mathrm dq - H\,\mathrm dt ,$$

with $p$ the momentum with which the true path arrives at $(q, t)$.

unit 11's first variation with a free endpoint, then the chain rule along the path — full proof in the notes (N)

Why a physicist wants this: $S$ knows every trajectory from $q_0$ at once, and its gradient is the momentum field of that whole family.

Full proof. The $q$-derivative. Move the endpoint from $q$ to $q + \varepsilon e_i$ at fixed $t$. The true paths $q_\varepsilon(s)$ form a $C^2$ family with $q_\varepsilon(t_0) = q_0$ for all $\varepsilon$, so $\eta(s) = \partial_\varepsilon q_\varepsilon(s)|_{\varepsilon=0}$ has $\eta(t_0) = 0$ and $\eta(t) = e_i$. Differentiating under the integral (legitimate: everything is $C^1$ on a compact interval), $$\frac{\partial S}{\partial q_i} = \int_{t_0}^{t}\big(L_q\cdot\eta + L_{\dot q}\cdot\dot\eta\big)\,\mathrm ds = \Big[L_{\dot q}\cdot\eta\Big]_{t_0}^{t} + \int_{t_0}^{t}\Big(L_q - \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}L_{\dot q}\Big)\cdot\eta\,\mathrm ds .$$ The integral vanishes because the path satisfies Euler–Lagrange, and the boundary term is $p(t)\cdot e_i - p(t_0)\cdot 0 = p_i(t)$. This is unit 11's natural-boundary computation with the endpoint moved rather than freed.

The $t$-derivative. Take one true path $q(\cdot)$ and a later time $t'$ on it. By uniqueness, the true path from $(q_0,t_0)$ to $(q(t'), t')$ is the same path restricted, so $S(q(t'), t') = \int_{t_0}^{t'} L\,\mathrm ds$ and its $t'$-derivative is $L$. By the chain rule (unit 03) the same derivative is $\partial_t S + \sum_i \partial_{q_i}S\,\dot q_i = \partial_t S + p\cdot\dot q$. Hence $\partial_t S = L - p\cdot\dot q = -H$, by the definition of $H$ as the Legendre transform (unit 14). $\square$

Where the hypothesis bites. "Exactly one true path, depending smoothly on the endpoint" holds for $t$ close to $t_0$ and fails at a conjugate point (unit 11). For the harmonic oscillator, $S = \dfrac{m\omega}{2\sin\omega T}\big[(q^2 + q_0^2)\cos\omega T - 2qq_0\big]$ with $T = t - t_0$, which blows up at $\omega T = \pi$: every path from $q_0$ refocuses at $-q_0$ half a period later, the endpoint no longer determines the path, and $S$ stops being a function. Problem P1 computes the free particle and the uniform field, where no conjugate point ever occurs.

Check. Free particle, $L = \tfrac12 m\dot q^2$: the true path is a straight line at speed $(q - q_0)/(t - t_0)$, so $S = \dfrac{m(q-q_0)^2}{2(t-t_0)}$. Then $\partial_q S = m(q-q_0)/(t-t_0) = p$ and $\partial_t S = -m(q-q_0)^2/2(t-t_0)^2 = -p^2/2m = -H$. Dimensions: $[S]$ is energy × time, so $[\partial_q S] = \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ and $[\partial_t S] = \mathrm J$, as they must be.

15 · the action function

The Hamilton–Jacobi equation

definition

Given $H(q, p, t)$ on an open set of $\mathbb R^n\times\mathbb R^n\times\mathbb R$, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation is the PDE for an unknown $C^2$ function $S(q, t)$:

$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial t} + H\Big(q,\ \frac{\partial S}{\partial q},\ t\Big) = 0 .$$

A solution on an open $U\subset\mathbb R^{n+1}$ is a $C^2$ function making the left side vanish identically on $U$.

corollary of the action-function theorem

Hamilton's principal function solves it: substitute $\partial_q S = p$ into $\partial_t S = -H(q,p,t)$.

what kind of equation

First order, and nonlinear: $H$ is quadratic in $p$, so $S$ enters squared. Unit 05's Poisson equation was linear; this one is not — no superposition.

For the running example, with $H$ derived on the spherical-coordinates slide: $\ \partial_t S + \dfrac{1}{2m}\Big[(\partial_r S)^2 + \dfrac{(\partial_\theta S)^2}{r^2} + \dfrac{(\partial_\phi S)^2}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\Big] - \dfrac{k}{r} = 0 .$

Why trade an ODE for a PDE? It looks like a bad bargain: Hamilton's equations are $2n$ ordinary differential equations, and this is a partial differential equation, a harder kind of object. The trade pays because of what a single solution buys. One solution $S$ carries a whole $n$-parameter family of trajectories (all those with $p = \partial_q S$), and one complete solution, defined on the complete-integrals slide, carries all of them. And the PDE can often be solved by a trick — separation of variables — that has no analogue for the ODEs. Kepler in spherical coordinates is the showcase.

History. Hamilton found the principal function in 1834–35, in two papers "On a general method in dynamics", having found the characteristic function first in optics in the 1820s — the optical origin is section 5's subject. He had two PDEs, one for each endpoint. Jacobi (1837) saw that one suffices, that any sufficiently rich solution will do rather than the action itself, and that this is what makes the method computational. The equation carries both names for that reason.

On the word "nonlinear". If $S_1$ and $S_2$ solve it, $S_1 + S_2$ generally does not: $(\partial_q(S_1+S_2))^2 \ne (\partial_q S_1)^2 + (\partial_q S_2)^2$. What does survive is adding a constant, since only derivatives of $S$ appear — which is why every complete integral below carries an inessential additive constant, never counted among its parameters.

Check, dimensions. Each term has the dimension of energy: $\partial_t S$ is action per time, and $(\partial_r S)^2/2m$ is momentum squared over mass.

Kepler's Hamiltonian in spherical coordinates

spherical coordinates

$(r, \theta, \phi)\mapsto \vec x = (r\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ r\sin\theta\sin\phi,\ r\cos\theta)$ on $r > 0$, $0 < \theta < \pi$, $\phi$ mod $2\pi$. The columns of its derivative are

$$\partial_r\vec x = \hat e_r,\quad \partial_\theta\vec x = r\,\hat e_\theta,\quad \partial_\phi\vec x = r\sin\theta\,\hat e_\phi$$

with $\hat e_r, \hat e_\theta, \hat e_\phi$ orthonormal. So by the chain rule

$$\lVert\dot{\vec x}\rVert^2 = \dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2 .$$
the running example

$L = \tfrac12 m\lVert\dot{\vec x}\rVert^2 + k/r$ gives $p_r = m\dot r$, $p_\theta = mr^2\dot\theta$, $p_\phi = mr^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi$, and

$$\begin{aligned}H = \frac{p_r^2}{2m} &+ \frac{p_\theta^2}{2mr^2}\\ &+ \frac{p_\phi^2}{2mr^2\sin^2\theta} - \frac kr .\end{aligned}$$

$p_\phi = L_z$, and $p_\theta^2 + p_\phi^2/\sin^2\theta = \lVert\vec L\rVert^2$.

Spherical coordinates are needed here only as generalised coordinates in unit 12's sense: a chart on $\mathbb R^3$ minus the $z$-axis. Unit 05 uses them for integration, with Jacobians; nothing of that is needed, only the chain rule.

Full proof of the speed formula. Differentiate $\vec x = r(\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta)$: $\partial_r\vec x = (\sin\theta\cos\phi, \sin\theta\sin\phi, \cos\theta) =: \hat e_r$, $\partial_\theta\vec x = r(\cos\theta\cos\phi, \cos\theta\sin\phi, -\sin\theta) =: r\hat e_\theta$, $\partial_\phi\vec x = r\sin\theta(-\sin\phi, \cos\phi, 0) =: r\sin\theta\,\hat e_\phi$. Each hatted vector has length 1 (sum of squares, using $\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$ twice), and the three pairwise dot products are $\sin\theta\cos\theta\cos^2\phi + \sin\theta\cos\theta\sin^2\phi - \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0$, $-\sin\theta\sin\phi\cos\phi + \sin\theta\sin\phi\cos\phi = 0$ and $-\cos\theta\sin\phi\cos\phi + \cos\theta\sin\phi\cos\phi = 0$. By the chain rule $\dot{\vec x} = \dot r\,\hat e_r + r\dot\theta\,\hat e_\theta + r\sin\theta\,\dot\phi\,\hat e_\phi$, and Pythagoras in an orthonormal basis gives the formula. $\square$

Where the chart fails. At $r = 0$ all three columns degenerate; on the $z$-axis $\partial_\phi\vec x = 0$. The derivative has rank less than 3 there, and $(r,\theta,\phi)$ is not a chart. This is why $\phi$'s momentum $p_\phi = mr^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi$ has the factor that vanishes on the axis.

The momentum identities. $p_\phi = m(x\dot y - y\dot x) = L_z$: with $\rho = r\sin\theta$, $x = \rho\cos\phi$, $y = \rho\sin\phi$, one gets $x\dot y - y\dot x = \rho^2\dot\phi$. For $\lVert\vec L\rVert^2$, unit 08's identity $\lVert\vec r\times\vec p\rVert^2 = r^2\lVert\vec p\rVert^2 - (\vec r\cdot\vec p)^2$ with $\vec p = p_r\hat e_r + (p_\theta/r)\hat e_\theta + (p_\phi/r\sin\theta)\hat e_\phi$ and $\vec r\cdot\vec p = rp_r$ gives $\lVert\vec L\rVert^2 = p_\theta^2 + p_\phi^2/\sin^2\theta$.

The Legendre transform. $L$ is a positive-definite quadratic form in the velocities plus a function of position, so unit 14's transform is $H = T + V$ with each $\dot q_i$ replaced by $p_i$ divided by its coefficient; that is the displayed $H$.

Complete integrals, and the solutions they generate

definition

A complete integral is a family $S(q, \alpha, t)$, $\alpha\in\mathbb R^n$, of solutions, $C^2$ in all variables, with $\ \det\big(\partial^2 S/\partial q_i\partial\alpha_j\big) \ne 0$. (An additive constant is not counted.)

lemma · envelopes are solutions

If each $S(\cdot,\cdot,a)$ solves the equation and $a(q,t)$ is $C^1$ with $\partial_a S(q,t,a(q,t)) = 0$, then $\Sigma(q,t) = S(q,t,a(q,t))$ solves it too.

Proof. $\partial_q\Sigma = \partial_q S + \partial_a S\,\partial_q a = \partial_q S$, and likewise $\partial_t\Sigma = \partial_t S$. $\square$

free particle

$S = a(q - q_0) - \dfrac{a^2(t - t_0)}{2m}$ is a complete integral ($\partial^2_{qa}S = 1$). $\partial_a S = 0$ gives $a = m(q-q_0)/(t-t_0)$, and the envelope is

$$\Sigma = \frac{m(q - q_0)^2}{2(t - t_0)} ,$$

the free particle's principal function, found on the action-function slide.

The general solution depends on an arbitrary function; envelopes of complete integrals over sub-families produce it.

What the determinant condition says. $\partial^2 S/\partial q\,\partial\alpha$ nonsingular means the map $\alpha\mapsto p = \partial_q S(q,\alpha,t)$ is a local diffeomorphism at each $(q,t)$ (inverse function theorem, unit 04): the family of momentum fields reaches every nearby momentum at every point. That is exactly what makes it rich enough to contain every trajectory, which is the content of Jacobi's theorem next. A family like $S = \alpha_1(q_1 + q_2) - \dots$ that depends on its parameters only through one combination fails it.

Check of the free-particle example. $\partial_t S + (\partial_q S)^2/2m = -a^2/2m + a^2/2m = 0$ for every $a$. At the envelope, $\Sigma = a(q-q_0) - a^2(t-t_0)/2m$ with $a(t - t_0) = m(q-q_0)$, so $\Sigma = m(q-q_0)^2/(t-t_0) - m(q-q_0)^2/2(t-t_0) = m(q-q_0)^2/2(t-t_0)$. The complete integral is the family of plane waves; the principal function, a "point source", is their envelope. Section 5 makes the optical words literal.

The general solution — cited. For a first-order PDE in $n$ variables, a solution depending on an arbitrary function of $n - 1$ variables is produced from any complete integral by restricting $\alpha$ to a sub-family and taking the envelope, as in the lemma; Lagrange showed that locally every solution which is not "singular" arises this way. Proof: Courant and Hilbert, Methods of Mathematical Physics, vol. II, chapter II, §4. This unit never needs the general solution: Jacobi's theorem needs only a complete integral, and that is the whole reason complete integrals are the object of interest.

Jacobi's theorem

Jacobi's theorem

Let $S(q,\alpha,t)$ be a complete integral and $\beta\in\mathbb R^n$ constant. Solve $\ \dfrac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_j}(q,\alpha,t) = \beta_j\ $ for $q(t)$ (implicit function theorem: the $q$-Jacobian is the nonsingular matrix $M_{ij} = \partial^2 S/\partial q_i\partial\alpha_j$), and set $\ p(t) = \dfrac{\partial S}{\partial q}\big(q(t),\alpha,t\big)$. Then $(q(t), p(t))$ solves Hamilton's equations.

proof · the $q$ half

Differentiate $\partial_{\alpha_j}S(q(t),\alpha,t) = \beta_j$ in $t$, and the HJ equation in $\alpha_j$:

$$\textstyle\sum_i M_{ij}\,\dot q_i + \partial^2_{t\alpha_j}S = 0, \qquad \sum_i M_{ij}\,\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} + \partial^2_{t\alpha_j}S = 0 .$$

Subtract (Clairaut makes the mixed partials agree): $\sum_i M_{ij}\big(\dot q_i - \partial H/\partial p_i\big) = 0$, and $M$ is invertible. $\square$

the $p$ half is the same move with a $q$-derivative — in the notes (N)

This is the verification direction: given a complete integral, the curves it defines are trajectories. It needs nothing but the chain rule, Clairaut and the implicit function theorem. The converse — that every trajectory arises this way, from any complete integral — follows because the $2n$ constants $(\alpha,\beta)$ can be matched to any initial data $(q(t_0), p(t_0))$ near a point where the construction works: choose $\alpha$ with $\partial_q S(q(t_0),\alpha,t_0) = p(t_0)$ (possible locally, since $M$ is invertible) and then $\beta = \partial_\alpha S(q(t_0),\alpha,t_0)$; by uniqueness (unit 01) the constructed solution is the trajectory.

Full proof, the $p$ half. Along the curve, by the chain rule, $\dot p_i = \partial^2_{q_i t}S + \sum_k \partial^2_{q_iq_k}S\,\dot q_k$. Differentiate the HJ equation $\partial_t S + H(q, \partial_q S, t) = 0$ with respect to $q_i$: $$\partial^2_{tq_i}S + \frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i} + \sum_k\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}\,\partial^2_{q_kq_i}S = 0 .$$ Using $\dot q_k = \partial H/\partial p_k$ (the $q$ half) and Clairaut, the first expression becomes $\dot p_i = \partial^2_{tq_i}S + \sum_k \partial^2_{q_iq_k}S\,\partial H/\partial p_k = -\partial H/\partial q_i$. $\square$

Why it works — unit 14's view. Take $S(q,\alpha,t)$ as a type-2 generating function $F_2(q, P, t)$ with new momenta $P = \alpha$. Unit 14's rules give $p = \partial_q S$, new coordinates $Q = \partial_\alpha S$, and new Hamiltonian $K = H + \partial_t S$, which the HJ equation makes zero. In the new variables every coordinate and every momentum is constant: $Q = \beta$, $P = \alpha$. Solving the HJ equation is finding the canonical transformation to a system that does not move. The determinant condition is what unit 14 requires of a type-2 generating function.

Check. Free particle, $S = aq - a^2t/2m$: $\partial_a S = q - at/m = \beta$, so $q = \beta + at/m$, and $p = \partial_q S = a$ — uniform motion at momentum $a$ with $q(0) = \beta$. Dimensions: $\beta = \partial S/\partial\alpha$ has dimension action over the dimension of $\alpha$; here $\alpha$ is a momentum and $\beta$ a length.

Worked: the oscillator, without its equation

$H = p^2/2m + \tfrac12 m\omega^2q^2$, by Jacobi's theorem

Step 1 — try $S = -Et + W(q)$. The HJ equation becomes an ODE for $W$: $\ \tfrac{1}{2m}W'^2 + \tfrac12 m\omega^2q^2 = E$, so

$$W(q, E) = \int^q\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2 q'^2}\,\mathrm dq' .$$

Step 2 — complete? One parameter $\alpha = E$, and $\partial^2_{qE}S = m/\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2q^2} \ne 0$. Yes.

Step 3 — Jacobi. $\ \beta = \partial_E S = -t + \displaystyle\int^q\frac{m\,\mathrm dq'}{\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2q'^2}} = -t + \frac1\omega\arcsin\Big(q\,\omega\sqrt{\tfrac{m}{2E}}\Big).$

Step 4 — solve for $q$:

$$q(t) = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\omega^2}}\,\sin\omega(t + \beta) .$$

Step 5 — the momentum, $p = \partial_q S = \sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2q^2} = \sqrt{2mE}\,\cos\omega(t+\beta)$, which is $m\dot q$ ✓.

Check. Amplitude $\sqrt{2E/m\omega^2}$ has dimension $\sqrt{\mathrm{J/(kg\,s^{-2})}} = \mathrm m$ ✓. $\beta$ has dimension time ✓: $-\beta$ is when $q$ last crossed zero upward.

No second-order equation was solved. One integral was done, and one differentiation.

The branch of the square root. $W$ is not single-valued: $W' = \pm\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2q^2}$, positive when moving right and negative when moving left, and the sign flips at the turning points $q = \pm\sqrt{2E/m\omega^2}$. Step 3 took the upper branch, which covers the quarter-cycle from $q = 0$ to the right turning point; continuing through the turning point on the other branch gives the same sine, since $\arcsin$ reflected is again the sine continued. This sign bookkeeping is the one real trap of the method and it recurs for Kepler.

The integral in step 3. With $q = \sqrt{2E/m\omega^2}\,\sin u$, $\mathrm dq = \sqrt{2E/m\omega^2}\cos u\,\mathrm du$ and $\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2q^2} = \sqrt{2mE}\cos u$, so the integrand is $m\sqrt{2E/m\omega^2}/\sqrt{2mE}\,\mathrm du = \mathrm du/\omega$. Hence the integral is $u/\omega = \omega^{-1}\arcsin(q\omega\sqrt{m/2E})$.

Limit. $E\to0$: the amplitude goes to zero and the particle sits at the bottom, as it should. And the action variable of section 3, the area of the ellipse $p^2/2mE + q^2/(2E/m\omega^2) = 1$ divided by $2\pi$, is $\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\,\pi\sqrt{2mE}\sqrt{2E/m\omega^2} = E/\omega$ — which we will meet again.

Problem P2 does the same for a uniform gravitational field, where the answer is a parabola in $t$.

15 · the action function

When H does not depend on time

time separates

If $\partial_t H = 0$, then $S = -Et + W(q)$ solves the HJ equation iff $W$ solves the time-independent equation

$$H\Big(q, \frac{\partial W}{\partial q}\Big) = E .$$

$W$ is Hamilton's characteristic function. Proof: substitute; $\partial_t S = -E$. $\square$

what $W$ measures

Along a trajectory with $p = \partial_q W$, $\ \dot W = \partial_q W\cdot\dot q = p\cdot\dot q$. So

$$W = \int p\cdot\mathrm dq ,$$

the abbreviated action: a line integral (unit 04) with no time in it.

where time went

Taking $E$ as one of the $\alpha$'s, Jacobi gives $\ \partial_E W = t - t_0$: the orbit is found from $W$ alone, and time is recovered afterwards by one derivative. Section 5 builds on this: $W$ sees only the path.

Proof of the box on the right. $\partial_E S = -t + \partial_E W$, and Jacobi's theorem says this is a constant $\beta_E$; write $\beta_E = -t_0$. So $\partial_E W = t - t_0$. The remaining $n-1$ equations $\partial_{\alpha_j}W = \beta_j$ ($j\ge2$) do not contain $t$ at all: they are the equations of the path in configuration space.

Why $W$ is not the action. The action is $\int L\,\mathrm dt = \int(p\cdot\dot q - H)\,\mathrm dt = W - E(t - t_0)$ at fixed energy — the two differ by a term that knows only the elapsed time. That difference is exactly the $-Et$ in $S = -Et + W$. In the physics literature $W$ is sometimes called the "reduced action", and $\int p\,\mathrm dq$ was Maupertuis's "action" long before Hamilton's.

Check. The worked oscillator: $\partial_E W = \int m\,\mathrm dq/\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2q^2}$ is the time taken to travel from $0$ to $q$, since $m/p = 1/\dot q$ and $\int\mathrm dq/\dot q = \int\mathrm dt$. The formula is just "time is distance over speed", assembled by a derivative.

The running example. Kepler's $H$ has no $t$ in it, so the next section looks for $S = -Et + W(r, \theta, \phi)$ and has to solve the time-independent equation in three variables.

15 · separation

Separation: one equation becomes n

definition

The time-independent HJ equation is separable in coordinates $q$ if it has a complete integral of the form

$$W(q, \alpha) = W_1(q_1, \alpha) + W_2(q_2, \alpha) + \dots + W_n(q_n, \alpha).$$
lemma · the mechanism

If $f(x) = g(y)$ for all $(x,y)$ in a product of intervals, then $f$ and $g$ are the same constant.

Proof. Fix $y_0$: $f(x) = g(y_0)$ for every $x$. $\square$

cyclic coordinates separate

If $q_n$ does not appear in $H$, then $p_n$ is conserved (unit 14) and $W = \alpha_n q_n + W'(q_1,\dots,q_{n-1})$: the equation loses a variable.

In Kepler's $H$, $\phi$ is cyclic; then the $\theta$-dependence occurs only in one bracket; then only $r$ is left. Three uses of the lemma.

How separation is used, in general. Substitute the sum into $H(q, \partial_q W) = E$. If the equation can be rearranged — by multiplying through by some function — into the form $F_1(q_1, W_1') = G(q_2,\dots,q_n, W_2',\dots, W_n')$, the lemma forces both sides to be a constant $\alpha_2$, and $F_1(q_1, W_1') = \alpha_2$ is an ODE in one variable, solved by a quadrature. Repeat on the right-hand side. Each step introduces one separation constant, and together with $E$ there are $n$ of them: exactly the count a complete integral needs.

Proof of the cyclic case. If $\partial H/\partial q_n = 0$, try $W = \alpha_nq_n + W'$. Then $\partial W/\partial q_n = \alpha_n$ is constant and the equation $H(q_1,\dots,q_{n-1}, \partial_{q'} W', \alpha_n) = E$ no longer involves $q_n$. Any complete integral of that $(n-1)$-variable equation, with $\alpha_n$ added as a parameter, is a complete integral of the original: the determinant is block triangular with a $1$ in the $(q_n, \alpha_n)$ slot.

The lemma's hypothesis matters. On a set that is not a product the conclusion can fail, which is why separation is a local statement, on a box in the coordinates.

Stäckel's conditions

theorem · Stäckel conditions

Let $H = \sum_i \psi_i(q)\big[\tfrac12 p_i^2 + w_i(q_i)\big]$, where $\psi$ is the first row of $\Phi^{-1}$ for a nonsingular $n\times n$ matrix $\Phi$ whose $i$-th row depends on $q_i$ only. Then the equation is separable: the $n$ ODEs

$$\tfrac12 W_i'(q_i)^2 + w_i(q_i) = \textstyle\sum_j \Phi_{ij}(q_i)\,\alpha_j$$

give a complete integral with $\alpha_1 = E$. Conversely, for orthogonal coordinates ($H = \tfrac12\sum g^{ii}p_i^2 + V$), separability forces this form.

sufficiency: two lines, in the notes (N) · necessity: cited

Kepler is Stäckel

Take $m = 1$. Rows $\Phi_r = (1, -r^{-2}, 0)$, $\Phi_\theta = (0, 1, -\sin^{-2}\theta)$, $\Phi_\phi = (0,0,1)$, $w_r = -k/r$, $w_\theta = w_\phi = 0$; then $\psi = (1,\ r^{-2},\ r^{-2}\sin^{-2}\theta)$ and $\alpha = (E,\ L^2/2,\ L_z^2/2)$.

Full proof of sufficiency. Let each $W_i$ solve its ODE and put $W = \sum_i W_i$, so $p_i = W_i'$. Then $$H = \sum_i\psi_i\Big[\tfrac12 W_i'^2 + w_i\Big] = \sum_i\psi_i\sum_j\Phi_{ij}\alpha_j = \sum_j(\psi\Phi)_j\,\alpha_j = \alpha_1 ,$$ because $\psi\Phi$ is the first row of $\Phi^{-1}\Phi = I$. So $W$ solves $H = E$ with $E = \alpha_1$. Completeness: $\partial^2 W/\partial q_i\partial\alpha_j = \partial_{\alpha_j}W_i' = \Phi_{ij}/W_i'$, by differentiating the $i$-th ODE in $\alpha_j$. So the matrix is $\mathrm{diag}(1/W_i')\,\Phi$, nonsingular wherever no $W_i'$ vanishes (away from turning points). $\square$

Check on Kepler. With $m$ restored (divide each $\tfrac12 W_i'^2$ by $m$, and $\alpha_2, \alpha_3$ become $L^2/2m$, $L_z^2/2m$), the three ODEs are $\tfrac1{2m}W_r'^2 - k/r = E - L^2/2mr^2$, $\ \tfrac1{2m}W_\theta'^2 = L^2/2m - L_z^2/2m\sin^2\theta$, $\ \tfrac1{2m}W_\phi'^2 = L_z^2/2m$ — exactly the separated equations of the slide "Kepler, separated". And $\psi\Phi = (1,\ -r^{-2} + r^{-2},\ -r^{-2}\sin^{-2}\theta + r^{-2}\sin^{-2}\theta) = (1,0,0)$ ✓.

Necessity — cited. Stäckel proved in 1891 that for orthogonal coordinates this form is also necessary for additive separability. The proof is a longer computation with the separated equations and is given in Goldstein, Classical Mechanics, 2nd edition, appendix D. You do not need it: in practice the theorem is used in the sufficient direction, to recognise coordinates that work.

Physics. The coordinates in which the equation separates are a property of the potential, not of the kinetic energy: Kepler separates in spherical and in parabolic coordinates, which is why a uniform field added to Kepler (the Stark problem) is still solvable — in parabolic coordinates but not spherical ones. The action variable

definition · one degree of freedom

Let the level set $\{H(q,p) = E\}$ contain a closed orbit $\gamma_E$. The action variable is

$$I(E) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma_E} p\,\mathrm dq ,$$

a line integral (unit 04) once round, in the direction of motion. Two cases:

  • libration: $\gamma_E$ bounds a region, and $2\pi I$ is its area;
  • rotation: $q$ is an angle and $\gamma_E$ goes once round the cylinder; $2\pi I$ is the area under it over one period of $q$.

The pendulum, $H = p^2/2 + 1 - \cos q$. Shaded: $2\pi I$ for a libration ($E = 1$) and for a rotation ($E = 2.9$). Dashed: the separatrix, where $I$ is defined but $T = \infty$.

Why the area. For a libration traversed clockwise in the $(q,p)$ plane — $p > 0$ on top moving right, $p < 0$ underneath moving left — the line integral $\oint p\,\mathrm dq$ is $\int(p_{\text{top}} - p_{\text{bottom}})\,\mathrm dq$, which is the enclosed area. Green's theorem (unit 04) says the same: $\oint p\,\mathrm dq$ round a curve is $\pm$ the area it bounds. For a rotation the curve does not bound anything on the cylinder; the integral of $p\,\mathrm dq$ over one period of $q$ is the area between the curve and $p = 0$.

Why $2\pi$. So that the conjugate angle has period $2\pi$ (the angle-variable slide), and so that for the oscillator $I = E/\omega$ exactly. With this normalisation $I$ has the dimension of $p\cdot q$: action, $\mathrm{J\,s}$, which is where the name comes from.

The figure. Both curves are exact level sets of the pendulum's $H$, computed and plotted, in units where $m\ell^2 = 1$ and $g/\ell = 1$. Unit 02 met this picture as a phase portrait; unit 14 read it as level sets of $H$. The new thing is to attach a number, the area, to each closed curve.

Which loop? In one degree of freedom there is no choice. With several, a torus has several independent loops and each gives an action; that is the many-degrees-of-freedom slide.

The frequency is a derivative

theorem · frequency theorem

For $H = p^2/2m + V(q)$ and a libration between simple turning points ($V' \ne 0$ there),

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dE}\oint p\,\mathrm dq = T(E), \qquad\text{so}\qquad \omega(E) = \frac{2\pi}{T} = \frac{\mathrm dE}{\mathrm dI} .$$

differentiate $2\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{2m(E - V)}\,\mathrm dx$; the integrable singularity needs a line of care — full proof in the notes (N)

check · the oscillator

$I = E/\omega$ (the ellipse's area over $2\pi$), so $\mathrm dE/\mathrm dI = \omega$ ✓ — independent of $E$, which is isochronism.

dimensions

$[E]/[I] = \mathrm J/(\mathrm{J\,s}) = \mathrm s^{-1}$ ✓. And $\partial p/\partial E = m/p = 1/\dot q$, so $\oint\partial_E p\,\mathrm dq = \oint\mathrm dt$.

The period is the rate at which phase-space area grows with energy. Find $I(E)$ — one integral — and the frequency follows by differentiation. No $q(t)$.

Full proof. Let $x_1(E) < x_2(E)$ be the turning points, $V(x_i) = E$, with $V' \ne 0$ there, so by the implicit function theorem they move $C^1$ with $E$, and near each, $E - V(x) \approx |V'(x_i)|\,|x - x_i|$, making $1/p$ integrable (unit 00). Write $A(E) = \oint p\,\mathrm dq = 2\int_{x_1(E)}^{x_2(E)} p(E,x)\,\mathrm dx$ with $p = \sqrt{2m(E - V)}$. Take $E' > E$ close to $E$. On $[x_1(E), x_2(E)]$, $$\frac{p(E',x) - p(E,x)}{E' - E} = \frac{2m}{p(E',x) + p(E,x)},$$ which increases to $m/p(E,x)$ as $E'\downarrow E$; by monotone convergence its integral tends to $\int m\,\mathrm dx/p = T/2$. The extra strips $[x_1(E'), x_1(E)]$ and $[x_2(E), x_2(E')]$ have width $O(E' - E)$, and on them $p(E',x) \le \sqrt{2m(E' - E)}$, so they contribute $O\big((E'-E)^{3/2}\big)$, which is $o(E' - E)$. The same argument for $E' \uparrow E$ with the roles swapped gives the left derivative. So $A'(E) = 2\cdot T/2 = T$. $\square$

Since $A = 2\pi I$: $2\pi\,\mathrm dI/\mathrm dE = T$, i.e. $\mathrm dE/\mathrm dI = 2\pi/T = \omega$. And $I(E)$ is strictly increasing (its derivative $T/2\pi$ is positive), so it can be inverted to give $E(I)$: energy as a function of action, which is the Hamiltonian in the new variables.

The general statement — for any $H(q,p)$, not only $p^2/2m + V$ — is the same argument with $\partial p/\partial E = 1/(\partial H/\partial p) = 1/\dot q$ from the implicit function theorem; the bookkeeping at turning points is identical wherever $\partial H/\partial q \ne 0$ there.

At the separatrix the turning points become equilibria, $V' = 0$, the singularity of $1/p$ is no longer integrable, and $T\to\infty$ while $I$ stays finite: $\mathrm dI/\mathrm dE$ blows up logarithmically. Section 4 needs this.

The angle conjugate to it

definition

Let $W(q, I) = \int^q p(q', E(I))\,\mathrm dq'$ — the characteristic function with $I$ as its constant. The angle variable is

$$w = \frac{\partial W}{\partial I}(q, I) .$$
theorem · action–angle variables

$(q,p)\mapsto(w, I)$ is canonical (type-2 generating function $W(q,I)$, unit 14), the new Hamiltonian is $E(I)$, and

$$\dot I = 0, \qquad \dot w = \frac{\mathrm dE}{\mathrm dI} = \omega(I), \qquad w(t) = \omega t + w_0 .$$

Once round the orbit, $\ \Delta w = \oint\dfrac{\partial p}{\partial I}\,\mathrm dq = \dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dI}\oint p\,\mathrm dq = \dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dI}(2\pi I) = 2\pi$.

So $w$ is an honest angle, the motion is a uniform rotation of it, and all the dynamics is in $E(I)$.

Full proof. $W(q,I)$ is a type-2 generating function: $p = \partial_q W$ holds by construction, the new coordinate is $w = \partial_I W$, and since $W$ has no explicit time dependence the new Hamiltonian is $K(w,I) = H(q, p) = E(I)$. This needs $\partial^2 W/\partial q\,\partial I = \partial p/\partial I = (\partial p/\partial E)(\mathrm dE/\mathrm dI) = \omega/\dot q \ne 0$, true away from turning points. Hamilton's equations in the new variables are $\dot I = -\partial K/\partial w = 0$ and $\dot w = \partial K/\partial I = E'(I) = \omega$. For $\Delta w$: interchanging $\mathrm d/\mathrm dI$ with the loop integral is the frequency theorem's computation over again (the loop moves with $I$, but its endpoints are where $p = 0$, so the moving-endpoint terms vanish, as in the proof there). $\square$

Check, the oscillator. $E = \omega I$, $p = \sqrt{2m\omega I - m^2\omega^2q^2}$, and $w = \partial_I W = \int m\omega\,\mathrm dq/\sqrt{2m\omega I - m^2\omega^2 q^2} = \arcsin\big(q\sqrt{m\omega/2I}\big)$. So $q = \sqrt{2I/m\omega}\,\sin w$ and $p = \sqrt{2m\omega I}\cos w$: the ellipse, parametrised by a uniformly rotating angle. Dimensions: $\sqrt{I/m\omega}$ is $\sqrt{\mathrm{J\,s/(kg\,s^{-1})}} = \mathrm m$ ✓.

Why this matters beyond one degree of freedom. $w$ is multivalued as a function of $(q,p)$ — it increases by $2\pi$ each time round — and that is the point: it is a coordinate on a circle. Unit 17 shows that near a torus of an integrable system this is the whole structure (the Liouville–Arnold theorem), and that its breakdown under perturbation is what chaos is.

Many degrees of freedom: one loop per coordinate

definition · separable case

If $W = \sum_i W_i(q_i, \alpha)$ and each $q_i$ oscillates (or rotates) between limits fixed by $\alpha$, the orbit fills a subset of the invariant torus $\{p_i = W_i'(q_i),\ \text{each } q_i \text{ in its range}\}$, and

$$I_i(\alpha) = \frac1{2\pi}\oint p_i\,\mathrm dq_i \quad\text{(the loop in the } (q_i, p_i)\text{ plane)}.$$
frequencies

Invert to $\alpha(I)$, so $H = E(I_1,\dots,I_n)$. Then as before $\dot I_i = 0$ and

$$\dot w_i = \omega_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial I_i} .$$
the general case — cited

Without separation the loops are the $n$ independent topological cycles on the torus. That these tori exist, and the loop integrals depend only on the cycle, is the Liouville–Arnold theorem: unit 17.

Full proof, separable case. $W(q, I) := \sum_i W_i(q_i, \alpha(I))$ is a type-2 generating function to $(w, I)$ with $w_j = \partial W/\partial I_j$ and new Hamiltonian $E(I)$, so $\dot I = 0$ and $\dot w_j = \partial E/\partial I_j$. The periodicity: carrying $q_i$ once round its own loop, with the others fixed, changes $w_j$ by $\oint\partial_{I_j}p_i\,\mathrm dq_i = \partial_{I_j}\oint p_i\,\mathrm dq_i = \partial_{I_j}(2\pi I_i) = 2\pi\delta_{ij}$. So $(w_1,\dots,w_n)$ are angles on an $n$-torus, each advanced by $2\pi$ by exactly one of the coordinate loops. The Jacobian condition is that $\alpha\mapsto I$ be invertible, which holds for Kepler (below) and generically.

Topological cycles. The torus $\mathbb T^n$ is $\mathbb R^n/2\pi\mathbb Z^n$, and its closed loops up to continuous deformation are labelled by $n$ integers — how many times the loop winds in each direction. (That classification is the fundamental group $\mathbb Z^n$; you need only the picture.) Why the loop integral depends only on the class: on the torus, $p = \partial_q W$ locally, so $p\cdot\mathrm dq$ restricted to it is locally exact, and unit 04's argument makes the integral over a loop invariant under deformation. Unit 17 proves this properly.

The torus picture. A motion with $n$ frequencies $\omega_i$ winds round the torus. If the $\omega_i$ are rationally independent it never closes and fills the torus densely (Kronecker's theorem, also in unit 17); if they are commensurate it closes. That dichotomy is the "three equal frequencies" slide, for Kepler.

For $A, B, C > 0$ with $B^2 > AC$, between the two zeros $r_1 < r_2$ of the root,

Checks. Angular: $\beta = 0$ gives $\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\alpha\,\mathrm d\theta = \alpha$ ✓; $\beta\to\alpha$ shrinks the interval to $\theta = \pi/2$, and $0$ ✓. Radial: $B^2 = AC$ makes $r_1 = r_2$, and $B/\sqrt A - \sqrt C = 0$ ✓.

Then $(b - u)(u - a) = \big(\tfrac{b-a}2\big)^2\sin^2 s$ and $\mathrm du = \tfrac{b-a}2\sin s\,\mathrm ds$, so $\int_a^b\mathrm du/\sqrt{(b-u)(u-a)} = \int_0^\pi\mathrm ds = \pi$, and $\int_a^b u\,\mathrm du/\sqrt{\cdots} = \int_0^\pi\big(\tfrac{a+b}2 - \tfrac{b-a}2\cos s\big)\mathrm ds = \pi\tfrac{a+b}2$.

Full proof, radial. $-A + 2B/r - C/r^2 = A(r_2 - r)(r - r_1)/r^2$ with $r_1 + r_2 = 2B/A$ and $r_1r_2 = C/A$. So the integrand is $\sqrt A\,(r_2 - r)(r - r_1)/\big(r\sqrt{(r_2-r)(r-r_1)}\big)$, and $(r_2 - r)(r - r_1)/r = (r_1 + r_2) - r - r_1r_2/r$. Three integrals against $\mathrm dr/\sqrt{(r_2-r)(r-r_1)}$: the constant gives $\pi(r_1 + r_2)$; the $r$ gives $\pi(r_1+r_2)/2$; and $\int\mathrm dr/(r\sqrt{\cdots}) = \pi/\sqrt{r_1r_2}$ by $u = 1/r$, which turns it into $\int\mathrm du/\sqrt{r_1r_2(u - 1/r_2)(1/r_1 - u)}$. Total: $\sqrt A\,\pi\big[(r_1+r_2)/2 - \sqrt{r_1r_2}\big] = \pi\big[B/\sqrt A - \sqrt C\big]$. $\square$

Full proof, angular. Write the integrand as $\sqrt{\alpha^2\sin^2\theta - \beta^2}/\sin\theta$ and put $c = \cos\theta$, $\mathrm dc = -\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta$: the integral becomes $\alpha\int_{-c_0}^{c_0}\sqrt{c_0^2 - c^2}\,\mathrm dc/(1 - c^2)$ with $c_0^2 = 1 - \beta^2/\alpha^2$. Split $\dfrac{c_0^2 - c^2}{1 - c^2} = 1 - \dfrac{1 - c_0^2}{1 - c^2}$. The first piece gives $\int\mathrm dc/\sqrt{c_0^2 - c^2} = \pi$ (base integral). The second needs $\int_{-c_0}^{c_0}\dfrac{\mathrm dc}{(1-c^2)\sqrt{c_0^2-c^2}}$: with $c = c_0\sin s$ it is $\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\dfrac{\mathrm ds}{1 - c_0^2\sin^2 s}$, and with $t = \tan s$ that is $\int_{\mathbb R}\dfrac{\mathrm dt}{1 + (1 - c_0^2)t^2} = \dfrac{\pi}{\sqrt{1 - c_0^2}}$. So the integral is $\alpha\big[\pi - (1 - c_0^2)\pi/\sqrt{1-c_0^2}\big] = \alpha\pi\big(1 - \sqrt{1-c_0^2}\big) = \pi(\alpha - \beta)$. $\square$

Three equal frequencies: why Kepler orbits close

degenerate frequencies

For Kepler, $H$ depends on the actions only through their sum, so $\omega_r = \omega_\theta = |\omega_\phi|$. Every coordinate completes its cycle in the same time: after one period all return together, and the orbit closes.

any central force

Isotropy alone makes $H = H(I_r,\ I_\theta + |I_\phi|)$, since $I_\theta + |I_\phi| = L$ whatever $V(r)$ is. So $\omega_\theta = |\omega_\phi|$ always: the plane never turns. But $\omega_r \ne \omega_\theta$ in general, and the ellipse precesses.

Proof that isotropy gives $H(I_r, L)$. For any $V(r)$ the separation of the $\theta$ and $\phi$ parts is word for word the Kepler computation, since only the radial equation contains $V$. So $I_\phi = L_z$ and $I_\theta = L - |L_z|$ always, and the radial action $I_r = \frac1\pi\int\sqrt{2m(E - V) - L^2/r^2}\,\mathrm dr$ is a function of $(E, L)$. Why closing needs a fixed rational ratio. In angle variables the motion is $w_r = \omega_r t$, $w_\theta = \omega_\theta t$ (up to phases), and the orbit in the plane closes iff both return to their starting values mod $2\pi$ at a common time, iff $\omega_r/\omega_\theta \in\mathbb Q$. If the ratio varies continuously with the orbit, some orbits have irrational ratio and do not close; so "all bounded orbits close" forces the ratio to be a constant rational.

Bertrand's theorem — that only $-k/r$ and $r^2$ achieve this among power laws and indeed among all central potentials with bounded orbits — was cited in unit 08, and it stays cited: Bertrand (1873); a proof via the small-oscillation frequency ratio is in Goldstein, Classical Mechanics, 3rd edition, §3.6 (Appendix A of the 2nd edition). What the action-angle view adds is the reason: closure is a statement about $H(I)$, and Kepler's $H$ is the special function of $I_r + L$ alone. The hidden symmetry unit 13 found (the Runge–Lenz vector, the $SO(4)$) is what forces that.

The precession, quantitatively. If $\omega_r \ne \omega_\theta$, the perihelion angle advances by $2\pi(\omega_\theta/\omega_r - 1)$ per radial period. Unit 37 computes that for general relativity's correction and gets $43''$ per century for Mercury.

How the action responds to a parameter

lemma

Let $H = p^2/2m + V(q, \lambda)$. At fixed energy,

$$\frac{\partial I}{\partial\lambda}\Big|_E = -\frac{1}{\omega}\Big\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial\lambda}\Big\rangle ,$$

with $\langle\cdot\rangle$ the time average over one period of the unperturbed orbit. Proof. $\partial_\lambda p|_{E,q} = -m\,\partial_\lambda V/p = -\partial_\lambda V/\dot q$, so $\oint\partial_\lambda p\,\mathrm dq = -\oint\partial_\lambda V\,\mathrm dt$; divide by $2\pi$. $\square$

frequency without motion

For $V = \tfrac12 m\omega_0^2q^2 + \lambda U(q)$, to first order in $\lambda$:

$$\begin{aligned}E(I) &= \omega_0 I + \lambda\,\langle U\rangle_I,\\ \omega(I) &= \omega_0 + \lambda\frac{\mathrm d\langle U\rangle_I}{\mathrm dI},\end{aligned}$$

averaging over the unperturbed ellipse of action $I$.

check · a cubic

$U = q^3$ is odd; on $q = \sqrt{2I/m\omega_0}\sin w$ its average is $0$. No first-order shift — true: a cubic term shifts the frequency only at order $\lambda^2$. The quartic is P6; the pendulum is P7.

Full proof of the first-order formula. At $\lambda = 0$, $I = E/\omega_0$. The lemma gives $\partial_\lambda I|_{E,\lambda=0} = -\langle U\rangle/\omega_0$, the average taken over the unperturbed orbit of energy $E$, i.e. of action $E/\omega_0$. So $I(E,\lambda) = E/\omega_0 - \lambda\langle U\rangle_{E/\omega_0}/\omega_0 + O(\lambda^2)$. Solve for $E$: $E = \omega_0 I + \lambda\langle U\rangle_{E/\omega_0} + O(\lambda^2)$, and replacing $\langle U\rangle_{E/\omega_0}$ by $\langle U\rangle_I$ costs another $O(\lambda^2)$ since the two arguments differ by $O(\lambda)$. Differentiate in $I$ for the frequency (the $O(\lambda^2)$ remainder is smooth in $I$, being built from smooth integrals). $\square$ This is first-order perturbation theory in one line; unit 21 builds the systematic version.

The lemma's integral. The interchange of $\partial_\lambda$ with the loop integral is justified exactly as in the frequency theorem: the turning points move with $\lambda$ but $p = 0$ there, and the difference quotient of $p$ is dominated near them. The unperturbed average appears because the orbit being integrated over is the one at the given $\lambda$, and at first order that is the unperturbed one.

Why this is remarkable. The frequency of a nonlinear oscillator is found from an average of the perturbation over the linear motion. No nonlinear ODE is solved, and none of the secular terms that plague naive perturbation theory (unit 21's opening problem) can appear, because nothing here depends on $t$.

Dimensions. $\lambda\langle U\rangle$ is an energy, $I$ an action, so $\lambda\,\mathrm d\langle U\rangle/\mathrm dI$ is a frequency ✓.

15 · adiabatic

Slow change, and what survives it

definition

Let $H = H(q, p, \lambda)$ with $\lambda = \lambda(\varepsilon t)$ for a fixed smooth function $\lambda(\cdot)$, and $\varepsilon > 0$ small. A quantity $F(q,p,\lambda)$ is an adiabatic invariant if there is $C$ independent of $\varepsilon$ with $|F(t) - F(0)| \le C\varepsilon$ for $0 \le t \le 1/\varepsilon$.

theorem · one degree of freedom

If every frozen system $H(\cdot,\cdot,\lambda)$ has closed orbits with $\omega$ bounded away from $0$, the action $I$ is an adiabatic invariant.

Why, at first order: $\dot I = \dot\lambda\,\partial_\lambda I|_{q,p}$, and by the lemma on how the action responds to a parameter

$$\dot I = \frac{\dot\lambda}{\omega}\Big(\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda} - \Big\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial\lambda}\Big\rangle\Big):$$

$O(\varepsilon)$ and of zero mean over each period. It wobbles; it does not drift.

the averaging argument in the notes (N) · the estimate over time $1/\varepsilon$: cited

The first-order computation in full. $I$ as a function on phase space is $I(H(q,p,\lambda), \lambda)$, where $I(E,\lambda)$ is the action of the frozen level set. So at fixed $(q,p)$, $\partial_\lambda I = \partial_E I\cdot\partial_\lambda H + \partial_\lambda I|_E = \omega^{-1}\partial_\lambda H - \omega^{-1}\langle\partial_\lambda H\rangle$, using $\partial_E I = 1/\omega$ (frequency theorem) and the lemma. Along the motion $I$ changes only through $\lambda$ — the frozen flow preserves it, since it is a function of $H$ — so $\dot I = \dot\lambda\,\partial_\lambda I$, and $\dot\lambda = \varepsilon\lambda'(\varepsilon t) = O(\varepsilon)$.

The averaging argument. Over one period $T$ of the motion, $\lambda$ changes by $O(\varepsilon T)$, so the orbit differs from a frozen orbit by $O(\varepsilon)$, and $\int_0^T\dot I\,\mathrm dt = \dot\lambda\,\omega^{-1}\int_0^T(\partial_\lambda H - \langle\partial_\lambda H\rangle)\,\mathrm dt + O(\varepsilon^2) = O(\varepsilon^2)$. Over time $1/\varepsilon$ there are $O(1/\varepsilon)$ periods, so the accumulated drift is $O(\varepsilon)$, on top of a within-period oscillation that is also $O(\varepsilon)$. That is the whole idea.

Cited: the theorem as stated. Making "differs from a frozen orbit by $O(\varepsilon)$" rigorous uniformly over $1/\varepsilon$ periods is done by changing to action-angle variables of the frozen system and averaging over the angle: Arnold, Mathematical Methods of Classical Mechanics, chapter 10, the section on adiabatic invariants; or Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §49, with §51 showing the stronger fact that for analytic $\lambda(\cdot)$ the total change $I(+\infty) - I(-\infty)$ is exponentially small in $1/\varepsilon$.

The hypothesis on $\omega$ matters. "Slow" means slow compared with the motion itself: $\varepsilon$ is really the ratio of the period to the timescale of $\lambda$. If $\omega\to0$ — at a separatrix — the motion is not fast compared with anything, and the theorem fails. That is the subject of "Where adiabatic invariance fails".

15 · adiabatic

Where adiabatic invariance fails

at a separatrix

Near the separatrix $\omega\to0$: the period is not short compared with anything, and no change is slow. A pendulum whose amplitude is driven slowly up to the separatrix can cross it, and $I$ jumps by an amount that depends sensitively on the phase at crossing.

at a resonance

With several degrees of freedom there are several frequencies. When a slowly varying system passes through $n_1\omega_1 + n_2\omega_2 = 0$, energy can be exchanged between the modes and the individual $I_i$ need not be invariant.

what does hold

One degree of freedom, away from separatrices: the theorem. Several: averaging holds for most initial conditions, with resonances the exceptions (cited). The comet is the first case — exact conservation of $\vec L$ leaves one radial degree of freedom, never near a separatrix.

Why at a separatrix. The frequency theorem's proof showed $T\to\infty$ logarithmically as $E$ approaches the separatrix energy. The adiabatic parameter is (period) × (rate of change of $\lambda$), so it grows without bound however small $\varepsilon$ is: the hypothesis "$\omega$ bounded away from zero" is violated, and the averaging argument collapses. The size and statistics of the jump are cited, not proved: the theory of separatrix crossing is surveyed in Arnold, Kozlov and Neishtadt, Mathematical Aspects of Classical and Celestial Mechanics, 3rd ed., chapter 6 (§6.4.7; it is chapter 5, §4 in the 2nd edition).

Why at a resonance. The averaging argument averaged $\partial_\lambda H$ over one angle. With two angles the average is over the torus, and that average is the time average only if the orbit fills the torus — i.e. if the frequencies are rationally independent. At a resonance the orbit lies on a closed curve in the torus, the time average differs from the torus average, and the zero-mean property is lost. This is the same phenomenon that unit 17 turns into chaos.

Physics. Adiabatic invariance is how magnetic bottles confine plasmas (the magnetic moment of a gyrating charge is an adiabatic invariant), why the Earth's radiation belts exist, and why a slowly compressed gas warms up in the way it does (problem P9 is the one-dimensional version). The name comes from thermodynamics: an "adiabatic" process is slow and exchanges no heat, and for a gas of oscillators the invariance of each $I$ is what makes such a process reversible.

Fermat and Maupertuis

Fermat, 1662 · light

Rays in a medium of refractive index $n(\vec x)$ are the extremals of optical length

$$\int n(\vec x)\,\mathrm ds .$$

Unit 11's Euler–Lagrange equation, parametrised by arc length, is the ray equation

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\Big(n\,\frac{\mathrm d\vec x}{\mathrm ds}\Big) = \nabla n .$$
Maupertuis, 1744 · particles

Trajectories of energy $E$ in a potential $V$ are the extremals of the abbreviated action

$$\int\vec p\cdot\mathrm d\vec x = \int\sqrt{2m\big(E - V(\vec x)\big)}\,\mathrm ds$$

among paths of energy $E$ between the same points — Maupertuis' principle, proved on the slide after this.

the dictionary

Same functional, with $\ n(\vec x) \longleftrightarrow \sqrt{2m(E - V(\vec x))} = \lVert\vec p\rVert$. A particle is a ray in a medium whose index is its own momentum.

Full proof of the ray equation. Parametrise a path as $\vec x(\sigma)$ with any parameter; the functional is $\int F\,\mathrm d\sigma$ with $F(\vec x, \vec x') = n(\vec x)\lVert\vec x'\rVert$. Unit 11's Euler–Lagrange equations in three dependent variables are $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\sigma}\partial_{\vec x'}F = \partial_{\vec x}F$, and $\partial_{\vec x'}F = n\,\vec x'/\lVert\vec x'\rVert$, $\partial_{\vec x}F = \lVert\vec x'\rVert\nabla n$. So $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\sigma}\big(n\vec x'/\lVert\vec x'\rVert\big) = \lVert\vec x'\rVert\nabla n$. Now choose $\sigma = s$, arc length, so $\lVert\vec x'\rVert = 1$. $\square$ The functional does not care about the parametrisation — the three equations are not independent, which is why one may fix $\sigma$ freely.

Check. Constant $n$: $\mathrm d^2\vec x/\mathrm ds^2 = 0$, straight lines ✓. Unit 11's Snell's law is the ray equation across a sharp interface: the component of $n\,\mathrm d\vec x/\mathrm ds$ along the interface is conserved because $\nabla n$ is normal to it.

History and a warning about names. Maupertuis stated his principle in 1744 as a metaphysical claim about economy in nature, in a form too vague to be wrong; Euler, in the same year, stated it correctly for a particle in a central field; Lagrange extended it; and Jacobi, in his Königsberg lectures of 1842–43, gave the form without time used here. It is often called the "principle of least action", which is doubly misleading: it is stationary, not least (unit 11's conjugate points), and the action in it is $W$, not $\int L\,\mathrm dt$.

The corpuscle puzzle. Rays bend towards larger $n$. In mechanics $n = \lVert\vec p\rVert$, so particles bend towards where they move faster — as Newton's corpuscular theory of light said light does, with light faster in glass. Wave theory says the opposite: in glass light is slower, $n = c/v$. Both "work" because the index is not the speed in both pictures; the wavefronts slide resolves it.

Trajectories are rays

theorem · Maupertuis–Jacobi

Let $n = \sqrt{2m(E - V)}$ on the region $\{V < E\}$. Every trajectory of energy $E$ satisfies the ray equation $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}(n\,\vec T) = \nabla n$, $\vec T = \mathrm d\vec x/\mathrm ds$. Conversely, a solution of it traversed at speed $\mathrm ds/\mathrm dt = n/m$ is a trajectory of energy $E$.

proof

On a trajectory $\lVert\dot{\vec x}\rVert = n/m$ (energy), so $\mathrm ds = (n/m)\,\mathrm dt$ and $n\vec T = m\dot{\vec x} = \vec p$. Hence

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\big(n\vec T\big) = \frac{m}{n}\,\dot{\vec p} = -\frac{m\nabla V}{n}, \qquad \nabla n = \nabla\sqrt{2m(E - V)} = -\frac{m\nabla V}{n} .$$

Equal. Conversely, with $\mathrm dt = m\,\mathrm ds/n$: $\ m\ddot{\vec x} = \dfrac{n}{m}\dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\big(n\vec T\big) = \dfrac{n}{m}\nabla n = -\nabla V$. $\square$

Check. $V = 0$: $n$ constant, straight lines at constant speed ✓. Dimensions: $n$ is a momentum, $\int n\,\mathrm ds$ an action ✓.

Precisely what is proved. The ray equation is the Euler–Lagrange equation of the abbreviated action $\int n\,\mathrm ds$ (previous slide), so this theorem is Maupertuis' principle: trajectories of energy $E$ are exactly its extremals, as unparametrised curves, with time recovered from $\mathrm dt = m\,\mathrm ds/n$. The mass has not been set to 1; several particles are handled by the same proof in the mass-weighted coordinates $\sqrt{m_i}\,\vec x_i$, in which the kinetic energy is $\tfrac12\lVert\dot y\rVert^2$.

What happens at $V = E$. On the boundary of the allowed region $n = 0$ and the proof breaks: $\mathrm ds/\mathrm dt = 0$ there, the particle stops and turns back. A ray reaching the boundary is a trajectory with a turning point, like a stone thrown straight up.

Why it is not circular. The proof uses energy conservation to link $s$ and $t$. That is legitimate on a trajectory, where energy is conserved (unit 04), and for the converse the parametrisation $\mathrm dt = m\,\mathrm ds/n$ is a choice that builds $\tfrac12 m\lVert\dot{\vec x}\rVert^2 + V = E$ in: $\tfrac12 m(n/m)^2 = n^2/2m = E - V$.

For the running example. Kepler's $n = \sqrt{2m(E + k/r)}$ is spherically symmetric: a spherically stratified medium, like the Earth's atmosphere for light. The worked example "Kepler orbits are geodesics" solves its rays.

15 · optics

Wavefronts are surfaces of constant W

the eikonal equation

The time-independent HJ equation for $H = \lVert\vec p\rVert^2/2m + V$ is

$$\lVert\nabla W\rVert^2 = 2m(E - V) = n^2,$$

the equation geometrical optics writes for its phase. The level sets of $W$ are wavefronts.

rays are orthogonal to wavefronts

The trajectories with $\vec p = \nabla W$ move along $\nabla W$, which is normal to the level sets of $W$ (unit 04). So trajectories cross wavefronts at right angles — Huygens' construction.

Uniform gravity: trajectories of one energy from one point (thin), levels of $W$ (thick), and the caustic (dashed), where $W$ folds.

Proof of the orthogonality. If $W$ solves the eikonal equation and $\vec x(t)$ solves $m\dot{\vec x} = \nabla W(\vec x)$, then $\vec x(t)$ is a trajectory: this is problem P3's theorem, that a single solution of the HJ equation already defines a family of trajectories. Along it $\dot{\vec x}$ is parallel to $\nabla W$, and the gradient is orthogonal to every tangent vector of a level set, because the derivative of $W$ along a curve lying in a level set is zero and equals $\nabla W$ dotted with the curve's tangent.

The figure. Units $v_0 = g = 1$, $m = 1$. Rays leave the origin in directions every $15^\circ$ at speed $1$; along each, $W = \int\vec p\cdot\mathrm d\vec x = \int v^2\,\mathrm dt$ was computed exactly and the curves $W = 0.2, 0.4, \dots, 1.0$ drawn. The dashed parabola $y = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12x^2$ is the envelope of the trajectories (the "safety parabola" of artillery), and every ray is drawn only up to where it touches it. Beyond the envelope several rays reach each point and $W$ is multivalued; the wavefronts cannot be continued as level sets of one function. That is exactly what a caustic is in optics — the bright curve on the bottom of a cup of coffee — and the conjugate points of unit 11 are where a ray touches it.

Moving wavefronts. The surfaces $S = -Et + W = $ const move: at time $t$ it is $W = $ const $+ Et$. The normal speed of a level set of $W$ moving so that $W$ grows at rate $E$ is $u = E/\lVert\nabla W\rVert = E/p$. This is the phase velocity of Hamilton's wave. It is inversely proportional to the particle's speed $p/m$. So the particle is fast exactly where the wave is slow — the wave picture's index $\propto 1/u \propto p$ agrees with the particle's index $n = p$. The corpuscle puzzle of the Fermat–Maupertuis slide resolves: mechanics' "light" slows down where the particle speeds up.

15 · optics

The eikonal limit

a wave, and its short-wavelength form

Let $\Delta = \sum_i\partial^2/\partial x_i^2$. A monochromatic wave of wavenumber $k_0$ in a medium obeys Helmholtz's equation $\ \Delta\psi + k_0^2n^2\psi = 0$ (cited: unit 24). Substitute $\psi = A\,e^{ik_0S}$ with real $A, S$. The product and chain rules give, exactly,

$$e^{-ik_0S}\big(\Delta\psi + k_0^2n^2\psi\big) = k_0^2A\big(n^2 - \lVert\nabla S\rVert^2\big) + ik_0\big(2\nabla A\cdot\nabla S + A\,\Delta S\big) + \Delta A .$$
the eikonal limit

For fixed $A \ne 0$ and $S$, the residual is $O(k_0)$ rather than $O(k_0^2)$ as $k_0\to\infty$ iff $\ \lVert\nabla S\rVert^2 = n^2$: the eikonal equation. Short waves travel along rays.

Run it backwards with $n = \sqrt{2m(E-V)}$ and $k_0 = 1/\hbar$, and a wave equation for particles is waiting. Schrödinger, 1926, did exactly that.

Full proof of the identity. $\nabla\psi = (\nabla A + ik_0A\nabla S)e^{ik_0S}$. Differentiating again and summing, $\Delta\psi = \big(\Delta A + 2ik_0\nabla A\cdot\nabla S + ik_0A\Delta S - k_0^2A\lVert\nabla S\rVert^2\big)e^{ik_0S}$. Add $k_0^2n^2\psi$. Complex exponentials are unit 06's algebra; nothing analytic about $\mathbb C$ is used. The "iff" is immediate: the right-hand side is a polynomial in $k_0$ whose $k_0^2$ coefficient is $A(n^2 - \lVert\nabla S\rVert^2)$. $\square$

What is and is not claimed. This shows that the eikonal equation is the condition for $Ae^{ik_0S}$ to solve Helmholtz's equation to leading order. It does not show that true solutions are close to such waves — that is a theorem about asymptotic expansions (the WKB method), which needs unit 21's language and is not proved here. Helmholtz's equation itself, for each component of monochromatic light in a slowly varying medium, is derived in unit 24. The next order, $2\nabla A\cdot\nabla S + A\Delta S = 0$, is the transport equation: it says how the amplitude changes along a ray, and it blows up at caustics, which is why caustics are bright.

History. Hamilton built the whole theory first for optics, in "Theory of systems of rays" (1828–37), and only then noticed that the same mathematics described particles. For ninety years the analogy was an elegance. Schrödinger's second paper of 1926, "Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem", argued explicitly: classical mechanics is to some wave mechanics as geometrical optics is to wave optics, so find the wave equation whose eikonal limit is the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. That argument, not a derivation, is how quantum mechanics got its wave equation. It is outside this course, but the classical half of it is now yours.

15 · geometry

A metric, defined properly

definition

A Riemannian metric on an open $U\subset\mathbb R^n$ assigns to each $x\in U$ a symmetric, positive-definite bilinear form $g_x$ on $\mathbb R^n$ (the velocities at $x$), with components $g_{ij}(x) = g_x(e_i, e_j)$ smooth in $x$. The length of a curve is $\ \mathcal L[\gamma] = \int\sqrt{g_{\gamma}(\gamma', \gamma')}\,\mathrm d\sigma$, and a geodesic is a critical point of $\mathcal L$ among curves with the same ends.

theorem · components

If $f_b = \sum_a A_{ab}e_a$ is another basis, then $g(f_a, f_b) = (A^{\mathsf T}GA)_{ab}$.

Proof. Bilinearity: $g(\sum_i A_{ia}e_i, \sum_jA_{jb}e_j) = \sum_{ij}A_{ia}g_{ij}A_{jb}$. $\square$

conformal rescaling

$\tilde g = \Omega^2g$ with $\Omega > 0$ smooth. Lengths change; angles do not: in $\cos\angle(u,v) = g(u,v)/\sqrt{g(u,u)g(v,v)}$ the $\Omega^2$ cancels. $\square$

Physics: kinetic energy is a metric, $T = \tfrac12 g(\dot x, \dot x)$ with $g = m\,\delta$. In spherical coordinates the theorem, with $A$ the derivative of the chart, gives $g = m\,\mathrm{diag}(1, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)$.

Mathematics first. The metric is a bilinear form at each point: that is the definition. The array $g_{ij}$ is its representation in one basis, and the rule $G\mapsto A^{\mathsf T}GA$ is a theorem about how that representation changes. Unit 16 builds all tensors this way — a multilinear map first, its components' transformation law as a consequence — and unit 31 replaces "velocities at $x$" with the tangent space of a manifold. Here nothing more than linear algebra is needed.

Notation. $g = m(\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\mathrm d\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\phi^2)$ is shorthand for $g(v,v) = m(v_r^2 + r^2v_\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,v_\phi^2)$ for a velocity with components $(v_r, v_\theta, v_\phi)$ in the coordinate basis. The computation is the spherical-coordinates slide: $A$'s columns are $\hat e_r$, $r\hat e_\theta$, $r\sin\theta\,\hat e_\phi$, and $A^{\mathsf T}A = \mathrm{diag}(1, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)$ by orthonormality.

Length is independent of parametrisation. If $\sigma = \varphi(\tau)$ with $\varphi' > 0$, then $\gamma'$ scales by $\varphi'$, the square root by $\varphi'$, and $\mathrm d\sigma = \varphi'\,\mathrm d\tau$ absorbs it (unit 04's argument for line integrals). So "critical point of $\mathcal L$" is a property of the unparametrised curve.

On the definition of geodesic. Unit 32 defines geodesics through a connection, as curves whose velocity is parallel-transported, and proves that for the metric's own (Levi-Civita) connection these are exactly the constant-speed critical points of length. The variational definition here is the one unit 11 already used on the sphere, and all that this unit needs.

Conformal. The word means "angle-preserving". Maps between two surfaces that preserve angles are conformal; a metric multiplied by a positive function is the simplest example, and the Jacobi metric is one.

The Jacobi metric

theorem · the Jacobi metric

Let $g_{\mathrm{kin}}$ be the kinetic metric ($T = \tfrac12 g_{\mathrm{kin}}(\dot x, \dot x)$) and $V < E$ on $U$. On $U$ put the conformal rescaling

$$g_J = 2\big(E - V\big)\,g_{\mathrm{kin}} .$$

The trajectories of energy $E$ are exactly the geodesics of $g_J$, reparametrised so that $g_{\mathrm{kin}}(\dot x,\dot x) = 2(E - V)$.

proof

With $g_{\mathrm{kin}} = m\delta$, $\ \sqrt{g_J(\gamma',\gamma')} = \sqrt{2m(E - V)}\,\lVert\gamma'\rVert = n\lVert\gamma'\rVert$: the $g_J$-length is the abbreviated action $\int n\,\mathrm ds$, whose extremals are the trajectories (Maupertuis–Jacobi). $\square$

what it is not

A different metric for each $E$, and time is gone. It degenerates on $V = E$, where the particle turns.

Check. $V = 0$: $g_J = 2E\,g_{\mathrm{kin}}$, a constant multiple of flat; geodesics are straight lines ✓. With $m = 1$, $g_J = 2(E - V)\,\delta_{ij}$.

Coordinates do not matter. The $g_J$-length of a curve is a number defined without coordinates; its critical points are therefore the same curves in every chart — unit 12's form-invariance argument, applied to this functional. So the theorem, proved in Cartesian coordinates, holds in spherical ones with $g_{\mathrm{kin}} = m(\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\mathrm d\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\phi^2)$. For a system with constraints the kinetic metric is the one induced on the configuration space, and the theorem still holds; the proof without Cartesian coordinates needs unit 32's geodesic equation, and is cited: Landau and Lifshitz, Mechanics, §44, which derives "Jacobi's form of the principle of least action" for any $T = \tfrac12\sum a_{ik}(q)\dot q_i\dot q_k$.

Dimensions. $g_J(v,v)$ is energy × mass × speed², and its square root per unit parameter integrates to an action: a $g_J$-length is measured in $\mathrm{J\,s}$, the unit of $\int p\,\mathrm dq$.

The reparametrisation. A geodesic is a curve; to be a trajectory it must be traversed at the right speed. The condition $g_{\mathrm{kin}}(\dot x, \dot x) = 2(E - V)$ is $2T = 2(E - V)$, energy conservation, which is $\mathrm ds/\mathrm dt = n/m$ in the Cartesian case.

Why "free motion is geodesic motion". With $V = 0$ Newton's first law says free particles move in straight lines at constant speed — geodesics of the flat kinetic metric. The theorem says that with a force, the particle is still free, in the geometry $g_J$. Force has been traded for geometry, and the price is that the geometry depends on $E$ and forgets $t$. Einstein's gravity, in units 30–32, makes the same trade without paying that price.

15 · geometry · example

Worked: Kepler orbits are geodesics

the geodesics of Kepler's Jacobi metric

Step 1 — the metric. In the orbital plane ($\theta = \pi/2$, a reflection-symmetric plane), with $n^2 = 2m(E + k/r)$:

$$g_J = n(r)^2\big(\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\,\mathrm d\phi^2\big),$$ $$\mathcal L = \int n\sqrt{r'^2 + r^2}\,\mathrm d\phi .$$

Step 2 — Beltrami (unit 11): no explicit $\phi$, so $F - r'F_{r'}$ is constant:

$$\frac{n\,r^2}{\sqrt{r'^2 + r^2}} = \ell .$$

This is $n\,r\sin\alpha = \ell$, $\alpha$ the angle to the radius: Bouguer's formula of atmospheric optics. Mechanically, $\lVert\vec p\rVert\,r\sin\alpha = L$: so $\ell = L$.

Step 3 — $u = 1/r$. $r' = -u'/u^2$, and step 2 squared becomes

$$u'^2 = \frac{n^2}{L^2} - u^2 = \frac{2mE}{L^2} + \frac{2mk}{L^2}u - u^2 .$$

Step 4 — differentiate and divide by $2u'$:

$$u'' + u = \frac{mk}{L^2},$$

unit 08's Binet equation. So $u = \frac{mk}{L^2}\big(1 + e\cos(\phi - \phi_0)\big)$, and step 3 at the perihelion fixes $e^2 = 1 + 2EL^2/mk^2$.

Check. Section 2's orbit, exactly ✓. $\ell$ has dimension $n\cdot r$ = momentum × length ✓.

Step 1: why the plane. The reflection $\theta\mapsto\pi - \theta$ preserves $g_J$ and fixes the equatorial plane pointwise. Let $\gamma$ be a curve in the plane that is critical for $\mathcal L$ among curves in the plane. For an out-of-plane variation $\eta$, the reflected variation $\bar\eta$ gives the same first variation (the length is reflection-invariant) but the opposite sign (the out-of-plane component is reversed), so that part of the first variation is zero. Hence $\gamma$ is critical among all curves. And every orbit lies in some plane through the Sun (section 2); rotate it to the equator, which is an isometry of $g_J$.

Step 2 in detail. $F = n(r)\sqrt{r'^2 + r^2}$, $F_{r'} = nr'/\sqrt{r'^2 + r^2}$, so $F - r'F_{r'} = n(r'^2 + r^2 - r'^2)/\sqrt{r'^2 + r^2} = nr^2/\sqrt{r'^2 + r^2}$. The angle $\alpha$ between the curve and the radial direction has $\sin\alpha = r/\sqrt{r'^2 + r^2}$ (the tangent has radial component $r'$ and transverse component $r$ per unit $\phi$), so the constant is $nr\sin\alpha$. Pierre Bouguer used exactly this invariant in the eighteenth century for light in a spherically layered atmosphere. For the particle, $n = \lVert\vec p\rVert$ and $r\lVert\vec p\rVert\sin\alpha = \lVert\vec r\times\vec p\rVert = L$: Bouguer's invariant is the angular momentum.

Step 3. $r'^2 + r^2 = n^2r^4/L^2$ from step 2 squared. With $r = 1/u$, $r' = -u'/u^2$: $u'^2/u^4 + 1/u^2 = n^2/(L^2u^4)$, so $u'^2 = n^2/L^2 - u^2$, and $n^2 = 2mE + 2mku$.

Step 4. $2u'u'' = (2mk/L^2)u' - 2uu'$. Dividing by $2u'$ is allowed where $u' \ne 0$; the resulting equation is linear with smooth coefficients, so it holds also at the isolated apsides by continuity. The circular orbit, $u' \equiv 0$, satisfies step 3 with $u^2 - (2mk/L^2)u - 2mE/L^2 = 0$ having a double root, which is $E = -mk^2/2L^2$ ✓. At the perihelion $u' = 0$ and $u_{\max} = (mk/L^2)(1 + e)$ must be a root of the step-3 quadratic: substituting gives $e^2 = 1 + 2EL^2/mk^2$.

Halley. Per unit mass the "index" $n/m$ is the speed: $54.570\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at perihelion and $0.91164\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ at aphelion, ratio $59.86$, so the metric's conformal factor varies by $59.86^2 = 3583$ along the orbit. The total $g_J$-length of one orbit is $\oint\vec p\cdot\mathrm d\vec x = 2\pi(I_r + L) = 2\pi I$ — for Halley, per unit mass, $1.1822\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}$.

15 · geometry

Free motion is geodesic motion

what has been shown

A particle of energy $E$ under a force $-\nabla V$ is a free particle — it follows geodesics — in the geometry $g_J = 2(E - V)g_{\mathrm{kin}}$. Halley's ellipse is a straightest line. This is Newtonian mechanics, 1842–43.

for gravity, the mass drops out

With $V = m\Phi$, $\ g_J/m^2 = 2(\varepsilon - \Phi)\,\delta$ with $\varepsilon = E/m$. Every body with the same $\varepsilon$ sees the same geometry, whatever its mass. For an electric force, $V = q\Phi_e$, the geometry depends on $q/m$.

why this is not yet general relativity
  • The geometry depends on $\varepsilon$: a fast and a slow body through the same point see different metrics.
  • Time has been thrown away; the geometry is of space, not of spacetime.

Unit 30 puts time back and finds one metric for every body: possible only because all bodies fall alike, which is the box on the left.

The proof of the left-hand box is one line: substitute $V = m\Phi$ and $E = m\varepsilon$ into $g_J = 2(E - V)\,m\delta$ to get $2m^2(\varepsilon - \Phi)\delta$. Scaling a metric by a constant does not change its geodesics, so the geodesics depend only on $\varepsilon$ and $\Phi$. For a charge, $g_J = 2m(E - q\Phi_e)\delta = 2m^2(\varepsilon - (q/m)\Phi_e)\delta$, and bodies with different $q/m$ at the same $\varepsilon$ follow different geodesics.

The thread to the end of the course. Unit 30 argues that the equality of inertial and gravitational mass — the $m$ cancelling here — is what allows gravity to be geometry at all. Unit 32 defines geodesics of a metric on a manifold and shows free fall is one; unit 37 finds, from the Schwarzschild geodesics, the orbit equation $u'' + u = GM/h^2 + 3GMu^2/c^2$ — step 4 of the Kepler-geodesics example with one extra term. The extra term breaks the degeneracy of the actions slide, which is the $43''$.

What the Jacobi picture is still good for. In unit 17, the curvature of the Jacobi metric is one way to see why some systems are chaotic: geodesics of negatively curved metrics diverge exponentially, and Hadamard and later Krylov made this an argument for instability. That needs unit 33's curvature and is only named here.
