Rigid bodies, and what a tensor actually is

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. For an explicitly given body you compute \(\mathsf I\) in two frames, verify \(\mathsf I' = R\mathsf IR^{\mathsf T}\) component by component, predict which axis is unstable, and can explain in one sentence why nine numbers per basis is not enough to be a tensor.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Thirteen problems, about six hours. Conventions throughout: \(\mathsf I_O(\vec u,\vec w) = \int_B\rho\big[(\vec u\cdot\vec w)\lVert\vec r\rVert^2-(\vec u\cdot\vec r)(\vec w\cdot\vec r)\big]\mathrm dV\) about the stated origin \(O\); a change of basis \(\vec f_a = S_{ia}\vec e_i\) has \(A = S^{-1}\), and for an orthonormal pair \(R := S^{\mathsf T}\) has the new basis vectors as its rows, so \(\mathsf I' = R\mathsf IR^{\mathsf T}\). Principal moments are ordered \(I_1\le I_2\le I_3\). Euler's equations are taken in the principal body frame.

Constants: \(g = 9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\); the Earth's dynamical ellipticity \((I_3-I_1)/I_3 = 3.2737949\times10^{-3}\); one sidereal day \(= 86164\) s.

Computing the inertia tensor

P1 A cylinder, from the integral routine

exercises: multiple integrals in cylindrical coordinates, symmetry killing off-diagonal terms, limits

A uniform solid cylinder has mass \(M\), radius \(R\), height \(h\), axis along \(\vec e_3\), and its centre of mass at the origin.

  1. Show from the definition that the off-diagonal components of \(\mathsf I\) vanish in this frame, naming the symmetry you use.
  2. Compute \(I_{33}\) and \(I_{11}\) by integrating in cylindrical coordinates.
  3. Check the limits \(h\to0\) (a disc) and \(R\to0\) (a rod), and check the triangle inequality \(I_1+I_2\ge I_3\): for which shape is it tight?
  4. Numbers: \(M = 2.00\) kg, \(R = 0.050\) m, \(h = 0.30\) m.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to 2 is two formulas in \(M, R, h\), and to 4 two numbers in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). Nothing here needs a theorem: the definition of \(\mathsf I\) and unit 05's change of variables are the whole method.

Rung 2

In cylindrical coordinates \((s,\varphi,z)\) the volume element is \(s\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm d\varphi\,\mathrm dz\), and \(x = s\cos\varphi\), \(y = s\sin\varphi\). Each off-diagonal integrand contains either \(\cos\varphi\sin\varphi\) or a single odd power of \(z\); integrate that factor first.

Rung 3

First line: \(\rho = M/(\pi R^2h)\), and \(I_{33} = \int\rho(x^2+y^2)\,\mathrm dV = \rho\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\!\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^R s^2\cdot s\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm d\varphi\,\mathrm dz\). For \(I_{11}\) use \(y^2+z^2 = s^2\sin^2\varphi + z^2\) and \(\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2 = \pi\).

Worked solution

Idea. Cylindrical coordinates turn every integral into a product of one-dimensional integrals, and the angular one kills all six off-diagonal components at once.

1. Off-diagonal. \(\rho\) is constant and the region is \(\{s\le R,\ |z|\le h/2\}\). Then \(I_{12} = -\int\rho\,xy\,\mathrm dV = -\rho\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\!\mathrm dz\int_0^R\!s^3\mathrm ds\int_0^{2\pi}\!\cos\varphi\sin\varphi\,\mathrm d\varphi = 0\), because \(\int_0^{2\pi}\cos\varphi\sin\varphi\,\mathrm d\varphi = \tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}\sin2\varphi = 0\). For \(I_{13} = -\int\rho\,xz\), the \(z\)-integral \(\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}z\,\mathrm dz\) vanishes by the reflection \(z\mapsto-z\); same for \(I_{23}\). The symmetries used are the rotational symmetry about \(\vec e_3\) and the mirror \(z\mapsto-z\).

2. Diagonal. With \(\rho = M/(\pi R^2h)\),

\[ I_{33} = \rho\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\!\!\mathrm dz\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\mathrm d\varphi\int_0^R s^3\,\mathrm ds = \rho\,h\cdot2\pi\cdot\frac{R^4}{4} = \tfrac12MR^2 . \]

For \(I_{11} = \int\rho(y^2+z^2)\mathrm dV\): the \(y^2\) part is \(\rho\,h\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2\varphi\,\mathrm d\varphi\int_0^Rs^3\mathrm ds = \rho h\pi R^4/4 = \tfrac14MR^2\), and the \(z^2\) part is \(\rho\,\pi R^2\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}z^2\mathrm dz = \rho\pi R^2h^3/12 = \tfrac1{12}Mh^2\). Hence

\[ I_{11} = I_{22} = \tfrac14MR^2+\tfrac1{12}Mh^2 = \tfrac{M}{12}\big(3R^2+h^2\big),\qquad I_{33} = \tfrac12MR^2 . \]

3. Checks. \(h\to0\): \(I_1 = I_2 = \tfrac14MR^2\) and \(I_3 = \tfrac12MR^2\), the disc, and \(I_1+I_2 = I_3\) exactly — tight, as the perpendicular-axis theorem (P2) requires for a lamina. \(R\to0\): \(I_1=I_2 = \tfrac1{12}Mh^2\) and \(I_3=0\), the rod. In general \(I_1+I_2-I_3 = \tfrac16Mh^2\ge0\) ✓, vanishing only for the disc.

4. Numbers. \(I_3 = 0.5\times2.00\times0.0025 = \mathbf{2.50\times10^{-3}}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\); \(3R^2+h^2 = 0.0075+0.09 = 0.0975\), so \(I_1 = I_2 = 2.00\times0.0975/12 = \mathbf{1.625\times10^{-2}}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\).

Check. Dimensions \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) throughout ✓. Trace \(2I_1+I_3 = 0.0325+0.0025 = 0.0350\), against \(2\int\rho\lVert\vec r\rVert^2\mathrm dV = 2M(\tfrac12R^2+\tfrac1{12}h^2) = 2\times2(0.00125+0.0075) = 0.0350\) ✓. Ordering: here \(I_3\lt I_1 = I_2\), so this cylinder is a prolate symmetric top — long and thin, easiest to spin about its long axis, exactly as the block's spine was.

Another way

Slice the cylinder into discs of mass \(\mathrm dm = M\,\mathrm dz/h\). Each has \(\mathrm dI_{33} = \tfrac12R^2\mathrm dm\) and, about its own centre, \(\mathrm dI_{11} = \tfrac14R^2\mathrm dm\); the parallel-axis theorem adds \(z^2\mathrm dm\) to the latter. Integrating \(z^2\) over \([-h/2,h/2]\) gives \(Mh^2/12\), and the same two answers appear with no triple integral at all.

P2 Proof: the perpendicular-axis theorem, and where it stops medium

exercises: proving a theorem from the definition, the triangle inequality, finding the hypothesis that does the work

A lamina is a body lying entirely in the plane \(x_3 = 0\).

  1. Prove that for a lamina, \(I_{33} = I_{11}+I_{22}\) in that frame — the perpendicular-axis theorem — and prove it in the basis-free form: for any orthonormal \(\vec u,\vec w\) in the plane and \(\hat k\) normal to it, \(\mathsf I(\hat k,\hat k) = \mathsf I(\vec u,\vec u)+\mathsf I(\vec w,\vec w)\).
  2. Apply it to a uniform rectangular plate with sides \(p\) along \(\vec e_1\) and \(q\) along \(\vec e_2\), about its centre; then to a uniform annulus of inner radius \(R_1\) and outer radius \(R_2\).
  3. Show by exhibiting a body that the identity fails for a general three-dimensional body, and state exactly which step of your proof breaks.
  4. Deduce the slides' triangle inequality \(I_1+I_2\ge I_3\) for a general body, with equality iff the body is a lamina in the \(1\)–\(2\) plane.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 4 want proofs; part 2 wants formulas; part 3 wants one counterexample plus one sentence naming a hypothesis. No integration is needed in part 1 at all — only the definition and an algebraic identity between three integrands.

Rung 2

Write the three diagonal components as integrals and add two of them. Ask what \((x_2^2+x_3^2)+(x_1^2+x_3^2)\) is, compared with \(x_1^2+x_2^2\). For the basis-free version, choose the orthonormal basis \((\vec u,\vec w,\hat k)\) and note that "lamina" is the statement that \(\hat k\cdot\vec r = 0\) on the body.

Rung 3

First line: \(I_{11}+I_{22} = \int\rho\big[(x_2^2+x_3^2)+(x_3^2+x_1^2)\big]\mathrm dV = \int\rho\big[(x_1^2+x_2^2)+2x_3^2\big]\mathrm dV = I_{33}+2\int\rho\,x_3^2\,\mathrm dV\). Everything in the problem follows from reading that line in different directions.

Worked solution

Idea. One identity carries all four parts: \(I_{11}+I_{22}-I_{33} = 2\int_B\rho\,x_3^2\,\mathrm dV\). It is a theorem when the right side is zero, an inequality when it is not, and its failure for a solid body is exactly the right side being positive.

1. From the definition, \(I_{kk} = \int\rho(\lVert\vec r\rVert^2-x_k^2)\mathrm dV\), so

\[ I_{11}+I_{22} = \int_B\rho\big(2\lVert\vec r\rVert^2-x_1^2-x_2^2\big)\mathrm dV = \int_B\rho\big(x_1^2+x_2^2\big)\mathrm dV + 2\int_B\rho\,x_3^2\,\mathrm dV, \]

whose first term is \(I_{33}\); or, working on the integrands alone,

\[ I_{11}+I_{22}-I_{33} = \int_B\rho\Big[(x_2^2+x_3^2)+(x_3^2+x_1^2)-(x_1^2+x_2^2)\Big]\mathrm dV = 2\int_B\rho\,x_3^2\,\mathrm dV . \]

For a lamina \(x_3 = 0\) on \(B\), so the right side is zero. Basis-free: take the orthonormal basis \((\vec u,\vec w,\hat k)\); the identity above is exactly \(\mathsf I(\vec u,\vec u)+\mathsf I(\vec w,\vec w)-\mathsf I(\hat k,\hat k) = 2\int\rho(\hat k\cdot\vec r)^2\mathrm dV\), and "lamina normal to \(\hat k\)" says \(\hat k\cdot\vec r\equiv0\). \(\;\blacksquare\)

2. Rectangular plate. It is the \(c\to0\) limit of the slides' block: \(I_{11} = \tfrac{M}{12}q^2\), \(I_{22} = \tfrac{M}{12}p^2\), and the theorem gives \(I_{33} = \tfrac{M}{12}(p^2+q^2)\) with no further integration ✓ — which is the block's third formula.

Annulus. \(I_{33} = \int\rho\,s^2\,\mathrm dA\) with \(\rho = M/\pi(R_2^2-R_1^2)\), so \(I_{33} = \rho\,2\pi\int_{R_1}^{R_2}s^3\mathrm ds = \tfrac12M(R_1^2+R_2^2)\). By symmetry \(I_{11} = I_{22}\), and the theorem halves it: \(I_{11} = I_{22} = \tfrac14M(R_1^2+R_2^2)\). For \(R_1 = R_2 = R\) this is the hoop, \(\tfrac12MR^2\) and \(\tfrac14MR^2\) ✓; for \(R_1 = 0\) the disc ✓.

3. Where it stops. Take the cube of side \(a\) about its centre. Every moment is \(\tfrac16Ma^2\), so \(I_{11}+I_{22} = \tfrac13Ma^2 \ne \tfrac16Ma^2 = I_{33}\) — off by a factor two. The broken step is the very last one: \(\int\rho\,x_3^2\,\mathrm dV = \tfrac1{12}Ma^2 \ne 0\). The theorem never used flatness anywhere else, which is why it is an identity plus one hypothesis and not a delicate argument.

4. The inequality. The identity's right side is \(2\int\rho\,x_3^2\mathrm dV\ge0\), with \(\rho\ge0\); so \(I_{11}+I_{22}\ge I_{33}\) in any orthonormal frame, in particular the principal one, giving \(I_1+I_2\ge I_3\). Equality forces \(\int\rho\,x_3^2 = 0\), hence \(x_3 = 0\) for almost every point carrying mass: a lamina in the \(1\)–\(2\) plane. \(\;\blacksquare\)

Check. Dimensions: both sides \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) ✓. The slides' block: \(I_1+I_2-I_3 = 0.00136+0.00296-0.00416 = 1.6\times10^{-4}\), and \(2\int\rho z^2 = \tfrac{M}{6}c^2 = 0.1\times0.0016 = 1.6\times10^{-4}\) ✓. Limit: as \(c\to0\) at fixed \(M\) the block becomes a plate and the slack closes ✓.

P3 Two frames, one tensor medium

exercises: the inertia tensor of point masses, the transformation law verified two ways, diagonalising a 2×2 block

Three point masses sit in the plane \(z=0\): \(m_1 = 1\) kg at \((1,0,0)\), \(m_2 = 2\) kg at \((0,1,0)\), \(m_3 = 3\) kg at \((1,1,0)\), all coordinates in metres.

  1. Compute \(\mathsf I\) about the origin in this frame, as a \(3\times3\) array.
  2. Let \(\vec f_1,\vec f_2\) be \(\vec e_1,\vec e_2\) rotated by \(30^\circ\) about \(\vec e_3\), and \(\vec f_3 = \vec e_3\). Compute \(\mathsf I'\) twice: once from \(\mathsf I' = R\mathsf IR^{\mathsf T}\), and once by re-doing the sum with the masses' coordinates in the new frame. Compare all six independent components.
  3. Find the principal moments and the angle the smallest principal axis makes with \(\vec e_1\).
  4. Verify that \(\operatorname{tr}\mathsf I\) and \(\det\mathsf I\) are the same in both frames, and say why that was guaranteed.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a \(3\times3\) array of numbers in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\); part 3 is two numbers and an angle. Because everything lies in a plane, the array is block diagonal and only a \(2\times2\) problem is ever really being solved.

Rung 2

For point masses the integral is a sum: \(I_{ij} = \sum_\alpha m_\alpha(\lVert\vec r_\alpha\rVert^2\delta_{ij}-x_{\alpha i}x_{\alpha j})\). For part 2's second route, the new coordinates of a mass are \(x'_a = R_{ai}x_i\) — the same matrix that transforms the tensor, which is the point. For part 3, the principal angle of \(\begin{pmatrix}A&B\\B&C\end{pmatrix}\) satisfies \(\tan2\theta = 2B/(A-C)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(m_3\) at \((1,1,0)\) has \(\lVert\vec r\rVert^2 = 2\) and contributes \(3\begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&1&0\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix}\). Add the other two and you should find \(I_{11} = 5\) exactly.

Worked solution

Idea. The transformation law is a theorem about a definition, so recomputing from the definition in the new frame must reproduce it. Doing both and comparing is the checkpoint of this unit in miniature.

1. Contribution of a mass \(m\) at \(\vec r\) is \(m(\lVert\vec r\rVert^2\delta-\vec r\vec r^{\mathsf T})\):

  • \(m_1\): \(\lVert\vec r\rVert^2=1\), giving \(\mathrm{diag}(0,1,1)\);
  • \(m_2\): \(2\,\mathrm{diag}(1,0,1) = \mathrm{diag}(2,0,2)\);
  • \(m_3\): \(3\big[2\delta-\vec r\vec r^{\mathsf T}\big]\) with \(\vec r\vec r^{\mathsf T}\) having every entry 1 in the upper \(2\times2\) block: \(\begin{pmatrix}3&-3&0\\-3&3&0\\0&0&6\end{pmatrix}\).
\[ \mathsf I \;\hat=\; \begin{pmatrix}5&-3&0\\-3&4&0\\0&0&9\end{pmatrix}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}. \]

2. Route A, the law. With \(c=\cos30^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}2\), \(s = \tfrac12\) and \(R = \begin{pmatrix}c&s&0\\-s&c&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} I'_{11} &= c^2(5)+2cs(-3)+s^2(4) = \tfrac{15}4-\tfrac{3\sqrt3}2+1 = 2.151924,\\ I'_{12} &= -cs(5)+(c^2-s^2)(-3)+cs(4) = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}4-\tfrac32 = -1.933013,\\ I'_{22} &= s^2(5)+2cs(3)+c^2(4) = \tfrac54+\tfrac{3\sqrt3}2+3 = 6.848076, \end{aligned} \]

with \(I'_{13} = I'_{23} = 0\) (both \(I_{13}\) and \(I_{23}\) vanish and the rotation mixes only them) and \(I'_{33} = 9\).

Route B, the definition. The new coordinates are \(x' = cx+sy\), \(y' = -sx+cy\): \(m_1\) sits at \((0.866025,-0.5,0)\); \(m_2\) at \((0.5,0.866025,0)\); \(m_3\) at \((1.366025,0.366025,0)\). Then \(I'_{11} = \sum m(y'^2+z'^2) = 1(0.25)+2(0.75)+3(0.133975) = 0.25+1.5+0.401924 = 2.151924\) ✓, and \(I'_{12} = -\sum m\,x'y' = -[1(-0.433013)+2(0.433013)+3(0.5)] = -[-0.433013+0.866025+1.5] = -1.933013\) ✓. Continuing gives \(I'_{22} = 6.848076\) and \(I'_{33} = 9\) ✓. All six agree.

3. Principal moments. The \(2\times2\) block has trace 9 and determinant \(20-9 = 11\), so \(\lambda^2-9\lambda+11 = 0\) and

\[ \lambda = \tfrac12\big(9\pm\sqrt{37}\big) = 1.458619,\ 7.541381\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}, \]

together with \(9\) from the \(z\)-axis. So \(I_1 = 1.4586\), \(I_2 = 7.5414\), \(I_3 = 9\). For the smallest, \((5-\lambda)v_1 = 3v_2\) gives \(v_2/v_1 = (5-1.458619)/3 = 1.180460\), i.e. an angle \(\arctan(1.180460) = \mathbf{49.73^\circ}\) from \(\vec e_1\). (Equivalently \(\tan2\theta = 2(-3)/(5-4) = -6\).)

4. Trace: \(5+4+9 = 18\) and \(2.151924+6.848076+9 = 18\) ✓. Determinant: \(9\times11 = 99\) in both, since the \(2\times2\) determinant is \((2.151924)(6.848076)-(1.933013)^2 = 14.736-3.736 = 11.000\) ✓. Guaranteed because \(\det(R\mathsf IR^{\mathsf T}) = \det R\,\det\mathsf I\,\det R^{\mathsf T} = \det\mathsf I\) and the trace is invariant under an orthogonal congruence, which is the same as a similarity.

Check. Dimensions \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}\) ✓. Perpendicular axis: the body is a lamina, and \(I_1+I_2 = 1.458619+7.541381 = 9 = I_3\) exactly ✓ — P2, confirming both the eigenvalues and the arithmetic at once. Limit: setting \(m_3=0\) gives \(\mathrm{diag}(2,1,3)\) with the axes back along \(\vec e_1,\vec e_2\), so the \(49.73^\circ\) tilt is produced entirely by the third mass ✓.

What is, and is not, a tensor

P4 Convict a collection of nine numbers medium

exercises: testing the transformation law, saying precisely what a rule fails, distinguishing "computable" from "tensorial"

Both of the following are rules that assign nine real numbers to every orthonormal basis of \(\mathbb R^3\). Neither is a tensor. Convict each by explicit computation, and then say in one sentence what it fails.

  1. The diagonal rule. \(N_{ab} :=\) the array \(\mathrm{diag}(I_{11},I_{22},I_{33})\) built from the inertia tensor's diagonal entries in that basis. Use P3's body and its \(30^\circ\) rotation.
  2. The absolute-value rule. Fix a vector \(\vec v\). Let \(M_{ab} := |v_a|\,|v_b|\), the components being those of \(\vec v\) in that basis. Take \(\vec v = \vec e_1+\vec e_2\) and the \(90^\circ\) rotation about \(\vec e_3\).
  3. For each, identify which piece of information the rule discards or distorts, and state the general criterion a basis-indexed array must satisfy to come from a tensor.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A conviction is two numbers that should be equal and are not. Compute the array in the new basis by the rule, and compute what the transformation law predicts from the old array; exhibit one component where they differ.

Rung 2

The law predicts \(R\,(\text{old array})\,R^{\mathsf T}\). For rule 1 the old array is \(\mathrm{diag}(5,4,9)\), and the rule applied in the new basis needs \(I'_{11}\), which P3 already gives. For rule 2, first work out the components of \(\vec v\) in the rotated basis.

Rung 3

First line, rule 1: \(\big(R\,\mathrm{diag}(5,4,9)\,R^{\mathsf T}\big)_{11} = c^2\cdot5+s^2\cdot4\) with \(c^2=\tfrac34\), \(s^2=\tfrac14\). Compare with \(N'_{11} = I'_{11}\).

Worked solution

Idea. A tensor is determined by its components in one basis, so it can only have one array in any other. Two different answers for the same basis is a complete disproof, and one component suffices.

1. The diagonal rule. In \(\{\vec e_i\}\), \(N = \mathrm{diag}(5,4,9)\). The law predicts

\[ \big(RNR^{\mathsf T}\big)_{11} = c^2\cdot5+2cs\cdot0+s^2\cdot4 = \tfrac34(5)+\tfrac14(4) = \mathbf{4.75}. \]

The rule applied in \(\{\vec f_a\}\) returns \(N'_{11} = I'_{11} = \mathbf{2.151924}\) (P3). Not equal, so no tensor produces both.

2. The absolute-value rule. \(\vec v = (1,1,0)\) gives \(M = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\). With \(\vec f_1 = \vec e_2\), \(\vec f_2 = -\vec e_1\), so \(R = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\-1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}\), the law predicts

\[ RMR^{\mathsf T} = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}. \]

The rule applied in the new basis: \(\vec v\) has components \(R\vec v = (1,-1,0)\), so \(|v'| = (1,1,0)\) and \(M' = \begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\1&1&0\\0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\). The off-diagonal entries differ in sign: \(+1\) against \(-1\). Convicted.

3. What each fails. Rule 1 discards the off-diagonal entries, and those are exactly what a rotated diagonal entry needs: the law gives \(I'_{11} = c^2I_{11}+2csI_{12}+s^2I_{22}\), and the missing \(2csI_{12} = -2.598\) is precisely the gap \(2.152-4.75\). Rule 2 distorts signs: without the bars, \(v_av_b\) are the components of the tensor \(\vec v^\flat\otimes\vec v^\flat\), and indeed \(R\,\vec v\vec v^{\mathsf T}R^{\mathsf T}\) is the predicted array above; the bars erase the sign of \(v'_2 = -1\), and which signs get erased depends on the basis, so the rule cannot commute with a rotation that turns a positive component negative.

The criterion. A rule assigning an array to each basis comes from a tensor if and only if it is equivariant: its arrays in any two bases are related by the transformation law. The "only if" is the theorem of the slides; the "if" is immediate, since one may define the tensor by the array in one basis, and the law then forces its components in every other basis to be the rule's.

Check. Sanity: rule 1 does agree with the law whenever the rotation is a permutation of the axes (then no off-diagonal entry is needed), which is why the failure is invisible to anyone who only ever relabels axes — and why "the moments about \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\)" survives so long in elementary treatments. Rule 2 agrees whenever \(R\) has non-negative entries only. Both sanity checks confirm that the counterexamples had to use a genuinely mixing rotation.

P5 Proof: which traces survive, and the quotient theorem hard

exercises: distinguishing similarity from congruence, proving a converse, index bookkeeping under a general change of basis

Throughout, \(\vec f_a = S_{ia}\vec e_i\) with \(S\) invertible but not assumed orthogonal, and \(A = S^{-1}\).

  1. Prove that for a \((1,1)\)-tensor, \(T'^a{}_a = T^i{}_i\).
  2. Prove that for a \((0,2)\)-tensor, \(\sum_aT'_{aa}\) need not equal \(\sum_iT_{ii}\), by taking \(T_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\) and \(S = \mathrm{diag}(2,1,1)\). Then say exactly what extra structure makes "\(\operatorname{tr}\mathsf I\)" a legitimate invariant in the slides.
  3. The quotient theorem. Suppose a rule assigns to each basis an array \(N_{ij}\), and suppose that for every pair of vectors \(\vec u,\vec w\) the number \(N_{ij}u^iw^j\) is the same in every basis. Prove that the rule is the component rule of a \((0,2)\)-tensor.
  4. Use part 3 to prove that the inertia tensor is a tensor, starting from the physical fact that the rotational kinetic energy \(\tfrac12 I_{ij}\omega^i\omega^j\) is a number independent of the frame in which it is computed.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1, 3 and 4 are proofs; part 2 is one counterexample plus one sentence. Everything follows from the two transformation laws — vectors by \(A\), covectors by \(S^{\mathsf T}\) — and from \(SA = AS = \mathbb 1\).

Rung 2

Part 1: write the law for a \((1,1)\)-tensor and set \(a=b\), summing. You will meet \(\sum_aA_{ai}S_{ja}\), which is a matrix product in disguise. Part 3: you are given a scalar and must produce a multilinear map — so define one, using the array in a single chosen basis, and then show the hypothesis forces the rule's other arrays to be its components.

Rung 3

Part 1, first line: \(T'^a{}_b = A_{ai}S_{jb}T^i{}_j\), so \(T'^a{}_a = \big(\sum_aS_{ja}A_{ai}\big)T^i{}_j = (SA)_{ji}T^i{}_j\). Part 3, first line: fix the basis \(\{\vec e_i\}\) and define \(T(\vec u,\vec w) := N_{ij}u^iw^j\), which is bilinear because the right side is.

Worked solution

Idea. A contraction survives a change of basis exactly when the two matrices it meets are inverse to one another. Upper and lower indices bring \(A\) and \(S\), so one of each cancels; two lower indices bring \(S\) twice, and \(S^{\mathsf T}S\ne\mathbb 1\) in general.

1. The law is \(T'^a{}_b = A_{ai}S_{jb}T^i{}_j\). Put \(b=a\) and sum:

\[ T'^a{}_a = \Big(\sum_a S_{ja}A_{ai}\Big)T^i{}_j = (SA)_{ji}\,T^i{}_j = \delta_{ji}T^i{}_j = T^i{}_i . \;\blacksquare \]

2. For a \((0,2)\)-tensor, \(T' = S^{\mathsf T}TS\). With \(T=\mathbb 1\) and \(S = \mathrm{diag}(2,1,1)\), \(T' = S^{\mathsf T}S = \mathrm{diag}(4,1,1)\), whose diagonal sum is \(\mathbf 6\), against \(\mathbf 3\) before. So \(\sum_iT_{ii}\) is not an invariant.

What rescues \(\operatorname{tr}\mathsf I\) in the slides is that only orthonormal bases are ever used, i.e. \(S\) is restricted to orthogonal matrices, for which \(S^{\mathsf T}S=\mathbb 1\) and congruence coincides with similarity. Said structurally: the Euclidean inner product is being used, silently, to raise one index — \(\operatorname{tr}\mathsf I\) really means \(\delta^{ij}I_{ij}\), and \(\delta^{ij}\) is the inverse metric, a genuine tensor. In unit 32 the same contraction is written \(g^{\mu\nu}T_{\mu\nu}\) and the metric is not the identity.

3. The quotient theorem. Fix one basis \(\{\vec e_i\}\), with the rule's array \(N_{ij}\) there. Define \(T:\mathbb R^3\times\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R\) by \(T(\vec u,\vec w) := N_{ij}u^iw^j\), the components being taken in that basis. \(T\) is bilinear, since the right side is linear in each of \(u^i\) and \(w^j\) separately — so \(T\) is a \((0,2)\)-tensor, and its components in \(\{\vec e_i\}\) are \(T(\vec e_i,\vec e_j) = N_{ij}\).

Now take any other basis \(\{\vec f_a\}\), and write \(N'_{ab}\) for the rule's array there and \(u'^a\) for the new components of \(\vec u\). The hypothesis says \(N'_{ab}u'^aw'^b = N_{ij}u^iw^j = T(\vec u,\vec w)\) for every \(\vec u,\vec w\). Take \(\vec u = \vec f_a\) and \(\vec w = \vec f_b\), whose new components are \(\delta\)'s: the left side collapses to \(N'_{ab}\), so \(N'_{ab} = T(\vec f_a,\vec f_b) = T'_{ab}\). The rule's array in every basis is therefore \(T\)'s component array. \(\;\blacksquare\)

4. The rotational kinetic energy is defined by an integral over the body, \(T_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac12\int\rho\lVert\vec\omega\times\vec r\rVert^2\mathrm dV\), with no basis anywhere in the definition; so its value is frame-independent, and equals \(\tfrac12I_{ij}\omega^i\omega^j\) in any frame. Write \(Q(\vec u) := I_{ij}u^iu^j\), twice that energy, so \(Q\) is frame-independent too. Polarisation extends this from the quadratic form to the bilinear form: \(I_{ij}u^iw^j = \tfrac12\big[Q(\vec u+\vec w)-Q(\vec u)-Q(\vec w)\big]\) is a difference of frame-independent numbers, hence frame-independent, for every \(\vec u,\vec w\). Part 3 then applies verbatim, and \(\mathsf I\) is a \((0,2)\)-tensor. \(\;\blacksquare\)

Check. Consistency with P4: the diagonal rule fails the hypothesis of part 3 — \(N_{ij}u^iw^j\) for \(N = \mathrm{diag}(5,4,9)\) and \(\vec u=\vec w=\vec f_1\) gives \(4.75\) in the old basis and \(2.152\) in the new, so the quotient theorem is not being contradicted ✓. Special case of part 2: for orthogonal \(S\), \(S^{\mathsf T}S = \mathbb 1\) and the two traces agree, as they must ✓. Dimensional check on part 4: \(I_{ij}\omega^i\omega^j\) is \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}} = \mathrm J\) in every frame ✓.

uses \(|\det C| = 1\) (so \(\mathrm dV\) is unchanged), \(C(B) = B\) and \(\rho(C\vec s)=\rho(\vec s)\) (the symmetry hypothesis), and \(C\vec u\cdot C\vec s = \vec u\cdot\vec s\), \(\lVert C\vec s\rVert = \lVert\vec s\rVert\) (orthogonality). So \(M = \mu\,\delta\), and \(\mu = I_{11} = \tfrac{M}{12}(a^2+a^2) = \tfrac16Ma^2\) from the block formula. First line: \(\lVert\vec L\rVert^2-2I_1T = \sum_kI_k^2\omega_k^2-I_1\sum_kI_k\omega_k^2 = \sum_kI_k(I_k-I_1)\omega_k^2\), and the \(k=1\) term vanishes identically.

Worked solution

Idea. The two conserved quantities are two ellipsoids; the right combination is one whose level sets near the axis are small ellipses in the transverse variables alone. Then conservation traps the trajectory with no approximation at all.

1. Write \(\vec\omega = (\Omega+v_1,v_2,v_3)\). The linearised Euler system is \(\dot v_1 = 0\), \(\dot v_2 = \tfrac{(I_3-I_1)\Omega}{I_2}v_3\), \(\dot v_3 = \tfrac{(I_1-I_2)\Omega}{I_3}v_2\). Eigenvalues of a traceless \(2\times2\) with positive determinant are \(\pm i\lambda_1\). A centre: unit 02 warned that linearisation decides nothing here.

2. \(F\) is a fixed linear combination of two conserved quantities, hence conserved. Expanding,

\[ F = \sum_kI_k^2\omega_k^2-I_1\sum_kI_k\omega_k^2 = \sum_kI_k(I_k-I_1)\omega_k^2 = I_2(I_2-I_1)\omega_2^2+I_3(I_3-I_1)\omega_3^2, \]

the \(k=1\) term vanishing identically. Both coefficients are strictly positive because \(I_1\lt I_2\lt I_3\), so \(F\ge c_1(\omega_2^2+\omega_3^2)\) with \(c_1 := \min\{I_2(I_2-I_1),\,I_3(I_3-I_1)\}\gt0\), and also \(F\le c_2(\omega_2^2+\omega_3^2)\) with \(c_2\) the corresponding maximum.

Given \(\varepsilon\gt0\), choose \(\delta := \varepsilon\sqrt{c_1/c_2}\). If \(\omega_2(0)^2+\omega_3(0)^2\lt\delta^2\) then \(F\lt c_2\delta^2 = c_1\varepsilon^2\) initially, hence for all time, hence \(\omega_2(t)^2+\omega_3(t)^2\lt\varepsilon^2\) for all \(t\). \(\;\blacksquare\)

3. Take \(G := 2I_3T-\lVert\vec L\rVert^2 = \sum_kI_k(I_3-I_k)\omega_k^2 = I_1(I_3-I_1)\omega_1^2+I_2(I_3-I_2)\omega_2^2\), again conserved, again positive definite in the two transverse variables, and the same argument applies at \(\vec u_3\).

At \(\vec u_2\), the analogous \(\lVert\vec L\rVert^2-2I_2T = \sum_kI_k(I_k-I_2)\omega_k^2 = I_1(I_1-I_2)\omega_1^2+I_3(I_3-I_2)\omega_3^2\) has coefficients of opposite signs: its level sets near the axis are hyperbolas, not ellipses, and it traps nothing. Likewise \(2I_2T-\lVert\vec L\rVert^2\) is its negative. This had to happen — the slides' intermediate-axis theorem makes that axis genuinely unstable, and a Lyapunov function would prove otherwise.

4. What is proved is that the transverse components stay small: for every \(\varepsilon\) there is \(\delta\) with \(\omega_2^2+\omega_3^2\lt\varepsilon^2\) for all time. It is not proved that \(\vec\omega(t)\) stays near the particular vector \(\Omega\vec u_1\): nothing above pins \(\omega_1\) to \(\Omega\). The sharpest true statement is that the axis \(\mathbb R\vec u_1\) is stable, with \(\omega_1\) controlled only indirectly, through \(I_1\omega_1^2 = 2T-I_2\omega_2^2-I_3\omega_3^2\), which fixes it to within \(O(\varepsilon^2)\) of its initial value. That is enough: it says the spin axis does not wander away from the body's long axis, which is the physical question.

Check. Dimensions: \(F\) is \(\mathrm{kg^2m^4s^{-2}}\), the same as \(\lVert\vec L\rVert^2\) ✓. Limits: as \(I_2\to I_1\), \(c_1\to0\) and the bound degenerates — correct, since a symmetric top's transverse components are then unconstrained by \(F\), and indeed \(\vec\omega\) precesses freely on a cone (P9) without the axis being unstable ✓. Consistency: the strength of the argument is that it uses no approximation, so it holds for large perturbations too — which the picture of nested closed polhodes on the slides confirms ✓.

Worked solution

Idea. Euler angles are three rotations about three different axes, so \(\vec\omega\) is a sum of three vectors — each easy once written in the body frame. The degeneracy appears when two of the three axes coincide, and no choice of angles can avoid it because the obstruction is topological, not algebraic.

1. In body components: the \(\dot\psi\) rotation is about \((0,0,1)\); the \(\dot\theta\) rotation is about the line of nodes, which is the body \(1\)-axis rotated back by \(\psi\), i.e. \((\cos\psi,-\sin\psi,0)\); the \(\dot\phi\) rotation is about the space \(z\)-axis, whose body components are \((\sin\theta\sin\psi,\ \sin\theta\cos\psi,\ \cos\theta)\). Adding \(\dot\phi\), \(\dot\theta\), \(\dot\psi\) times these three gives the three displayed lines.

2. The matrix is

\[ J = \begin{pmatrix}\sin\theta\sin\psi & \cos\psi & 0\\ \sin\theta\cos\psi & -\sin\psi & 0\\ \cos\theta & 0 & 1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det J = -\sin\theta\,(\sin^2\psi+\cos^2\psi) = -\sin\theta . \]

It vanishes at \(\theta = 0\) and \(\theta = \pi\). At \(\theta = 0\) the first and third columns both read \((0,0,1)^{\mathsf T}\), so \((\dot\phi,\dot\theta,\dot\psi) = (1,0,-1)\) gives \(\vec\omega = 0\): changing \(\phi\) and \(\psi\) oppositely produces no rotation at all. Physically, the precession and spin axes have become the same line, one degree of freedom has become invisible, and the angular velocity cannot be solved for from the angle rates — gimbal lock.

3. \(\omega_1^2+\omega_2^2 = (\dot\phi\sin\theta\sin\psi+\dot\theta\cos\psi)^2+(\dot\phi\sin\theta\cos\psi-\dot\theta\sin\psi)^2\). The cross terms are \(+2\dot\phi\dot\theta\sin\theta\sin\psi\cos\psi\) and \(-2\dot\phi\dot\theta\sin\theta\cos\psi\sin\psi\), which cancel; the squares leave \(\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta(\sin^2\psi+\cos^2\psi)+\dot\theta^2 = \dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta+\dot\theta^2\) ✓. Hence for \(I_1=I_2\),

\[ T = \tfrac12I_1(\omega_1^2+\omega_2^2)+\tfrac12I_3\omega_3^2 = \tfrac12I_1\big(\dot\theta^2+\dot\phi^2\sin^2\theta\big)+\tfrac12I_3\big(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta\big)^2 , \]

which is P12's Lagrangian kinetic term.

4. \(SO(3)\subset\mathbb R^9\) is bounded — every entry of an orthogonal matrix has modulus at most 1 — and closed, being the preimage of \((\mathbb 1,1)\) under the continuous map \(R\mapsto(R^{\mathsf T}R,\det R)\). By Heine–Borel it is compact. Suppose \(h:SO(3)\to U\) were a homeomorphism onto an open \(U\subseteq\mathbb R^3\). Then \(U\) is compact, hence closed in \(\mathbb R^3\); being open and closed in the connected space \(\mathbb R^3\), it is \(\varnothing\) or \(\mathbb R^3\); it is not empty, and \(\mathbb R^3\) is not compact. Contradiction. \(\;\blacksquare\)
