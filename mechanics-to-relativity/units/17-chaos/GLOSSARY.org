The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: the Hénon–Heiles system
\(H = \tfrac12(p_x^2+p_y^2) + \tfrac12(x^2+y^2) + x^2y - \tfrac13y^3\), bounded
for \(0 < E < 1/6\). Its section \(\{x = 0,\ \dot x > 0\}\) is drawn at
\(E = 1/100,\ 1/12,\ 1/8,\ 1/6\), where the measured chaotic fraction of the
accessible area is \(0\%,\ 0.1\%,\ 52\%,\ 96\%\). At \(E = 1/8\) the orbit
through \((y,p_y) = (-0.20, 0)\) has \(\lambda_1 = 0.044\), measured over
\(T = 10^6\), and the orbit through \((0.30, 0)\) has \(\lambda_1 = 0\), its
separation growing linearly with a fitted slope of \(0.997\).

* Overview

- Hénon–Heiles system :: \(H = \tfrac12(p_x^2+p_y^2) + \tfrac12(x^2+y^2) +
  x^2y - \tfrac13y^3\): two degrees of freedom, written down by Hénon and
  Heiles in 1964 as the cubic truncation of an axially symmetric galactic
  potential. Dimensionless; restoring units, energies are measured in
  \(m\omega^2a^2\) and rates in \(\omega\). The only known integral is \(H\).
  /Slide: Determinism was the easy part./

* What it means to solve a system

- in involution :: Two functions on phase space with \(\{F,G\} = 0\). For
  integrals of \(H\) this says their flows commute, which is what forces the
  geometry of the level set. /Slide: Integrals in involution./
- completely integrable :: A system with \(n\) degrees of freedom admitting
  \(n\) functions \(F_1 = H, \dots, F_n\), pairwise in involution, with
  linearly independent differentials at every point of an open set \(U\).
  A property of a system /and a domain/: every system is integrable on a small
  enough flow box. /Slide: Complete integrability, defined./
- Liouville–Arnold theorem :: If \(F_1 = H,\dots,F_n\) are in involution with
  independent differentials, each compact connected common level set is
  diffeomorphic to \(\mathbb T^n\), the flow on it is linear in suitable
  angles, and nearby there are canonical coordinates \((\vec I,\vec\theta)\)
  with \(H = H(\vec I)\). Liouville 1855 (the quadratures), Arnold 1963 (the
  topology). /Slide: Liouville and Arnold: the level set is a torus./
- invariant torus :: A compact connected common level set of \(n\) integrals in
  involution, carrying the straight-line flow \(\dot{\vec\theta} =
  \vec\omega\). Called /resonant/ when \(\vec k\cdot\vec\omega = 0\) for some
  nonzero integer vector \(\vec k\), and /non-resonant/ otherwise, in which
  case every orbit on it is dense.
- action–angle variables :: The canonical coordinates \((\vec I,\vec\theta)\)
  of the Liouville–Arnold theorem, with \(I_i = \frac1{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma_i}
  \vec p\cdot\mathrm d\vec q\) over a generating loop and \(\omega_i =
  \partial H/\partial I_i\). \(I\) has the dimensions of an action, \(\theta\)
  is an angle, so \(\omega\) is a frequency.

* Looking at a flow through a plane

- Poincaré section :: A hypersurface \(\Sigma\) transverse to a flow, used to
  replace the flow by the map that sends each point of \(\Sigma\) to its next
  crossing. For a two-degree-of-freedom system, \(\Sigma\) inside a fixed
  energy surface is two-dimensional and can be drawn.
  /Slide: The section and its return map./
- transverse (transversality) :: The condition that the vector field is
  nowhere tangent to \(\Sigma\). It makes the return time smooth, by the
  implicit function theorem, and it is what the sign condition \(\dot x > 0\)
  enforces for the running example.
- return map :: \(P(z) = \varphi_{\tau(z)}(z)\), where \(\tau(z)\) is the first
  positive time at which the orbit through \(z\in\Sigma\) meets \(\Sigma\)
  again. Also called the first-return or Poincaré map.
- area-preserving map :: A map of a plane region with \(\det DP \equiv 1\). The
  return map of a two-degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian flow on an energy surface
  is one; the proof uses only Liouville's theorem and the chain rule.
  Consequences: no attractors, recurrence, and fixed points that are elliptic
  or hyperbolic and never spiral. /Slide: The return map preserves area./
- island chain :: \(q\) closed curves visited in turn by the return map,
  surrounding \(q\) elliptic fixed points of \(P^q\). The remnant of a
  resonant torus, with \(q\) hyperbolic points between the islands.
  /Slide: What integrability looks like on a section./
- chaotic sea :: A region of the section in which the crossings of a /single/
  orbit spread over an area rather than lying on a curve. Its presence on an
  open set refutes the existence of a second smooth integral there; the
  absence of one proves nothing.

* Measuring sensitivity

- variational equation :: \(\dot M = DX(\varphi_t(z_0))\,M\), \(M(0) = I\),
  satisfied by \(M(t) = D\varphi_t(z_0)\). Unlike unit 02's linearisation about
  an equilibrium, the coefficient matrix depends on \(t\), so there is no
  eigenvalue to read off. /Slide: The linearised flow./
- Lyapunov exponent :: \(\lambda(z_0,\delta_0) = \limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac1t
  \ln\lVert D\varphi_t(z_0)\delta_0\rVert\). Independent of the norm, constant
  along an orbit, and taking at most \(2n\) values. For a Hamiltonian system
  the spectrum is symmetric about zero and sums to zero, so a
  two-degree-of-freedom system has the single spectrum
  \(\{\lambda_1, 0, 0, -\lambda_1\}\). /Slide: The Lyapunov exponent./
- chaotic :: Said of an orbit with \(\lambda_1 > 0\). Neither randomness nor
  non-uniqueness nor unboundedness: the solution is unique, deterministic and
  confined, and what grows exponentially is the uncertainty in which orbit
  it is.
- Benettin's algorithm :: Integrate the variational equation for a fixed
  interval \(\Delta\), record the growth factor \(a_k\), renormalise the vector
  to unit length, repeat; then \(\lambda_1 \approx \frac1{N\Delta}\sum_k \ln
  a_k\). Exactly equal to the definition by linearity, and necessary in
  practice because \(\lVert\delta\rVert\) would otherwise overflow.
  /Slide: Computing it without overflowing./
- Lyapunov time :: \(t_\lambda = 1/\lambda_1\), the time in which an error
  grows by a factor \(e\). Twenty-three time units for the running example at
  \(E = 1/8\); about five million years for the inner solar system.
  /Slide: What a positive exponent does not say./

* Perturbing an integrable system

- small denominators :: The factors \(\vec k\cdot\vec\omega\) appearing in the
  denominator of the first-order generating function that would remove the
  angle dependence of a perturbation. Zero on a resonant torus and arbitrarily
  small near one, which is why the classical perturbation series diverges.
  /Slide: The small denominators./
- Diophantine condition :: \(|\vec k\cdot\vec\omega| \ge \gamma\lVert\vec
  k\rVert^{-\tau}\) for every nonzero integer vector \(\vec k\): a
  quantitative statement that \(\vec\omega\) stays away from every resonance.
  For \(\tau > 1\) the frequencies failing it can be covered by intervals of
  total length \(O(\gamma)\), so almost all frequencies satisfy it. The golden
  ratio satisfies it best, by Hurwitz's theorem.
  /Slide: Rational frequencies are the enemy, and there are few of them./
- KAM theorem :: For an analytic \(H_0(\vec I) + \varepsilon H_1(\vec
  I,\vec\theta)\) with \(H_0\) non-degenerate and \(\varepsilon\) small, every
  torus whose frequencies are \((\gamma,\tau)\)-Diophantine with \(\gamma
  \gtrsim \sqrt\varepsilon\) survives as a slightly deformed invariant torus,
  and the survivors fill all but a set of measure \(O(\sqrt\varepsilon)\).
  Kolmogorov 1954, Arnold 1963, Moser 1962; /cited/, not proved here.
  /Slide: KAM, stated honestly./
- non-degenerate :: Of \(H_0\): \(\det(\partial^2 H_0/\partial I_i\partial
  I_j)\ne 0\), so that the frequencies genuinely vary from torus to torus.
  The isotropic oscillator fails it, which is why the standard KAM statement
  does not apply to the low-energy limit of the running example as it stands.
- Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem :: An area-preserving twist map of an annulus that
  rotates the two boundary circles in opposite senses has at least two fixed
  points. Applied to \(P^q\), it says a resonant invariant circle is replaced
  by an even number of periodic points, alternately elliptic and hyperbolic —
  an island chain. Poincaré 1912, Birkhoff 1913; /cited/.
  /Slide: What becomes of the resonant tori./
- homoclinic point :: A point other than \(z^*\) lying in both the stable and
  the unstable set of a hyperbolic fixed point \(z^*\). Its whole orbit is
  homoclinic too, so one such point forces infinitely many.
  /Slide: The tangle Poincaré found./
- transversal homoclinic point :: A homoclinic point at which the stable and
  unstable sets cross transversally. By Smale's theorem it forces a horseshoe:
  an invariant Cantor set carrying the shift on two symbols, with infinitely
  many periodic orbits and a positive Lyapunov exponent. This is the
  configuration Poincaré had wrongly excluded in the prize memoir.
- Melnikov function :: \(M(t_0) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\{H_0,H_1\}(z_0(t),
  t+t_0)\,\mathrm dt\), the first-order gap between the stable and unstable
  sets along an unperturbed homoclinic loop. A simple zero implies a
  transversal homoclinic point for small \(\varepsilon\). Melnikov 1963;
  /cited/.
- standard map :: \(p_{n+1} = p_n + K\sin\theta_n\), \(\theta_{n+1} = \theta_n
  + p_{n+1}\bmod 2\pi\): the kicked rotor, the simplest area-preserving twist
  map with one parameter. Its last invariant circle, the one with golden
  rotation number, breaks at \(K_c = 0.971635406\) (Greene).
  /Slide: The kicked rotor and the standard map./
- Chirikov's overlap criterion :: Chaos becomes global when neighbouring
  resonance bands touch: \(2\Delta p \ge \delta p\). For the standard map the
  primary half-width is \(2\sqrt K\) and the spacing \(2\pi\), giving
  \(K \ge \pi^2/4 = 2.4674\) — high by a factor \(2.54\) against Greene's
  \(0.9716\), because higher-order resonances and separatrix layers help bridge
  the gap.
  /Slide: Chirikov: when do resonances overlap?/
- cantorus :: What an invariant circle becomes just past its critical
  parameter: an invariant Cantor set with the same rotation number, through
  whose gaps orbits leak at a rate vanishing like \((K - K_c)^{3.01}\). It is
  why a finite transport experiment always reports a threshold that is too
  high. Aubry and Mather, 1982; /cited/.
  /Slide: The last torus, numerically./

* Out in the solar system

- mean-motion resonance :: A commensurability \(p\,T_a = q\,T_{\mathrm J}\)
  between two orbital periods. By Kepler's third law it sits at
  \(a = a_{\mathrm J}(q/p)^{2/3}\). /Slide: The Kirkwood gaps./
- Kirkwood gaps :: The depleted bands in the asteroid belt at the strong
  mean-motion resonances with Jupiter — \(2.065\), \(2.502\), \(2.825\),
  \(2.958\) and \(3.279\) AU for \(4\!:\!1\), \(3\!:\!1\), \(5\!:\!2\),
  \(7\!:\!3\) and \(2\!:\!1\). Found by Kirkwood in 1866; explained by the
  chaotic growth of eccentricity at the resonance, computed by Wisdom in 1983.
  A resonance need not clear an orbit: the \(3\!:\!2\) and \(1\!:\!1\)
  resonances hold the Hilda family and the Trojans instead.
