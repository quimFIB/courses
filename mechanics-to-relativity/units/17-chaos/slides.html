Integrability and chaos: determinism without predictability

Solutions exist, are unique, and depend continuously on the data — and are still useless for prediction after a few dozen orbits. Both halves are theorems, and they do not conflict.

needs 14, 02, 01~2½ h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./mr problems 17

Unit 01 proved that a Lipschitz force determines the future completely, and threw in continuous dependence on the initial data for free. It is tempting to read that as a promise about prediction. It is not, and this unit says exactly why: the continuous-dependence bound is $\lVert\delta(t)\rVert\le\lVert\delta(0)\rVert e^{Lt}$, and there is no $t$ in front of the $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ quantifier. Continuity in the initial data is not uniform in $t$, and that single word is the whole gap between determinism and predictability.

The unit then does the positive work. It says what the rare solvable systems have in common (Liouville–Arnold: $n$ integrals in involution, and the bounded motion lies on tori), it builds the two instruments that decide which case you are in for a system you cannot solve — the Poincaré section and the Lyapunov exponent — and it states what survives a small perturbation of an integrable system, which is the KAM theorem.

This is a branch unit: nothing later in the course needs it. It is here because a course that goes from Newton to Einstein without saying that almost nothing is solvable leaves the learner with a badly wrong picture of what mechanics is.

17 · overview

Determinism was the easy part

the claim to be dismantled

"The equations determine the future, so the future is predictable." The first clause is unit 01's theorem. The second does not follow, and this unit exhibits a system where it is false — with a number attached to how false.

the two instruments

1 The Poincaré section: cut the flow with a plane and look at where it lands. An integrable system draws curves; a chaotic one fills an area. 2 The Lyapunov exponent: the asymptotic rate at which neighbouring orbits separate. Positive means chaos, and it is a number you can quote.

the running example

The Hénon–Heiles system, $H = \tfrac12(p_x^2+p_y^2) + \tfrac12(x^2+y^2) + x^2y - \tfrac13y^3$: two degrees of freedom, one known integral, and a free parameter $E$. At $E = 1/100$ its section is a family of smooth closed curves; by $E = 1/8$ half of it is a chaotic sea; at $E = 1/6$ only one pair of islands is left. We shall measure that, not admire it.

getting around

A dotted underline is a glossary term: click it, or press G. N opens the notes, where the longer proofs live. A solid underline, like the running example, shows it on hover.

Why Hénon–Heiles and not the three-body problem. The three-body problem is where the subject started and section 1 tells that story, but it has nine degrees of freedom before reduction and nothing about it can be drawn. Hénon–Heiles has two, which is the smallest number for which a bounded Hamiltonian flow can be chaotic, and two is exactly the number for which the Poincaré section is a two-dimensional picture on a page. Michel Hénon and Carl Heiles wrote it down in 1964 (Astronomical Journal 69, 73) for a real question: stars in an axially symmetric galaxy have two obvious integrals, the energy and the angular momentum about the axis, and the observed velocity distributions looked as though there had to be a third. Their cubic potential is not any particular galaxy — it is the simplest truncation with the right symmetry — and they ran it to see whether a third integral existed. The answer turned out to depend on the energy, which nobody expected.

Dimensions. Hénon–Heiles as written is dimensionless. Restoring units, the Hamiltonian is $\tfrac1{2m}(p_x^2+p_y^2) + \tfrac12 m\omega^2(x^2+y^2) + \frac{m\omega^2}{a}(x^2y - \tfrac13y^3)$; measuring lengths in $a$, times in $\omega^{-1}$ and energies in $m\omega^2a^2$ returns the form above. So the numbers in this deck read as follows: $E = 1/8$ means an energy of $m\omega^2a^2/8$, and a Lyapunov exponent of $0.044$ means $0.044\,\omega$, an inverse time. Every result in the unit will be checked against that dictionary.

The shape of the argument. Sections 1 and 2 are about what is true of a system exactly; sections 3 and 4 are about what survives when it is perturbed. The hinge between them is that the tori of section 1 are not all equally fragile: which ones survive is decided by a number-theoretic property of their frequencies, which is the strangest fact in classical mechanics and is proved, not asserted, in the sources section 4 cites.

A prize that cost more than it paid

In 1885 King Oscar II of Sweden offered a prize for the stability of the solar system. Poincaré entered with a memoir on the restricted three-body problem and won: 2500 kronor and a gold medal.

While setting the memoir for Acta Mathematica, the editor Edvard Phragmén asked about a step. Poincaré had assumed that the stable and unstable curves emerging from an unstable periodic orbit, when they met again, coincided. They do not: they cross, and having crossed once they must cross infinitely often. His conclusion of stability was wrong, and the corrected memoir — 270 pages against 158 — says the opposite.

Poincaré paid 3585 kronor to recall and destroy the printed issue. The error cost him more than the prize, and it is the origin of this subject. (cited: Barrow-Green, Poincaré and the Three Body Problem, 1997, ch. 3–5.)

what was already known

Bruns, 1887: the three-body problem has no integral algebraic in the Cartesian positions and momenta beyond the classical ten. Poincaré, 1890: the restricted problem has no integral, other than $H$, depending analytically on the mass ratio near zero. Both cited.

the lesson to carry

"No formula" is not a confession of ignorance. For most systems it is a theorem. The question then changes from what is the solution to what is true of the solutions, which is unit 02's question asked one dimension up.

The competition, precisely. Four questions were set; Poincaré answered a restricted version of the first. Weierstrass, Mittag-Leffler and Hermite judged. The prize was announced in January 1889 on Oscar's sixtieth birthday. Phragmén's query reached Poincaré in July 1889; by December Poincaré had written to Mittag-Leffler admitting the error. Volume 13 of Acta containing the flawed memoir had already been distributed to subscribers, and Mittag-Leffler spent some months quietly retrieving copies. A handful survive.

What the error became. The crossing Poincaré was forced to admit is a homoclinic point, and section 4 draws the tangle it forces. His own sentence about it is the most quoted in the subject: "one is struck by the complexity of this figure, which I shall not even attempt to draw". He is not being lazy — the figure cannot be drawn, and section 2's numerical section is the modern substitute.

Bruns and Poincaré, on what they exclude. Bruns' theorem is a statement about algebraic integrals in one particular coordinate system, and is therefore weaker than it sounds: it does not forbid an integral that is analytic but not algebraic. Poincaré's is the sharper result — it forbids analytic integrals of the perturbed system that reduce smoothly to integrals of the unperturbed one, and its proof is exactly the small-denominator computation of section 4. Neither theorem says the three-body problem is chaotic; they say the classical route to solving it is closed.

Integrals in involution

conserved, and commuting

$F$ is a first integral of $H$ if $\{F,H\} = 0$, which by unit 14 is exactly $\dot F = 0$ along every orbit. Two functions are in involution if $\{F,G\} = 0$.

why involution and not merely independence

Each $F_i$ generates its own flow. $\{F_i,F_j\} = 0$ says those flows commute, so together they define an action of $\mathbb R^n$ on the level set — and it is that action, not the counting of integrals, that forces the geometry.

Independence here means: the differentials $\mathrm dF_1,\dots,\mathrm dF_n$ are linearly independent at each point of the level set, so the level set is an $n$-dimensional surface (unit 04's implicit function theorem).

Kepler, counted

Unit 14 found seven integrals for $H = p^2/2\mu - k/r$: $H$, three components of $\vec L$, three of $\vec A$ — five of them independent. But $n = 3$, so at most three can be in involution. Unit 14's brackets show $\{L_i,L_j\} = \epsilon_{ijk}L_k \ne 0$: the three components of $\vec L$ are not in involution with each other.

A commuting triple is $H$, $L_3$, $\lVert\vec L\rVert^2$. Check: $\{H,\cdot\} = 0$ as both are conserved; $\{L_3, \lVert\vec L\rVert^2\} = \sum_i 2L_i\{L_3,L_i\} = 2\sum_i L_i\,\epsilon_{3ik}L_k = 0$, since $\epsilon_{3ik}$ is antisymmetric in $i,k$ while $L_iL_k$ is symmetric.

seven is not better than three

More integrals than $n$ makes a system superintegrable: the orbits close, and lie on tori of dimension below $n$. Kepler's ellipses are one-dimensional, not three.

Full proof that the bracket of two integrals is an integral. If $\{F,H\} = \{G,H\} = 0$ then the Jacobi identity of unit 14, $\{\{F,G\},H\} + \{\{G,H\},F\} + \{\{H,F\},G\} = 0$, has its last two terms zero, so $\{\{F,G\},H\} = 0$. The conserved quantities form a Lie algebra under the bracket — that was unit 14's statement, and the reason involution is the right hypothesis is that a set of integrals in involution is an abelian subalgebra of it.

Why commuting flows matter. Write $\varphi^i_{t}$ for the flow of $F_i$. Unit 14's bracket identity gives $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(F_j\circ\varphi^i_t) = \{F_j, F_i\} = 0$, so each flow preserves every $F_j$ and therefore maps a common level set to itself.

Full proof that the flows commute. Let $\{F,G\} = 0$, fix $t$, and write $\psi = \varphi^G_t$, $M = D\psi$. Unit 14 proved $M^{\!\top}JM = J$; transposing and inverting gives $MJM^{\!\top} = J$ as well. The chain rule applied to $F = (F\circ\psi^{-1})\circ\psi$ gives $\nabla F(z) = M^{\!\top}\nabla(F\circ\psi^{-1})(\psi z)$, so $M X_F(z) = MJM^{\!\top}\nabla(F\circ\psi^{-1})(\psi z) = X_{F\circ\psi^{-1}}(\psi z)$. But $F\circ\psi^{-1} = F\circ\varphi^G_{-t} = F$, because $F$ is constant along $G$'s flow. So the curve $s\mapsto\psi(\varphi^F_s z)$ has velocity $M X_F(\varphi^F_s z) = X_F(\psi\varphi^F_s z)$: it solves $\dot z = X_F$ and starts at $\psi z$. By the uniqueness of unit 01 it is $s\mapsto\varphi^F_s(\psi z)$, that is, $\varphi^G_t\circ\varphi^F_s = \varphi^F_s\circ\varphi^G_t$. $\square$

The upshot is a map $\mathbb R^n\times M_c \to M_c$, $(t_1,\dots,t_n)\cdot z = \varphi^1_{t_1}\cdots\varphi^n_{t_n}(z)$, which is the object the next theorem is really about.

The bound "at most $n$ in involution". At a point where $k$ independent integrals are in involution, their differentials span a $k$-dimensional subspace on which the symplectic form vanishes; such a subspace has dimension at most $n$ in a $2n$-dimensional symplectic vector space. The linear-algebra fact — an isotropic subspace has dimension at most half — is unit 14's $M^{\!\top}JM = J$ machinery in disguise, and is problem P3.

Complete integrability, defined

completely integrable

A Hamiltonian system with $n$ degrees of freedom is completely integrable on an open set $U\subset\mathbb R^{2n}$ if there are $n$ functions $F_1 = H, F_2,\dots,F_n\in C^\infty(U)$ with $$\{F_i,F_j\} = 0 \quad\text{for all } i,j, \qquad \mathrm dF_1,\dots,\mathrm dF_n \ \text{linearly independent at every point of } U.$$

integrable

Any $n = 1$ system: $H$ alone. Central forces (unit 08): $H, L_3, \lVert\vec L\rVert^2$. $n$ uncoupled oscillators: each $E_i$. The Toda lattice. The free rigid body (unit 16).

not, provably

The restricted three-body problem (Poincaré). The double pendulum. The Sitnikov problem. Proving non-integrability is hard; the modern tool is Morales-Ramis theory, cited.

not, apparently

Hénon–Heiles. No second integral is known and none is believed to exist for general $E$, but the numerical evidence of section 2 is evidence, not proof.

the honest statement of the definition's weakness

"Integrable" is not a property of a system alone but of a system and an open set. Every Hamiltonian system is locally integrable away from equilibria — for $n = 2$ the notes prove it, for general $n$ it is cited. The content is always global: integrals defined on a large region, not on a flow box.

Why $n = 1$ is always integrable. One degree of freedom, one integral $H$, and the orbits are the level curves of $H$ in the plane — which is unit 02's phase portrait and unit 00's reduction to quadrature. Everything in this unit is therefore about $n\ge2$; two degrees of freedom is where the subject begins.

Local integrability, and why it is empty. Near a point $z_0$ where $X$ does not vanish, take a small piece $S$ of hyperplane through $z_0$ transverse to $X$, with coordinates $w\in\mathbb R^{2n-1}$, and map $(s,w)\mapsto\varphi_s(w)$. Its derivative at $(0,z_0)$ has columns $X(z_0)$ and a basis of $S$, independent by transversality, so unit 04's inverse function theorem makes it a local diffeomorphism (the flow is $C^1$ in its data — cited in unit 14). In the new coordinates the motion is $\dot s = 1$, $\dot w = 0$, so $w_1,\dots,w_{2n-1}$ are local integrals. For $n = 2$ that already gives local integrability: pick one $w_i$ whose differential is independent of $\mathrm dH$ — some must be, since the $\mathrm dw_i$ span everything orthogonal to $\mathrm ds$ — and it is automatically in involution with $H$, being an integral. For general $n$ one needs $n-1$ local integrals in involution with each other too; that they exist is the Carathéodory–Jacobi–Lie theorem, cited: Libermann and Marle, Symplectic Geometry and Analytical Mechanics, 1987. Either way this is true of every system without exception, chaotic ones included, and it shows the definition has no content until the domain is fixed. The chaos of section 2 lives in how these local boxes fail to patch together over a large region.

Morales-Ramis, in one sentence. Linearise along one particular known solution, and ask about the differential Galois group of the resulting linear equation; if that group's identity component is not abelian, the system has no meromorphic integrals. This has been used to prove Hénon–Heiles non-integrable for all but a few exceptional coefficient ratios — ours is not one of them. Cited: Morales-Ruiz, Differential Galois Theory and Non-Integrability of Hamiltonian Systems, 1999. It uses complex analysis and Galois theory well past this course.

The double pendulum. Two degrees of freedom, no second integral, and its chaos is visible on a kitchen table. It is the right thing to picture throughout this unit; Hénon–Heiles is used instead only because its section is easier to compute accurately and its energy is a single clean knob.

Liouville and Arnold: the level set is a torus

Liouville–Arnold theorem

Let $F_1 = H,\dots,F_n$ be in involution with independent differentials on $M_c = \{F_1 = c_1,\dots,F_n = c_n\}$. Then:

1 $M_c$ is a smooth $n$-dimensional surface invariant under all $n$ flows. 2 If $M_c$ is compact and connected, it is diffeomorphic to the $n$-torus $\mathbb T^n = (\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z)^n$ — an invariant torus. 3 There are coordinates $(\theta_1,\dots,\theta_n)$ on it in which every one of the $n$ flows is $\dot{\vec\theta} = $ constant. In particular $H$'s flow is $\theta_i(t) = \theta_i(0) + \omega_i t$. 4 In a neighbourhood there are canonical coordinates $(\vec I,\vec\theta)$, the action–angle variables, with $H = H(\vec I)$ alone and $\omega_i = \partial H/\partial I_i$.

dimensions

$I_i$ has the dimensions of $\oint p\,\mathrm dq$ — energy $\times$ time, an action — and $\theta_i$ is an angle, dimensionless. So $\omega_i = \partial H/\partial I_i$ is energy over action $=$ 1/time ✓, as a frequency must be.

the hypotheses do work

Compactness is what makes it a torus rather than a cylinder or a plane: drop it and, provided the flows are complete, $\mathbb T^k\times\mathbb R^{n-k}$ is the answer. Connectedness picks one component — a pendulum's level set at fixed energy below the separatrix has two, one per direction of swing.

proof: structure on "Why a torus, and nothing else", full proof cited — Arnold, Mathematical Methods, §49

Attribution. Liouville proved in 1855 that $n$ integrals in involution reduce the system to quadratures — an analytic statement with no geometry in it. The topological half, that the compact level set is a torus and the motion on it is linear, is Arnold's, from 1963. The modern name runs them together.

Why this is the right generalisation of unit 00. Unit 00 reduced one-dimensional motion to quadrature and found the period as an integral. Liouville–Arnold says: $n$ integrals, $n$ quadratures, $n$ periods. The actions are exactly the $n$ independent versions of unit 00's $\oint p\,\mathrm dq$, one per independent loop on the torus, and the frequencies are the derivatives of the energy with respect to them — which for $n = 1$ is the statement $T = 2\pi\,\mathrm dI/\mathrm dE$ that unit 14 proved for Halley's comet.

The torus, and the straight-line flow on it

For $n = 2$: in the angles of item 3 the motion is $\theta_i(t) = \theta_i(0) + \omega_i t$. Cut the torus open along two circles and it becomes a square whose opposite edges are glued; the orbit becomes straight lines of slope $\omega_2/\omega_1$, re-entering on the opposite edge.

Left: each angle runs round one circle of the torus; cut along both and it opens into a square whose opposite edges are glued. Middle: $\omega_2:\omega_1 = 3:2$, a resonant torus — $2\omega_2 - 3\omega_1 = 0$ — on which every orbit closes after two circuits one way and three the other. Right: $\omega_2/\omega_1 = (1+\sqrt5)/2$, non-resonant, drawn for nine circuits; the lines never close, and carried on for ever they come arbitrarily close to every point.

What "the flow is linear" buys. On a torus, a straight-line flow is completely understood: if the frequency vector $\vec\omega$ is non-resonant — meaning $\vec k\cdot\vec\omega \ne 0$ for every nonzero integer vector $\vec k$ — every orbit is dense in the torus and equidistributed on it. If it is resonant, the orbit closes up or fills a subtorus. So for an integrable system the qualitative question "what does an orbit do" is answered completely by an arithmetic question about $n$ real numbers. Section 4 is about what happens to that clean dichotomy when the system is perturbed, and the answer is that the arithmetic survives, which is the last thing anyone expected.

Reading the figure. The square is the torus with coordinates $(\theta_1,\theta_2)\in[0,2\pi)^2$: the arrows say which edges are the same circle, single arrows the bottom and top, double arrows the left and right. A line leaving through the top re-enters at the bottom at the same $\theta_1$. For the $3:2$ orbit, follow the middle square from any point: after $\theta_1$ has gone round twice, $\theta_2$ has gone round exactly three times, and the line meets its start. For the golden ratio no number of circuits brings it back, because $\omega_2/\omega_1$ is irrational: returning would need $m\,\omega_2/\omega_1 = k$ for integers $m,k$. On the torus on the left, the blue circle is $\theta_2 = $ const and the teal one, partly hidden behind the tube, is $\theta_1 = $ const.

Why dense, in one line. The points where the orbit crosses the circle $\theta_1 = 0$ are $\theta_2 = \theta_2(0) + 2\pi m\,\omega_2/\omega_1 \bmod 2\pi$, $m = 1,2,\dots$: a rotation of the circle by an irrational fraction of a turn. Two of the first $N+1$ such points lie within $2\pi/N$ of each other (pigeonhole), so some power of the rotation moves every point by less than $2\pi/N$, and its iterates then visit every arc of length $2\pi/N$. Hence the crossings are dense in the circle and the orbit is dense in the torus.

Why a torus, and nothing else

the geometry, in four steps

1 On $M_c$ the $n$ vector fields $X_{F_1},\dots,X_{F_n}$ are tangent, independent, and commute. So the map $$g:\mathbb R^n\times M_c\to M_c,\qquad g(\vec t, z) = \varphi^1_{t_1}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi^n_{t_n}(z)$$ is a well-defined action of the group $\mathbb R^n$ — defined for all $\vec t$ because $M_c$ is compact, so the flows last for ever, and an action because the flows commute.

2 Fix $z_0$. The orbit map $\vec t\mapsto g(\vec t,z_0)$ has independent partial derivatives, so it is a local diffeomorphism; $M_c$ connected makes it onto.

3 Its stabiliser $\Gamma = \{\vec t : g(\vec t, z_0) = z_0\}$ is a subgroup of $\mathbb R^n$, and by step 2 it is discrete. A discrete subgroup of $\mathbb R^n$ is a lattice $\mathbb Z\vec e_1\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb Z\vec e_k$ — this is the one substantive lemma, and it is pure group theory.

4 Hence $M_c\cong\mathbb R^n/\Gamma \cong \mathbb T^k\times\mathbb R^{n-k}$. Compact forces $k = n$: a torus.

where each hypothesis entered

involution → step 1, the action exists.
independence → step 2, no degeneracy.
connected → step 2, onto.
compact → step 1, complete flows; step 4, $k = n$.

Every hypothesis is used, and compactness twice. That is how to remember the theorem.

Full proof of step 3's lemma. Let $\Gamma\le\mathbb R^n$ be a discrete subgroup. Induct on $n$. If $\Gamma = \{0\}$ we are done. Otherwise pick $\vec e_1\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}$ of minimal norm — a minimum exists because $\Gamma$ is discrete and closed, so $\Gamma\cap\overline{B(0,R)}$ is finite. Then $\Gamma\cap\mathbb R\vec e_1 = \mathbb Z\vec e_1$: if some $\vec t = s\vec e_1$ had $s\notin\mathbb Z$, then $\vec t - \lfloor s\rfloor\vec e_1$ is a nonzero element of $\Gamma$ of norm $\lt\lVert\vec e_1\rVert$, contradicting minimality. Now project $\Gamma$ along $\mathbb R\vec e_1$ onto its orthogonal complement $\cong\mathbb R^{n-1}$, call it $\pi$. The image is again discrete, and this is the only fiddly point: given $\vec t\in\Gamma$ with $\pi\vec t\ne0$, subtract the integer multiple of $\vec e_1$ that leaves $\vec t' = s\vec e_1 + \pi\vec t$ with $|s|\le\tfrac12$. Then $\vec t'\in\Gamma\setminus\{0\}$, so minimality gives $\lVert\vec e_1\rVert^2\le\lVert\vec t'\rVert^2 \le \tfrac14\lVert\vec e_1\rVert^2 + \lVert\pi\vec t\rVert^2$, that is, $\lVert\pi\vec t\rVert\ge\tfrac{\sqrt3}2\lVert\vec e_1\rVert$. A subgroup whose nonzero elements are bounded away from $0$ is discrete, so the induction hypothesis applies to $\pi\Gamma$. Since $\ker\pi\cap\Gamma = \mathbb Z\vec e_1$, choosing preimages of the basis of $\pi\Gamma$ and adding $\vec e_1$ gives a basis of $\Gamma$.

An example you can do by hand. The two-dimensional isotropic oscillator $H = \tfrac12(p_x^2+p_y^2) + \tfrac12(x^2+y^2)$ has $F_1 = H$ and $F_2 = \tfrac12(p_y^2 + y^2)$ in involution; the level set is $\{p_x^2+x^2 = 2(c_1-c_2)\}\times\{p_y^2+y^2 = 2c_2\}$, a product of two circles — visibly a 2-torus, with no theorem required. The actions are $I_1 = c_1 - c_2$ and $I_2 = c_2$, each a circle's enclosed area over $2\pi$, and $H = I_1 + I_2$ gives $\omega_1 = \omega_2 = 1$. Problem P2 runs the same computation for the anisotropic case.

The lattice of return times

Steps 2–4 for $n = 2$. Every time vector in $\Gamma$ brings $z_0$ back to itself, so the orbit map sends the whole plane onto $M_c$ and is periodic with the periods $\vec e_1,\vec e_2$. One shaded cell, its opposite edges glued, is the torus: the glued square of the torus slide, skewed.

$\Gamma$ is the set of return times: run the flow of $F_1$ for time $t_1$ and that of $F_2$ for $t_2$, and $\vec t = (t_1,t_2)\in\Gamma$ exactly when you are back at $z_0$.

It is closed under addition and negation, and isolated (step 2), so it is a lattice $\mathbb Z\vec e_1\oplus\mathbb Z\vec e_2$; compactness rules out $k < 2$.

what is still missing

Step 4 gives the angles. Getting the actions, and the fact that $(\vec I,\vec\theta)$ is canonical, needs one more argument — that the torus is Lagrangian, so $\oint\vec p\cdot\mathrm d\vec q$ around a loop depends only on the loop's homotopy class. That is the part this unit cites.

Reading the figure. The left panel is not phase space: it is the plane of time vectors $\vec t = (t_1,t_2)$, one time for each of the two commuting flows. The map $\vec t\mapsto g(\vec t,z_0)$ wraps this plane round the level set. Two time vectors land on the same point exactly when they differ by an element of $\Gamma$, so the level set is the plane with points identified modulo $\Gamma$ — the parallelogram with opposite edges glued. The angles of the theorem are the coordinates in the basis $\vec e_1,\vec e_2$, rescaled so that each runs over $[0,2\pi)$.

What compactness rules out. If $\Gamma$ were $\mathbb Z\vec e_1$ alone ($k = 1$), the quotient would be a circle times a line — a cylinder, which is not compact. If $\Gamma$ were $\{0\}$, the orbit map would be one-to-one and $M_c$ a copy of the plane. Neither can be a compact level set, so $k = 2$.

Where the actions come from. On the torus, choose $n$ loops $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_n$ generating the lattice, and set $I_i = \frac1{2\pi}\oint_{\gamma_i}\vec p\cdot\mathrm d\vec q$. That this does not depend on which representative loop is chosen is the statement that the integrand's circulation vanishes on any loop that can be contracted — unit 04's criterion, applied on the torus rather than in $\mathbb R^3$, and true because the torus is a Lagrangian submanifold, on which the form $\sum\mathrm dp_i\wedge\mathrm dq_i$ restricts to zero. The vocabulary for that sentence arrives in unit 34; the fact is cited here from Arnold §49–50.

Hénon and Heiles ask for a third integral

worked example 1 · the low-energy limit is integrable

Scale $H$ by $z\mapsto\epsilon z$ on all four variables and divide by $\epsilon^2$: $$\tilde H = \tfrac12(\pi_\xi^2+\pi_\eta^2) + \tfrac12(\xi^2+\eta^2) + \epsilon\left(\xi^2\eta - \tfrac13\eta^3\right),$$ with $E = \epsilon^2\tilde E$. Small energy is small perturbation — this is the whole reason the energy is the knob.

At $\epsilon = 0$, the isotropic oscillator: $F_1 = \tilde H$ and $F_2 = \tfrac12(\pi_\eta^2+\eta^2)$ are in involution, because $F_1-F_2$ involves only $(\xi,\pi_\xi)$ and $F_2$ only $(\eta,\pi_\eta)$. Each level set is a product of two circles: a 2-torus.

Frequencies. $I_1 = F_1-F_2$, $I_2 = F_2$, $\tilde H = I_1+I_2$, so $\omega_1 = \omega_2 = 1$. That is resonant — an integer combination $k_1\omega_1 + k_2\omega_2$ with $(k_1,k_2)\ne0$ vanishes, here $\omega_1-\omega_2$, so every orbit closes — and as resonant as a vector can be, which section 4 calls the worst possible starting point.

the question of 1964

Does a second integral $F_2$, independent of $H$, exist for $\epsilon\ne0$? Equivalently: do the tori survive?

what the deck will answer

Yes at small $E$, no at large $E$, partly at $E = 1/8$ — so no global $F_2$ exists, since one would force curves everywhere.

The scaling argument, in full. Under $z\mapsto\epsilon z$ on all four variables, the quadratic part of $H$ scales by $\epsilon^2$ and the cubic by $\epsilon^3$. Dividing $H$ by $\epsilon^2$ leaves the quadratic part alone and multiplies the cubic by $\epsilon$. The equations of motion keep their form with no change of time: if $z = \epsilon w$ then $\dot w = \epsilon^{-1}J\nabla_zH(\epsilon w) = \epsilon^{-2}J\nabla_w\big[H(\epsilon w)\big] = J\nabla_w\tilde H(w)$, by the chain rule, since $\nabla_w[H(\epsilon w)] = \epsilon\,\nabla_zH$. The conclusion to hold onto: for Hénon–Heiles, energy is the perturbation parameter, and $\epsilon = \sqrt{E}$ up to constants. That $E = 1/8$ is already thoroughly chaotic while $E = 1/12$ is not tells you the transition is sharp in a way $\epsilon$ does not suggest.

The Hénon–Heiles potential, drawn

Level curves of $V$ at the four energies of section 2: circles near the minimum (the oscillator), rounded triangles by $1/8$, and at $1/6$ a triangle through the three saddles (×). Above $1/6$ the curves open (dashed, $V = 0.21$): three ways out.

bound motion

$V$ has a minimum $0$ at the origin and three saddles at $V = 1/6$, at $(0,1)$ and $(\pm\sqrt3/2,-\tfrac12)$. Since $\tfrac12\lVert\vec p\rVert^2\ge0$, an orbit of energy $E$ stays where $V\le E$: confined for $0<E<1/6$; above, it can escape.

why a triangle

$V - \tfrac16 = -\tfrac13\,\big(y+\tfrac12\big)\big(y - 1 - \sqrt3x\big)\big(y - 1 + \sqrt3x\big)$, a product of three linear factors. Each vanishes on a line through two of the saddles, and the threefold symmetry of the cubic term is the symmetry of the triangle.

The saddles, computed. $V = \tfrac12(x^2+y^2)+x^2y-\tfrac13y^3$ gives $V_x = x(1+2y)$, $V_y = y + x^2 - y^2$. From $V_x = 0$: either $x = 0$ or $y = -\tfrac12$. With $x = 0$: $y - y^2 = 0$, so $y = 0$ (the minimum) or $y = 1$, where $V = \tfrac12 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac16$. With $y = -\tfrac12$: $-\tfrac12 + x^2 - \tfrac14 = 0$, so $x = \pm\sqrt3/2$, and $V = \tfrac12(\tfrac34+\tfrac14) - \tfrac34\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac18 = \tfrac12 - \tfrac38 + \tfrac1{24} = \tfrac{12-9+1}{24} = \tfrac16$ ✓. The three saddles sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle, which is the threefold symmetry of the cubic term.

The factorisation, checked. The last two factors multiply to $(y-1)^2 - 3x^2$. Then $(y+\tfrac12)\big(y^2 - 2y + 1 - 3x^2\big) = y^3 - \tfrac32y^2 - 3x^2y - \tfrac32x^2 + \tfrac12$, and multiplying by $-\tfrac13$ gives $-\tfrac13y^3 + \tfrac12y^2 + x^2y + \tfrac12x^2 - \tfrac16$, which is $V - \tfrac16$ ✓. So the level set $V = 1/6$ is exactly the three lines $y = -\tfrac12$ and $y = 1\pm\sqrt3x$, and the region $V < 1/6$ containing the origin is the open triangle they bound. Each line is at distance $\tfrac12$ from the origin — for $y = 1\pm\sqrt3x$ it is $1/\sqrt{1+3}$ — and the corners at distance $1$; restoring units, $a/2$ and $a$, with $a$ the length scale of the overview's notes.

Why $1/6$ is the interesting scale. Below it the level set of $V$ is a closed curve and the motion is bounded for ever; at exactly $1/6$ the three saddles join up into a triangle; above it, orbits leak out along the three channels. So the whole story of this unit has to be told in the window $0 < E < 1/6$, and the four energies of section 2 — $1/100$, $1/12$, $1/8$, $1/6$ — are $6\%$, $50\%$, $75\%$ and $100\%$ of the way up it.

The section and its return map

Poincaré section

Let $\dot z = X(z)$ be a flow and $\Sigma$ a hypersurface with $X\not\parallel\Sigma$ at every point of it — $\Sigma$ is transverse. For $z\in\Sigma$ let $\tau(z)$ be the first time $t>0$ with $\varphi_t(z)\in\Sigma$, if it exists. The return map is $$P:\Sigma\to\Sigma,\qquad P(z) = \varphi_{\tau(z)}(z).$$

$\tau$ and $P$ are smooth

Write $\Sigma = \{g = 0\}$. Then $\frac{\partial}{\partial t}g(\varphi_t(z)) = \nabla g\cdot X \ne 0$ by transversality, so unit 04's implicit function theorem solves $g(\varphi_t(z)) = 0$ for $t = \tau(z)$ smoothly near any $z$ with a return. $P$ is then a composition of smooth maps.

Schematic. The orbit $\varphi_t(z)$ winds round and passes through $\Sigma$ once per turn, always in the same direction. $P$ forgets the journey and keeps only where it lands: $z\mapsto P(z)\mapsto P^2(z)$. The time taken for one turn is $\tau(z)$.

Why two dimensions is the whole point. The energy surface of a two-degree-of-freedom system is three-dimensional; an orbit in it is a curve, and curves in three dimensions cannot be drawn usefully on paper. Cutting with $\Sigma$ trades the continuous time variable — the one piece of information you do not care about — for a picture. Everything qualitative survives the trade: a periodic orbit becomes a finite set of points, a torus becomes a closed curve, and a dense orbit becomes a dense set.

Reading the figure. The sheet is a piece of a hypersurface; the orbit is drawn solid in front of it and dashed behind. Each crossing is in the same direction — that is transversality plus a sign condition — so each point of $\Sigma$ has a well-defined next crossing, and the return map is a function. Had the orbit been allowed to cross both ways, each turn would give two points and $P$ would alternate between two different maps.

The section for Hénon–Heiles

the section used here

For Hénon–Heiles take $\Sigma_E = \{x = 0,\ \dot x>0\}$ inside $\{H = E\}$. Transversality is the condition $\dot x = p_x > 0$, imposed by hand.

Count dimensions. Phase space 4; fix $H = E$, leaving 3; impose $x = 0$, leaving 2. Coordinates $(y,p_y)$, with the fourth variable recovered as $$p_x = +\sqrt{2E - p_y^2 - y^2 + \tfrac23y^3},$$ which is why the sign condition is needed: without it each $(y,p_y)$ would name two states.

The accessible region is where that square root is real: $\tfrac12p_y^2 + \tfrac12y^2 - \tfrac13y^3 \le E$, a closed curve. Its boundary, $p_x = 0$, is the periodic orbit along the $y$-axis.

One orbit at $E = 1/8$, started on $\Sigma_E$ at $(y,p_y) = (0.10,\,0)$ and run for $60$ time units, in the $(x,y)$ plane. Of its $20$ crossings of $x = 0$, the ten moving right are its points on $\Sigma_E$, each recorded as $(y,p_y)$; all lie on one closed curve of the section.

What the section loses. Speed along the orbit, and any orbit that never crosses $\Sigma$. The $y$-axis orbit does not cross — it lies inside the closure of $\Sigma_E$, as its boundary. Whether every other orbit returns is not obvious; for this system, at these energies, experiment says yes, and the general reason is Poincaré's recurrence theorem, which follows from unit 14's Liouville theorem and is problem P4.

The transversality condition is not a technicality. Where $X$ is tangent to $\Sigma$, $\tau$ is not even continuous: an orbit grazing the surface either touches it or misses it, and an arbitrarily small change decides which. The condition $p_x>0$ keeps us away from the tangency locus $p_x = 0$, at the cost of losing exactly the boundary curve.

Reading the figure. This is configuration space, not the section: the section is what you get by keeping, at each filled dot, the height $y$ and the vertical momentum $p_y$. The dashed curve $V = 1/8$ is the wall the orbit cannot cross, since the kinetic energy would be negative beyond it. The orbit touches that wall only where its velocity momentarily vanishes. The return map preserves area

area preservation

Let $H$ have two degrees of freedom and let $\Sigma_E = \{q_1 = 0,\ \dot q_1 > 0,\ H = E\}$ with coordinates $(q_2,p_2)$. Then the return map $P$ satisfies $\det DP \equiv 1$: it is an area-preserving map.

proof, in outline

Build $\Psi(E,q_2,p_2,t) = \varphi_t\big(0,\,q_2,\,p_1(E,q_2,p_2),\,p_2\big)$, a change of coordinates on phase space near $\Sigma$ (transversality makes it one).

1 $\Psi(\cdot,t+s) = \varphi_s\circ\Psi(\cdot,t)$, so $D\Psi(\cdot,t+s) = D\varphi_s\cdot D\Psi(\cdot,t)$. Unit 14's Liouville theorem gives $\det D\varphi_s = 1$, so $\det D\Psi$ does not depend on $t$.

2 At $t = 0$, expanding the determinant along the $q_1$ row leaves $\det D\Psi = \pm\,\dot q_1\cdot\dfrac{\partial p_1}{\partial E} = \pm\,\dot q_1\cdot\dfrac1{\partial H/\partial p_1} = \pm1.$

3 Repeating step 2 at the returned point gives $\det D\Psi = \pm\det DP$. Compare with step 1: $\det DP = 1$. $\square$

full proof in the notes

Full proof

Work in $\mathbb R^4$ with coordinates $(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)$ and suppose $\partial H/\partial p_1 \ne 0$ on the region of interest, so that $H = E$ can be solved for $p_1 = p_1(E,q_2,p_2)$ with $\partial p_1/\partial E = 1/(\partial H/\partial p_1)$ — unit 04's implicit function theorem, differentiated. Define $\sigma(E,q_2,p_2) = (0,\ q_2,\ p_1(E,q_2,p_2),\ p_2)$ and $\Psi(E,q_2,p_2,t) = \varphi_t(\sigma(E,q_2,p_2))$.

Step 1. The flow property $\varphi_{t+s} = \varphi_s\circ\varphi_t$ gives $\Psi(u,t+s) = \varphi_s(\Psi(u,t))$ for $u = (E,q_2,p_2)$. Differentiating in all four variables, $D\Psi(u,t+s) = D\varphi_s(\Psi(u,t))\cdot D\Psi(u,t)$ — the $t$-column included, since $\partial_t\Psi(u,t+s) = D\varphi_s\cdot\partial_t\Psi(u,t)$ by the chain rule. Taking determinants and using $\det D\varphi_s = 1$ (unit 14) gives $\det D\Psi(u,t+s) = \det D\Psi(u,t)$ for all $s$: the determinant is a function of $u$ alone.

Step 2. At $t = 0$ the four columns of $D\Psi$, in the row order $(q_1,q_2,p_1,p_2)$, are $\partial_E\Psi = (0,0,\partial_Ep_1,0)$, $\partial_{q_2}\Psi = (0,1,\partial_{q_2}p_1,0)$, $\partial_{p_2}\Psi = (0,0,\partial_{p_2}p_1,1)$ and $\partial_t\Psi = (\dot q_1,\dot q_2,\dot p_1,\dot p_2)$. Only the last has a nonzero first entry, so expanding along the $q_1$ row gives $\det D\Psi = \pm\dot q_1\cdot\det\!\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\ \partial_Ep_1&\partial_{q_2}p_1&\partial_{p_2}p_1\\ 0&0&1\end{pmatrix} = \mp\,\dot q_1\,\partial_Ep_1 = \mp1$, using $\dot q_1 = \partial H/\partial p_1$ and $\partial_Ep_1 = 1/(\partial H/\partial p_1)$.

Step 3. Let $\tau(u)$ be the return time of $\sigma(u)$ and $u' = (E, P(q_2,p_2))$ the returned point — the energy is unchanged because $H$ is conserved. For small $s$, $\Psi(u,\tau(u)+s) = \Psi(u',s)$. The map $(u,s)\mapsto(u',s)$ has Jacobian in block form: $E\mapsto E$, $(q_2,p_2)\mapsto P(q_2,p_2)$ independent of $s$, and $s\mapsto s$ up to a shift by $\tau(u)$, which contributes only off-diagonal entries. Its determinant is therefore $\det DP$. So $\det D\Psi(u,\tau(u)) = \det D\Psi(u',0)\cdot\det DP = \pm\det DP$. Step 1 says the left side is $\det D\Psi(u,0) = \pm1$ with the same sign — both are evaluated with $\dot q_1>0$ — so $\det DP = 1$. $\square$

What was used. Liouville's theorem (unit 14), the flow property (unit 02), the chain rule (unit 03) and the implicit function theorem (unit 04). Nothing else, and in particular no differential forms: the slick proof, that $P$ pulls back the symplectic form, waits for unit 34. That $\det DP = 1$ means $P$ preserves area — $\operatorname{area}P(U) = \int_U|\det DP|$ — is the change-of-variables formula for double integrals, proved in unit 05 and used here as unit 14 used it for volumes.

Why the sign condition matters here too. Step 3 compares two evaluations of the same determinant; if the section allowed both signs of $\dot q_1$, the two evaluations could differ in sign and the conclusion would weaken to $\det DP = \pm1$. Orientation is preserved precisely because the flow crosses in one direction.

Elliptic and hyperbolic, drawn

What $P$ does near a fixed point, to first order: $DP$ is a $2\times2$ matrix with $\det DP = 1$, so its eigenvalues satisfy $\mu_1\mu_2 = 1$. Either they are $e^{\pm i\alpha}$ and the iterates (blue) turn round ellipses, or they are real, $\mu$ and $1/\mu$, and the iterates slide along hyperbolas, out along one line and in along the other. A spiral would need $|\mu_1\mu_2| < 1$.

consequences of $\det DP = 1$

No attractors. A sink would shrink area, so nothing here ever settles down.

Recurrence. On a finite-area region, every open patch contains points that come back to it, infinitely often (problem P4).

Two kinds of fixed point. The eigenvalues of $DP$ multiply to $1$: either on the unit circle (elliptic) or real reciprocals (hyperbolic). No spirals.

Why these are the only two cases. The characteristic polynomial of a real $2\times2$ matrix with determinant $1$ is $\mu^2 - (\operatorname{tr}DP)\,\mu + 1$. If $|\operatorname{tr}DP| < 2$ the roots are complex conjugates of modulus $1$: elliptic. If $|\operatorname{tr}DP| > 2$ they are real with product $1$: hyperbolic. The boundary case $|\operatorname{tr}DP| = 2$ is parabolic — a shear — and is non-generic. A spiral sink needs complex roots of modulus less than $1$, which would make the determinant, their product, less than $1$.

What to look for. In the elliptic panel the dots step round one ellipse by a fixed angle $\alpha$ each time; the ellipse is a level curve of a quadratic form that $DP$ preserves, the linear shadow of an invariant curve round an island centre. In the hyperbolic panel the dots move away along the red line by a factor $\mu$ per step and approach along the green line by $1/\mu$: these two lines are the linear shadows of the unstable and stable sets that section 4 follows out into Poincaré's tangle. The island chains of the coming sections are built from exactly these two pieces, alternating.

Recurrence, in one line. If an open patch $U$ of finite area never returned to itself, its images $U, P(U), P^2(U),\dots$ would be disjoint — if $P^m(U)$ met $P^n(U)$ with $m<n$, then $U$ would meet $P^{n-m}(U)$ — all of the same area, inside a region of finite area. Impossible. Problem P4 makes it a proof.

What integrability looks like on a section

the test

Suppose a second integral $F$ exists on the region. Then every orbit stays in $\{H = E, F = f\}$, whose intersection with $\Sigma_E$ is one-dimensional: the crossings of a single orbit lie on a curve.

So: curves everywhere $\Rightarrow$ consistent with integrability; one orbit filling a patch $\Rightarrow$ no such $F$ on that patch.

the three things you see

Invariant curves — a torus cut by the section.
Island chains — $m$ closed curves visited in turn, around $m$ elliptic fixed points of $P^m$. A resonance.
A chaotic sea — one orbit's crossings spread over an area, never repeating and never settling.

the direction of the logic is one-way

A filled patch is strong evidence against a second integral, and the argument above makes that precise. Curves are not evidence for one: a chaotic layer thinner than a pixel, or an orbit that has not yet escaped, looks exactly like a curve.

This is why section 3 exists. The Lyapunov exponent is a number, not an impression, and it is what turns a picture into evidence.

a sanity check before computing

At $E\to0$ the system is the isotropic oscillator, integrable, so the section must be curves. If the computation does not show that, the integrator is wrong. It is the only case where the answer is known in advance, and it is the one to test on.

Making the test precise. "Filling a patch" needs care, because a countable set of points has zero area whatever it looks like. The honest statement is: if a smooth $F$ independent of $H$ existed on an open set $U$, then $F$ restricted to $\Sigma_E\cap U$ is a non-constant smooth function constant along orbits of $P$, so the closure of any orbit lies in one of its level curves, which has empty interior. Exhibiting an orbit whose closure contains a disc therefore rules out $F$ on $U$. Numerically one cannot exhibit a closure, only $850$ points scattered over a region — which is why the argument is completed by the Lyapunov exponent rather than by the picture.

Island chains, and where they come from. An island chain of $m$ islands is the signature of an orbit of the return map with period $m$, which means a periodic orbit of the flow that goes round $m$ times in one direction for every $q$ times in the other — a resonance $m:q$. Section 4 explains why the resonant tori are exactly the ones that break, and why what replaces them is a chain of islands.

How the pictures in this deck were made. Fixed-step fourth-order Runge–Kutta with step $0.005$, crossings located by bisection on the step to sixty iterations. The check that matters is energy drift: over the longest run here, $T = 10^6$, it never exceeds $1.2\times10^{-8}$ out of $E = 0.125$, a relative $9\times10^{-8}$, and over the $T = 20\,000$ runs of section 3 it stays below $3\times10^{-9}$ relative, so no feature of the picture larger than a pixel is an artefact of the integration. A run whose energy drifts is not evidence of anything.

The linearised flow

variational equation

For $\dot z = X(z)$ with flow $\varphi_t$, the derivative in the initial condition, $M(t) = D\varphi_t(z_0)$, satisfies the linear system $$\dot M(t) = DX\big(\varphi_t(z_0)\big)\,M(t),\qquad M(0) = I.$$ A single tangent vector $\delta(t) = M(t)\delta_0$ obeys the same equation.

proof

$\varphi_t$ is $C^1$ in $z_0$ (cited in unit 14: Hirsch, Smale and Devaney), so $\partial_t\partial_{z_0}\varphi_t = \partial_{z_0}\partial_t\varphi_t$ by Clairaut (unit 03). The right-hand side is $\partial_{z_0}\big[X(\varphi_t(z_0))\big] = DX(\varphi_t(z_0))\,D\varphi_t(z_0)$ by the chain rule. $\square$

this is not unit 02's linearisation

Unit 02 linearised about an equilibrium, getting $\dot\delta = A\delta$ with $A$ constant. Here the base point moves, so $DX(\varphi_t(z_0))$ is a time-dependent matrix and there is no characteristic polynomial, no eigenvalue, and no Hartman–Grobman. What replaces the eigenvalue is the Lyapunov exponent.

the matrix, for the running example

For Hénon–Heiles, $X = (p_x,\,p_y,\,-x-2xy,\,-y-x^2+y^2)$ and $$DX = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&1&0\\ 0&0&0&1\\ -1-2y&-2x&0&0\\ -2x&-1+2y&0&0\end{pmatrix}.$$ The lower-left block is $-\nabla^2V$, symmetric by Clairaut ✓. Its entries change as the orbit moves, and that is the entire mechanism of chaos in this system.

Where the eight-dimensional system comes from. To compute anything, integrate the original four equations and the four variational ones together — an eight-dimensional system whose first four components do not depend on the last four. The pictures in this deck were made that way. Integrating the variational equation separately, using a stored trajectory, is a classic way to get a wrong answer: the interpolation error in the stored orbit feeds straight into $DX$.

Why $DX$ is the whole mechanism. If $V$ were quadratic, $-\nabla^2V$ would be constant and the variational equation would be a linear system with constant coefficients, of the kind unit 02 solved in the plane — solvable exactly and bounded for a stable equilibrium. The cubic term makes $\nabla^2V$ depend on position, so the orbit visits regions where the local behaviour is a saddle and regions where it is a centre. Stretching in the saddle regions and folding back in the bounded region is, in one sentence, how a deterministic bounded flow manufactures exponential separation.

A check on the matrix. The eigenvalues of $-\nabla^2V$ at the origin are both $-1$, giving the two unit frequencies of the integrable limit. At the saddle $(0,1)$, $\nabla^2V = \begin{pmatrix}1+2y&2x\\2x&1-2y\end{pmatrix} = \operatorname{diag}(3,-1)$, one positive and one negative — a saddle of $V$ ✓, consistent with the escape channel found earlier.

The Lyapunov exponent

Lyapunov exponent

For an orbit through $z_0$ and a tangent vector $\delta_0\ne0$, $$\lambda(z_0,\delta_0) \;=\; \limsup_{t\to\infty}\frac1t\,\ln\big\lVert D\varphi_t(z_0)\,\delta_0\big\rVert .$$ The largest Lyapunov exponent $\lambda_1(z_0)$ is the supremum over $\delta_0$. An orbit is called chaotic when $\lambda_1 > 0$.

independent of the norm

Two norms on $\mathbb R^{2n}$ satisfy $c\lVert\cdot\rVert_1\le\lVert\cdot\rVert_2\le C\lVert\cdot\rVert_1$, and $\tfrac1t\ln c\to0$. So $\lambda$ does not depend on which norm is used — the only thing about it that is free.

constant along an orbit

$D\varphi_{t}(\varphi_s z_0) = D\varphi_{t+s}(z_0)\,D\varphi_s(z_0)^{-1}$, and the fixed factor contributes $O(1)$ to $\ln$, hence $0$ to the limit. So $\lambda_1$ labels the orbit, not the point.

at most $2n$ values

$\{\delta_0:\lambda(z_0,\delta_0)\le c\}$ is a linear subspace, so as $c$ grows the subspaces form a flag with at most $2n$ jumps. The jump values are the spectrum.

Oseledets' multiplicative ergodic theorem — cited

For a flow preserving a finite measure, at almost every $z_0$ the $\limsup$ is a genuine limit and the flag is measurable and invariant. Oseledets 1968; a readable proof is Ruelle 1979, and Barreira and Pesin, Lyapunov Exponents and Smooth Ergodic Theory, 2002. It needs ergodic theory, which this course does not build.

Why the $\limsup$ and not the limit. Without a theorem there is no reason for $\frac1t\ln\lVert\delta(t)\rVert$ to converge: it could oscillate for ever between two values. The $\limsup$ always exists in $[-\infty,+\infty]$ and makes the definition unconditional. Oseledets says the oscillation does not happen except on a set of measure zero — with respect to an invariant measure, which for a Hamiltonian system on a compact energy surface is the Liouville measure of unit 14 restricted to it.

Full proof that the sublevel sets are subspaces. If $\lambda(\delta)\le c$ and $\lambda(\eta)\le c$ then for any $a,b$, $\lVert M(t)(a\delta+b\eta)\rVert\le|a|\lVert M\delta\rVert + |b|\lVert M\eta\rVert \le (|a|+|b|)e^{(c+\varepsilon)t}$ for $t$ large, so $\frac1t\ln\lVert M(a\delta+b\eta)\rVert\le c+\varepsilon + \frac1t\ln(|a|+|b|)$, and letting $t\to\infty$ then $\varepsilon\to0$ gives $\lambda(a\delta+b\eta)\le c$. Closure under scalars is immediate. The nesting $V_{c}\subseteq V_{c'}$ for $c\le c'$ then gives a finite flag, because a strictly increasing chain of subspaces of $\mathbb R^{2n}$ has at most $2n+1$ members.

A consequence worth stating. For a random $\delta_0$, $\lambda(z_0,\delta_0) = \lambda_1$: the vectors with a smaller exponent lie in a proper subspace, which has zero volume. This is why Benettin's algorithm, below, starts from a random vector and does not worry about the choice — and also why, if you start it exactly on the flow direction, you get $0$ and a wrong answer.

What a Hamiltonian spectrum must look like

three constraints

1 $\lambda = 0$ occurs: take $\delta_0 = X(z_0)$. Then $D\varphi_t\,X(z_0) = X(\varphi_t z_0)$, which is bounded on a compact energy surface and bounded below away from equilibria, so $\tfrac1t\ln\lVert\cdot\rVert\to0$.

2 The exponents sum to zero: $\sum_i\lambda_i = \lim\tfrac1t\ln|\det D\varphi_t| = 0$ by unit 14's Liouville theorem.

3 The spectrum is symmetric: $\lambda$ an exponent $\Rightarrow$ $-\lambda$ is one, because $D\varphi_t$ is symplectic (unit 14) and a symplectic matrix's singular values come in reciprocal pairs.

two degrees of freedom, therefore

Four exponents. One is $0$ (the flow direction); one more is $0$ (the direction across the energy surface, where the separation does not grow at all). Symmetry and the zero sum then force $$\{\lambda_1,\,0,\,0,\,-\lambda_1\}.$$ There is one number to measure, and it decides everything.

Full proof of the reciprocal-pair fact. Let $M$ be symplectic, $M^{\!\top}JM = J$ (unit 14). Then $M^{-1} = J^{-1}M^{\!\top}J$, so $M^{-1}$ is similar to $M^{\!\top}$, which has the same characteristic polynomial as $M$. Hence the eigenvalue sets of $M$ and $M^{-1}$ coincide: the spectrum is closed under $\mu\mapsto1/\mu$. Now $M^{\!\top}$ is symplectic too (transpose $M^{\!\top}JM = J$ and use $J^{-1} = -J$), so $S = M^{\!\top}M$ is symplectic, symmetric and positive definite; its eigenvalues — the squared singular values of $M$ — therefore come in pairs $\mu,1/\mu$. Taking $\tfrac1{2t}\ln$ turns that into $\pm$ pairs of exponents.

Why the transverse direction gives zero. Move the initial condition to a nearby energy $E + \varepsilon$. The two orbits stay on their own surfaces for ever, and on a compact region the surfaces stay a bounded distance apart, so that component of the separation neither grows nor shrinks exponentially. The same statement for the flow direction is item 1. Together they use up two of the four exponents, which is why a two-degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian system has exactly one number in it.

Computing it without overflowing

Benettin's algorithm

Choose a renormalisation interval $\Delta$ and a unit vector $\delta^{(0)}$. For $k = 1,2,\dots,N$: integrate the variational equation over $[0,\Delta]$ from $\delta^{(k-1)}$, call the result $\eta^{(k)}$, record $a_k = \lVert\eta^{(k)}\rVert$, and restart from $\delta^{(k)} = \eta^{(k)}/a_k$. Then $$\lambda_1 \;\approx\; \frac1{N\Delta}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\ln a_k .$$

why this is the same number

Linearity: $D\varphi_{N\Delta}\,\delta^{(0)} = \big(\prod_k a_k\big)\,\delta^{(N)}$, because each restart only rescales, and rescaling commutes with a linear map. Taking $\tfrac1{N\Delta}\ln$ of the norm gives the sum above plus $\tfrac1{N\Delta}\ln\lVert\delta^{(N)}\rVert = 0$. $\square$

why it is needed at all

With $\lambda_1 = 0.044$ and $T = 20\,000$, $\lVert\delta\rVert$ would reach $e^{880}$. The largest double-precision number is about $e^{709}$. Without renormalisation the computation overflows before the answer converges — and long before that, $\delta$ has aligned so exactly with the expanding direction that its short components are lost to rounding.

The trap this algorithm replaces. The naive method — integrate two nearby orbits and watch the distance — fails twice. It saturates: once the separation reaches the size of the energy surface it stops growing, and the measured rate collapses. And before saturating it is contaminated by the nonlinear terms the definition excludes. The variational equation has neither problem, because it is exactly linear and the renormalisation keeps $\delta$ at unit length for ever.

The other exponents. Benettin's algorithm generalises: carry $k$ vectors and re-orthonormalise them (Gram–Schmidt) at each step; the logarithms of the $k$ normalisation factors give $\lambda_1+\cdots+\lambda_k$. For the Hamiltonian systems of this unit the symmetry of the spectrum makes that unnecessary — one number is all there is.

Gronwall's revenge

what unit 01 actually proved

If $f$ is Lipschitz with constant $L$ on a region, two solutions with data $y_0$, $\tilde y_0$ satisfy $$\lVert y(t)-\tilde y(t)\rVert \;\le\; \lVert y_0-\tilde y_0\rVert\,e^{Lt}.$$ Continuous dependence follows: given $\varepsilon$ and a fixed horizon $T$, take $\delta = \varepsilon e^{-LT}$.

where the promise is void

The $\delta$ depends on $T$, and goes to zero as $T$ grows. Continuity in the data is not uniform in $t$. Nothing in unit 01 says otherwise, and no strengthening is possible: the bound is attained.

the two rates, for the running example

On the accessible region at $E = 1/8$, $\sup\lVert DX\rVert_2 = 2.35$, so Gronwall gives $e^{2.35t}$ — a guarantee, valid for every orbit, and useless: at $t = 1000$ it permits a growth factor of $e^{2350}$.

The measured rate on the chaotic orbit is $\lambda_1 = 0.044$, smaller by a factor $53$. Gronwall bounds the worst case; the Lyapunov exponent is the achieved average. Both are exponential, and that is the point.

The logical structure, stated once, because it is the checkpoint. Picard–Lindelöf: for each initial condition there is exactly one solution. Continuous dependence: for each $\varepsilon>0$ and each $T$ there is a $\delta>0$ such that data within $\delta$ stay within $\varepsilon$ up to time $T$. Positive Lyapunov exponent: $\delta$ must be taken exponentially small in $T$. The three are consistent because the second quantifies over $T$ before choosing $\delta$. A contradiction would require a single $\delta$ working for all $T$ — that is uniform continuity in the data, which unit 01 never claimed and which is false here.

The bound is attained, which is the part people skip. For $\dot y = Ly$ the Lipschitz constant is exactly $L$ and the separation is exactly $\lVert\delta_0\rVert e^{Lt}$: equality. So Gronwall cannot be improved as a general bound. What can be improved is the constant for a particular system, and the Lyapunov exponent is the best possible such constant in the asymptotic sense — problem P5 does both halves.

What a positive exponent does not say

four things it is not

Not non-uniqueness. Unit 01's hypothesis is a Lipschitz condition on $f$; the Hénon–Heiles field is a polynomial, Lipschitz on every bounded set. Uniqueness holds, everywhere, always.

Not randomness. Nothing stochastic appears anywhere. The orbit that fills the sea is a single solution of a polynomial differential equation.

Not instability of the system. The motion stays bounded for ever, and the energy is exact. What is unstable is the labelling of orbits by initial data.

Not a property of a single point. $\lambda_1$ is an asymptotic average along an orbit; a finite-time estimate on a finite segment is a different and smaller thing.

what it does say

Information about the initial state is destroyed at a definite rate. $\lambda_1/\ln2 = 0.063$ bits per unit time is the rate at which digits of the initial condition stop mattering to the past and start mattering to the future — Pesin's identity makes that precise, and it is cited (Barreira and Pesin, 2002).

Lyapunov time

$t_\lambda = 1/\lambda_1$, the time for an error to grow by $e$. For the running example, $23$ time units. For the inner solar system, about $5$ million years — section 5.

The confusion this slide exists to prevent is the belief that chaos is a failure of determinism, or a sign that the model is incomplete, or that it somehow requires noise to get going. All three are wrong, and the first is the one worth being able to refute on demand — which is why the checkpoint asks for it with the theorem quoted precisely.

Where the bits come from. Measure the initial condition to $k$ binary digits. After time $t$ the uncertainty has grown by $e^{\lambda_1t}$, so about $\lambda_1t/\ln2$ digits have become meaningless; equivalently the orbit has revealed that many digits of information that the original measurement did not contain. Pesin's identity says that for a large class of systems the Kolmogorov–Sinai entropy — the information production rate, in the sense of Shannon — equals the sum of the positive exponents, here $\lambda_1$ alone.

Finite-time exponents are a real tool, not a poor relation. $\frac1{T}\ln\lVert\delta(T)\rVert$ for moderate $T$ varies strongly over the section, and mapping it is how the pictures in this deck were coloured. It is a different quantity from $\lambda_1$ and should be named differently; conflating them produces the common mistake of reporting a positive exponent for a regular orbit that has simply not run long enough.

17 · perturbation

The small denominators

Take $H = H_0(\vec I) + \varepsilon H_1(\vec I,\vec\theta)$, with $\vec\omega = \partial H_0/\partial\vec I$, and remove the $\vec\theta$-dependence with unit 14's type-2 generating function $S = \vec I{\,}'\!\cdot\vec\theta + \varepsilon S_1(\vec I{\,}',\vec\theta)$, so $\vec I = \vec I{\,}' + \varepsilon\,\partial_{\vec\theta}S_1$ and

$$K = H_0(\vec I{\,}') + \varepsilon\Big[\vec\omega\cdot\partial_{\vec\theta}S_1 + H_1\Big] + O(\varepsilon^2).$$

The first-order term can be made independent of $\vec\theta$, leaving only $\langle H_1\rangle$, the average of $H_1$ over the angles (over the torus, at fixed $\vec I$), if $\vec\omega\cdot\partial_{\vec\theta}S_1 = -(H_1 - \langle H_1\rangle)$.

one harmonic, solved exactly

Take $H_1 = a\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec\theta)$, $\vec k\ne0$, and try $S_1 = b\sin(\vec k\cdot\vec\theta)$. Then $\vec\omega\cdot\partial_{\vec\theta}S_1 = b(\vec k\cdot\vec\omega)\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec\theta)$, so the equation forces $\;b = -a/(\vec k\cdot\vec\omega)$.

the obstruction, in one symbol

$\vec k\cdot\vec\omega$ is in the denominator. At a resonance, $\vec k\cdot\vec\omega = 0$ and there is no solution at all; merely near one, the "correction" exceeds what it corrects and the expansion is void.

small denominators

A general $H_1$ is a sum over all $\vec k$; for $n\ge2$ the denominators come arbitrarily close to $0$, and the series converges only if the numerators shrink faster. That is the whole difficulty of KAM.

dimensions

$a$ is an energy, $\vec k\cdot\vec\omega$ a frequency, and $S_1$ must be an action: energy over frequency ✓.

Why the infinite sum is stated rather than used. Decomposing a general periodic $H_1$ into harmonics $\sum_{\vec k}a_{\vec k}e^{\mathrm i\vec k\cdot\vec\theta}$ is a Fourier series, and the theory of those — convergence, completeness, the decay of coefficients for smooth functions — arrives later in the course. Nothing on this slide needs it: with $H_1$ a finite trigonometric sum the computation above is exact and elementary, term by term, and it already exhibits the obstruction. When the sum is infinite the same formula produces the coefficients, and the question becomes whether they are summable, which is the question KAM answers.

The averaging that motivates the whole attempt. If the transformation could be carried through, $K$ would depend on $\vec I{\,}'$ alone, so $\vec I{\,}'$ would be conserved and the system would be integrable — the perturbed motion would live on tori just like the unperturbed. Poincaré's theorem is precisely that this cannot be done globally: the formal series exists at every order but does not converge. Laplace and Lagrange had carried the first order through for the solar system and concluded stability; the small denominators are exactly what their argument swept aside.

The resonant case is not a failure of technique. When $\vec k\cdot\vec\omega = 0$, the unperturbed torus is foliated by closed orbits rather than by one dense orbit, and the perturbation's average around each closed orbit varies from orbit to orbit. There is then a real dynamical effect to capture, not a formal one to remove, and the correct treatment — secular perturbation theory near the resonance — produces a pendulum equation whose separatrix is the island chain of section 2.

Rational frequencies are the enemy, and there are few of them

Diophantine condition

$\vec\omega$ is $(\gamma,\tau)$-Diophantine if $$|\vec k\cdot\vec\omega| \;\ge\; \frac{\gamma}{\lVert\vec k\rVert^{\tau}}\qquad\text{for all }\vec k\in\mathbb Z^n\setminus\{0\}.$$ It is a quantitative statement that $\vec\omega$ is far from every resonance, with the tolerance shrinking as the resonance gets higher order.

almost every $\vec\omega$ satisfies it

For $n = 2$ write $\alpha = \omega_1/\omega_2$. For $\tau > 1$, the set of $\alpha\in[0,1]$ failing $|\alpha - p/q|\ge\gamma q^{-(\tau+1)}$ for some $p/q$ can be covered by intervals of total length at most $$\sum_{q\ge1}(q+1)\cdot\frac{2\gamma}{q^{\tau+1}} \;\le\; 4\gamma\sum_{q\ge1}q^{-\tau},$$ a convergent series. So the bad set shrinks to nothing as $\gamma\to0$. $\square$

a strange sentence, but a true one

Whether a particular orbit of a mechanical system survives a perturbation depends on the continued-fraction expansion of a ratio of its frequencies. Nothing else in classical mechanics is like this.

The covering argument, spelled out. Fix $\gamma>0$ and $\tau>1$. For each $q\ge1$ and each $p$ with $0\le p\le q$, the set of $\alpha\in[0,1]$ with $|\alpha-p/q|<\gamma q^{-(\tau+1)}$ is an interval of length at most $2\gamma q^{-(\tau+1)}$. There are $q+1$ choices of $p$, so all $\alpha$ failing the condition at denominator $q$ are covered by total length $\le 2\gamma(q+1)q^{-(\tau+1)}\le4\gamma q^{-\tau}$. Summing over $q$ and using convergence of $\sum q^{-\tau}$ for $\tau>1$ gives total length $\le4\gamma\zeta(\tau)$, which tends to $0$ with $\gamma$. Hence for every $\eta>0$ there is a $\gamma$ making the bad set coverable by length $\eta$ — the elementary substitute for "the bad set has measure zero", and all this unit needs.

The two competing intuitions, and which one is right. Rationals are dense, so every frequency is arbitrarily close to a resonance — that intuition says all tori break. Rationals are countable and can be covered by arbitrarily short intervals — that intuition says almost none break. The Diophantine condition is the referee: closeness to $p/q$ is forgiven if it is not too close relative to $q$. The second intuition wins, but only for the tori whose frequencies keep a definite distance, and the surviving set, while of nearly full measure, has empty interior. That is why the chaotic and regular regions of the section are intertwined at every scale.

KAM, stated honestly

KAM theorem — cited

Let $H_\varepsilon = H_0(\vec I) + \varepsilon H_1(\vec I,\vec\theta)$ with $H_0,H_1$ analytic on a domain, and let $H_0$ be non-degenerate: $\det\big(\partial^2H_0/\partial I_i\partial I_j\big)\ne0$. Fix $\tau>n-1$. Then there is $\varepsilon_0>0$ such that for $|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_0$, every torus whose frequency vector is $(\gamma,\tau)$-Diophantine with $\gamma\ge C\sqrt{|\varepsilon|}$ survives: it is deformed into a nearby invariant torus carrying the same linear flow. The surviving tori fill the phase space up to a set of measure $O(\sqrt{|\varepsilon|})$. Proof: Arnold, Mathematical Methods, appendix 8, in outline; Pöschel, A lecture on the classical KAM theorem, 2001, in full.

what "small" means

The proofs give explicit $\varepsilon_0$, and they are brutal. Hénon computed in 1966 that Arnold's version, applied to the restricted three-body problem, required a mass ratio below $10^{-333}$; Moser's, below $10^{-48}$. Jupiter's is $10^{-3}$. (cited: Dumas, The KAM Story, 2014, §6.)

what it does not give

For $n\ge3$ the surviving tori do not separate the energy surface — an $n$-torus in a $(2n-1)$-dimensional surface has codimension $n-1$. Orbits can then drift through the gaps: Arnold diffusion, cited, and still largely open.

what it does give, for $n = 2$

A $2$-torus in a $3$-dimensional energy surface does separate it. A chaotic orbit trapped between two surviving tori can never leave. Hence a genuine stability theorem — for two degrees of freedom only.

The history, briefly. Kolmogorov announced the result at the 1954 International Congress, with a sketch rather than a proof. Arnold proved the analytic case in 1963, Moser the finitely-differentiable case (originally $C^{333}$, since reduced to $C^{3+}$) in 1962. The technique is a Newton-like iteration in function space whose quadratic convergence beats the deteriorating small denominators — the same idea as Newton's method converging even when each step's linear problem is badly conditioned, provided the error is squared each time.

Why non-degeneracy is needed. $\det(\partial^2H_0/\partial I^2)\ne0$ says the map $\vec I\mapsto\vec\omega(\vec I)$ is a local diffeomorphism, so the frequencies genuinely vary from torus to torus and the Diophantine ones form a set of nearly full measure among them. For the isotropic oscillator $H_0 = I_1+I_2$ this determinant is zero: every torus has the same frequency vector $(1,1)$, which is resonant. So the standard KAM theorem does not apply to the low-energy Hénon–Heiles limit as it stands — an important honesty. Isoenergetic versions of the theorem, which require only that the frequency ratio vary along the energy surface, do apply once the cubic term shifts the frequencies apart, and those are what the picture at $E = 1/100$ illustrates.

The gap between the theorem and the numerics. Hénon's numbers are not a criticism of KAM; they are the price of a proof that must work for every system satisfying the hypotheses. The observed threshold in any particular system is enormously larger — for the standard map the proved bound and the true $K_c = 0.9716$ differ by orders of magnitude the same way. This is the normal relationship between a hard existence theorem and an experiment, and the reason section 2's pictures are not redundant.

What becomes of the resonant tori

Poincaré–Birkhoff theorem — cited

The rotation number of an invariant circle is the average fraction of a turn a point on it advances per iteration. Let $P$ be an area-preserving twist map of an annulus — rotation number varying monotonically from one edge to the other — mapping each boundary circle to itself, the two rotating in opposite directions. Then $P$ has at least two fixed points.

Applied to $P^q$: a circle of rotation number $p/q$ — every point back after $q$ steps, having gone round $p$ times — is replaced by $2mq$ points of period $q$, for some integer $m\ge1$, alternately elliptic and hyperbolic; typically $m = 1$. Poincaré 1912, Birkhoff 1913; proof: Brown and Neumann 1977.

Schematic, $q = 5$, in a frame turning with the resonant circle. Its points were all of period $5$; five survive as elliptic centres (gold), each surrounded by curves, and five as hyperbolic points (×) between them, where the chaos is born on the separatrices (red). Count the islands and you have read off the resonance.

The counting argument behind the theorem, in the spirit of unit 02. Consider the unperturbed twist map: the circle with rotation number $p/q$ consists entirely of fixed points of $P^q$. Perturb. Points just inside rotate one way under $P^q$, points just outside the other, by the twist. So on each radial line there is a point whose image lies on the same line. The curve of such points is mapped to itself and must, by area preservation, cross the original curve — and a closed curve crossing another closed curve does so an even number of times. That is the count $2mq$. This is a heuristic, not a proof: making it rigorous is a genuinely topological argument, of the same family as unit 02's index of a vector field around a closed curve, and it is the part cited.

Why alternately elliptic and hyperbolic. Between two consecutive fixed points of an area-preserving map on a curve, the map's rotation sense must reverse; the linearisation at a fixed point has determinant $1$, so its eigenvalues are either on the unit circle or real reciprocals, and the two types must alternate for the index to come out right. The elliptic ones are the island centres; the hyperbolic ones are where Poincaré's tangle lives.

The tangle Poincaré found

homoclinic point

Let $z^*$ be a hyperbolic fixed point of an area-preserving map, with stable set $W^s$ (points approaching $z^*$ forwards) and unstable set $W^u$ (backwards). A point $\ne z^*$ in $W^s\cap W^u$ is homoclinic. If the crossing is transversal, it is a transversal homoclinic point.

one crossing forces infinitely many

$W^s,W^u$ are invariant, so $h\in W^s\cap W^u$ gives $P^n(h)\in W^s\cap W^u$ for every $n$ — infinitely many crossings, accumulating on $z^*$ at both ends. Area preservation forbids the enclosed lobes from shrinking, so they stretch and fold back through every other lobe. $\square$

This is the figure Poincaré declined to draw, and the correction that cost him the price of the journal issue.

Schematic. Past the first crossing $h$, the unstable set must pass through $P(h)$, $P^2(h),\dots$, closer and closer to $z^*$. Each lobe it encloses with $W^s$ is the image of the one before, so all have the same area: as they narrow, they grow taller without bound.

Reading the figure. Only half the tangle is drawn. $W^s$ is shown as the straight green line into $z^*$; the true $W^s$ oscillates about $W^u$ in the same way as it is followed backwards towards $z^*$, so the two sets cross in a mesh that thickens near $z^*$ without limit — the figure Poincaré would not draw. The crossings drawn are $P^n(h)$, approaching $z^*$ geometrically, by the factor $1/\mu$ of the incoming direction. Each lobe is mapped by $P$ onto the next, so area preservation makes their areas equal; the narrowing by $1/\mu$ is paid for by a growth in height by $\mu$. The lobes therefore stretch out across the whole region, through one another — which is how a single transversal crossing manufactures the stretching and folding that a positive exponent measures.

What is honestly not proved anywhere in this unit. That the Hénon–Heiles chaotic region at $E = 1/8$ has positive area. Numerically it plainly does; proving positive-area chaos for a specific smooth Hamiltonian system is extremely hard, and the general belief that such regions exist is not a theorem. This is the one place where the subject's standard of evidence is numerical, and saying so is part of using it honestly.

What the tangle forces, and how to detect it

Smale's horseshoe — cited

A transversal homoclinic point implies some power $P^N$ has an invariant Cantor set on which it is the shift on two symbols — hence periodic orbits of every period, a dense orbit, and $\lambda_1>0$ there. Smale 1965; Guckenheimer & Holmes §§5.1–5.3.

Melnikov function — cited

For $\dot z = X_{H_0}(z) + \varepsilon X_{H_1}(z,t)$ with an unperturbed homoclinic loop $z_0(t)$, $$M(t_0) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\{H_0,H_1\}\big(z_0(t),\,t+t_0\big)\,\mathrm dt .$$ A simple zero of $M$ implies a transversal homoclinic point for small $\varepsilon$. $M$ measures, to first order, the gap between $W^s$ and $W^u$. Melnikov 1963.

Why Melnikov matters practically. It converts "is this system chaotic?" into "does this explicit integral have a simple zero?", and the integral can often be done in closed form because the unperturbed homoclinic orbit is known — for the pendulum $\ddot\theta = -\sin\theta$ it is $\theta_0(t) = 4\arctan(e^{t}) - \pi$, running from the upright position at $-\pi$ to the one at $\pi$, and the Melnikov integral can be done by residues (unit 20), producing a $\operatorname{sech}$. The result is a threshold in the perturbation parameter above which chaos is guaranteed. It is the only criterion in this unit that is both rigorous and computable by hand; it is stated rather than proved here because the proof needs the implicit function theorem in a function space and an interchange of limit and integral.

The relation to the exponent. The horseshoe gives $\lambda_1>0$ on a Cantor set, which has zero area — so it does not by itself explain a chaotic sea. What it explains is that positive exponents are present as soon as the manifolds cross at all, arbitrarily weakly. The sea appears when these thin layers, one per resonance, grow enough to merge, which is the subject of the standard map and Chirikov's criterion.

The kicked rotor and the standard map

A free rotor, $H = p^2/2$, struck once per unit time by a short torque pulse derived from the potential $K\cos\theta$. During a pulse of duration $\eta\to0$ with fixed total impulse, $\theta$ cannot change but $p$ jumps by $K\sin\theta$; between pulses $\theta$ advances by $p$. Reading $(\theta_n,p_n)$ just before the $n$-th kick:

the standard map $$\begin{aligned}p_{n+1} &= p_n + K\sin\theta_n,\\ \theta_{n+1} &= \theta_n + p_{n+1}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2\pi).\end{aligned}$$
it is area-preserving

$\dfrac{\partial(\theta_{n+1},p_{n+1})}{\partial(\theta_n,p_n)} = \begin{pmatrix}1+K\cos\theta_n&1\\ K\cos\theta_n&1\end{pmatrix}$, determinant $(1+K\cos\theta_n) - K\cos\theta_n = 1$ ✓ — as the previous section's theorem demands of any Hamiltonian return map.

why this map and not another

The simplest area-preserving twist map with one parameter; every two-degree-of-freedom system looks like it near a resonance; and its critical parameter is known to nine digits. It is the drosophila of the subject.

the primary resonances

$K = 0$: $p$ is conserved, and the invariant curves are the horizontal lines $p = $ const, with rotation number $p/2\pi$. Resonances sit where $p = 2\pi m$, $m\in\mathbb Z$: there, $\theta$ returns exactly and every point is fixed.

$K>0$: near $p = 0$ the map is close to the flow of the pendulum $H_{\text{eff}} = \tfrac12p^2 + K\cos\theta$, whose $\dot p = K\sin\theta$. Its separatrix leaves the hyperbolic point $(0,0)$ at energy $K$; at the island centre $\theta = \pi$, $\tfrac12p^2 - K = K$, a half-width $$\Delta p = 2\sqrt K.$$

The limit, done carefully. Replace the impulsive kick by a torque $K\sin\theta\cdot f_\eta(t) = -\partial_\theta(K\cos\theta)\,f_\eta(t)$ where $f_\eta$ is a bump of width $\eta$ and unit integral supported at the integers. Over one bump, $\Delta p = \int -\partial_\theta(K\cos\theta) f_\eta = K\sin\theta\cdot(1+O(\eta))$, since $\theta$ changes by $O(\eta p)$ during the bump; and $\Delta\theta = O(\eta)$. Letting $\eta\to0$ gives the map exactly. Nothing distributional is needed — the delta function is convenient notation for this limit, and the notation is legitimised later in the course.

Where the pendulum approximation comes from. Over one step, if $p$ and the change in $\theta$ are small, the map is the Euler step of $\dot\theta = p$, $\dot p = K\sin\theta$, which is Hamiltonian with $H_{\text{eff}} = \tfrac12p^2 + K\cos\theta$. The approximation is good when $\sqrt K\ll1$, which is exactly the regime where the widths are small — so the criterion built on it is being used at the edge of its validity, and Chirikov's factor-of-$2.5$ discrepancy should be no surprise.

The sign conventions vary. Some authors write $p_{n+1} = p_n + K\sin\theta_n$ and others $-K\sin\theta_n$, and some put the mod on $p$ as well (making the phase space a torus rather than a cylinder). The critical constant $K_c = 0.971635\dots$ is the same in all of them; the location of the elliptic fixed point is not — in this deck's convention it is $(\pi,0)$, as problem P5 finds. This deck keeps $p$ unbounded, because the question of interest — can an orbit travel from $p=0$ to $p=2\pi$? — is meaningless on the torus.

Chirikov: when do resonances overlap?

worked example 4 · the overlap criterion, to a number

The idea. Each resonance traps orbits in a band of half-width $\Delta p$. While the bands are disjoint the motion stays local; once neighbouring bands touch, an orbit can pass from one to the next and travel across the whole cylinder.

Step 1 — the widths. $\Delta p = 2\sqrt K$, from the pendulum separatrix. Step 2 — the spacing. Primary resonances sit at $p = 2\pi m$, so $\delta p = 2\pi$.

Step 3 — the criterion. Chirikov's overlap criterion is $2\Delta p\ge\delta p$, that is $4\sqrt K\ge2\pi$, giving $K\ge K_{\text{Chirikov}} = \pi^2/4 = 2.4674$.

Step 4 — the truth. Greene's residue method gives $K_c = 0.9716354$: the criterion is high by a factor $\mathbf{2.54}$.

checks

Dimensions. $\Delta p\sim\sqrt K$ looks wrong until the kick period $T$ is restored: $\Delta p = 2\sqrt{K/T}$ against a spacing $2\pi/T$, so the criterion reads $4\sqrt{K/T}\ge2\pi/T$ and $K_c$ comes out independent of $T$ ✓.

Limit $K\to0$. Widths $\to0$, no overlap, every curve survives — the integrable rotor ✓.

Why a factor of 2.5 counts as a success. The criterion takes ten minutes, uses no computer, and applies to any system where the resonance widths can be estimated — including ones with no exact answer available. Greene's method takes a careful numerical study of periodic orbits of increasing period and applies to one map at a time. An order-of-magnitude criterion that is right within a factor of $2.5$ is the standard tool of the field, and the discrepancy is systematic and in a known direction, which makes it usable: treat $K_{\text{Chirikov}}$ as an upper bound and expect the truth at roughly $0.4$ of it.

Greene's method, in outline, since the number is quoted. For each rational $p/q$ near the golden mean, find the periodic orbit of $P$ with that rotation number and compute the residue $R = \tfrac14(2 - \operatorname{tr}DP^q)$, which is $0$ for a parabolic orbit, positive-and-small for elliptic and negative for hyperbolic. Greene's observation, later substantially proved by MacKay, is that the golden torus exists precisely when the residues of the convergent orbits tend to $0$, and is destroyed when they diverge. The threshold between the two behaviours is $K_c = 0.971635406$, obtained by bisection on $K$. Cited: Greene, J. Math. Phys. 20 (1979) 1183; MacKay, Renormalisation in Area-Preserving Maps, 1993.

why it converges from above, and slowly

Above $K_c$ the broken torus does not vanish: it becomes an invariant cantorus, a Cantor set with gaps, and orbits leak through the gaps at a rate that vanishes as $K\downarrow K_c$ like $(K-K_c)^{3.01}$ (cited: MacKay, Meiss and Percival 1984). the check that the picture is right

$(1.020-K_c)/(0.983-K_c) = 4.26$, and $4.26^{3.01} = 78$, against the hundredfold change in $N$ the exponent predicts. The same order, within the crudeness of "first escape in $N$ steps" ✓.

Why this is the right way to look for a torus. Not by staring at a plot: an orbit confined to a band looks the same whether it is confined by a curve or is simply slow. The transport test asks a yes/no question with a definite answer, and its failure mode — reporting "no transport" when the flux is merely tiny — is in a known direction and quantified by the scaling law above. Designing the experiment so that its errors all point one way is the difference between a measurement and an anecdote.

The cantorus is the object that makes the transition continuous. Below $K_c$ there is an invariant circle and the flux is exactly zero. Above, there is an invariant Cantor set with the same rotation number and the flux is positive but tiny. Nothing jumps; what changes is the topology of the invariant set. This is the picture Aubry and Mather constructed independently around 1982, and it is why the transition can be both sharp and continuous at once.

what is actually known

what a $5$-Myr Lyapunov time costs

Over $100$ Myr, an error is multiplied by $e^{100/5} = e^{20} = 4.85\times10^{8}$. So an initial uncertainty of one metre in a planet's position becomes $$4.85\times10^{8}\ \mathrm m = 4.85\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{km},$$ about $1.3$ times the Earth–Moon distance. Positions beyond roughly $100$ Myr are not predictable by any integration, however good.

Which claim this is. Not that the planets move away — that the phase is lost. Statistical statements survive, which is why item 3 is a frequency.

the honest summary

Marginally stable: chaotic on $10^7$ years, apparently intact on $10^9$, with a small real chance of losing a planet. Newton's laws determine all of it, and the phase of none of it can be computed.

How a Lyapunov exponent is measured for the solar system. Exactly as in section 3: integrate the variational equation alongside the equations of motion and renormalise. The difficulty is that the integration itself must be trustworthy for $10^8$ years, which is why these calculations use symplectic integrators — unit 14's remark that a good integrator for mechanics should preserve the symplectic structure, cashed in. A non-symplectic method drifts in energy and produces a spurious exponent.

What Laplace and Lagrange proved, and did not. Their secular theory shows that to first order in the masses the semi-major axes have no secular drift — the planets do not spiral in or out. That result is correct and still used. What it does not control is the eccentricities and inclinations at higher order, which is where the chaos lives, and it is the small denominators of section 4 that block the extension. The nineteenth century's confidence in stability rested on stopping the series at the order where it still looked convergent.

An honest caveat about item 3. The $1\%$ is a frequency among numerically integrated solutions with slightly different initial conditions, not a probability in any physical sense — the actual solar system has one history. It is the correct thing to compute when the Lyapunov time is a thousandth of the integration time, and the correct way to read it is: the equations plus present-day data do not exclude Mercury's loss, and they do exclude, say, the Earth's, in every solution computed.

The running example, closed

what was asked in 1964

Does Hénon–Heiles have a second integral? The answer this unit produced:

At $E = 1/100$ the section is nested curves; the system behaves as though it had one, and KAM explains why — most tori of the integrable limit survive a small perturbation.

At $E = 1/8$ a single orbit's crossings fill the chaotic sea, $52\%$ of the section, and its exponent is $0.044$, while an orbit a quarter of the section away has exponent zero, confirmed by a growth law with slope $0.997$.

No smooth function independent of $H$ can be constant on both. There is no second integral, and the numbers say so rather than the picture.

what was not settled

That the chaotic region has positive area is not proved here or anywhere. Numerics show it; nobody has a proof for a specific smooth Hamiltonian system of this kind.

The $t\sim500$ figure. Taking the initial data as known to one part in $10^{9}$, as a good physical measurement might be, the uncertainty reaches order unity at $t = \ln(10^{9})/0.044 = 471$ — about $75$ orbits. Even with perfect double-precision data, uncertain at $2.2\times10^{-16}$, it is $t = 819$. Either way a few hundred time units, and no improvement in the integrator changes it: nine more digits buy $471$ more time units.

What Hénon and Heiles themselves concluded. That a third isolating integral exists for low energies and not for high ones, and that the transition is sharp. Their paper reports the fraction of the section covered by curves as a function of energy, which is the ancestor of this unit's table. They were computing on an IBM 7090 in 1964, and their pictures have the same features as the ones here.

Where this leaves the course. Part B's thesis is that a reformulation is worth exactly what it makes obvious. The Hamiltonian formulation made this unit possible: the section, its area preservation, the symmetry of the Lyapunov spectrum, action–angle variables and the whole perturbation theory are statements about the symplectic structure, and none of them is visible in Newton's equations or even in Lagrange's. That is the best argument for Part B that the course can make, and it is why this branch unit sits here rather than anywhere else.

three habits, not results

Quote the budget with the number. A Lyapunov exponent without its $T$, or a threshold without its iteration count, is not a measurement.

For the third part, the failure mode to watch for in your own answer is an appeal to practicality — "in practice you cannot measure the initial condition well enough". That is true and is not the point. The point is a quantifier: unit 01 promises a $\delta$ for each $T$, and never a single $\delta$ good for all $T$. If your explanation would still work with infinitely precise measurement and exact arithmetic, it is the right one.

If the second part stalls, the missing piece is usually the direction of the inference. Write the argument out: if a second integral existed on an open set, then every orbit's closure in the section would lie in a level curve, which has empty interior; an orbit whose crossings are dense in a disc therefore refutes it. Curves, by contrast, refute nothing — which is why the exponent has to be quoted alongside.
