The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: a guitar's top E string, \(L = 0.6477\) m,
\(\mu = 3.978\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}\), \(T = 72.53\) N, hence
\(c = \sqrt{T/\mu} = 427.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(f_1 = 329.6\) Hz;
plucked to \(h = 3\) mm at \(a = L/5\), with period \(2L/c = 3.034\) ms and
total energy \(3.149\) mJ.

* From N masses to a field

- field :: A quantity defined at every point of space and every time, here the
  transverse displacement \(u(x,t)\) of the string. The unknown of the theory is
  a function of two variables rather than a finite list of functions of \(t\), and
  that is the whole difference between Parts A–B and Part C. /Slide: One string,
  four questions./
- wavenumber :: \(q\), the spatial analogue of frequency: the mode
  \(\sin(qx)\) repeats after a wavelength \(2\pi/q\). For a fixed–fixed string of
  length \(L\) the allowed values are \(q_k = k\pi/L\). Dimension
  \(\mathrm m^{-1}\). /Slide: Unit 07's chain, one more time./
- dispersion relation :: The function \(\omega(q)\) giving a medium's frequency
  at each wavenumber. Unit 07's chain has the exact relation
  \(\omega(q) = (2c/a)\sin(qa/2)\); the string has \(\omega = cq\). /Slide:
  Unit 07's chain, one more time./
- second-difference operator :: \(\big(f(x+a) - 2f(x) + f(x-a)\big)/a^2\). For
  \(f \in C^4\) it equals \(f''(x) + \tfrac{a^2}{12}f^{(4)}(\xi)\): second-order
  accurate, because it is even in \(a\) and every odd Taylor term cancels.
  /Slide: The second difference is a second derivative./
- wave equation :: \(u_{tt} = c^2 u_{xx}\), with \(c = \sqrt{T/\mu}\) for a
  string of tension \(T\) and mass per unit length \(\mu\). Derived three times in
  this unit: as a continuum limit, from a force balance, and from an action.
  /Slide: The limit, and the equation it leaves./
- non-dispersive :: Of a medium in which \(\omega = cq\) exactly, so every
  wavenumber travels at the same speed and a pulse keeps its shape. The ideal
  string is non-dispersive. /Slide: What the limit threw away./
- dispersive :: Of a medium in which \(\omega/q\) depends on \(q\), so a pulse
  spreads. The bead chain is dispersive, with \(\omega/cq = 1 - (qa)^2/24 + \cdots\).
  Nothing to do with damping: a dispersive medium may be perfectly conservative.
  /Slide: What the limit threw away./

* The string itself

- small-slope approximation :: Replacing \(\sqrt{1+u_x^2}\) by \(1\) in the exact
  transverse equation, which is what turns it from nonlinear into the wave
  equation. The assumption is on the dimensionless slope \(u_x\), never on \(u\);
  the relative error is \(2u_x^2 + O(u_x^4)\), and for the running example it is
  \(1.07\times10^{-3}\), or \(0.11\) per cent. /Slide: The small-slope approximation, and its size./

* What kind of equation is this?

- principal symbol :: For \(P = \sum A^{ij}\partial_i\partial_j + \sum B^i\partial_i + C\)
  with \(A\) symmetric, the quadratic form \(\sigma_P(\xi) = \sum A^{ij}\xi_i\xi_j\)
  on the dual space. It is the operator's action on fast oscillations:
  \(P(v e^{\mathrm i\lambda\xi\cdot x}) = -\lambda^2\sigma_P(\xi)\,v e^{\mathrm i\lambda\xi\cdot x} + O(\lambda)\).
  /Slide: The principal symbol./
- characteristic covector :: A nonzero \(\xi\) with \(\sigma_P(\xi) = 0\): a
  direction of oscillation the leading part of the operator does not control.
  /Slide: The principal symbol./
- discriminant :: For \(a\,u_{xx} + 2b\,u_{xy} + c\,u_{yy}\), the number
  \(D = b^2 - ac = -\det A\). Its /sign/ is invariant under change of coordinates,
  because \(\det \tilde A = (\det J)^2\det A\), and that sign is the type. /Slide:
  Three types, in two variables./
- hyperbolic :: Symbol nondegenerate and indefinite (\(D \gt 0\) in two
  variables): two real families of characteristics, finite propagation speed,
  Cauchy data on a non-characteristic curve. Model: \(u_{tt} - c^2u_{xx} = 0\).
  /Slide: Three types, in two variables./
- parabolic :: Symbol degenerate (\(D = 0\)): one family of characteristics,
  infinite propagation speed, instant smoothing, one time direction only. Model:
  \(u_t - ku_{xx} = 0\), whose arrow of time lives in the lower-order term and not
  in the symbol. /Slide: Three types, in two variables./
- elliptic :: Symbol definite (\(D \lt 0\)): no real characteristics, solutions
  real-analytic inside, data on the whole boundary of a region. Model:
  \(u_{xx} + u_{yy} = 0\). Cauchy data on a curve makes it ill posed. /Slide:
  Three types, in two variables./
- characteristic curve :: A curve \(\psi = \mathrm{const}\) whose conormal
  \(\mathrm d\psi\) is characteristic. Its slope obeys the /ordinary/ equation
  \(a\,m^2 - 2b\,m + c = 0\), \(m = \mathrm dy/\mathrm dx\). For the wave operator,
  the lines \(x \mp ct = \mathrm{const}\). /Slide: Reduction to canonical form./
- canonical form :: What an equation becomes in coordinates adapted to its
  characteristics, up to lower-order terms: \(u_{\xi\eta} = \Phi\) (hyperbolic),
  \(u_{\eta\eta} = \Phi\) (parabolic), \(u_{\alpha\alpha} + u_{\beta\beta} = \Phi\)
  (elliptic). Three equations, and no fourth. /Slide: Reduction to canonical
  form./
- Cauchy data :: A function and its normal derivative prescribed on a curve —
  the PDE analogue of position and velocity at an instant. Legitimate for a
  hyperbolic equation on a non-characteristic curve; fatal for an elliptic one.
  /Slide: What each type will accept./

* d'Alembert

- domain of dependence :: The set of initial points whose data can affect
  \(u(x_0,t_0)\): the interval \([x_0 - ct_0,\ x_0 + ct_0]\), read straight off
  d'Alembert's formula. /Slide: Domain of dependence, range of influence./
- range of influence :: The set of spacetime points that data at \(x_0\) can
  affect: the cone \(|x - x_0| \le c|t|\). The domain of dependence turned upside
  down. /Slide: Domain of dependence, range of influence./
- finite propagation speed :: The theorem that data vanishing outside
  \([\alpha,\beta]\) gives a solution vanishing outside
  \([\alpha - c|t|,\ \beta + c|t|]\). Not an assumption: \(c\) entered the equation
  as \(\sqrt{T/\mu}\), a ratio of static material properties, and its role as a
  speed limit is deduced. /Slide: Domain of dependence, range of influence./

* The finite string, by images

- method of images :: Solving a boundary-value problem on \([0,L]\) by extending
  the data to the whole line so that the free-space solution automatically
  satisfies the boundary conditions: oddly for a fixed end, evenly for a free one.
  /Slide: A fixed end is an odd extension./
- odd periodic extension :: The extension of \(f\) on \([0,L]\) that is odd about
  \(0\) and \(2L\)-periodic — hence also odd about \(L\). It requires the
  compatibility conditions \(f(0) = f(L) = 0\), and it makes every motion of the
  fixed–fixed string periodic with period \(2L/c\). /Slide: A fixed end is an odd
  extension./
- initial-boundary-value problem :: An evolution equation with data at one time
  /and/ conditions at the spatial ends, for all time. The string's problem; not to
  be confused with the pure Cauchy problem on the whole line. /Slide: A fixed end
  is an odd extension./
- reflection with inversion :: What a pulse does at a fixed end: it returns
  reversed left-to-right /and/ upside down. At a free end it returns the same way
  up. Same amplitude in both cases. /Slide: A free end is an even extension./
- Dirichlet boundary condition :: \(u = 0\) at an end — the string is tied down.
  /Slide: A free end is an even extension./
- Neumann boundary condition :: \(u_x = 0\) at an end — the transverse tension
  \(Tu_x\) vanishes, so the end is free. Section 8 derives it as unit 11's natural
  boundary condition rather than assuming it. /Slide: A free end is an even
  extension./

* Energy

- energy density :: \(e = \tfrac12\mu u_t^2 + \tfrac12Tu_x^2\), in
  \(\mathrm{J\,m^{-1}}\): kinetic plus elastic energy per unit length. Equals the
  Legendre transform \(u_t\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial u_t - \mathcal L\) of the
  Lagrangian density. /Slide: Energy density and energy flux./
- energy flux :: \(S = -T\,u_t u_x\), in watts: the power carried past a point in
  the \(+x\) direction. For a right-moving wave \(S = c\,e\); for a left-moving one
  \(S = -c\,e\). /Slide: Energy density and energy flux./
- local conservation law :: \(\partial_t e + \partial_x S = 0\): the energy in any
  region changes only by flowing through its boundary. The template for every
  conservation law later in the course, including \(\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0\).
  /Slide: Energy density and energy flux./
- Leibniz's rule (differentiation under the integral sign) :: If \(F\) and
  \(\partial_t F\) are continuous on \([0,L]\times[t_1,t_2]\) then
  \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\int_0^L F\,\mathrm dx = \int_0^L \partial_t F\,\mathrm dx\).
  Proved in this unit from uniform continuity on a compact rectangle. /Slide:
  Differentiating under the integral sign./
- energy method :: Proving uniqueness by showing the difference of two solutions
  has zero energy: the energy is conserved, nonnegative, and vanishes only when
  \(w_t \equiv w_x \equiv 0\). It replaces unit 01's Lipschitz argument, needs no
  formula for the solution, and also yields continuous dependence and finite
  propagation speed. /Slide: Uniqueness, by energy./

* Well-posedness

- well-posedness :: Hadamard's three conditions on a problem "data \(\mapsto\)
  solution": existence, uniqueness, and continuity of the map in named norms.
  Naming the norms is part of the statement. /Slide: Hadamard's three
  conditions./
- continuous dependence :: Hadamard's third clause, and the one with content:
  small changes in the data give small changes in the solution. Proved for every
  Lipschitz ODE in unit 01; not automatic for a PDE. /Slide: Hadamard's three
  conditions./
- ill-posed :: Failing any one of the three conditions. The Cauchy problem for
  Laplace's equation fails only the third, and does so with data
  \(\tfrac1n\sin(nx)\) whose solution at unit distance is \(\sinh(n)/n^2\):
  \(6\times10^{5}\) at \(n = 20\). /Slide: Hadamard's example: Cauchy data on
  Laplace./

* The string as a field theory

- Lagrangian density :: \(\mathcal L = \tfrac12\mu u_t^2 - \tfrac12Tu_x^2\), a
  number attached to a /point/, built from the field and its first derivatives
  there. Locality is the whole content, and is why field theory can be made
  compatible with relativity. /Slide: From the chain's Lagrangian to a density./
- action (for a field) :: \(\mathcal S[u] = \int\!\!\int \mathcal L\,\mathrm dx\,\mathrm dt\)
  over a spacetime rectangle. Its stationary points satisfy
  \(\partial_t\mathcal L_{u_t} + \partial_x\mathcal L_{u_x} - \mathcal L_u = 0\),
  which for the string is \(\mu u_{tt} = Tu_{xx}\). /Slide: The Euler–Lagrange
  equation for a field./
