The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: a circular drum of radius \(a\), clamped at the rim, obeying
\(\sigma u_{tt} = T\nabla^2u\) with \(c = \sqrt{T/\sigma}\). Numbers:
\(a = 0.150\) m, \(\sigma = 0.265\) kg m\(^{-2}\), \(T = 3200\) N m\(^{-1}\),
\(c = 109.89\) m s\(^{-1}\), fundamental \(280.39\) Hz. Its radial problem is
the Sturm–Liouville problem \(p = w = r\), \(q = m^2/r\), singular at the
centre and Dirichlet at the rim.

* Separation of variables

- separation of variables :: Seeking solutions of a linear PDE as a product of
  functions of one variable each, \(u(x,t) = X(x)T(t)\). The PDE becomes a
  family of ODEs indexed by a constant, which the boundary conditions then
  restrict to a discrete set. /Slide: Splitting the string./
- separation constant :: The common value \(-\lambda\) of the two sides after
  the variables have been separated. It is a constant because one side depends
  only on \(x\) and the other only on \(t\); it turns out to be an eigenvalue.
- eigenvalues :: The separation constants \(\lambda\) for which the spatial
  problem has a nonzero solution obeying the boundary conditions. For the
  clamped string, \(\lambda_n = (n\pi/L)^2\) and nothing else. /Slide: The
  boundary conditions do the selecting./
- eigenfunctions :: The nonzero solutions that go with them — here
  \(X_n = \sin(n\pi x/L)\). Determined up to a scalar when the eigenvalue is
  simple.
- normal mode :: One product solution \(u_n = X_n(x)(\alpha\cos\omega_nt +
  \beta\sin\omega_nt)\): every point of the object oscillating at one frequency
  with a fixed shape. Unit 07's normal modes of a chain of masses, in the
  continuum.
- Bessel's equation :: \(s^2\ddot R + s\dot R + (s^2 - m^2)R = 0\), solved by
  Frobenius in unit 09, with solutions \(J_m\) (regular at the origin) and
  \(Y_m\) (logarithmically singular). It is what the drum's radial factor obeys
  after separating in polar coordinates. /Slide: The drum, separated./

* Orthogonality and the best approximation

- weight :: A continuous \(w > 0\) defining the inner product \(\langle f,g
  \rangle_w = \int_a^b fgw\). It is never a free choice: the geometry supplies
  it — \(\mathrm dx\) on an interval, \(r\,\mathrm dr\) on a disc, the mass
  density for a non-uniform string — and it is the same \(w\) that appears in
  the Sturm–Liouville equation. /Slide: An inner product on a space of
  functions./
- best approximation :: Among all combinations of \(e_1,\dots,e_N\), the one
  closest to \(f\) in norm has the coefficients \(\hat f_n = \langle f,e_n
  \rangle/\lVert e_n\rVert^2\), and then \(f - S_Nf \perp e_1,\dots,e_N\).
  Proved by completing the square, which is where orthogonality is spent.
  /Slide: Coefficients are a projection, and that is optimal./
- Fourier coefficient :: \(\hat f_n = \langle f,e_n\rangle/\lVert e_n\rVert^2\),
  the coordinate of \(f\) along \(e_n\). Defined for any orthogonal system in
  any inner-product space; independent of how many other terms are kept.
- Bessel's inequality :: \(\sum_n \hat f_n^2\lVert e_n\rVert^2 \le \lVert
  f\rVert^2\), for every \(f\) and every orthogonal system, with no further
  hypothesis. /Slide: Bessel's inequality, and what it hands you free./
- Riemann–Lebesgue :: Its corollary: \(\hat f_n\lVert e_n\rVert \to 0\), hence
  \(\int f\sin nx \to 0\) and \(\int f\cos nx \to 0\) for piecewise continuous
  \(f\). The engine of Dirichlet's pointwise theorem.
- completeness :: An orthogonal system is complete in \(V\) if \(\lVert f -
  S_Nf\rVert \to 0\) for every \(f \in V\). It implies that the only \(f\)
  orthogonal to every \(e_n\) is zero; the converse holds in a metrically
  complete space such as \(L^2_w\), not in general in \(PC\). A property of the
  system, not of \(f\).
  /Slide: Completeness, and Parseval./
- Parseval's identity :: \(\lVert f\rVert^2 = \sum_n\hat f_n^2\lVert
  e_n\rVert^2\): Bessel's inequality with equality, which holds for every \(f\)
  exactly when the system is complete. Physically, the energy is the sum of the
  modes' energies, with no cross terms.
- Fejér's theorem :: The averaged partial sums \(\sigma_N = \frac1{N+1}\sum_{k
  \le N}S_k\) converge uniformly to \(f\) for continuous periodic \(f\), because
  the Fejér kernel is nonnegative and concentrates. It is the route to
  completeness of the trigonometric system, and the repair for Gibbs.
- complex Fourier series :: \(f \sim \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}c_n\mathrm e^{2\pi
  \mathrm inx/L}\) with \(c_n = \frac1L\int_0^L f\mathrm e^{-2\pi\mathrm inx/L}\).
  Real \(f\) iff \(c_{-n} = \overline{c_n}\). /Slide: Sine, cosine, complex: one
  system per boundary condition./

* Convergence

- Dirichlet's theorem :: For \(2\pi\)-periodic piecewise \(C^1\) \(f\),
  \(S_Nf(x) \to \frac12(f(x+) + f(x-))\) at every \(x\): the value where \(f\)
  is continuous, the midpoint of the jump elsewhere. /Slide: Dirichlet: what
  the series converges to at a jump./
- piecewise \(C^1\) :: Continuous except at finitely many points, with \(f'\)
  piecewise continuous and one-sided limits \(f(x\pm)\), \(f'(x\pm)\) everywhere.
  The hypothesis that bounds the difference quotient fed to Riemann–Lebesgue;
  continuity alone is not enough, by du Bois-Reymond's 1876 example.
- Gibbs phenomenon :: Near a jump of size \(J\) the partial sums overshoot by
  \((\frac2\pi\mathrm{Si}(\pi) - 1)\frac J2 = 0.08948987\,J\) — 8.9490 per cent
  of the jump — on each side. The overshoot does not shrink with \(N\); it only
  moves closer to the jump, at distance \(\pi/2M\). /Slide: Worked: Gibbs, and
  the number 8.9490 %./

* Sturm–Liouville

- Sturm–Liouville operator :: \(\mathcal Lu = -(pu')' + qu\) with \(p \in C^1\),
  \(p > 0\), \(q\) real continuous. Every second-order linear equation
  \(Au'' + Bu' + Cu + \lambda Du = 0\) with \(A > 0\) takes this form after
  multiplication by \(\mu = A^{-1}\exp\int(B/A)\). /Slide: The operator, and the
  weight that comes with it./
- Sturm–Liouville problem :: Find \(\lambda\) and \(u \not\equiv 0\) with
  \(\mathcal Lu = \lambda wu\) plus boundary conditions. Equivalently an
  eigenvalue problem for \(\mathcal A = w^{-1}\mathcal L\) on the space carrying
  \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_w\) — the same \(w\) in both places, which is what
  makes \(\mathcal A\) symmetric.
- Lagrange's identity :: \(\bar v\mathcal Lu - u\overline{\mathcal Lv} =
  \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}[p(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v)]\), for any \(u,v \in
  C^2\), with no hypothesis at all. Integrating it turns symmetry of
  \(\mathcal A\) into a statement about two endpoints. /Slide: Lagrange's
  identity: one line, everything follows./
- bilinear concomitant :: \([u,v](x) = p(x)(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v)(x)\), a
  weighted Wronskian, and the only obstruction to symmetry. For real \(u,v\) it
  is \(p\) times the Wronskian of unit 06, and Abel's identity is the case where
  both solve the same equation.
- formally self-adjoint :: Symmetric up to the boundary term — true of
  \(\mathcal L\) for every \(p, q, w\), by Lagrange's identity. Genuine
  self-adjointness is then a finite-dimensional condition on the boundary data.
- separated :: Boundary conditions \(\alpha_1u(a) + \alpha_2u'(a) = 0\) and
  \(\beta_1u(b) + \beta_2u'(b) = 0\) with \(\alpha,\beta\) *real*. Each end
  constrains its own data, so the concomitant vanishes end by end; Dirichlet,
  Neumann and Robin are the cases. Realness is load-bearing: a complex
  coefficient makes the eigenvalues complex. /Slide: Three families of boundary
  conditions, and one that fails./
- periodic :: \(p(a) = p(b)\), \(u(a) = u(b)\), \(u'(a) = u'(b)\). The
  concomitant takes the same value at both ends, so the difference vanishes.
  The one family where eigenvalues need not be simple: the drum's angular
  problem has a two-dimensional eigenspace for every \(m \ge 1\).
- singular :: An endpoint where \(p \to 0\) (or \(q\) blows up, or the interval
  is unbounded). No ordinary boundary condition is imposed; boundedness of \(u\)
  and \(u'\) makes the concomitant vanish and does the same job. Bessel at
  \(r = 0\), Legendre at \(x = \pm1\).
- Sturm comparison :: If \(u'' + Qu = 0\), \(v'' + \tilde Qv = 0\) with
  \(\tilde Q \ge Q\) and \(\tilde Q \not\equiv Q\), then \(v\) vanishes between
  any two consecutive zeros of \(u\). Proved in five lines from \((u'v - uv')' =
  (\tilde Q - Q)uv \ge 0\). /Slide: Sturm's comparison theorem, and counting
  nodes./
- Sturm oscillation theorem :: For a regular problem with separated conditions
  and \(\lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \cdots\), the eigenfunction \(u_n\) has exactly
  \(n-1\) zeros in the open interval. Cited; comparison gives the monotone half
  and the Prüfer angle the exactness.

* The Rayleigh quotient

- Rayleigh quotient :: \(R[u] = \int(pu'^2 + qu^2)\big/\int wu^2 = \langle
  \mathcal Au,u\rangle_w/\langle u,u\rangle_w\), stiffness over inertia. Its
  minimum over admissible \(u\) is \(\lambda_1\), attained on the multiples of
  \(u_1\), so every trial function bounds \(\lambda_1\) from *above* and never
  below. The error is quadratic in the error of the trial function. Unit 07's
  \(v^\top Kv/v^\top Mv\), with sums become integrals. /Slide: The quotient, and
  the identity behind it./
- natural boundary condition :: What the first variation forces at an endpoint
  where nothing was imposed: the boundary term \([pu'\eta]_a^b\) must vanish for
  all \(\eta\), giving \(pu' = 0\) — the Neumann condition, arriving rather than
  being assumed. Unit 11's notion, in its second job. /Slide: Unit 11's second
  job: the eigenvalue problem is an Euler–Lagrange equation./

* Three geometries

- spherical harmonics :: \(Y_\ell^m(\theta,\varphi) = N_{\ell m}P_\ell^m(\cos
  \theta)\mathrm e^{\mathrm im\varphi}\), the eigenfunctions of the Laplacian on
  the sphere with \(-\Delta_{S^2}Y = \ell(\ell+1)Y\): \(2\ell+1\) per \(\ell\),
  orthonormal and complete in \(L^2(S^2)\) (cited). The integer \(\ell\) comes
  from demanding boundedness at the poles, where \(p = 1 - x^2\) vanishes —
  nothing is quantised by hand. /Slide: The sphere, separated./
