Fourier and Sturm–Liouville: the modes are a basis, and that is a theorem

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. Given a Sturm–Liouville problem you can, on paper: prove its eigenvalues real and its eigenfunctions \(w\)-orthogonal from Lagrange's identity, naming where the boundary conditions are spent; say how many zeros the third eigenfunction has in the open interval, and be right; bound the lowest eigenvalue from above to within a few per cent with a trial function of your own choosing, and say which side of the truth the bound is on and why; and expand a given initial profile in the eigenfunctions, stating which kind of convergence you have and the hypothesis that buys it.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Thirteen problems, about seven hours. Conventions throughout: the Sturm–Liouville operator is \(\mathcal Lu = -(pu')' + qu\) with \(p \in C^1\), \(p > 0\), \(q\) real continuous, and the problem is \(\mathcal Lu = \lambda wu\) with weight \(w > 0\); the inner product is \(\langle f,g\rangle_w = \int_a^b fg\,w\,\mathrm dx\). Fourier coefficients of a \(2\pi\)-periodic \(f\) are \(c_n = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\mathrm e^{-\mathrm inx}\mathrm dx\).

Numbers you may need: \(j_{01} = 2.4048256\), \(j_{11} = 3.8317060\), \(j_{21} = 5.1356223\), \(j_{02} = 5.5200781\) (zeros of \(J_m\)); \(J_1(j_{01}) = 0.5191475\); \(\mathrm{Si}(\pi) = 1.8519371\); \(\pi^2/6 = 1.6449341\), \(\pi^2/8 = 1.2337006\), \(\pi^4/96 = 1.0146780\); thermal diffusivity of copper \(\kappa = 1.11\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\). Fourier series, and what they converge to

P1 Three expansions of one function routine

exercises: computing coefficients, odd and even extensions, Dirichlet's theorem at an endpoint

Let \(f(x) = x\) on \([0,L]\).

  1. Compute its sine series \(\sum_{n\ge1}\alpha_n\sin(n\pi x/L)\), its cosine series \(\tfrac{a_0}2 + \sum_{n\ge1}a_n\cos(n\pi x/L)\), and its full \(L\)-periodic series \(\tfrac{A_0}2 + \sum_{n\ge1}\big(A_n\cos\tfrac{2\pi nx}{L} + B_n\sin\tfrac{2\pi nx}{L}\big)\).
  2. For each, say what the series converges to at \(x = 0\) and at \(x = L\), and justify it from Dirichlet's theorem rather than by summing.
  3. Rank the three by their coefficient decay, and explain each rate by naming the lowest derivative of the relevant periodic extension that jumps.
  4. Only one of the three converges uniformly on \([0,L]\). Which, and why can the other two not?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three sequences of numbers, then six values (two endpoints for each series), then three rates, then one letter with a reason. Nothing here needs a theorem you have not met: the coefficient formulas are the projection formulas, and the endpoint values are Dirichlet's midpoint rule applied to a picture you draw yourself.

Rung 2

Before computing anything, draw the three periodic extensions of \(f(x) = x\) on a domain three periods wide: the odd \(2L\)-periodic one, the even \(2L\)-periodic one, and the \(L\)-periodic one. Mark every discontinuity and every corner. Parts 2, 3 and 4 can be read off those three pictures before a single integral is done.

Rung 3

First line of the sine series: \(\alpha_n = \frac2L\int_0^Lx\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\mathrm dx\), integrated by parts with \(u = x\) and \(\mathrm dv = \sin(n\pi x/L)\mathrm dx\); the surviving term is the boundary one, because \(\int_0^L\cos(n\pi x/L)\mathrm dx = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. One function, three different operators — Dirichlet, Neumann and periodic boundary conditions — and therefore three different eigenfunction systems. Everything that differs between the three answers is a fact about the extension, not about \(f\).

1. The coefficients. Write \(k_n = n\pi/L\).

  • Sine. \(\alpha_n = \frac2L\int_0^Lx\sin k_nx\,\mathrm dx = \frac2L\Big(\big[-\tfrac{x}{k_n}\cos k_nx\big]_0^L + \tfrac1{k_n}\int_0^L\cos k_nx\,\mathrm dx\Big) = \frac2L\cdot\big(-\tfrac{L}{k_n}(-1)^n\big) = \dfrac{2L(-1)^{n+1}}{n\pi}.\)
  • Cosine. \(a_0 = \frac2L\int_0^Lx\,\mathrm dx = L\). For \(n \ge 1\), \(a_n = \frac2L\int_0^Lx\cos k_nx\,\mathrm dx = \frac2L\Big(\big[\tfrac{x}{k_n}\sin k_nx\big]_0^L - \tfrac1{k_n}\int_0^L\sin k_nx\,\mathrm dx\Big) = \frac{2L\big((-1)^n - 1\big)}{n^2\pi^2},\) which is \(-4L/n^2\pi^2\) for odd \(n\) and \(0\) for even \(n\).
  • Full, period \(L\). \(A_0 = \frac2L\int_0^Lx\,\mathrm dx = L\); \(A_n = \frac2L\int_0^Lx\cos\frac{2\pi nx}{L}\mathrm dx = 0\) (the boundary term is \(\frac2L\cdot\frac{L}{2\pi n}\big[x\sin\big]_0^L = 0\) and the remaining integral vanishes); \(B_n = \frac2L\int_0^Lx\sin\frac{2\pi nx}{L}\mathrm dx = \frac2L\big[-\tfrac{Lx}{2\pi n}\cos\big]_0^L = -\dfrac{L}{\pi n}.\)

2. The endpoint values. Draw the extensions.

  • Sine \(\to\) odd \(2L\)-periodic extension. It is continuous at \(0\) with value \(0\), so the series gives \(\mathbf 0\) there — as it must, every term being \(\sin 0\). At \(x = L\) the extension jumps from \(L\) (approaching from the left) to \(-L\) (the value just right of \(L\), by oddness and \(2L\)-periodicity), so Dirichlet gives the midpoint \(\mathbf 0\) — again visible, since \(\sin n\pi = 0\).
  • Cosine \(\to\) even \(2L\)-periodic extension, which is continuous everywhere (it is the triangular wave). So the series converges to \(f\): \(\mathbf 0\) at \(x=0\) and \(\mathbf L\) at \(x = L\).
  • Full \(\to\) \(L\)-periodic extension, which jumps from \(L\) to \(0\) at every multiple of \(L\). Dirichlet gives the midpoint \(\mathbf{L/2}\) at both ends.

3. Decay. Sine: \(|\alpha_n| \sim 2L/\pi n\), rate \(1/n\), because the odd extension itself jumps (lowest jumping derivative: the zeroth). Cosine: \(|a_n| \sim 4L/\pi^2n^2\), rate \(1/n^2\), because the even extension is continuous but its derivative jumps from \(+1\) to \(-1\) at \(x = L\) (lowest jumping derivative: the first). Full: \(|B_n| = L/\pi n\), rate \(1/n\), jump in the function. So the cosine series is one power better than the other two, and this is the practical reason to prefer it.

4. Uniform convergence. Only the cosine series. Its limit is continuous and \(\sum|a_n| < \infty\), so the \(M\)-test applies. The other two cannot converge uniformly on any interval containing \(x = L\) (sine) or \(x = 0\) (full), because a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous while their limits jump there. No hypothesis on \(f\) could rescue them: the obstruction is in the extension, which the boundary conditions chose.

Check. Dimensions: \(\alpha_n\), \(a_n\), \(B_n\) all carry a length, as they must, \(f\) being a length ✓. Limit: as \(n \to \infty\) all three tend to \(0\), which is Riemann–Lebesgue ✓. A number: at \(x = L/2\) the cosine series gives \(L/2 - \frac{4L}{\pi^2}\sum_{\text{odd}}\frac{\cos(n\pi/2)}{n^2} = L/2 - 0 = L/2\), since \(\cos(n\pi/2) = 0\) for every odd \(n\) — and \(f(L/2) = L/2\) ✓.

Another way

Get the cosine series from the sine series without integrating. The even extension of \(x\) on \([0,L]\) is the triangular wave; the odd extension is the sawtooth; and the triangular wave is the integral of a square wave. Term-by-term integration is always legal (the uniform-convergence slide's note), so integrating the square wave's sine series once produces the triangular wave's cosine series, complete with the extra factor of \(n\) in the denominator. The gain of one power of \(n\) under integration is exactly the gain of one power in the decay rate.

P2 Parseval, and three sums routine

exercises: Parseval's identity, choosing the system that makes a sum fall out, norms of the trigonometric system

Using the expansions of P1 where convenient, and Parseval's identity in the form \(\lVert f\rVert^2 = \sum_n\hat f_n^2\lVert e_n\rVert^2\):

  1. Expand \(f \equiv 1\) on \([0,L]\) in sines and deduce \(\sum_{k\ge0}(2k+1)^{-2} = \pi^2/8\).
  2. From the sine series of \(f(x) = x\), deduce \(\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-2} = \pi^2/6\).
  3. From the cosine series of \(f(x) = x\), deduce \(\sum_{k\ge0}(2k+1)^{-4} = \pi^4/96\). Be careful: the constant eigenfunction has norm\(^2\) equal to \(L\), not \(L/2\).
  4. Check parts 1 and 3 against each other: \(\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-2} = \frac43\sum_{\text{odd}}n^{-2}\) and \(\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-4} = \frac{16}{15}\sum_{\text{odd}}n^{-4}\). Verify both identities, and use them to produce \(\sum n^{-4} = \pi^4/90\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four numbers, each the value of a series. Parseval is an equation with a known left side (an elementary integral) and a known right side (a sum you want); every part is the same two lines with different inputs.

Rung 2

The left side is always \(\int_0^L f^2\,\mathrm dx\) — for part 1 that is \(L\), for parts 2 and 3 it is \(L^3/3\). The right side needs the norm\(^2\) of each eigenfunction: \(\lVert\sin(n\pi x/L)\rVert^2 = \lVert\cos(n\pi x/L)\rVert^2 = L/2\) for \(n \ge 1\), but \(\lVert 1\rVert^2 = L\). Part 4 is pure arithmetic: split \(\sum n^{-s}\) into odd and even terms and notice the even part is \(2^{-s}\sum n^{-s}\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(\alpha_n = \frac2L\int_0^L\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\mathrm dx = \frac{2}{n\pi}\big(1 - (-1)^n\big)\), so \(\alpha_n = 4/n\pi\) for odd \(n\) and \(0\) for even; then Parseval reads \(L = \frac L2\sum_{\text{odd}}\frac{16}{n^2\pi^2}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Parseval turns "how big is \(f\)?", answered by a one-line integral, into "how big are its coefficients?", answered by a series. Every classical sum of this kind is that trade, done with a well-chosen \(f\).

1. With \(\alpha_n = 4/n\pi\) for odd \(n\) and \(\lVert f\rVert^2 = \int_0^L1 = L\), \[ L = \frac L2\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{16}{n^2\pi^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac1{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{8} = 1.2337006 . \]

2. \(\alpha_n = 2L(-1)^{n+1}/n\pi\) and \(\lVert f\rVert^2 = \int_0^Lx^2 = L^3/3\): \[ \frac{L^3}{3} = \frac L2\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{4L^2}{n^2\pi^2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \sum_{n\ge1}\frac1{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6} = 1.6449341 . \]

3. The cosine system is \(\{1, \cos(n\pi x/L)\}\) with \(\lVert1\rVert^2 = L\) and \(\lVert\cos\rVert^2 = L/2\). The expansion is \(x = \frac{a_0}2 + \sum a_n\cos\), so the coefficient along the eigenfunction \(1\) is \(a_0/2 = L/2\). Parseval: \[ \frac{L^3}{3} = \Big(\frac L2\Big)^2\! L + \frac L2\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{16L^2}{n^4\pi^4} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{L^3}{3} - \frac{L^3}{4} = \frac{8L^3}{\pi^4}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac1{n^4} , \] and \(\frac13 - \frac14 = \frac1{12}\), so \(\sum_{\text{odd}}n^{-4} = \pi^4/96 = 1.0146780\).

4. For \(s > 1\), \(\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-s} = \sum_{\text{odd}} + \sum_{\text{even}} = \sum_{\text{odd}} + 2^{-s}\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-s}\), so \(\sum_{\text{odd}}n^{-s} = (1 - 2^{-s})\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-s}\). At \(s = 2\): factor \(3/4\), so \(\sum n^{-2} = \frac43\sum_{\text{odd}}n^{-2} = \frac43\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{8} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}\) ✓, consistent with part 2. At \(s = 4\): factor \(15/16\), so \(\sum n^{-4} = \frac{16}{15}\cdot\frac{\pi^4}{96} = \frac{\pi^4}{90} = 1.0823232\).

Check. Dimensions: every Parseval identity above has \(L^3\) or \(L\) on both sides ✓. Numbers: \(\pi^2/8 = 1.2337006\) against the partial sum \(1 + \frac19 + \frac1{25} + \frac1{49} + \frac1{81} = 1.1839\), converging slowly from below as Bessel's inequality demands ✓. \(\pi^4/90 = 1.0823232\) against \(1 + \frac1{16} + \frac1{81} = 1.0748\) ✓. Limit: both sums exceed their first term and are below it plus \(\int_1^\infty x^{-s}\), i.e. \(\pi^2/6 \in (1,2)\) and \(\pi^4/90 \in (1, 4/3)\) ✓.

Another way

Part 3 without the cosine series: apply Parseval to the derivative instead. The triangular wave's derivative is the square wave, and the coefficients differ by a factor \(n\), so \(\sum_{\text{odd}}n^{-4}\) and \(\sum_{\text{odd}}n^{-2}\) are the Parseval sums of a function and its derivative. That is the smoothness–decay dictionary being read as an arithmetic identity, and it generalises: integrating the square wave \(k\) times evaluates \(\sum_{\text{odd}}n^{-2k}\).

P3 Hear the coefficients medium

exercises: coefficients of a piecewise-linear profile, truncation error by Parseval, reading a decay rate as a sound

A string of length \(L\), fixed at both ends, with wave speed \(c\).

  1. Plucked. It is pulled aside by \(h\) at \(x = a\) and released from rest. Show that the mode amplitudes are \[ \alpha_n = \frac{2hL^2}{\pi^2a(L-a)}\cdot\frac{1}{n^2}\,\sin\frac{n\pi a}{L}. \]
  2. Take \(a = L/7\). Show \(\alpha_7 = 0\) exactly, and tabulate \(\alpha_n/\alpha_1\) for \(n = 1,\dots,8\) to three decimals.
  3. Struck. Now the string starts flat and is hit by a hammer of half-width \(\varepsilon\) centred at \(x = a\), which gives it velocity \(V\) on \((a-\varepsilon, a+\varepsilon)\) and zero elsewhere. Show that the resulting mode amplitudes are \(\beta_n \propto n^{-2}\sin(n\pi a/L)\sin(n\pi\varepsilon/L)\), and that for \(n \ll L/\pi\varepsilon\) this behaves as \(n^{-1}\sin(n\pi a/L)\).
  4. Using Parseval, show that truncating at \(N\) modes leaves a relative mean-square error \(\sim N^{-3}\) for the pluck and \(\sim N^{-1}\) for the strike (in its \(n^{-1}\) regime). Evaluate both at \(N = 10\) with \(a = L/7\) and say which instrument sounds brighter, and why.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a formula; part 2 a table of eight numbers; part 3 a formula again; part 4 two numbers and a one-sentence verdict. The only physics is that a pluck sets \(u(x,0)\) and a strike sets \(u_t(x,0)\); everything else is integration by parts and Parseval.

Rung 2

For a strike, the general solution \(u = \sum\sin(n\pi x/L)(\alpha_n\cos\omega_nt + \beta_n\sin\omega_nt)\) has \(u_t(x,0) = \sum\omega_n\beta_n\sin(n\pi x/L)\). So expand the velocity profile in sines to get \(\omega_n\beta_n\), then divide by \(\omega_n = n\pi c/L\). That division is where the extra power of \(n\) is lost — the whole answer to part 4 is in it.

Rung 3

Part 4: relative mean-square truncation error is \(\big(\sum_{n>N}\alpha_n^2\big)\big/\big(\sum_{n\ge1}\alpha_n^2\big)\), because Parseval makes \(\lVert f\rVert^2\) and \(\lVert f - S_Nf\rVert^2\) into those sums (the common factor \(L/2\) cancels). Then \(\sum_{n>N}n^{-4} \approx \int_N^\infty x^{-4}\mathrm dx = \frac1{3N^3}\) and \(\sum_{n>N}n^{-2} \approx 1/N\); the \(\sin^2(n\pi a/L)\) factor averages to \(\tfrac12\) and does not change the rate.

Rung 4

First line of part 3: \(\dfrac{2}{L}\displaystyle\int_{a-\varepsilon}^{a+\varepsilon}\!V\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\mathrm dx = \frac{2V}{L}\cdot\frac{L}{n\pi}\Big[\cos\frac{n\pi(a-\varepsilon)}{L} - \cos\frac{n\pi(a+\varepsilon)}{L}\Big] = \frac{4V}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi a}{L}\sin\frac{n\pi\varepsilon}{L},\) using \(\cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B) = 2\sin A\sin B\).

Worked solution

Idea. Timbre is a decay rate. A pluck puts a kink into the string, a strike puts a step into its velocity, and one integration by parts separates the two: the kink gives \(n^{-2}\), the step-in-velocity gives \(n^{-1}\) after dividing by \(\omega_n \propto n\).

1. The initial shape is \(f = hx/a\) on \([0,a]\) and \(h(L-x)/(L-a)\) on \([a,L]\), and \(\alpha_n = \frac2L\int_0^Lf\sin k_nx\,\mathrm dx\) with \(k_n = n\pi/L\). On the first leg, \(\int_0^ax\sin k_nx\,\mathrm dx = \frac{\sin k_na}{k_n^2} - \frac{a\cos k_na}{k_n}\). On the second, substituting \(s = L - x\) and using \(\sin k_nL = 0\), \(\cos k_nL = (-1)^n\), \(\int_a^L(L-x)\sin k_nx\,\mathrm dx = \frac{\sin k_na}{k_n^2} + \frac{(L-a)\cos k_na}{k_n}\). Multiply the first by \(2h/La\) and the second by \(2h/L(L-a)\) and add: the \(\cos k_na\) terms are \(-\frac{2h}{Lk_n}\) and \(+\frac{2h}{Lk_n}\) and cancel, while the \(\sin k_na\) terms give \(\frac{2h}{Lk_n^2}\big(\frac1a + \frac1{L-a}\big)\sin k_na = \frac{2hL}{k_n^2a(L-a)}\sin k_na\). With \(k_n^2 = n^2\pi^2/L^2\) that is the stated formula. ∎

2. \(\sin(7\pi/7) = \sin\pi = 0\), so \(\alpha_7 = 0\) exactly. With \(\alpha_n/\alpha_1 = n^{-2}\sin(n\pi/7)/\sin(\pi/7)\) and \(\sin(\pi/7) = 0.4338837\):

3. A strike sets \(u(x,0) = 0\) and \(u_t(x,0) = g\), so \(\alpha_n = 0\) and \(\omega_n\beta_n = \frac2L\int_0^Lg\sin k_nx\,\mathrm dx\). With \(g = V\) on \((a-\varepsilon, a+\varepsilon)\), \[ \omega_n\beta_n = \frac{2V}{L}\cdot\frac{L}{n\pi}\Big[\cos k_n(a-\varepsilon) - \cos k_n(a+\varepsilon)\Big] = \frac{4V}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi a}{L}\sin\frac{n\pi\varepsilon}{L}. \] Divide by \(\omega_n = n\pi c/L\): \(\beta_n = \dfrac{4VL}{\pi^2c}\cdot\dfrac{1}{n^2}\sin\dfrac{n\pi a}{L}\sin\dfrac{n\pi\varepsilon}{L}\), which is the stated proportionality. For \(n\pi\varepsilon/L \ll 1\), \(\sin(n\pi\varepsilon/L) \approx n\pi\varepsilon/L\), and one power of \(n\) is eaten: \(\beta_n \approx \frac{4V\varepsilon}{\pi c}\cdot\frac1n\sin\frac{n\pi a}{L}\). Beyond \(n \approx L/\pi\varepsilon\) the second sine stops growing and the true \(n^{-2}\) resumes — a wide, soft hammer is a dull one, and that is the design parameter.

4. By Parseval the relative mean-square truncation error is \(E(N) = \sum_{n>N}\alpha_n^2 \big/ \sum_{n\ge1}\alpha_n^2\). For the pluck, \(\alpha_n^2 \propto n^{-4}\sin^2(n\pi a/L)\) and \(\sum_{n>N}n^{-4} \approx \frac1{3N^3}\); for the strike in its \(n^{-1}\) regime, \(\beta_n^2 \propto n^{-2}\sin^2\) and \(\sum_{n>N}n^{-2} \approx \frac1N\). So \(E \sim N^{-3}\) and \(E \sim N^{-1}\) respectively.

Another way

Part 2 without any arithmetic: a mode with a node at \(x = a\) cannot be excited by anything done at \(x = a\), because its amplitude is a projection against \(\sin(n\pi a/L)\), which is the mode's own displacement there. Mode 7 has nodes at \(L/7, 2L/7, \dots\); plucking at \(L/7\) therefore misses it entirely. The same sentence is Helmholtz's explanation of why piano hammers strike near \(L/7\) to \(L/9\): a strike at or near a node of the seventh partial weakens it, and the seventh is the first partial that lies well off the tempered scale.

P4 Smoothness, decay, and the cost of differentiating medium

exercises: integration by parts on coefficients, proving a decay bound, building a counterexample

  1. Prove that if \(f\) is \(2\pi\)-periodic, of class \(C^{k-1}\), and \(f^{(k)}\) is piecewise continuous, then \(c_n(f^{(k)}) = (\mathrm in)^kc_n(f)\), and deduce both \(|c_n| = o(|n|^{-k})\) and the explicit bound \(\lVert f - S_Nf\rVert_2 \le \lVert f^{(k)}\rVert_2\big/N^k\).
  2. Where exactly does the hypothesis \(f \in C^{k-1}\) enter? Show by example that dropping it (keeping only "\(f^{(k)}\) piecewise continuous where defined") destroys the conclusion.
  3. Prove that \(g(x) = \sum_{n\ge1}n^{-2}\sin(n^2x)\) converges uniformly on \(\mathbb R\), so that \(g\) is continuous and \(2\pi\)-periodic.
  4. Show that the term-by-term derivative of that series diverges at every \(x\). Which hypothesis of the term-by-term differentiation theorem does \(g\) fail — continuity, or piecewise \(C^1\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1, 3 and 4 are proofs; part 2 is a counterexample plus one sentence. Everything rests on a single computation — integrating \(\int f'\mathrm e^{-\mathrm inx}\) by parts — and on watching what the boundary term does.

Rung 2

For part 1, do one step and then induct. For the explicit bound, use Parseval on \(f^{(k)}\): \(\sum_n n^{2k}|c_n|^2 = \lVert f^{(k)}\rVert_2^2/2\pi\), and then bound \(\sum_{|n|>N}|c_n|^2\) by pulling \(N^{-2k}\) out. For part 3, the \(M\)-test with \(M_n = n^{-2}\). For part 4, ask what the \(n\)-th term of \(\sum\cos(n^2x)\) does as \(n \to \infty\) — a series whose terms do not tend to zero cannot converge, and that is the whole argument once you know \(\cos(n^2x) \not\to 0\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(2\pi c_n(f') = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f'\mathrm e^{-\mathrm inx}\mathrm dx = \big[f\mathrm e^{-\mathrm inx}\big]_{-\pi}^{\pi} + \mathrm in\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f\mathrm e^{-\mathrm inx}\mathrm dx\), and the bracket is \((-1)^n\big(f(\pi) - f(-\pi)\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. One integration by parts trades a derivative for a factor of \(\mathrm in\). Every statement in this problem is that trade, run forwards (smoothness buys decay) or backwards (differentiating a series costs decay), with the boundary term deciding whether the trade is legal.

1. One step: for \(f\) continuous, \(2\pi\)-periodic with \(f'\) piecewise continuous, \[ 2\pi c_n(f') = \int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\!f'\mathrm e^{-\mathrm inx}\mathrm dx = \big[f\mathrm e^{-\mathrm inx}\big]_{-\pi}^{\pi} + \mathrm in\!\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\!f\mathrm e^{-\mathrm inx}\mathrm dx = 0 + 2\pi\,\mathrm in\,c_n(f), \] the bracket vanishing because \(\mathrm e^{-\mathrm in\pi} = \mathrm e^{\mathrm in\pi}\) and \(f(\pi) = f(-\pi)\). (If \(f'\) has finitely many jumps, split the integral at them; the interior boundary terms cancel in pairs because \(f\) is continuous.) Now induct: \(f \in C^{k-1}\) means \(f, f', \dots, f^{(k-2)}\) are all continuous and periodic, so the step applies \(k\) times and \(c_n(f^{(k)}) = (\mathrm in)^kc_n(f)\).

Bessel's inequality for \(f^{(k)}\) gives \(\sum_n n^{2k}|c_n(f)|^2 = \sum_n|c_n(f^{(k)})|^2 < \infty\); the terms of a convergent series tend to zero, so \(n^k|c_n| \to 0\), i.e. \(|c_n| = o(|n|^{-k})\). For the error bound, Parseval for \(f^{(k)}\) reads \(\sum_n|c_n(f^{(k)})|^2 = \lVert f^{(k)}\rVert_2^2/2\pi\), and \[ \lVert f - S_Nf\rVert_2^2 = 2\pi\!\!\sum_{|n|>N}\!|c_n|^2 \;\le\; \frac{2\pi}{N^{2k}}\!\!\sum_{|n|>N}\! n^{2k}|c_n|^2 \;\le\; \frac{2\pi}{N^{2k}}\cdot\frac{\lVert f^{(k)}\rVert_2^2}{2\pi} = \frac{\lVert f^{(k)}\rVert_2^2}{N^{2k}} . \ \blacksquare\]

2. It enters only in killing the boundary term — that is its entire job, and it is not a formality. Take the sawtooth \(f(x) = x\) on \((-\pi,\pi)\), extended \(2\pi\)-periodically. Off the jumps \(f' \equiv 1\), which is as piecewise continuous as anything could be, so the naive reading of the hypothesis holds with \(k = 1\). But \(c_n(f') = c_n(1) = 0\) for \(n \ne 0\), whereas \(\mathrm inc_n(f) = \mathrm in\cdot\frac{\mathrm i(-1)^n}{n} = -(-1)^n \ne 0\). The identity fails, and with it the decay: \(|c_n| = 1/|n|\) is not \(o(n^{-1})\). The missing piece is exactly \(\big[f\mathrm e^{-\mathrm inx}\big]_{-\pi}^{\pi} = (-1)^n(\pi - (-\pi)) = 2\pi(-1)^n\).

3. \(|n^{-2}\sin(n^2x)| \le n^{-2} =: M_n\) for all \(x\), and \(\sum M_n = \pi^2/6 < \infty\). By the Weierstrass \(M\)-test the series converges uniformly on \(\mathbb R\); a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, and each partial sum is \(2\pi\)-periodic, so \(g\) is continuous and \(2\pi\)-periodic. ∎

4. Differentiating term by term gives \(\sum_{n\ge1}\cos(n^2x)\). Fix \(x\) and suppose the series converges there. Then its terms tend to zero: \(\cos(n^2x) \to 0\). Apply the double-angle formula twice: \[ \cos(2n^2x) = 2\cos^2(n^2x) - 1 \to -1, \qquad \cos(4n^2x) = 2\cos^2(2n^2x) - 1 \to 2(-1)^2 - 1 = 1 . \] But \(4n^2 = (2n)^2\), so \(\cos(4n^2x) = \cos\big((2n)^2x\big)\) is a subsequence of \(\cos(m^2x)\), which we assumed tends to \(0\). Hence \(1 = 0\) — a contradiction. So \(\cos(n^2x)\not\to0\) and the differentiated series diverges, at every real \(x\). ∎

\(g\) satisfies the continuity hypothesis and fails piecewise \(C^1\): it has no piecewise continuous derivative, because the formal derivative does not exist as a function anywhere. So the second hypothesis is the one doing the work, which is precisely what this counterexample is for.

Check. Dimensions: all quantities are pure numbers ✓. Special case of part 1: \(f = \cos x\), \(k\) arbitrary, \(c_{\pm1} = 1/2\) and the bound reads \(\lVert f - S_Nf\rVert_2 = 0 \le \lVert f^{(k)}\rVert_2/N^k\) for \(N \ge 1\) ✓ (trivially true). Number: for the sawtooth of part 2, \(\lVert f - S_Nf\rVert_2^2 = 2\pi\sum_{|n|>N}n^{-2} \approx 4\pi/N\), so the error falls only as \(N^{-1/2}\) — the slowest rate in the dictionary, and the right one for a function that jumps ✓.

Another way

Part 4 also follows from a classical fact, if one is willing to import it: for \(x/\pi\) irrational the sequence \(n^2x \bmod 2\pi\) is equidistributed (Weyl, 1916), so \(\cos(n^2x)\) visits every value in \([-1,1]\) and certainly does not tend to zero; for \(x/\pi\) rational the sequence is eventually periodic and again does not. That is cited, not proved — Weyl's equidistribution theorem is outside this course — and the double-angle argument above needs nothing at all, which is why it is the one given.

Rung 2

Worked solution

Idea. The partial sum has a closed-form derivative, so its first peak can be located exactly; at that peak the sum is a Riemann sum for \(\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin u}{u}\mathrm du\), and that integral exceeds \(\pi/2\) by a fixed amount. The overshoot never shrinks because the peak tracks the jump.

1. With \(z = \mathrm e^{2\mathrm ix}\), \[ \sum_{j=0}^{M-1}\cos(2j+1)x = \operatorname{Re}\Big(\mathrm e^{\mathrm ix}\frac{z^M-1}{z-1}\Big) = \operatorname{Re}\Big(\mathrm e^{\mathrm ix}\cdot\frac{\mathrm e^{\mathrm iMx}\,2\mathrm i\sin Mx}{\mathrm e^{\mathrm ix}\,2\mathrm i\sin x}\Big) = \operatorname{Re}\Big(\mathrm e^{\mathrm iMx}\frac{\sin Mx}{\sin x}\Big) = \frac{\cos Mx\,\sin Mx}{\sin x} = \frac{\sin 2Mx}{2\sin x}. \] Multiplying by \(4/\pi\) gives \(S_{2M-1}'(x) = \frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\sin 2Mx}{\sin x}\). On \((0,\pi)\) the denominator is positive, so the sign of \(S'\) is the sign of \(\sin 2Mx\): positive on \((0,\pi/2M)\), negative just after. Hence \(x_M = \pi/2M\) is the first maximum. ∎

2. \(S_{2M-1}\) is odd, so \(S_{2M-1}(0) = 0\), and \[ S_{2M-1}(x_M) = \int_0^{\pi/2M}\!\!\frac{2}{\pi}\frac{\sin 2Mt}{\sin t}\,\mathrm dt \;\overset{u = 2Mt}{=}\; \frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi}\frac{\sin u}{\sin(u/2M)}\cdot\frac{\mathrm du}{2M} = \frac2\pi\int_0^{\pi}\frac{\sin u}{2M\sin(u/2M)}\,\mathrm du . \] For the limit, put \(s = u/2M \in (0, \pi/2M] \subset (0,\pi/2]\). Then \[ \Big|\frac{1}{2M\sin s} - \frac{1}{2Ms}\Big| = \frac{s - \sin s}{2Ms\sin s} \le \frac{s^3/6}{2Ms\cdot(2s/\pi)} = \frac{\pi s}{24M} = \frac{\pi u}{48M^2} \le \frac{\pi^2}{48M^2}, \] using \(0 \le s - \sin s \le s^3/6\) and \(\sin s \ge 2s/\pi\) on \([0,\pi/2]\). Since \(\frac1{2Ms} = \frac1u\), the integrand differs from \(\frac{\sin u}{u}\) by at most \(\pi^2/48M^2\) uniformly on \((0,\pi]\), so the integrals differ by at most \(\pi^3/48M^2 \to 0\). ∎

3. Therefore \[ S_{2M-1}(x_M) \longrightarrow \frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin u}{u}\,\mathrm du = \frac2\pi\,\mathrm{Si}(\pi) = \frac{2\times1.8519371}{\pi} = 1.1789797 . \] The limiting value of \(f\) just right of the jump is \(+1\), so the overshoot in absolute terms is \(0.1789797\); the jump itself is \(1 - (-1) = 2\); so as a fraction of the jump the overshoot is \(0.1789797/2 = 0.0894899\), i.e. 8.9490 % (to four decimals, \(8.9490\%\); the next digits are \(8.948987\%\)). The same number occurs at every jump of every piecewise-\(C^1\) function: subtract \(\tfrac J2\) times a square wave with its jump at the same point, and what is left is continuous there, so its series converges uniformly near that point — by the uniform-convergence theorem of the slides when there is one jump per period, and in general by a uniform form of Riemann's localisation principle (cited: Zygmund, Trigonometric Series, vol. I, ch. II).

4. No contradiction, because the overshoot lives on a shrinking interval. The ripple occupies \(x \in (0, C/M)\) for a fixed \(C\) of order \(\pi\), on which \(|f - S_N| \le 0.18 + 2 = O(1)\); so its contribution to \(\lVert f - S_Nf\rVert_2^2\) is \(O(1)\times O(1/M) \to 0\). Mean-square convergence cares about area, and the overshoot has none in the limit.

Check. Dimensions: everything is a pure number ✓. Numbers: summing \(S_{2M-1}(\pi/2M)\) directly gives \(9.1164\%\) at \(M = 5\), \(8.9594\%\) at \(M = 20\), \(8.9494\%\) at \(M = 100\) and \(8.94899\%\) at \(M = 1000\) — approaching \(8.948987\%\) from above ✓. Special case: at \(x = \pi/2\), \(S_{2M-1}(\pi/2) = \frac4\pi(1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \cdots) \to \frac4\pi\cdot\frac\pi4 = 1\) with no overshoot, because \(\pi/2\) is far from the jump ✓.

Another way

The whole phenomenon can be read off the Dirichlet kernel without any of this computation. \(S_Nf = f * D_N/2\pi\), and \(D_N\) is not nonnegative: it has a central spike of area about \(4\,\mathrm{Si}(\pi) = 7.408\), some \(18\) per cent more than \(2\pi\), surrounded by negative lobes. Convolving a step with a kernel whose positive part has area exceeding the total is exactly an overshoot, and its size is \(\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\frac{\sin u}{u}\mathrm du - 1\), the relative excess area of that central lobe. The Fejér kernel, being nonnegative, has no excess and \(\sigma_N\) never overshoots — which is the practical cure.

P6 Self-adjointness does the work medium

exercises: Lagrange's identity, proving reality and orthogonality, finding where a hypothesis is spent

Let \(\mathcal Lu = -(pu')' + qu\) on \([a,b]\) with \(p \in C^1\), \(p > 0\), \(q\) real continuous, \(w > 0\) continuous, and \(\mathcal A = w^{-1}\mathcal L\).

  1. Prove Lagrange's identity \(\bar v\,\mathcal Lu - u\,\overline{\mathcal Lv} = \big[p(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v)\big]'\), and integrate it to obtain \(\langle\mathcal Au,v\rangle_w - \langle u,\mathcal Av\rangle_w = \big[p(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v)\big]_a^b\). Say precisely where the reality of \(p\) and \(q\) is used, and why the weight \(w\) disappears.
  2. Assume separated boundary conditions \(\alpha_1u(a) + \alpha_2u'(a) = 0\), \(\beta_1u(b) + \beta_2u'(b) = 0\) with \(\alpha, \beta \in \mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\). Prove the boundary term vanishes, and deduce (i) every eigenvalue is real and (ii) eigenfunctions for distinct eigenvalues are \(w\)-orthogonal.
  3. Now break it. Take \(p = w = 1\), \(q = 0\), \(a = 0\), \(b = 1\), with \(u(0) = 0\) and \(u'(1) = \mathrm i\,u(1)\). Show the eigenvalue condition is \(\tan k = -\mathrm ik\) with \(\lambda = k^2\), that no real \(k \ne 0\) satisfies it, and verify numerically that \(k = 1.77927 - 0.54554\,\mathrm i\) is a root, so \(\lambda = 2.86819 - 1.94131\,\mathrm i\).
  4. Which conclusion of part 2 fails first, and does the other fail as a consequence or independently? Say exactly which line of your part-2 proof breaks.
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 are proofs; part 3 is a computation ending in two complex numbers; part 4 is a diagnosis — the name of one line. The whole problem is the observation that a symmetric operator's theorems are consequences of one boundary term vanishing, so breaking the boundary term breaks the theorems in a predictable order.

Rung 2

For part 1, differentiate the right-hand side and collect. For part 2, the key is that two vectors of \(\mathbb C^2\) annihilated by the same nonzero real functional are linearly dependent, so the \(2\times2\) determinant \(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v\) vanishes at that endpoint. For part 3, \(u = \sin kx\) is forced by \(u(0) = 0\).

Rung 3

Part 3, ruling out real \(k\): if \(k\) is real and nonzero then \(\tan k\) is real (or undefined) while \(-\mathrm ik\) is purely imaginary and nonzero. For purely imaginary \(k = \mathrm is\) with \(s\) real, \(\tan(\mathrm is) = \mathrm i\tanh s\) while \(-\mathrm ik = s\), so \(\mathrm i\tanh s = s\) forces \(s = 0\). So no real \(\lambda\) at all.

Rung 4

First line of part 1: differentiate, getting \(\big[p(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v)\big]' = p'(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v) + p(u\bar v{}'' - u''\bar v)\), and regroup the four terms as \((p\bar v{}')'u - (pu')'\bar v\).

Worked solution

Idea. All four Sturm–Liouville theorems are one identity plus one vanishing boundary term. Part 3 keeps the identity and destroys the boundary term, and part 4 watches the theorems fall in the order the proof predicts.

1. Since \(q\) is real, \(\bar v(qu) - u\overline{(qv)} = q u\bar v - q u\bar v = 0\), so the left-hand side is \(u(p\bar v{}')' - \bar v(pu')'\) — this is the first place reality of \(q\) is spent, and reality of \(p\) is spent in writing \(\overline{(pv')'} = (p\bar v{}')'\). Differentiating the right-hand side, \[ \big[p(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v)\big]' = p'(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v) + p\big(u'\bar v{}' + u\bar v{}'' - u''\bar v - u'\bar v{}'\big) = (p'\bar v{}' + p\bar v{}'')u - (p'u' + pu'')\bar v = (p\bar v{}')'u - (pu')'\bar v, \] which is the left-hand side. ∎

Integrating over \([a,b]\): \(\int_a^b(\mathcal Lu)\bar v - \int_a^bu\overline{(\mathcal Lv)} = \big[p(u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v)\big]_a^b\). And \(\langle\mathcal Au,v\rangle_w = \int_a^b(w^{-1}\mathcal Lu)\bar v\,w\,\mathrm dx = \int_a^b(\mathcal Lu)\bar v\,\mathrm dx\): the weight in the operator cancels the weight in the inner product exactly, which is why the eigenvalue problem must be written with the same \(w\) in both places.

2. At \(a\), both \((u(a),u'(a))\) and \((v(a),v'(a))\) satisfy \(\alpha\cdot(y,y') = 0\). Conjugating the second — legitimate only because \(\alpha\) is real — gives \(\alpha\cdot(\bar v(a),\bar v{}'(a)) = 0\). Two vectors of \(\mathbb C^2\) annihilated by the same nonzero real functional span at most a line, so \(\det\begin{pmatrix}u & \bar v\\ u' & \bar v{}'\end{pmatrix}(a) = u\bar v{}' - u'\bar v = 0\). Same at \(b\). Hence \(\langle\mathcal Au,v\rangle_w = \langle u,\mathcal Av\rangle_w\).

(i) If \(\mathcal Au = \lambda u\) with \(u \not\equiv 0\), then \(\lambda\lVert u\rVert_w^2 = \langle\mathcal Au,u\rangle_w = \langle u,\mathcal Au\rangle_w = \bar\lambda\lVert u\rVert_w^2\), and \(\lVert u\rVert_w^2 > 0\) because \(w > 0\), so \(\lambda = \bar\lambda\). (ii) If also \(\mathcal Av = \mu v\) with \(\mu \ne \lambda\), both real by (i), then \(\lambda\langle u,v\rangle_w = \langle\mathcal Au,v\rangle_w = \langle u,\mathcal Av\rangle_w = \bar\mu\langle u,v\rangle_w = \mu\langle u,v\rangle_w\), so \((\lambda - \mu)\langle u,v\rangle_w = 0\) and \(\langle u,v\rangle_w = 0\). ∎

3. For \(\lambda = 0\), \(u(0) = 0\) forces \(u = x\), and \(u'(1) = 1 \ne \mathrm i = \mathrm iu(1)\), so \(0\) is not an eigenvalue. Otherwise \(u(0) = 0\) forces \(u = \sin kx\) up to scale, with \(\lambda = k^2\), \(k \ne 0\). Then \(u'(1) = k\cos k\) and \(u(1) = \sin k\), so the condition \(u'(1) = \mathrm iu(1)\) reads \(k\cos k = \mathrm i\sin k\), i.e. \(\tan k = k/\mathrm i = -\mathrm ik\). If \(k\) is real and nonzero, \(\tan k\) is real while \(-\mathrm ik\) is purely imaginary and nonzero: impossible. If \(k = \mathrm is\) with \(s\) real and nonzero, \(\tan(\mathrm is) = \mathrm i\tanh s\) while \(-\mathrm ik = s\), and \(\mathrm i\tanh s = s\) needs both sides real and imaginary at once, so \(s = 0\). Hence no \(\lambda\) is real. Numerically, at \(k = 1.7792684 - 0.5455364\,\mathrm i\) one finds \(\tan k + \mathrm ik = 0\) to ten digits, and \(\lambda = k^2 = 2.8681861 - 1.9413113\,\mathrm i\).

4. Reality fails first, and orthogonality fails as a consequence — but only partly. The line that breaks is the conjugation step in part 2: the boundary functional at \(x = 1\) is \(\gamma = (-\mathrm i, 1)\), which is not real, so \(\gamma\cdot(v,v') = 0\) does not give \(\gamma\cdot(\bar v,\bar v{}') = 0\), the determinant does not vanish, and \(\mathcal A\) is not symmetric. With symmetry gone, step (i) has nothing to stand on and \(\lambda\) may be complex; and step (ii) used (i) in replacing \(\bar\mu\) by \(\mu\), so it fails too. Orthogonality fails because reality did: what survives for a non-self-adjoint problem is a weaker "bi-orthogonality" between the eigenfunctions of \(\mathcal A\) and those of its adjoint, which is not the same statement.

Check. Dimensions: with \(p = w = 1\), \(\lambda\) carries \(\mathrm L^{-2}\) and \(k\) carries \(\mathrm L^{-1}\), so \(\tan k = -\mathrm ik\) is consistent on the unit interval where lengths are pure numbers ✓. Limit: replace \(\mathrm i\) by a real \(\sigma\) in the boundary condition \(u'(1) = \sigma u(1)\). Then the condition is \(\tan k = k/\sigma\), every root of which gives a real \(\lambda\) (\(k\) real, or purely imaginary when \(\sigma > 1\)), and reality is restored ✓ — the failure is caused precisely by the \(\mathrm i\), not by the Robin form. Physical reading: \(\operatorname{Im}\lambda \ne 0\) means a complex frequency, i.e. energy leaving through the boundary, which is exactly what an absorbing boundary does ✓.

Another way

Part 4 can be seen without any algebra by an energy argument. For a real separated condition the boundary term \(p(u\bar u{}' - u'\bar u) = 2\mathrm i\,p\operatorname{Im}(u\bar u{}')\) vanishes, which says no energy flux crosses the ends. With \(u'(1) = \mathrm iu(1)\) the concomitant at \(x = 1\) is \(-2\mathrm i\,|u(1)|^2 \ne 0\): energy is being removed at a definite rate. A mode losing energy must decay, so its frequency must be complex — and the imaginary part of \(\lambda\) is that decay rate.

Worked solution

Idea. Legendre's equation is already self-adjoint; nothing has to be forced on it. The orthogonality that unit 10 obtained from Rodrigues' formula is really a statement that \(p = 1-x^2\) vanishes at both ends while the eigenfunctions do not blow up — and that is all.

1. Note \(\big((1-x^2)y'\big)' = (1-x^2)y'' - 2xy'\) exactly. So Legendre's equation is \(\big((1-x^2)y'\big)' + \lambda y = 0\), i.e. \[ -\big((1-x^2)y'\big)' = \lambda y : \qquad p(x) = 1-x^2,\quad q \equiv 0,\quad w \equiv 1 . \] Both endpoints are singular, because \(p(\pm1) = 0\). At each, no boundary condition is imposed; what replaces one is the demand that \(y\) stay bounded (with bounded derivative), which is enough to make \(p(u v' - u'v) \to 0\) there.

2. Write \(\lambda_\ell = \ell(\ell+1)\) and \(\lambda_n = n(n+1)\). Lagrange's identity for real solutions reads \(P_n\mathcal LP_\ell - P_\ell\mathcal LP_n = \big[p(P_\ell P_n' - P_\ell'P_n)\big]'\). The left side is \((\lambda_\ell - \lambda_n)P_\ell P_n\). Integrating over \([-1,1]\), \[ (\lambda_\ell - \lambda_n)\!\int_{-1}^{1}\!P_\ell P_n\,\mathrm dx = \Big[(1-x^2)\big(P_\ell P_n' - P_\ell'P_n\big)\Big]_{-1}^{1} = 0, \] because \(1-x^2\) vanishes at \(x = \pm1\) while \(P_\ell, P_n, P_\ell', P_n'\) are polynomials and hence finite there. Finally \(\lambda_\ell \ne \lambda_n\) when \(\ell \ne n\), since \(t \mapsto t(t+1)\) is strictly increasing on \([0,\infty)\). Divide. ∎

3. \(Q_0' = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac1{1+x} + \tfrac1{1-x}\big) = \tfrac{1}{1-x^2}\), so \((1-x^2)Q_0' = 1\) identically. With \(P_1 = x\) (and \(\lambda = 2\)) against \(Q_0\) (\(\lambda = 0\)), the concomitant is \[ (1-x^2)\big(P_1Q_0' - P_1'Q_0\big) = x\cdot 1 - (1-x^2)Q_0 = x - \tfrac{1-x^2}{2}\log\tfrac{1+x}{1-x}. \] As \(x \to 1^-\), \((1-x^2)\log\frac{1+x}{1-x} \to 0\) (the logarithm beats no power), so the concomitant \(\to 1\); as \(x \to -1^+\) it \(\to -1\). The bracket is therefore \(1 - (-1) = 2 \ne 0\). So admitting \(Q_0\) destroys symmetry: Lagrange's identity would then give \((\lambda_1 - 0)\int_{-1}^1 P_1Q_0\,\mathrm dx = 2\), and \(P_1\) and \(Q_0\) are not orthogonal despite having different eigenvalues. Boundedness at a singular endpoint is not tidiness; it is the hypothesis.

4. Feed a general \(\lambda\) into Legendre's equation and solve by power series about the ordinary point \(x = 0\) — unit 09's series method, and unit 10's derivation. Substituting \(y = \sum a_mx^m\) gives the two-term recurrence \(a_{m+2} = \frac{m(m+1) - \lambda}{(m+1)(m+2)}a_m\), so every solution is an even series plus an odd series, each converging on \((-1,1)\). A bounded solution has bounded even and odd parts \(\tfrac12(y(x) \pm y(-x))\), so it is enough to show that a non-terminating one of these series is unbounded near \(x = 1\). Write the ratio as \(\frac{m}{m+2}\big(1 - \frac{\lambda}{m(m+1)}\big)\). Once \(m(m+1) > \lambda\) every factor is positive, the coefficients keep one sign, and multiplying the ratios from some \(m_0\) on gives \(a_{m} = a_{m_0}\frac{m_0}{m}\prod\big(1 - \frac{\lambda}{k(k+1)}\big)\), the product over \(k = m_0, m_0 + 2, \dots, m - 2\). That product converges to a nonzero limit, because \(\sum\lambda/k(k+1) < \infty\), so \(|a_m| \ge c/m\) for some \(c > 0\). Then \(|y_{\text{part}}(x)| \ge c\sum_{m \ge m_0}x^m/m - (\text{a polynomial})\), which tends to \(\infty\) as \(x \to 1^-\), because \(\sum x^m/m\) over every other \(m\) does. So boundedness at \(x = \pm1\) forces each series to terminate or vanish, and termination needs \(m(m+1) = \lambda\) for some \(m\): \(\lambda = \ell(\ell+1)\) with \(\ell\) a non-negative integer, the solution then a polynomial of degree \(\ell\). The quantisation is the boundedness condition at the singular endpoints, read through the series.

Check. Dimensions: \(x\) is dimensionless and \(\lambda\) is a pure number ✓. Special cases: \(\int_{-1}^1P_0P_1 = \int_{-1}^1x\,\mathrm dx = 0\) ✓ and \(\int_{-1}^1P_0P_2 = \int_{-1}^1\tfrac12(3x^2-1)\mathrm dx = \tfrac12(2-2) = 0\) ✓. Nodes: \(P_3 = \tfrac12(5x^3-3x)\) has zeros at \(0, \pm\sqrt{3/5} = \pm0.7746\) — three of them, and \(\lambda_3 = 12\) is the fourth eigenvalue counting from \(\lambda_0 = 0\), so "index minus one" holds ✓.

Another way

For polynomials only, orthogonality also follows from degree counting plus one integration by parts: \(\int_{-1}^1 P_\ell\,x^m\mathrm dx = 0\) for \(m < \ell\) can be proved by integrating \(\big((1-x^2)P_\ell'\big)' = -\lambda_\ell P_\ell\) against \(x^m\) twice and inducting on \(m\). That gives orthogonality to every polynomial of lower degree, hence to \(P_n\) for \(n < \ell\). It is shorter for this family and it does not generalise — the Lagrange argument works verbatim for Bessel, Hermite, Laguerre and everything else, which is the point of having it.

  4. Suppose a regular separated problem has two eigenfunctions \(u, v\) for the same \(\lambda\). Show directly that their Wronskian \(W = uv' - u'v\) satisfies \(pW = \) constant, and that the constant is zero. Which of the three theorems of the unit does that constant being zero encode?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three proofs and one example. Nothing analytic is needed: this is linear algebra on the two-dimensional solution space of a second-order ODE, plus one uniqueness theorem.

Rung 2

Part 1: the boundary condition at \(a\) says the vector \((y(a), y'(a)) \in \mathbb R^2\) lies in a one-dimensional subspace. Part 4: Abel's identity, from unit 06, in the form appropriate to \((py')'\).

Rung 3

First line of part 4: \((pW)' = (puv' - pu'v)' = u(pv')' - v(pu')'\) by the same expansion as Lagrange's identity; and both \((pu')'\) and \((pv')'\) equal \((q - \lambda w)\) times their own function, so the right side is \((q-\lambda w)(uv - vu) = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. The solution space at a fixed \(\lambda\) is two-dimensional; a separated boundary condition at one end cuts it to one dimension; and one dimension cannot contain two independent functions. Periodic conditions do not cut at one end, so they cut nothing until both ends are used, and then they may cut nothing at all.

1. Let \(u\) and \(v\) both satisfy \(\mathcal Ly = \lambda wy\) and \(\alpha_1y(a) + \alpha_2y'(a) = 0\) with \(\alpha \ne 0\). The vectors \((u(a),u'(a))\) and \((v(a),v'(a))\) lie in \(\alpha^\perp\), a one-dimensional subspace of \(\mathbb R^2\). If \((u(a),u'(a)) = (0,0)\) then \(u \equiv 0\) by uniqueness and \(u\) is not an eigenfunction; so it is nonzero and spans \(\alpha^\perp\), giving \((v(a),v'(a)) = c\,(u(a),u'(a))\) for some scalar \(c\). Now \(v\) and \(cu\) solve the same linear second-order ODE with the same value and derivative at \(a\), so \(v = cu\) everywhere by Picard–Lindelöf (unit 01, applied to the equivalent first-order system; unit 06 states the linear case). Hence the eigenspace is spanned by \(u\). ∎

2. For \(\lambda = m^2\) with \(m \ge 1\), both \(\cos mx\) and \(\sin mx\) satisfy \(-y'' = m^2y\) and are \(2\pi\)-periodic with \(2\pi\)-periodic derivative, so both meet the boundary conditions; they are linearly independent; so the eigenspace contains a two-dimensional subspace, and by part 3 it is exactly two-dimensional. The proof of part 1 has nothing to start from: the periodic conditions constrain the pair \(\big((y(0),y'(0)),(y(2\pi),y'(2\pi))\big)\) jointly and place no restriction on \((y(0),y'(0))\) by itself — indeed for \(\lambda = m^2\) every initial vector works.

For \(\lambda = 0\) the general solution is \(y = A + Bx\); periodicity of \(y\) forces \(B = 0\), so the eigenspace is the constants, one-dimensional. The periodic problem therefore has a simple lowest eigenvalue and doubled ones thereafter, which is exactly the drum's angular spectrum.

3. Fix \(\lambda\). The set of solutions of \(\mathcal Ly = \lambda wy\) on \([a,b]\) is a vector space of dimension exactly two: the map \(y \mapsto (y(a), y'(a))\) into \(\mathbb R^2\) is linear, injective by uniqueness of the initial value problem, and surjective by existence (Picard–Lindelöf, unit 01; the equation is linear with continuous coefficients, so the solution exists on all of \([a,b]\)). An eigenspace is a subspace of that solution space that additionally satisfies the boundary conditions, hence has dimension at most two. ∎ (The same counting says an \(n\)-th-order problem has multiplicity at most \(n\).)

4. \((pW)' = (p(uv' - u'v))' = u(pv')' - v(pu')'\), by the expansion used in Lagrange's identity. Both \(u\) and \(v\) satisfy \((py')' = (q - \lambda w)y\), so the right side is \(u(q-\lambda w)v - v(q-\lambda w)u = 0\). Hence \(pW\) is constant — which is Abel's identity of unit 06, specialised to this form. Evaluate it at \(x = a\): the separated condition makes \((u,u')(a)\) and \((v,v')(a)\) parallel, so \(W(a) = 0\) and the constant is \(0\). Then \(W \equiv 0\) (since \(p > 0\)), so \(u\) and \(v\) are linearly dependent — which is part 1 again.

That the constant is zero is the vanishing of the bilinear concomitant \([u,v]\) in the real case with \(u, v\) sharing an eigenvalue. So the three facts — simplicity, self-adjointness, and Abel's identity — are one computation seen from three sides.

Check. Dimensions: \(pW\) carries \([p][u]^2\mathrm L^{-1}\) throughout, and a constant is dimensionally consistent ✓. Special case: for \(p = w = 1\), \(q = 0\), Dirichlet on \([0,L]\), the eigenspace of \(\lambda_n = (n\pi/L)^2\) is spanned by \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\) alone — \(\cos(n\pi x/L)\) has the same \(\lambda\) but fails \(y(0) = 0\), so it is excluded, exactly as part 1 predicts ✓. Number: for the periodic problem the eigenvalue list is \(0, 1, 1, 4, 4, 9, 9, \dots\), so the \(n\)-th distinct eigenvalue is \((n-1)^2\) and the count of eigenfunctions up to \(\lambda = 9\) is \(7\) ✓.

Another way

Part 2 is also a symmetry statement, and that is the version worth remembering. The periodic problem is invariant under the rotation \(x \mapsto x + \theta\), which maps eigenfunctions to eigenfunctions of the same eigenvalue; for \(m \ge 1\) it maps \(\cos mx\) to \(\cos m(x+\theta) = \cos m\theta\cos mx - \sin m\theta\sin mx\), genuinely new, so the eigenspace must be at least two-dimensional. For \(\lambda = 0\) the eigenfunction is constant and the rotation does nothing, so nothing is forced. Degeneracy usually has a symmetry behind it (the exceptions are called accidental) — which is why perturbing the symmetry splits the pair, and why a coin stuck to a drum makes it beat.

  3. Evaluate \(R(0)\) exactly and compare with the true \(\lambda_1 = 6.548395\). Then minimise over \(\beta\) numerically and give the improved bound and its error.
  4. Both bounds are above the true value. Say why that is guaranteed, and why the error is so much smaller than the error in the trial function itself.

Rung 1

Part 1: three functions and a quotient. Part 2: three integrals. Part 3: two numbers with errors. Part 4: two sentences, one of them about a stationary point.

Rung 2

The Dirichlet condition makes the boundary term vanish, so the numerator is just \(\int_0^1u'^2\,\mathrm dx\) — no extra boundary contribution here, unlike a Robin problem. Use \(\int_0^1\cos^2 k\pi x = \tfrac12\) for integer \(k \ge 1\), and \(\int_0^1\cos(\pi x)\cos(2\pi x)\,\mathrm dx = 0\), to get the numerator in one line.

Rung 3

The cross integral: \(\sin\pi x\sin2\pi x = \tfrac12[\cos\pi x - \cos3\pi x]\), and \(\int_0^1 x\cos(k\pi x)\,\mathrm dx = \big(\cos k\pi - 1\big)/(k\pi)^2\). The \(1\) in \((1+x)\) contributes nothing, since \(\int_0^1\cos(k\pi x) = 0\) for integer \(k \ne 0\).

Rung 4

First line: \(u' = \pi\cos\pi x + 2\pi\beta\cos2\pi x\), so \(\int_0^1u'^2 = \pi^2\cdot\tfrac12 + 4\pi^2\beta^2\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}(1+4\beta^2)\), the cross term vanishing by orthogonality of the cosines.

Worked solution

Idea. The lowest eigenvalue is the minimum of a quotient, so any admissible guess overestimates it, and a one-parameter family lets you minimise within the guess. The accuracy is spectacular because \(\lambda_1\) is a stationary value.

1. The equation is \(-(1\cdot u')' + 0\cdot u = \lambda(1+x)u\), so \(p \equiv 1\), \(q \equiv 0\), \(w(x) = 1+x\). Both endpoints are regular with Dirichlet conditions, so the boundary term in the Rayleigh identity vanishes and \[ R[u] = \frac{\int_0^1 u'^2\,\mathrm dx}{\int_0^1 (1+x)\,u^2\,\mathrm dx}. \]

2. Numerator. \(u' = \pi\cos\pi x + 2\pi\beta\cos2\pi x\), and \(\int_0^1\cos^2\pi x = \int_0^1\cos^22\pi x = \tfrac12\) while \(\int_0^1\cos\pi x\cos2\pi x = \tfrac12\int_0^1[\cos\pi x + \cos3\pi x] = 0\). So \(\int_0^1u'^2 = \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}\big(1 + 4\beta^2\big)\).

Denominator. Three integrals, using \(\int_0^1\sin^2k\pi x = \tfrac12\), \(\int_0^1 x\sin^2k\pi x = \tfrac14\) and \(\int_0^1x\cos k\pi x\,\mathrm dx = \big((-1)^k - 1\big)/(k\pi)^2\):

  • \(\int_0^1(1+x)\sin^2\pi x = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34\);
  • \(\int_0^1(1+x)\sin^22\pi x = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34\);
  • \(\int_0^1(1+x)\sin\pi x\sin2\pi x = \int_0^1 x\cdot\tfrac12[\cos\pi x - \cos3\pi x]\,\mathrm dx = \tfrac12\Big(\tfrac{-2}{\pi^2} - \tfrac{-2}{9\pi^2}\Big) = -\tfrac{8}{9\pi^2}\) (the \(1\) contributes nothing, since \(\int_0^1\cos k\pi x = 0\)).

Hence \(\int_0^1(1+x)u^2 = \tfrac34(1+\beta^2) + 2\beta\big(-\tfrac{8}{9\pi^2}\big) = \tfrac34(1+\beta^2) - \tfrac{16\beta}{9\pi^2}\), which is the stated \(R(\beta)\). ∎

3. \(R(0) = \dfrac{\pi^2/2}{3/4} = \dfrac{2\pi^2}{3} = 6.579736\), against the true \(\lambda_1 = 6.548395\): high by \(0.0313\), i.e. 0.479 %.

Minimising \(R\) over \(\beta\) numerically gives \(\beta_{\min} = -0.0397748\) and \(R(\beta_{\min}) = 6.548459\), high by \(6.3\times10^{-5}\), i.e. 0.00097 % — one part in a hundred thousand, from a two-term guess.

4. Guaranteed above, because \(\lambda_1 = \min_{u\ne0}R[u]\) over admissible \(u\) and our \(u\) is admissible (it is smooth and vanishes at both ends). So \(R[u] \ge \lambda_1\) for every choice of \(\beta\); no trial function can ever undershoot.

The error is small because \(u_1\) is a stationary point of \(R\), not merely a minimum-value point: writing \(u = u_1 + \varepsilon\eta\) with \(\eta \perp_w u_1\) and \(\lVert u_1\rVert_w = 1\), \(R[u] = \lambda_1 + \varepsilon^2\big(\langle\mathcal A\eta,\eta\rangle_w - \lambda_1\lVert\eta\rVert_w^2\big) + O(\varepsilon^4)\), with no first-order term. A shape wrong by \(\varepsilon\) gives an eigenvalue wrong by \(\varepsilon^2\). Here the one-term trial differs from the true \(u_1\) by about \(4\%\) in shape (the size of \(\beta_{\min}\)), and \(0.04^2 \approx 1.6\times10^{-3}\) — the right order for the \(0.48\%\) observed.

Check. Dimensions: with \(T = L = \rho_0 = 1\) everything is a pure number; restoring units, the problem is \(-(Tu')' = \lambda\rho u\), so \(R = \int Tu'^2/\int\rho u^2\) carries \(\mathrm N/(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm m^2) = \mathrm s^{-2}\): here \(\lambda\) is \(\omega^2\) itself ✓. Special case: set \(w \equiv 1\) (uniform string) and the same one-term trial gives \(R = (\pi^2/2)/(1/2) = \pi^2\), the exact answer, because \(\sin\pi x\) is then the exact eigenfunction ✓. Limit: replacing \(w = 1+x\) by its mean value \(3/2\) gives \(\lambda_1 \approx \pi^2/1.5 = 6.5797\) — which is exactly \(R(0)\), so the one-term bound is "the uniform string with the average density", a reading worth keeping ✓.

Another way

Minimising \(R(\beta)\) over a two-dimensional trial space is the Rayleigh–Ritz method, and it need not be done by calculus on a quotient: setting \(K_{ij} = \int_0^1\phi_i'\phi_j'\) and \(M_{ij} = \int_0^1(1+x)\phi_i\phi_j\) for \(\phi_1 = \sin\pi x\), \(\phi_2 = \sin2\pi x\) turns the problem into the generalised eigenvalue problem \(Kc = \lambda Mc\) of unit 07, with \(K = \tfrac{\pi^2}{2}\mathrm{diag}(1,4)\) and \(M = \begin{pmatrix}3/4 & -8/9\pi^2\\ -8/9\pi^2 & 3/4\end{pmatrix}\). Its smaller root is \(6.548459\), the same number, and the eigenvector is \((1, -0.03977)\). Adding more trial functions enlarges the pencil and the bound falls monotonically — which is the finite element method in miniature.

P12 Orthogonal is not complete hard

exercises: distinguishing orthogonality from completeness, a strict Bessel inequality, diagnosing which hypothesis was dropped

On \([0,\pi]\) with weight \(w \equiv 1\), consider the system \(\mathcal E = \{\sin 2nx : n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\}\).

  1. Show \(\mathcal E\) is orthogonal, with \(\lVert\sin2nx\rVert^2 = \pi/2\) for every \(n\).
  2. Prove that \(\int_0^\pi\sin x\,\sin2nx\,\mathrm dx = 0\) for every \(n \ge 1\), and conclude that \(\mathcal E\) is not complete in \(PC[0,\pi]\).
  3. For \(f(x) = \sin x\), compute all the coefficients \(\hat f_n\), the left-hand side of Bessel's inequality, and \(\lVert f\rVert^2\). By how much does Parseval fail?
  4. \(\{\sin 2nx\}\) is the complete eigenfunction system of some Sturm–Liouville problem. Which one, on which interval? Explain in one paragraph why that does not rescue part 2 — i.e. name the hypothesis of the expansion theorem that is being violated on \([0,\pi]\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1: a computation. Part 2: a proof and a conclusion. Part 3: a sequence of numbers and a gap. Part 4: a problem and a diagnosis. The lesson is one sentence long and worth the whole problem: orthogonality is cheap, completeness is a theorem, and only completeness licences an expansion.

Rung 2

Part 2: \(\sin A\sin B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B)]\), and \(\int_0^\pi\cos(m x)\,\mathrm dx = \sin(m\pi)/m\), which vanishes for every nonzero integer \(m\) — note that \(2n \pm 1\) is always an odd integer. Part 4: what boundary conditions does \(\sin2nx\) satisfy, and on what interval are \(2n\) the right wavenumbers?

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\int_0^\pi\sin x\sin2nx\,\mathrm dx = \tfrac12\int_0^\pi\big[\cos(2n-1)x - \cos(2n+1)x\big]\mathrm dx = \tfrac12\Big[\tfrac{\sin(2n-1)\pi}{2n-1} - \tfrac{\sin(2n+1)\pi}{2n+1}\Big]\).

Worked solution

Idea. Take a perfectly good complete system, delete half of it, and watch every theorem that depended on completeness fail while every theorem that depended only on orthogonality survives. It is the cleanest way to see which is which.

1. For \(m \ne n\), \(\int_0^\pi\sin2mx\sin2nx\,\mathrm dx = \tfrac12\int_0^\pi\big[\cos2(m-n)x - \cos2(m+n)x\big]\mathrm dx\), and \(\int_0^\pi\cos2kx\,\mathrm dx = \sin(2k\pi)/2k = 0\) for every nonzero integer \(k\). So the system is orthogonal. For \(m = n\) the first cosine is \(1\), giving \(\lVert\sin2nx\rVert^2 = \tfrac12\cdot\pi = \pi/2\). ✓

2. \(\int_0^\pi\sin x\sin2nx\,\mathrm dx = \tfrac12\int_0^\pi\big[\cos(2n-1)x - \cos(2n+1)x\big]\mathrm dx = \tfrac12\Big[\frac{\sin(2n-1)\pi}{2n-1} - \frac{\sin(2n+1)\pi}{2n+1}\Big] = 0\), since \(2n\pm1\) are integers and \(\sin(k\pi) = 0\). ∎

So \(f = \sin x\) is a nonzero element of \(PC[0,\pi]\) — indeed a smooth one, vanishing at both endpoints — orthogonal to every member of \(\mathcal E\). Every coefficient \(\hat f_n\) is then zero, so every partial sum \(S_Nf\) is the zero function and \(\lVert f - S_Nf\rVert = \lVert f\rVert > 0\) for all \(N\): the definition of completeness fails at \(f\), and \(\mathcal E\) is not complete. (This is the safe direction of the orthogonality test; the slides' notes explain why the other direction needs care in \(PC\).)

3. \(\hat f_n = \langle f, e_n\rangle/\lVert e_n\rVert^2 = 0/(\pi/2) = 0\) for every \(n\). So the left side of Bessel's inequality is \(\sum_n\hat f_n^2\lVert e_n\rVert^2 = 0\), while \(\lVert f\rVert^2 = \int_0^\pi\sin^2x\,\mathrm dx = \pi/2 = 1.5707963\). Bessel's inequality holds — \(0 \le \pi/2\) — and Parseval fails by the whole of \(\lVert f\rVert^2\), a gap of \(\pi/2\). The "expansion" of \(\sin x\) in this system is the zero function, and \(\lVert f - S_Nf\rVert = \lVert f\rVert = \sqrt{\pi/2} = 1.2533141\) for every \(N\): it does not converge to \(f\) at all.

4. \(\{\sin2nx\}\) is the Dirichlet eigenfunction system of \(-u'' = \lambda u\) on \(\big[0, \tfrac\pi2\big]\), with eigenvalues \(\lambda_n = (2n)^2\) — set \(L = \pi/2\) in \(\sin(n\pi x/L)\). On that interval it is complete, and every theorem of the unit applies.

On \([0,\pi]\) it is not the full eigenfunction system of any regular Sturm–Liouville problem — if it were, the cited expansion theorem would make it complete, contradicting part 2 — and the hypothesis being violated is the one word "all": the expansion theorem asserts completeness for the full set of eigenfunctions of one problem on one interval, and \(\{\sin2nx\}\) is only the even-indexed half of \(\{\sin nx\}\), the Dirichlet system on \([0,\pi]\). Deleting the odd-indexed members deletes exactly the directions that \(\sin x\) lives in. Nothing about the surviving members changed — they are still orthogonal, still eigenfunctions of \(-\mathrm d^2/\mathrm dx^2\) with Dirichlet conditions at \(0\) and \(\pi\) — which is why orthogonality is no evidence at all for completeness.

Check. Dimensions: all pure numbers ✓. Special case: restore the missing half. Then \(\{\sin nx\}_{n\ge1}\) is complete on \([0,\pi]\), \(\sin x\) has coefficients \((1,0,0,\dots)\), and Parseval reads \(\pi/2 = 1^2\cdot\pi/2\) ✓ — the gap closes exactly, and it closes on the one term that was deleted. Number: the partial sums of the deficient expansion give mean-square error \(\pi/2 = 1.5708\) at every \(N\), against the complete system's \(0\) for \(N \ge 1\) ✓.

Another way

A faster route to part 2, and one that explains rather than computes: on \([0,\pi]\), every \(\sin2nx\) is antisymmetric about the midpoint \(x = \pi/2\), because \(\sin2n(\pi - x) = -\sin2nx\). And \(\sin x\) is symmetric there: \(\sin(\pi - x) = \sin x\). A symmetric function and an antisymmetric one are automatically orthogonal on an interval symmetric about that midpoint, so the integral vanishes without any trigonometry. The deleted half of the system is exactly the symmetric half, and \(\sin x\) is the cheapest witness to its absence.

P13 Fourier's own problem: the rod routine

exercises: separation for the heat equation, mode decay times, smoothing as a statement about coefficients

A copper rod of length \(L = 1\ \mathrm m\), thermally insulated along its sides, obeys \(\theta_t = \kappa\theta_{xx}\) with \(\kappa = 1.11\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}\). Both ends are held at \(\theta = 0\). At \(t = 0\) the rod is at a uniform \(\theta_0 = 100\ {}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\).

  1. Separate the equation and write the solution as a sine series, giving \(\alpha_n\) explicitly and the decay time \(\tau_n\) of mode \(n\).
  2. Compute \(\tau_1\) in minutes and \(\tau_{10}\) in seconds.
  3. Compute the midpoint temperature \(\theta(L/2, t)\) at \(t = 0\), \(t = 100\ \mathrm s\) and \(t = \tau_1\), keeping enough terms to be sure of three significant figures, and say how many terms you needed at each time.
  4. At \(t = 0\) the profile is discontinuous at the ends and its Fourier coefficients fall only as \(1/n\). Explain, with the decay factor in hand, why \(\theta(\cdot, t)\) is \(C^\infty\) for every \(t > 0\) — and why running the equation backwards is hopeless.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1: a series. Parts 2 and 3: numbers, with units. Part 4: a paragraph whose whole content is the size of \(\mathrm e^{-\kappa\lambda_nt}\) as a function of \(n\).

Rung 2

Separation gives the same \(X_n = \sin(n\pi x/L)\) as the string, because the boundary conditions are the same; only the time factor changes, from \(\cos\omega_nt\) to \(\mathrm e^{-\kappa\lambda_nt}\) with \(\lambda_n = (n\pi/L)^2\). For part 3, at \(x = L/2\) the terms alternate: \(\sin(n\pi/2)\) is \(+1, 0, -1, 0, +1,\dots\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\alpha_n = \frac2L\int_0^L\theta_0\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\mathrm dx = \frac{2\theta_0}{n\pi}\big(1 - (-1)^n\big)\), which is \(4\theta_0/n\pi\) for odd \(n\) and \(0\) for even.

Worked solution

Idea. The same eigenfunctions as the string, because the same operator with the same boundary conditions; only the time factor differs, and the exponential in \(n^2\) is what makes diffusion smooth and irreversible.

1. With \(\theta = X(x)T(t)\), \(T'/\kappa T = X''/X = -\lambda\). The spatial problem with \(X(0) = X(L) = 0\) gives \(\lambda_n = (n\pi/L)^2\) and \(X_n = \sin(n\pi x/L)\), and \(T_n = \mathrm e^{-\kappa\lambda_nt}\). So \[ \theta(x,t) = \sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{4\theta_0}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm e^{-t/\tau_n}, \qquad \tau_n = \frac{1}{\kappa\lambda_n} = \frac{L^2}{\kappa n^2\pi^2} = \frac{\tau_1}{n^2}, \] with \(\alpha_n = 4\theta_0/n\pi\) for odd \(n\) and \(0\) for even (a uniform profile is symmetric about the midpoint, so it excites no antisymmetric mode).

2. \(\tau_1 = \dfrac{L^2}{\kappa\pi^2} = \dfrac{1}{1.11\times10^{-4}\times9.8696} = 912.8\ \mathrm s = \mathbf{15.21\ minutes}\), and \(\tau_{10} = \tau_1/100 = \mathbf{9.128\ s}\). The tenth harmonic is gone a hundred times sooner than the first.

3. At \(x = L/2\), \(\sin(n\pi/2) = (-1)^{(n-1)/2}\) for odd \(n\), so \[ \theta(L/2, t) = \frac{4\theta_0}{\pi}\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-1)^k}{2k+1}\,\mathrm e^{-(2k+1)^2t/\tau_1}. \]

  • \(t = 0\): the series is \(\frac{4\theta_0}{\pi}(1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \cdots) = \theta_0 = \mathbf{100.0}\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), by Leibniz — but it converges very slowly. For an alternating series with decreasing terms the error after \(N\) terms is below the first omitted term, \(\frac{400}{\pi(2N+1)}\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\), so being sure of three figures (error under \(0.5\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\)) takes 127 terms, and being sure of \(100.0\) to the first decimal takes \(1273\). This is the discontinuity at the ends making itself felt, and the right answer here is to sum the series in closed form rather than numerically.
  • \(t = 100\ \mathrm s\): \(t/\tau_1 = 0.10955\), so the factors are \(\mathrm e^{-0.1096} = 0.8962\), \(\mathrm e^{-0.9860} = 0.3731\), \(\mathrm e^{-2.739} = 0.0646\), \(\mathrm e^{-5.368} = 0.00466\), \(\mathrm e^{-8.874} = 1.4\times10^{-4}\). Summing, \(\frac{400}{\pi}\big(0.8962 - \tfrac{0.3731}{3} + \tfrac{0.0646}{5} - \tfrac{0.00466}{7} + \tfrac{0.00014}{9}\big) = \frac{400}{\pi}(0.784155) = \mathbf{99.8}\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) — five terms suffice, and four give the same three figures.
  • \(t = \tau_1 = 912.8\ \mathrm s\): factors \(\mathrm e^{-1} = 0.36788\), \(\mathrm e^{-9} = 1.234\times10^{-4}\), \(\mathrm e^{-25} = 1.4\times10^{-11}\). So \(\theta(L/2,\tau_1) = \frac{400}{\pi}\big(0.36788 - \tfrac{1.234\times10^{-4}}{3}\big) = \mathbf{46.8}\,{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}\) — one term already gives three figures (\(46.840\) against the two-term \(46.835\)), and the second is below the last figure kept.

The number of terms needed collapses from "infinitely many" to two within one decay time. That collapse is the smoothing.

4. The coefficient of mode \(n\) at time \(t\) is \(\frac{4\theta_0}{n\pi}\mathrm e^{-n^2t/\tau_1}\). For any fixed \(t > 0\), and any \(k\), \(n^k\mathrm e^{-n^2t/\tau_1} \to 0\) faster than any power of \(1/n\) — the exponential beats every polynomial — so \(\sum_n n^k|\alpha_n|\mathrm e^{-n^2t/\tau_1} < \infty\) for every \(k\). Each \(x\)-derivative multiplies term \(n\) by at most \((n\pi/L)^k\), so the \(k\)-times differentiated series converges uniformly by the \(M\)-test, and the real-analysis theorem on uniformly convergent derivatives lets it be differentiated term by term, for every \(k\). So \(\theta(\cdot,t)\) has continuous derivatives of every order — the smoothness–decay dictionary read backwards: the profile is \(C^\infty\) at once, however rough the data.

Running backwards multiplies mode \(n\) by \(\mathrm e^{+n^2|t|/\tau_1}\), which amplifies a coefficient of size \(\epsilon\) in mode \(n\) by a factor \(\mathrm e^{n^2|t|/\tau_1}\). At \(|t| = \tau_1\) and \(n = 10\) that factor is \(\mathrm e^{100} = 2.7\times10^{43}\): an error in the data of one part in \(10^{40}\) becomes an error of thousands of degrees. No continuous dependence on the data means ill-posed in the Hadamard sense of unit 18 — and now with a rate attached.

Check. Dimensions: \([\kappa] = \mathrm{L^2T^{-1}}\), so \(L^2/\kappa\) is a time ✓; \(\alpha_n\) carries \(\theta_0\), a temperature ✓. Limit: as \(t \to \infty\), every term dies and \(\theta \to 0\), which is right for a rod with both ends held at zero ✓. Special case: at \(t = 0\) the series must return the constant \(100\), and the Leibniz sum \(\frac4\pi\cdot\frac\pi4 = 1\) says it does, at every interior point — while at \(x = 0\) and \(x = L\) it returns \(0\), the midpoint of the jump, exactly as Dirichlet's theorem predicts ✓.

Another way

For very short times the mode sum is useless and the right picture is the other one: on a timescale \(t \ll \tau_1\) the ends have only influenced a layer of thickness \(\sim\sqrt{\kappa t}\), so \(\theta(L/2,t) = \theta_0\) to any accuracy you like. Setting \(\sqrt{\kappa t} = L/2\) gives \(t \approx 2250\ \mathrm s\) as the time at which the ends are first felt at the middle — the same order as \(\tau_1 = 913\ \mathrm s\), which is the consistency one wants between a similarity argument and a mode sum. The two descriptions swap over at \(t \sim \tau_1\), and unit 20's Fourier transform is what makes the short-time one exact.
