The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: unit 06's galvanometer, \(\ddot\theta + \gamma\dot\theta +
\omega_0^2\theta = \tau/I\) with \(\omega_0 = 8\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(\gamma =
0.4\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(Q = 20\). In this unit's convention its susceptibility
is \(\chi(\omega) = 1/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega)\), with poles at
\(\omega_\pm = \pm7.99750 - 0.2i\), and its Green's function is \(G(t) =
e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin(\omega_\mathrm{d}t)/\omega_\mathrm{d}\) for \(t > 0\), zero
before.

* The Fourier transform

- Fourier transform :: For absolutely integrable \(f\), \(\hat f(\omega) =
  \int f(t)e^{i\omega t}\,\mathrm dt\). This course's sign: a single frequency
  is \(e^{-i\omega t}\), and its spectrum sits at \(+\omega\). Unit 06 used the
  opposite sign; \(\chi_{20}(\omega) = \chi_{06}(-\omega)\). /Slide: From one
  frequency to all of them./
- Riemann–Lebesgue lemma :: The transform of an absolutely integrable
  function is continuous and tends to zero at infinity. Proved by approximating
  with step functions.
- Fourier inversion theorem :: If \(f\) is continuous and \(f, \hat f\) are
  absolutely integrable, \(f(t) = \frac1{2\pi}\int\hat f(\omega)e^{-i\omega
  t}\,\mathrm d\omega\). So the transform is injective. /Slide: Inversion./
- Plancherel's theorem :: \(\int|f|^2\,\mathrm dt = \frac1{2\pi}\int|\hat
  f|^2\,\mathrm d\omega\), and the polarised form \(\int f\bar g =
  \frac1{2\pi}\int\hat f\bar{\hat g}\): energy in time equals energy in
  frequency. /Slide: Plancherel and convolution./
- convolution theorem :: \(\widehat{f*g} = \hat f\hat g\). Applied to \(x = G *
  f\), it identifies \(\hat G = \chi\).
- uncertainty relation :: \(\Delta t\,\Delta\omega\ge\frac12\) for normalised
  \(f\), with spreads measured in \(|f|^2\) and \(|\hat f|^2/2\pi\); equality
  exactly for Gaussians. Proved by one integration by parts and Cauchy–Schwarz.
  /Slide: The uncertainty relation./

* Distributions

- test functions :: \(\mathcal D = C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)\), smooth functions of
  bounded support, with convergence meaning common bounded support and uniform
  convergence of every derivative. The bump \(e^{-1/(1-t^2)}\) shows they exist.
  /Slide: Test functions and distributions./
- distribution :: A continuous linear functional on test functions, \(\varphi
  \mapsto \langle T,\varphi\rangle\). Every locally integrable function is one,
  by \(\varphi\mapsto\int f\varphi\).
- Dirac delta :: The distribution \(\langle\delta,\varphi\rangle =
  \varphi(0)\). Not a function; the limit, in distributions, of pulses of unit
  area.
- principal value :: \(\langle\mathrm{PV}\frac1t,\varphi\rangle = \lim_{\varepsilon
  \to 0}\int_{|t|>\varepsilon}\varphi/t\), the symmetric excision that makes
  \(1/t\) a distribution.
- distributional derivative :: \(\langle T',\varphi\rangle = -\langle
  T,\varphi'\rangle\). Integration by parts made a definition; every
  distribution has derivatives of all orders, and \(H' = \delta\). /Slide:
  Derivatives by duality, and $LG = \delta$./
- jump formula :: For a piecewise \(C^1\) function, \(f' = \{f'\} +
  [f]\,\delta\): classical derivative plus the jump times a delta. Applied twice,
  it turns unit 06's jump condition into the theorem \(LG = \delta\).
- tempered distribution :: A continuous linear functional on the Schwartz class
  \(\mathcal S\) of rapidly decreasing smooth functions. Its transform is defined
  by duality, \(\langle\hat T,\varphi\rangle = \langle T,\hat\varphi\rangle\):
  \(\hat\delta = 1\), \(\hat 1 = 2\pi\delta\), \(\widehat{\operatorname{sgn}} =
  2i\,\mathrm{PV}\frac1\omega\). /Slide: Tempered distributions and their
  transforms./
- Sokhotski–Plemelj :: \(\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}1/(x\mp i\varepsilon) =
  \mathrm{PV}\frac1x \pm i\pi\delta\) as distributions. It is why an undamped
  oscillator absorbs only at resonance, as a spike.

* Holomorphic functions

- holomorphic :: Complex-differentiable at every point of an open set: the
  difference quotient has a limit as \(h\to0\) through complex values. Entire:
  holomorphic on all of \(\mathbb C\). /Slide: Complex differentiability, and
  Cauchy–Riemann./
- Cauchy–Riemann equations :: \(u_x = v_y\), \(u_y = -v_x\) for \(f = u + iv\)
  complex-differentiable; conversely, with continuous partials they imply
  holomorphy.
- contour integral :: \(\int_\gamma f\,\mathrm dz = \int_a^b
  f(\gamma(s))\gamma'(s)\,\mathrm ds\) for a piecewise \(C^1\) curve — an
  ordinary one-variable integral. /Slide: Integrals along curves./
- ML inequality :: \(|\int_\gamma f\,\mathrm dz| \le \max_\gamma|f|\cdot
  L(\gamma)\). Every arc estimate is this, used carefully.
- Goursat's theorem :: The integral of a holomorphic function round the
  boundary of a triangle in its domain is zero. Proved by subdivision, using only
  the existence of \(f'\). /Slide: Goursat's theorem./
- Cauchy's theorem :: On a convex open set a holomorphic function has a
  primitive, so its integral round every closed curve is zero. Extended to
  keyholes and indented contours by cutting; the general homology form is cited
  from Ahlfors. /Slide: Cauchy's theorem and the integral formula./
- Cauchy's integral formula :: \(f(z) = \frac1{2\pi i}\oint
  \frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta - z}\mathrm d\zeta\) round a circle enclosing \(z\):
  interior values are fixed by boundary values.
- analytic :: Equal to its Taylor series near each point. Every holomorphic
  function is analytic, with radius at least the distance to the nearest
  singularity. /Slide: What the integral formula buys./
- Liouville's theorem :: A bounded entire function is constant. One line from
  the Cauchy estimates.
- fundamental theorem of algebra :: Every nonconstant polynomial has a complex
  root — otherwise its reciprocal would be bounded and entire. Unit 06 and unit
  07 cited it; this is its proof.
- maximum modulus :: A holomorphic function on a connected open set whose
  modulus has a local maximum is constant.
- Morera :: A continuous function whose integral round every triangle vanishes
  is holomorphic. The standard way to show a function defined by an integral is
  holomorphic.
- identity theorem :: Two holomorphic functions on a connected open set that
  agree on a set with a limit point agree everywhere. /Slide: Analytic
  continuation./
- analytic continuation :: A holomorphic extension of a function to a larger
  domain; unique when it exists, by the identity theorem. It gives the Gaussian
  integral with complex width.

* Singularities and residues

- Laurent series :: \(f = \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}a_n(z - z_0)^n\) on an annulus;
  the negative powers are the principal part. Isolated singularities are
  removable, poles or essential according to the principal part. /Slide: Laurent
  series and isolated singularities./
- residue :: The coefficient \(a_{-1}\) of the Laurent series, equal to
  \(\frac1{2\pi i}\) times the integral round a small circle. For a simple pole
  of \(g/h\), \(g(z_0)/h'(z_0)\).
- residue theorem :: \(\oint_\gamma f = 2\pi i\sum\mathrm{Res}\) over the
  singularities inside an anticlockwise contour; \(-2\pi i\) clockwise. /Slide:
  The residue theorem./
- Jordan's lemma :: On the upper semicircle of radius \(R\),
  \(|\int g(z)e^{iaz}\mathrm dz|\le\pi\max|g|/a\) for \(a > 0\): enough to kill
  the arc when \(g\) only tends to zero, as \(1/R\). /Slide: Closing the contour:
  Jordan's lemma./
- Jordan's inequality :: \(\sin\theta\ge2\theta/\pi\) on \([0,\pi/2]\), from
  concavity; the engine of Jordan's lemma and of the Fresnel octant estimate.
- indentation lemma :: A small arc of angle \(\alpha\) round a simple pole on
  the contour contributes \(i\alpha\) times the residue in the limit — half a
  residue for a semicircle. /Slide: Worked: the Dirichlet integral, with an
  indentation./
- branch cut :: A curve removed from the plane so that a multivalued function
  such as \(\log z\) or \(z^a\) has a single-valued holomorphic branch on what
  remains. The cut is a choice. /Slide: Branch points and cuts./
- branch point :: A point round which continuing a function does not return it
  to its starting value: \(z^a\) is multiplied by \(e^{2\pi ia}\) per turn round
  \(0\). Not a choice.
- argument principle :: \(\frac1{2\pi i}\oint f'/f = Z - P\): zeros minus poles
  inside, the winding number of \(f(\gamma)\) round \(0\). /Slide: Counting
  zeros: the argument principle and Rouché./
- Rouché's theorem :: If \(|g| < |f|\) on \(\gamma\), then \(f\) and \(f + g\)
  have the same number of zeros inside. It proves the Laplace limit.

* Causality

- \(i0\) prescription :: Replacing \(\omega\) by \(\omega + i0\) in a response
  with poles on the real axis: the contour passes above them, and the inverse
  transform is the retarded, causal one. /Slide: The step function, explained./
- causality theorem :: For \(G\), \(tG\), \(\chi\) integrable: \(G = 0\) for
  \(t < 0\) iff \(\chi\) is holomorphic in the upper half-plane and decays
  there iff \(\chi\) satisfies the dispersion relations. The sharp
  square-integrable form is Titchmarsh's theorem. /Slide: Causality,
  analyticity, Kramers–Kronig: one theorem./
- Kramers–Kronig relations :: \(\chi' = \frac1\pi\mathrm{PV}\int\frac{\chi''(\omega')}{\omega' - \omega}\mathrm d\omega'\)
  and \(\chi'' = -\frac1\pi\mathrm{PV}\int\frac{\chi'(\omega')}{\omega' - \omega}\mathrm d\omega'\):
  the dispersive and absorptive parts of a causal response are Hilbert transforms
  of each other. /Slide: The dispersion relations, proved./
- sum rule :: An integral identity over the whole absorption spectrum forced
  by the dispersion relations and a known limit; here \(\int_0^\infty\omega
  \chi''\,\mathrm d\omega = \pi/2\), from the free-mass behaviour at high
  frequency. /Slide: Worked: Kramers–Kronig on the galvanometer./
- Sellmeier formula :: \(n^2 = 1 + \sum B_i\lambda^2/(\lambda^2 - C_i)\), a sum
  of undamped oscillators fitted to a glass. For BK7, resonances at 77.5 nm,
  141.5 nm and 10.18 µm. /Slide: Why glass separates colours./
- Laplace transform :: \(\mathcal L[f](s) = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\mathrm dt\),
  holomorphic in a right half-plane; the Fourier transform turned a quarter-turn,
  with \(\chi(\omega) = \mathcal L[G](-i\omega)\). /Slide: The Laplace transform
  and the Bromwich contour./
- Bromwich integral :: \(f(t) = \frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}
  F(s)e^{st}\mathrm ds\), along a vertical line to the right of every
  singularity. Closing left collects the poles for \(t > 0\).

* Asymptotics of integrals

- asymptotic expansion :: \(f\sim\sum a_n\varphi_n\) if the error after \(N\)
  terms is \(O(\varphi_N)\) for every \(N\) — convergent or not. Unit 21 develops
  the idea. /Slide: Watson's lemma./
- Watson's lemma :: If \(g(t)\sim\sum a_nt^{\alpha_n}\) as \(t\to0^+\), then
  \(\int_0^\infty g e^{-\lambda t}\sim\sum a_n\Gamma(\alpha_n+1)\lambda^{-\alpha_n-1}\).
  Only \(t\lesssim1/\lambda\) matters.
- Laplace's method :: \(\int g e^{\lambda h}\sim g(t_0)e^{\lambda h(t_0)}
  \sqrt{2\pi/\lambda|h''(t_0)|}\) at an interior nondegenerate maximum.
  /Slide: Laplace's method./
- Stirling's formula :: \(n!\sim\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^n\), Laplace's method on
  \(\int t^ne^{-t}\mathrm dt\); low by \(0.83\%\) at \(n = 10\).
- stationary phase :: \(\int g e^{i\lambda\psi}\) is dominated by points where
  \(\psi' = 0\), each contributing \(g\sqrt{2\pi/\lambda|\psi''|}\,e^{i\lambda
  \psi\pm i\pi/4}\), with error \(O(1/\lambda)\). Gives the constants in
  \(J_n\)'s large-\(x\) form. /Slide: Stationary phase./
- saddle point :: A zero of \(\varphi'\) for holomorphic \(\varphi\); since
  \(\mathrm{Re}\,\varphi\) has no local maximum, the best a contour can do is cross
  a saddle. /Slide: Steepest descent./
- path of steepest descent :: The curve through a saddle on which
  \(\mathrm{Im}\,\varphi\) is constant: no oscillation, and
  \(\mathrm{Re}\,\varphi\) falling fastest.
- method of steepest descent :: Deform the contour through a saddle along the
  steepest path, justify every arc, and apply Laplace's method there. Finds
  exponentially small answers that real methods cannot see.
- dispersion relation :: \(\omega = \omega(k)\), the frequency of a plane wave
  of wavenumber \(k\) in a medium. (Distinct from the Kramers–Kronig dispersion
  relations, which share the name.) /Slide: Wave packets: phase and group
  velocity./
- phase velocity :: \(v_\mathrm p = \omega/k\), the speed of the crests; \(c/n\)
  for light in glass.
- group velocity :: \(v_\mathrm g = \mathrm d\omega/\mathrm dk\), the speed of a
  packet, found by stationary phase; \(c/n_\mathrm g\) with \(n_\mathrm g = n -
  \lambda\,\mathrm dn/\mathrm d\lambda\). In BK7 at 587.6 nm, \(0.64878c\)
  against a phase velocity of \(0.65928c\).
