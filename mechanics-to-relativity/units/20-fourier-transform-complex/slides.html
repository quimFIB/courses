The Fourier transform, complex analysis, and why causality is a statement about poles

A linear system that cannot answer before it is asked has a frequency response with no poles in one half of the complex plane — and that one fact ties refraction to absorption, turns unit 06's step function into a theorem, and evaluates integrals nothing else can.

Unit 06 solved one oscillator completely and left four promissory notes: the delta function it avoided, the Fourier transform it computed without naming, the causality slide that said poles sit on one side of the real axis without saying why that matters, and the fundamental theorem of algebra, cited. Unit 09 left three more: the Laplace limit $0.6627\ldots$, the constants in the large-$x$ form of $J_n$, and how fast $I_n(ne)$ grows with $n$. This unit pays all seven.

It does so by building three pieces of mathematics that belong together. The Fourier transform diagonalises every linear time-invariant system, turning the differential equation into division. Distributions make the delta function and the Green's function honest. Complex analysis — holomorphy, Cauchy's theorem, residues — is what makes the transform invertible in practice, and it is also where causality turns out to live: a response function that vanishes before $t = 0$ is exactly one whose transform is holomorphic in the upper half-plane. Then asymptotics of integrals, which is contour deformation used for estimation rather than exact evaluation.

What you need, and what you do not

The dependency is unit 06 only, so nothing from units 03–05 is assumed: partial derivatives are defined here in the one line Cauchy–Riemann needs, and integrals along curves are defined here from scratch, as ordinary one-variable integrals of a parametrised path. The Fourier transform

A drive that is not periodic is still a superposition of frequencies — continuously many of them.

20 · transform

From one frequency to all of them

$f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C$ is absolutely integrable if $\int_{-\infty}^\infty|f(t)|\,\mathrm dt < \infty$ (an improper Riemann integral). For such $f$:

the Fourier transform $$\hat f(\omega) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(t)\,e^{i\omega t}\,\mathrm dt,\qquad \omega\in\mathbb R .$$

It converges absolutely, since $|e^{i\omega t}| = 1$, and $|\hat f(\omega)| \le \int|f|$.

Riemann–Lebesgue lemma

$\hat f$ is continuous and $\hat f(\omega)\to0$ as $|\omega|\to\infty$.

Shift, modulation and scaling, each one substitution: $f(t-a)\mapsto e^{i\omega a}\hat f$, $\ e^{-i\nu t}f\mapsto\hat f(\omega-\nu)$, $\ f(t/a)\mapsto |a|\hat f(a\omega)$.

a convention, changed from unit 06

Unit 06 drove with $e^{i\omega t}$ and got $\chi_{06} = 1/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + i\gamma\omega)$. From here on a single frequency is $e^{-i\omega t}$ — the convention of every later unit and of the physics literature — and

$$\chi(\omega) = \frac{1}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega} = \chi_{06}(-\omega).$$

For real $\omega$ that is the complex conjugate: same modulus, lag $\varphi$ now read as $\arg\chi$. The poles move to the lower half-plane.

Why a transform at all: the galvanometer's response to $e^{-i\omega t}$ is $\chi(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}$. If a drive is a superposition of such exponentials, linearity gives the response as the same superposition, each term multiplied by $\chi$. The transform finds the weights.

Where the normalisation goes. Conventions differ in three places: the sign in the exponent, the placement of $2\pi$, and whether $\omega$ or $\nu = \omega/2\pi$ is used. This course puts the whole $1/2\pi$ in the inverse and uses $+i\omega t$ in the forward transform, so that a signal $e^{-i\omega_1 t}$ has its spectrum at $+\omega_1$. With $e^{-i\omega t}$ as the unit of oscillation, a wave travelling towards $+x$ is $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ with $k, \omega > 0$: that is the reason for the change, and it is what unit 24 and every later unit use.

Full proof of the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma

Continuity. $|\hat f(\omega) - \hat f(\nu)| \le \int|f(t)||e^{i(\omega-\nu)t} - 1|\,\mathrm dt$. Given $\varepsilon$, choose $T$ with $\int_{|t|>T}|f| < \varepsilon$; on $|t|\le T$, $|e^{i(\omega-\nu)t} - 1|\le|\omega-\nu|T$. So the difference is at most $2\varepsilon + |\omega-\nu|T\int|f|$, which is $<3\varepsilon$ once $|\omega - \nu|$ is small.

Decay. For the indicator of an interval, $\int_a^be^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt = (e^{i\omega b} - e^{i\omega a})/i\omega$, of modulus at most $2/|\omega|$. So step functions (finite combinations of indicators) have transforms tending to zero. A Riemann-integrable function on $[-T, T]$ is within $\varepsilon$ in $\int|\cdot|$ of a step function $s$ (lower Darboux sums), and the tails beyond $T$ are within $\varepsilon$ too; then $|\hat f(\omega)| \le |\hat s(\omega)| + 2\varepsilon$, and the first term is below $\varepsilon$ for large $|\omega|$. $\square$

The three rules. $\int f(t-a)e^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt$ with $u = t - a$ is $e^{i\omega a}\hat f(\omega)$. $\int e^{-i\nu t}f(t)e^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt = \hat f(\omega - \nu)$ by inspection. $\int f(t/a)e^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt$ with $u = t/a$ is $|a|\int f(u)e^{ia\omega u}\mathrm du$, the modulus because a negative $a$ reverses the limits.

Dimensions. If $t$ is a time and $f$ has dimension $[f]$, then $\hat f$ has dimension $[f]\,\mathrm T$ and its argument $\omega$ is an inverse time. Every formula in this section can be checked against that.

Differentiation becomes multiplication

the dictionary

If $f$ is $C^1$ and $f, f'$ are absolutely integrable, then $\widehat{f'}(\omega) = -i\omega\hat f(\omega)$. If $f$ and $tf$ are absolutely integrable, then $\hat f$ is $C^1$ and $\widehat{tf} = -i\,\hat f\,{}'$.

Proof of the first. Integrate by parts on $[-T, T]$: $\int f'e^{i\omega t} = [fe^{i\omega t}]_{-T}^{T} - i\omega\int fe^{i\omega t}$, and $f(\pm T)\to0$ because $f$ has limits at $\pm\infty$ ($f'$ integrable) which must be $0$ ($f$ integrable). $\square$

Apply it to the galvanometer, $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = f$:

$$(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega)\,\hat x = \hat f,\quad\text{so}\quad\hat x = \chi\,\hat f .$$

A differential equation has become division. Dimensions: $[\chi] = \mathrm T^2$, $[\hat f] = [x]\mathrm T^{-2}\cdot\mathrm T$, so $[\hat x] = [x]\mathrm T$ ✓.

worked example 1 — the Gaussian, by an ODE

$f(t) = e^{-t^2/2}$. It satisfies $f' = -tf$. Transform both sides with the dictionary:

$$-i\omega\hat f = i\hat f\,{}',\quad\text{so}\quad\hat f\,{}' = -\omega\hat f .$$

Separable (unit 00), unique through its initial value (unit 01), and $\hat f(0) = \int e^{-t^2/2}\mathrm dt = \sqrt{2\pi}$ (notes):

$$\hat f(\omega) = \sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\omega^2/2}.$$

A Gaussian is its own transform, up to $\sqrt{2\pi}$. By scaling, $e^{-t^2/2\sigma^2}\mapsto\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\sigma^2\omega^2/2}$: width $\sigma$ in time, $1/\sigma$ in frequency.

Check: $\sigma\to\infty$, a constant signal, piles the spectrum up at $\omega = 0$; both sides carry $\mathrm T$ ✓.

Full proof of the second rule

$\frac{\hat f(\omega + h) - \hat f(\omega)}{h} = \int f(t)e^{i\omega t}\frac{e^{iht} - 1}{h}\,\mathrm dt$, and $|e^{iht} - 1|/|h| \le |t|$. The integrand is dominated by $|tf(t)|$, which is integrable, and converges pointwise to $itf(t)e^{i\omega t}$; dominated convergence (cited, Stein–Shakarchi, Real Analysis, ch. 2 Thm 1.13) gives $\hat f\,{}' = \widehat{itf}$, which is the rule. Continuity of $\hat f\,{}'$ is Riemann–Lebesgue applied to $tf$. $\square$

Full proof that $\int e^{-t^2/2}\mathrm dt = \sqrt{2\pi}$, without polar coordinates

Polar coordinates are unit 05, which this unit does not assume, so here is a one-variable proof. Let $A(x) = \big(\int_0^xe^{-s^2}\mathrm ds\big)^2$ and $B(x) = \int_0^1\frac{e^{-x^2(1+u^2)}}{1+u^2}\mathrm du$. Then $A'(x) = 2e^{-x^2}\int_0^xe^{-s^2}\mathrm ds$, and differentiating under the integral sign (the integrand and its $x$-derivative are continuous on the compact $[0,1]$, so this is the elementary Leibniz rule) gives $B'(x) = -2x\int_0^1e^{-x^2(1+u^2)}\mathrm du = -2e^{-x^2}\int_0^xe^{-s^2}\mathrm ds$ with $s = xu$. So $A + B$ is constant, equal to $A(0) + B(0) = \int_0^1\frac{\mathrm du}{1+u^2} = \frac\pi4$. As $x\to\infty$, $B(x)\le e^{-x^2}\to0$, so $\big(\int_0^\infty e^{-s^2}\big)^2 = \pi/4$. Hence $\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-s^2} = \sqrt\pi$, and $s = t/\sqrt2$ gives $\sqrt{2\pi}$. $\square$

Why the ODE route matters. It computes the transform without evaluating a single oscillatory integral. Section 3 extends the answer to a complex width — a step that needs analytic continuation to be legitimate — and section 6 uses that extension for a spreading wave packet.

The oscillator equation, transformed. The dictionary applies twice ($\widehat{\ddot x} = (-i\omega)^2\hat x$) provided $x, \dot x, \ddot x$ are integrable — true for the response to an integrable drive once the transient has died, and exactly true for $x = G * f$.

Inversion

the Fourier inversion theorem

If $f$ is continuous and both $f$ and $\hat f$ are absolutely integrable, then for every $t$

$$f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(\omega)\,e^{-i\omega t}\,\mathrm d\omega .$$

In particular $\hat f = 0$ forces $f = 0$: the transform is injective, so an identity between transforms is an identity between functions.

the hypothesis on $\hat f$ is not decoration

The box $f = \mathbf 1_{[-1,1]}$ has $\hat f(\omega) = 2\sin\omega/\omega$, which is not absolutely integrable — because $f$ jumps. At the jump the symmetric limit $\lim_{W\to\infty}\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-W}^{W}$ still converges, to the midpoint $\frac12$ (notes).

The idea of the proof. Insert a Gaussian $e^{-\varepsilon\omega^2/2}$ to make the double integral absolutely convergent, swap the order, and recognise the result:

$$\frac1{2\pi}\!\int\!\hat f e^{-i\omega t}e^{-\varepsilon\omega^2/2}\mathrm d\omega = \!\int\! f(s)K_\varepsilon(t - s)\mathrm ds,$$

where $K_\varepsilon(u) = e^{-u^2/2\varepsilon}/\sqrt{2\pi\varepsilon}$ is worked example 1 read backwards: positive, total integral $1$, concentrating at $u = 0$ as $\varepsilon\to0$. So the right side tends to $f(t)$, and the left side to the inversion integral.

On the running example: $G$ is continuous and integrable and $|\chi|\le C/(1+\omega^2)$ is integrable, so $G$ is recovered from $\chi$ by this formula. Section 5 evaluates it.

Full proof

Step 1 — the swap. $\int\!\!\int|f(s)|e^{-\varepsilon\omega^2/2}\,\mathrm ds\,\mathrm d\omega < \infty$, so Fubini (cited) allows $\frac1{2\pi}\int\big(\int f(s)e^{i\omega s}\mathrm ds\big)e^{-i\omega t}e^{-\varepsilon\omega^2/2}\mathrm d\omega = \int f(s)\Big(\frac1{2\pi}\int e^{-i\omega(t-s)}e^{-\varepsilon\omega^2/2}\mathrm d\omega\Big)\mathrm ds$. The inner integral is the transform of a Gaussian of width $\sigma = 1/\sqrt\varepsilon$, evaluated at $-(t-s)$: by worked example 1 and scaling, $\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{2\pi/\varepsilon}\,e^{-(t-s)^2/2\varepsilon} = K_\varepsilon(t - s)$.

Step 2 — the right side tends to $f(t)$. $\int f(s)K_\varepsilon(t-s)\,\mathrm ds - f(t) = \int\big(f(t - u) - f(t)\big)K_\varepsilon(u)\,\mathrm du$. Given $\eta > 0$ choose $\delta$ with $|f(t-u) - f(t)| < \eta$ for $|u| < \delta$ (continuity). The part $|u| < \delta$ is below $\eta$. The part $|u|\ge\delta$ is at most $\sup_{|u|\ge\delta}K_\varepsilon(u)\int|f| + |f(t)|\int_{|u|\ge\delta}K_\varepsilon$, and both terms tend to $0$ with $\varepsilon$ because $K_\varepsilon(\delta)\to0$ and the Gaussian's mass concentrates.

Step 3 — the left side tends to the inversion integral. $|\hat f(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}(e^{-\varepsilon\omega^2/2} - 1)|\le2|\hat f(\omega)|$, integrable by hypothesis, and pointwise the difference tends to $0$: dominated convergence. $\square$

The box. $\int_{-1}^1e^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt = (e^{i\omega} - e^{-i\omega})/i\omega = 2\sin\omega/\omega$. Not absolutely integrable: over $[k\pi, (k+1)\pi]$ the integral of $|\sin\omega|/\omega$ is at least $2/((k+1)\pi)$, and the harmonic series diverges. The midpoint, for the box. At $t = 1$, $\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-W}^{W}\frac{2\sin\omega}{\omega}e^{-i\omega}\,\mathrm d\omega = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-W}^{W}\frac{2\sin\omega\cos\omega}{\omega}\,\mathrm d\omega = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-W}^{W}\frac{\sin2\omega}{\omega}\,\mathrm d\omega$ (the odd part integrates to zero), which tends to $\frac1{2\pi}\cdot\pi = \frac12$ by worked example 2: halfway between $f(1^-) = 1$ and $f(1^+) = 0$. The general statement — the symmetric limit gives the midpoint at every jump of a piecewise-$C^1$ integrable $f$ — is cited (Titchmarsh, Introduction to the Theory of Fourier Integrals, ch. 1) and is not needed later in this course.

History. Fourier wrote the inversion formula in 1807–1822 for heat flow on an infinite rod, without proof. Cauchy and Poisson both had versions; the approximate-identity argument above is essentially Weierstrass's (1885). Inserting a convergence factor and removing it at the end is the single most useful trick in this subject, and it returns in section 2 and section 5.

Plancherel and convolution

Plancherel's theorem

For $f, g$ as in the inversion theorem,

$$\int f\,\bar g\,\mathrm dt = \frac1{2\pi}\int\hat f\,\bar{\hat g}\,\mathrm d\omega,$$

and in particular $\int|f|^2\mathrm dt = \frac1{2\pi}\int|\hat f|^2\mathrm d\omega$.

Proof, one line. Write $\bar g$ by inversion, swap (Fubini): $\int f(t)\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\bar{\hat g}(\omega)e^{i\omega t}\mathrm d\omega\,\mathrm dt = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int\bar{\hat g}\hat f\,\mathrm d\omega$. $\square$

Energy in time equals energy in frequency: the transform is, up to $\sqrt{2\pi}$, an isometry. It extends to all square-integrable functions (cited: Stein–Shakarchi, Real Analysis, ch. 5 §1).

the convolution theorem

If $f, g$ are absolutely integrable and bounded, $(f*g)(t) = \int f(s)g(t-s)\,\mathrm ds$ satisfies

$$\widehat{f*g} = \hat f\,\hat g .$$

Proof: swap the two integrals and substitute $u = t - s$, which splits $e^{i\omega t} = e^{i\omega s}e^{i\omega u}$. $\square$ So unit 06's $x = G*f$ becomes $\hat x = \hat G\hat f$ — and comparing with the dictionary slide,

$$\hat G(\omega) = \chi(\omega).$$
checked on the running example

$\int_0^\infty G^2\,\mathrm dt = \dfrac{1}{2\gamma\omega_0^2} = 0.01953125\ \mathrm{s^3}$, and $\dfrac1{2\pi}\displaystyle\int|\chi|^2\mathrm d\omega = \dfrac{0.1227185}{2\pi} = 0.01953125$ ✓.

$\hat G = \chi$, directly. $\int_0^\infty e^{-\gamma t/2}\frac{\sin\omega_\mathrm dt}{\omega_\mathrm d}e^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt$: write $\sin = (e^{i\omega_\mathrm dt} - e^{-i\omega_\mathrm dt})/2i$ and integrate the exponentials, $\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\mathrm dt = 1/a$ for $\operatorname{Re}a>0$, with $a_\pm = \gamma/2 - i\omega \mp i\omega_\mathrm d$. Then $\hat G = \frac{1}{2i\omega_\mathrm d}\big(\frac1{a_+} - \frac1{a_-}\big) = \frac{1}{2i\omega_\mathrm d}\cdot\frac{a_- - a_+}{a_+a_-} = \frac{1}{a_+a_-}$, and $a_+a_- = (\gamma/2 - i\omega)^2 + \omega_\mathrm d^2 = \omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega$ ✓. This is unit 06's worked example 3 with the new sign of $\omega$.

The Plancherel numbers. $\int_0^\infty e^{-\gamma t}\sin^2(\omega_\mathrm dt)\,\mathrm dt = \frac12\big(\frac1\gamma - \frac{\gamma}{\gamma^2 + 4\omega_\mathrm d^2}\big) = \frac{2\omega_\mathrm d^2}{\gamma\cdot4\omega_0^2}$ using $\gamma^2 + 4\omega_\mathrm d^2 = 4\omega_0^2$; divided by $\omega_\mathrm d^2$ that is $1/(2\gamma\omega_0^2) = 1/51.2$. The frequency side, $\int\frac{\mathrm d\omega}{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2} = \frac{\pi}{\gamma\omega_0^2}$, is problem P5, by residues; numerically $0.12271846$. Dimensions: $[G] = \mathrm T$, so $\int G^2\mathrm dt$ is $\mathrm T^3$; $[\chi]^2[\omega] = \mathrm T^4\mathrm T^{-1}$ ✓. Limit: as $\gamma\to0$ both sides diverge like $1/\gamma$ — an undamped oscillator rings forever and its response is concentrated on two infinitely sharp lines.

Full proof of the convolution theorem

$f*g$ is bounded by $\sup|g|\int|f|$, and $\int|f*g|\le\int|f|\int|g|$ by Tonelli (the nonnegative case of Fubini, cited with it). Then $\widehat{f*g}(\omega) = \int\!\!\int f(s)g(t-s)e^{i\omega t}\mathrm ds\,\mathrm dt$; the double integral converges absolutely, so Fubini lets the $t$-integral go inside, and with $u = t - s$ it is $\int f(s)e^{i\omega s}\big(\int g(u)e^{i\omega u}\mathrm du\big)\mathrm ds = \hat f\hat g$. $\square$

The structural point. Every exponential $e^{-i\omega t}$ is an eigenfunction of every time-invariant linear operator — time translation acts on it by a scalar, and anything commuting with all translations must too. The Fourier transform is the change of basis to those eigenfunctions, and in that basis every such operator is diagonal: multiplication by its eigenvalue $\chi(\omega)$. That is why one function of frequency is the whole system.

The uncertainty relation

For $f$ with $\int|f|^2 = 1$ define the spreads about the means $\bar t$, $\bar\omega$:

$$\begin{aligned}\Delta t^2 &= \textstyle\int(t-\bar t)^2|f|^2\,\mathrm dt,\\ \Delta\omega^2 &= \textstyle\frac1{2\pi}\int(\omega - \bar\omega)^2|\hat f|^2\,\mathrm d\omega .\end{aligned}$$
the uncertainty relation

If $f$ is $C^1$ with $f, tf, f'$ square-integrable and $\sqrt{|t|}f\to0$ at $\pm\infty$,

$$\Delta t\,\Delta\omega \ge \tfrac12,$$

with equality exactly for Gaussians $f = Ce^{-a(t - \bar t)^2 - i\bar\omega t}$, $a > 0$.

Physics: a short pulse cannot be monochromatic. A 10 fs laser pulse carries a spread of frequencies of order $10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, whatever the laser.

proof, by Cauchy–Schwarz

Translate and modulate so $\bar t = \bar\omega = 0$ (neither changes $\Delta t$, $\Delta\omega$). Integrate by parts:

$$1 = -\!\int t\,(|f|^2)'\,\mathrm dt = -2\operatorname{Re}\!\int t\bar ff'\,\mathrm dt .$$

Cauchy–Schwarz: $1\le2\,\|tf\|\,\|f'\|$. By the dictionary and Plancherel, $\|f'\|^2 = \frac1{2\pi}\int\omega^2|\hat f|^2 = \Delta\omega^2$, and $\|tf\|^2 = \Delta t^2$. So $1\le 2\Delta t\,\Delta\omega$. $\square$

checks

Equality: $e^{-t^2/2}/\pi^{1/4}$ has $\Delta t^2 = \Delta\omega^2 = \frac12$ ✓. Dimensions: $\mathrm T\cdot\mathrm T^{-1}$ ✓. The running example: $G$ gives $\Delta t\,\Delta\omega = \sqrt{Q^2 - \frac12 + \frac1{4Q^2}} = 19.9875$ — far from minimal, and P2 says why.

Full proof, the steps the slide compresses

The integration by parts. $(|f|^2)' = (f\bar f)' = f'\bar f + f\bar f' = 2\operatorname{Re}(\bar ff')$. On $[-T, T]$, $\int t(|f|^2)' = [t|f|^2]_{-T}^T - \int|f|^2$, and the boundary term vanishes as $T\to\infty$ by the hypothesis $\sqrt{|t|}f\to0$. Cauchy–Schwarz in the inner product $\langle u, v\rangle = \int u\bar v$: $|\int(t f)\overline{f'}| \le \|tf\|\|f'\|$, and $|\operatorname{Re}z|\le|z|$. Plancherel for $f'$ with $\widehat{f'} = -i\omega\hat f$ gives $\int|f'|^2 = \frac1{2\pi}\int\omega^2|\hat f|^2$.

Removing the means. $g(t) = e^{i\bar\omega t}f(t + \bar t)$ has $|g(t)| = |f(t+\bar t)|$, so its time spread is the same about mean $0$; its transform is $e^{-i(\omega+\bar\omega)\bar t}\,\hat f(\omega + \bar\omega)$ (shift and modulation rules), whose modulus is $|\hat f(\omega+\bar\omega)|$, so its frequency spread is the same about mean $0$.

The equality case. Cauchy–Schwarz is an equality iff $f' = ctf$ for a constant $c$, and the real part must equal the modulus, which forces $c$ real; integrability forces $c < 0$. The ODE $f' = ctf$ has the unique solution $f = f(0)e^{ct^2/2}$ (unit 01). Undoing the translation and modulation gives the family on the slide. P2 proves the converse.

The 10 fs pulse. For a Gaussian pulse of intensity full width $10$ fs, $\Delta t = 10/(2\sqrt{2\ln2}) = 4.25$ fs in the $|f|^2$ sense, so $\Delta\omega\ge1/(2\Delta t) = 1.18\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. The optical carrier at $800$ nm is $\omega = 2.35\times10^{15}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, so the pulse spans about $\pm5\%$ of its own frequency: it is white-ish, and section 6 shows what glass does to it.

Quantum mechanics, which this course does not reach, reads this theorem with $t\to x$, $\omega\to p/\hbar$, and calls it Heisenberg's; the mathematics is the slide, unchanged. Weyl gave this proof in 1928.

The galvanometer's value, derived in P2: $\Delta\omega = \omega_0$ exactly and $\Delta t^2 = \gamma^{-2} - \frac{1}{2\omega_0^2} + \frac{\gamma^2}{4\omega_0^4}$, product $\sqrt{400 - 0.5 + 0.000625} = 19.9875$ at $Q = 20$. The spread in frequency is measured about $\bar\omega = 0$, and a real signal has two spectral peaks at $\pm\omega_\mathrm d$: most of $\Delta\omega$ is the distance between the peaks, not their width.

Test functions and distributions

test functions

$\mathcal D = C_c^\infty(\mathbb R)$: smooth functions vanishing outside a bounded set. $\varphi_n\to\varphi$ in $\mathcal D$ means: all supports in one bounded set, and $\varphi_n^{(k)}\to\varphi^{(k)}$ uniformly for every $k$.

They exist: $\psi(t) = e^{-1/(1-t^2)}$ for $|t|<1$, $0$ otherwise, is $C^\infty$.

a distribution

A linear map $T:\mathcal D\to\mathbb C$, written $\varphi\mapsto\langle T,\varphi\rangle$, that is continuous: $\varphi_n\to\varphi$ implies $\langle T,\varphi_n\rangle\to\langle T,\varphi\rangle$.

Physics first, here too: a ballistic galvanometer reads $\int i\,\mathrm dt$ and nothing else about the pulse (unit 06). An idealised kick is exactly that: defined by what it does to smooth responses, not by its values.

Three examples.

  • Functions. A locally integrable $f$ gives $\langle T_f,\varphi\rangle = \int f\varphi$. Different continuous $f$ give different $T_f$, so functions sit inside distributions.
  • The Dirac delta: $\langle\delta,\varphi\rangle = \varphi(0)$.
  • The principal value: $\big\langle\mathrm{PV}\tfrac1t,\varphi\big\rangle = \displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\int_{|t|>\varepsilon}\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\,\mathrm dt = \int_0^\infty\frac{\varphi(t) - \varphi(-t)}{t}\,\mathrm dt$.
$\delta$ is not a function

Full proof that $\psi$ is smooth

The principal value is a distribution. $\int_{|t|>\varepsilon}\varphi/t$: the odd part of $1/t$ pairs the region $t>\varepsilon$ with $t<-\varepsilon$, giving $\int_\varepsilon^\infty(\varphi(t) - \varphi(-t))/t\,\mathrm dt$. the jump condition, as a theorem

Let $L = \frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm dt^2} + \gamma\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt} + \omega_0^2$ and $G$ vanish for $t < 0$, $C^2$ on $t > 0$ with one-sided limits. Then $LG = \delta$ in distributions iff

The galvanometer's $G$ was built in unit 06 to meet exactly these three conditions. So $LG = \delta$ — the defining property, now a statement about a well-defined object.

Full proof of the jump formula

$\langle f',\varphi\rangle = -\int_{-\infty}^0f\varphi' - \int_0^\infty f\varphi'$. On $(-\infty,0)$: $-[f\varphi]_{-\infty}^{0^-} + \int_{-\infty}^0f'\varphi = -f(0^-)\varphi(0) + \int_{-\infty}^0f'\varphi$. On $(0,\infty)$: $-[f\varphi]_{0^+}^\infty + \int_0^\infty f'\varphi = f(0^+)\varphi(0) + \int_0^\infty f'\varphi$. The second application. $\dot G$ is itself piecewise $C^1$ with jump $[\dot G]$, so $\ddot G = \{\ddot G\} + [\dot G]\delta + ([G]\delta)'$ — the last term because the distributional derivative of $\{\dot G\} + [G]\delta$ is $\{\ddot G\} + [\dot G]\delta + [G]\delta'$. Complex differentiability, and Cauchy–Riemann

holomorphic

$f$ on an open $U\subseteq\mathbb C$ is complex-differentiable at $z$ if $\displaystyle f'(z) = \lim_{h\to0,\ h\in\mathbb C}\frac{f(z+h) - f(z)}{h}$ exists; holomorphic on $U$ if at every point. Entire: holomorphic on $\mathbb C$.

Sums, products, quotients and compositions obey the real rules, with the real proofs. So polynomials and rational functions are holomorphic off the zeros of their denominators — $\chi$ on $\mathbb C\setminus\{\omega_+,\omega_-\}$.

Physics: $\chi$ at complex $\omega = \omega_r + i\omega_i$ is the response to $e^{-i\omega t} = e^{\omega_it}e^{-i\omega_rt}$ — a drive growing in time when $\omega_i > 0$, switched on from nothing at $t = -\infty$.

Partial derivatives, all this unit needs of them: for $u(x, y)$, $u_x$ is the derivative of $x\mapsto u(x,y)$ with $y$ held fixed, and $u_y$ likewise.

the Cauchy–Riemann equations

If $f = u + iv$ is complex-differentiable at $z = x + iy$, then

$$u_x = v_y,\qquad u_y = -v_x,\qquad f' = u_x + iv_x .$$

Proof. Let $h\to0$ along the reals: $f' = u_x + iv_x$. Along $h = ik$: $f' = \frac1i(u_y + iv_y) = v_y - iu_y$. Equate. $\square$

most smooth functions are not holomorphic

$\bar z = x - iy$: $u_x = 1\ne-1 = v_y$ everywhere. $|z|^2$ is differentiable at $0$ only. Holomorphy is a strong condition, and everything in this section is its price.

The converse, with its full proof

Theorem. If $u, v$ have continuous partial derivatives on $U$ satisfying Cauchy–Riemann, then $f = u + iv$ is holomorphic on $U$.

Proof. Write $h = a + ib$. Split the increment of $u$ in two one-variable steps: $u(x+a, y+b) - u(x,y) = [u(x+a,y+b) - u(x, y+b)] + [u(x,y+b) - u(x,y)]$. By the one-variable mean value theorem this is $u_x(x + \theta a, y + b)a + u_y(x, y + \theta'b)b$ with $\theta,\theta'\in(0,1)$, and by continuity of the partials it is $u_xa + u_yb + o(|h|)$. Same for $v$. Then $f(z+h) - f(z) = (u_x a + u_y b) + i(v_x a + v_y b) + o(|h|)$, and Cauchy–Riemann turns the bracket into $(u_x + iv_x)(a + ib)$: indeed $(u_x + iv_x)(a + ib) = u_xa - v_xb + i(v_xa + u_xb) = u_xa + u_yb + i(v_xa + v_yb)$. So $(f(z+h) - f(z))/h\to u_x + iv_x$. $\square$

The exponential. $e^z = e^x(\cos y + i\sin y)$ (unit 06's definition): $u_x = e^x\cos y = v_y$, $u_y = -e^x\sin y = -v_x$, all continuous, so $e^z$ is entire with $(e^z)' = u_x + iv_x = e^z$. Then $\cos z = (e^{iz} + e^{-iz})/2$ and $\sin z$ are entire too, and $\sin(\pi/2 + ix) = \cosh x$, which P10 needs.

What CR says geometrically. The real derivative of $(x,y)\mapsto(u,v)$ is the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}u_x&u_y\\v_x&v_y\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a&-b\\b&a\end{pmatrix}$, which is multiplication by $a + ib$: a rotation and a dilation. Holomorphic maps are the ones that are infinitesimally rotations-and-scalings — they preserve angles wherever $f'\ne0$.

The physics of complex frequency is worth holding on to, because section 5 turns on it. A drive $e^{\omega_it}e^{-i\omega_rt}$ with $\omega_i > 0$ was vanishingly small in the distant past; a causal system's response to it is well defined, $\int_0^\infty G(\tau)e^{i\omega\tau}\mathrm d\tau$, because the weight $e^{-\omega_i\tau}$ kills the old history. With $\omega_i < 0$ the drive was enormous in the past and the response integral may diverge. The half-plane in which a response function is automatically defined is fixed by the direction of time.

Integrals along curves

the contour integral

For a piecewise $C^1$ curve $\gamma:[a,b]\to U$ and continuous $f$,

$$\int_\gamma f(z)\,\mathrm dz := \int_a^b f(\gamma(s))\,\gamma'(s)\,\mathrm ds,$$

an ordinary integral of a complex-valued function of one real variable. Reparametrising does not change it (substitution); reversing the direction flips its sign.

the ML inequality $$\Big|\int_\gamma f\,\mathrm dz\Big|\le\max_\gamma|f|\cdot L(\gamma),$$

with $L(\gamma) = \int_a^b|\gamma'(s)|\,\mathrm ds$ the length.

Every arc estimate in this unit is this inequality, used carefully.

primitives kill closed integrals

If $F' = f$ on $U$ then $\int_\gamma f\,\mathrm dz = F(\gamma(b)) - F(\gamma(a))$, and $\oint_\gamma f\,\mathrm dz = 0$ round every closed curve.

Proof: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}F(\gamma(s)) = F'(\gamma(s))\gamma'(s)$, from the definition of $F'$; then the fundamental theorem of calculus. $\square$

the one integral that is not zero

Round $\gamma(s) = re^{is}$, $0\le s\le2\pi$, the integrand is $ir^{n+1}e^{i(n+1)s}$, so

$$\oint z^n\,\mathrm dz = \begin{cases}2\pi i, & n = -1,\\ 0, & n\ne-1.\end{cases}$$

So $1/z$ has no primitive on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$. Residue theory is this computation, organised.

Why no line-integral machinery is needed. Unit 04 (not a prerequisite here) builds line integrals of vector fields in the plane. A contour integral is the same object in disguise — $f\,\mathrm dz = (u + iv)(\mathrm dx + i\,\mathrm dy)$ is two real line integrals — but nothing in this unit uses that: the definition above is a one-variable integral, and every property needed follows from one-variable calculus.

Proof of the ML inequality. For a complex-valued $g$ on $[a,b]$, $|\int g|\le\int|g|$: write $\int g = |\int g|e^{i\theta}$; then $|\int g| = \operatorname{Re}\int e^{-i\theta}g = \int\operatorname{Re}(e^{-i\theta}g)\le\int|g|$. With $g = f(\gamma)\gamma'$ that gives $\le\int|f(\gamma)||\gamma'|\le\max|f|\,L$. $\square$

The chain rule step. $\frac{F(\gamma(s+\eta)) - F(\gamma(s))}{\eta} = \frac{F(\gamma(s+\eta)) - F(\gamma(s))}{\gamma(s+\eta) - \gamma(s)}\cdot\frac{\gamma(s+\eta) - \gamma(s)}{\eta}$ when $\gamma(s+\eta)\ne\gamma(s)$; the first factor tends to $F'(\gamma(s))$ and the second to $\gamma'(s)$. (Where $\gamma' (s) = 0$ write $F(w) - F(\gamma(s)) = (F'(\gamma(s)) + \epsilon(w))(w - \gamma(s))$ with $\epsilon\to0$ instead.)

The circle. $\gamma'(s) = ire^{is}$, so $\gamma^n\gamma' = ir^{n+1}e^{i(n+1)s}$, whose integral over a period is $0$ unless $n + 1 = 0$. For $n\ne-1$ the primitive $z^{n+1}/(n+1)$ gives the same answer. The failure at $n = -1$ is the failure of $\log z$ to be single-valued: going once round adds $2\pi i$ to its imaginary part, $\arg z$. Section 4 turns that into branch cuts.

Goursat's theorem

Goursat's theorem

If $f$ is holomorphic on $U$ and $T\subset U$ is a closed solid triangle, then $\displaystyle\oint_{\partial T}f\,\mathrm dz = 0$.

proof, by subdivision

Join the midpoints of the sides: four triangles, whose boundary integrals sum to $\oint_{\partial T}$ (inner edges cancel). One, $T_1$, has $|\oint_{\partial T_1}|\ge\frac14|\oint_{\partial T}|$. Repeat: nested $T_n$ of diameter $2^{-n}d$, perimeter $2^{-n}p$, and $|\oint_{\partial T_n}f|\ge4^{-n}|\oint_{\partial T}f|$.

Compactness: $\bigcap T_n = \{z_0\}$, and $f(z) = f(z_0) + f'(z_0)(z - z_0) + \epsilon(z)(z - z_0)$ with $\epsilon\to0$. The linear part has a primitive; ML on the rest:

$4^{-n}|\oint_{\partial T}f|\le\sup_{T_n}|\epsilon|\cdot2^{-n}d\cdot2^{-n}p$. Multiply by $4^n$; let $n\to\infty$. $\square$

what was used, and what was not

Only the existence of $f'$ at each point: not its continuity, not Green's theorem, not a double integral. Cauchy (1825) assumed $f'$ continuous; Goursat showed it superfluous — as it must be, for $f'$ to come out smooth on the next slides.

one exceptional point is allowed

The conclusion still holds if $f$ is continuous on $U$ and holomorphic on $U\setminus\{p\}$ (notes). This is what the integral formula will need.

For $\chi$: any triangle avoiding $\omega_\pm$ has $\oint\chi = 0$. A triangle enclosing $\omega_+$ need not — the next section computes what it gives.

Full proof, the three details

The inner edges cancel because each is traversed once in each direction by the two triangles sharing it, and reversal flips the sign. Choosing $T_1$: if all four had $|\oint| < \frac14|\oint_{\partial T}|$ the triangle inequality would give $|\oint_{\partial T}| < |\oint_{\partial T}|$. Nested compact sets with diameters tending to $0$ meet in exactly one point (the learner's metric-space topology: pick $z_n\in T_n$, a Cauchy sequence, whose limit lies in every $T_n$), and $z_0\in T\subset U$ so $f$ is differentiable there. The remainder estimate: on $\partial T_n$, $|z - z_0|\le\operatorname{diam}T_n$, so $|\epsilon(z)(z - z_0)|\le\sup_{T_n}|\epsilon|\,2^{-n}d$, and the ML inequality multiplies by $L(\partial T_n) = 2^{-n}p$.

Full proof of the exceptional-point version

If $p\notin T$ this is Goursat. If $p$ is a vertex of $T$, cut off a tiny triangle $T_\eta$ at that vertex, of perimeter $\le\eta$; the rest of $T$ is a union of triangles not containing $p$, whose integrals vanish, so $|\oint_{\partial T}f| = |\oint_{\partial T_\eta}f|\le\eta\max_T|f|\to0$ (continuity makes $f$ bounded on the compact $T$). If $p$ is on an edge or inside, split $T$ into two or three triangles having $p$ as a vertex. $\square$

History. Cauchy's integral theorem appears in his 1825 memoir on integrals between imaginary limits, proved with what amounts to Green's theorem and so with $f'$ assumed continuous. Goursat's subdivision argument is from 1884, published in its final form in 1900; Pringsheim noticed triangles are simpler than Goursat's rectangles. The argument is the model for many later proofs: assume the quantity is not small, localise by bisection, and contradict a local property.

Cauchy's theorem and the integral formula

Cauchy's theorem (convex version)

If $f$ is holomorphic on a convex open set $U$, then $F(z) = \int_{[c,z]}f$ (straight segment from a fixed $c$) is a primitive of $f$, and so $\oint_\gamma f\,\mathrm dz = 0$ for every closed curve $\gamma$ in $U$.

Proof. Goursat on the triangle $c, z, z+h$: $F(z+h) - F(z) = \int_{[z,z+h]}f = hf(z) + \int_{[z,z+h]}(f - f(z))$, and the last term is at most $|h|\max_{[z,z+h]}|f - f(z)| = o(|h|)$. So $F' = f$; then the primitive rule. $\square$

contours that are not in a convex set — cited

Semicircles, rectangles and sectors are convex. For keyholes and indented lines the theorem holds too: cut the region into convex pieces (notes). The general form — $\oint_\gamma f = 0$ when $\gamma$ winds zero times round every point outside $U$ — is cited: Ahlfors, Complex Analysis, ch. 4 §4.

Cauchy's integral formula

If $f$ is holomorphic on an open set containing the closed disc $|\zeta - z_0|\le r$, then for $|z - z_0| < r$

$$f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{|\zeta - z_0| = r}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta - z}\,\mathrm d\zeta .$$

Proof. $g(\zeta) = \frac{f(\zeta) - f(z)}{\zeta - z}$, with $g(z) = f'(z)$, is continuous, and holomorphic except at $z$; so $\oint g = 0$ (exceptional-point Goursat, convex version, on a slightly larger disc). Hence $\oint\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta - z} = f(z)\oint\frac{\mathrm d\zeta}{\zeta - z} = 2\pi i\,f(z)$ (notes). $\square$

The values of a holomorphic function inside a circle are determined by its values on the circle. Nothing like this is true of real $C^\infty$ functions — the bump function of section 2 is zero on a whole interval and not zero.

Full proof that $\oint\frac{\mathrm d\zeta}{\zeta - z} = 2\pi i$ for every $z$ inside the circle

Write $\frac{1}{\zeta - z} = \frac{1}{(\zeta - z_0) - (z - z_0)} = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(z - z_0)^n}{(\zeta - z_0)^{n+1}}$, a geometric series with ratio $|z - z_0|/r < 1$ uniformly on the circle, so it may be integrated term by term. By the circle computation of the previous slide (centred at $z_0$), only $n = 0$ survives, giving $2\pi i$. $\square$

Why the convex version reaches every contour this unit uses

A keyhole (P8) — a large circle and a small one joined along both edges of a cut — bounds a region that the two perpendicular lines through the centre cut into four pieces, each lying in a convex set (a slit sector of opening less than $\pi$) on which the integrand is holomorphic; the boundaries of the four pieces add up to the keyhole, the internal cuts cancelling. An indented line closed by a semicircle is the difference of a half-disc and a small half-disc, cut in the same way along the imaginary axis. The annulus needed for Laurent series is handled the same way. So in this unit the cited general theorem is a convenience: each application could be checked by cutting. Stein & Shakarchi, Complex Analysis, ch. 2, formalise this as "toy contours".

The general formula. For any closed curve $\gamma$ in $U$ and $z\notin\gamma$, the winding number $n(\gamma, z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{\mathrm d\zeta}{\zeta - z}$ is an integer, and $n(\gamma, z)f(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta - z}\mathrm d\zeta$ whenever $\gamma$ is null-homologous in $U$ (Ahlfors, ch. 4 §5.1, cited). Every contour here winds once, anticlockwise, round the points inside it.

History. Cauchy, 1831, in Turin, in the exile he chose rather than swear allegiance to the new king. The formula is the single most productive identity in analysis: the next slide takes six theorems from it.

A bounded entire function is constant. Proof: $|f'(z_0)|\le M/r$ for every $r$. $\square$

the fundamental theorem of algebra

A nonconstant polynomial $p$ has a complex root. Proof: otherwise $1/p$ is entire, and $\to0$ as $|z|\to\infty$, so bounded, so constant. $\square$ Unit 06's debt, paid.

two more

Maximum modulus: if $|f|$ has a local maximum in a connected $U$, $f$ is constant. Morera: a continuous $f$ with $\oint_{\partial T}f = 0$ for every triangle is holomorphic.

Full proofs

Derivatives. Differentiate the integral formula under the integral sign in $z$: the integrand $f(\zeta)(\zeta - z)^{-1}$ has $z$-derivatives $n!f(\zeta)(\zeta - z)^{-n-1}$, and for $z$ in a smaller disc these difference quotients converge uniformly in $\zeta$ on the circle, so the limit passes under the (one-variable) integral. Induction gives every order.

Power series. In the integral formula expand $\frac1{\zeta - z} = \sum\frac{(z-z_0)^n}{(\zeta - z_0)^{n+1}}$ as before, uniformly convergent on the circle; integrate term by term: $f(z) = \sum a_n(z - z_0)^n$ with $a_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta - z_0)^{n+1}}\mathrm d\zeta = f^{(n)}(z_0)/n!$. This works for every $r$ less than the distance from $z_0$ to the complement of $U$, which gives the radius claim. Consequently the radius of convergence of the Taylor series is at least the distance to the nearest singularity — and exactly that distance when the function cannot be continued to any larger disc (a power series is holomorphic inside its disc of convergence). P10 uses precisely this.

Maximum modulus. The integral formula at the centre, $\zeta = z_0 + re^{is}$, is the mean value property $f(z_0) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(z_0 + re^{is})\,\mathrm ds$. If $|f(z_0)|$ is a local maximum, then $|f(z_0)|\le\frac1{2\pi}\int|f(z_0 + re^{is})|\mathrm ds\le|f(z_0)|$, forcing $|f| = |f(z_0)|$ on small circles; a holomorphic function of constant modulus on a disc is constant (Cauchy–Riemann applied to $u^2 + v^2 = c$), and the identity theorem (next slide) spreads this over connected $U$.

Morera. In any disc in $U$, $F(z) = \int_{[c,z]}f$ satisfies $F' = f$ by the argument of the convex Cauchy theorem, which used only the vanishing of triangle integrals. So $F$ is holomorphic; by the first result, so is $F' = f$. $\square$ Morera is how one proves that a function defined by an integral, such as $\int_0^\infty G(t)e^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt$, is holomorphic: swap the triangle integral with the $t$-integral (Fubini) and use Goursat on $e^{i\omega t}$.

The running example's radius. $|\omega_\pm|^2 = \omega_\mathrm d^2 + \gamma^2/4 = \omega_0^2$ exactly, so the poles lie on the circle of radius $\omega_0$ for every $\gamma < 2\omega_0$. The expansion: $\chi = \omega_0^{-2}(1 - u)^{-1}$ with $u = (\omega^2 + i\gamma\omega)/\omega_0^2$, giving $\omega_0^{-2}(1 + i\gamma\omega/\omega_0^2 + \dots)$.

History. Liouville stated his theorem in lectures in 1847, for doubly periodic functions; Cauchy had published it in 1844. The proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra above is the shortest there is, and it is honest: all the analysis is in Liouville, and all of Liouville is in the integral formula.

Analytic continuation

the identity theorem

If $f, g$ are holomorphic on a connected open $U$ and agree on a set with a limit point in $U$ — a segment of the real axis, say — then $f = g$ on all of $U$.

Proof, in outline. Let $h = f - g$. The set of points where every derivative of $h$ vanishes is closed, and open (the Taylor series there is $0$ on a disc), and nonempty (at the limit point, the first nonzero Taylor coefficient would make the zeros isolated). Connectedness. $\square$

analytic continuation

A holomorphic $F$ on $U\supset V$ extending $f$ on $V$. By the identity theorem it is unique — so a formula valid on part of a domain is valid wherever both sides make sense.

a Gaussian with a complex width

For $a > 0$, $\int e^{-at^2/2}\mathrm dt = \sqrt{2\pi/a}$ (scaling). Both sides are holomorphic in $a$ on $\operatorname{Re}a > 0$ (left: Morera; right: the principal square root). They agree on the positive reals, so

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-at^2/2}\,\mathrm dt = \sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{a}},\qquad\operatorname{Re}a > 0 .$$

Section 6 uses this with $a = \sigma_0^2 + i\beta t$: a spreading wave packet.

The running example. The integral $\int_0^\infty G(t)e^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt$ converges only for $\operatorname{Im}\omega > -\gamma/2$. The rational formula for $\chi$ is its continuation to $\mathbb C\setminus\{\omega_\pm\}$: the integral fails below the line through the poles; the function does not.

Full proof of the identity theorem

Let $h = f - g$ and $A = \{z\in U : h^{(n)}(z) = 0\ \forall n\}$. Closed in $U$: an intersection of zero sets of continuous functions. Open: if $z\in A$, the Taylor series of $h$ at $z$ is identically $0$ and converges to $h$ on a disc, so $h = 0$ there and the disc is in $A$. Nonempty: let $w$ be the limit point of the set $\{h = 0\}$; $h(w) = 0$ by continuity. If some derivative at $w$ were nonzero, let $m$ be the first: $h(z) = (z - w)^mk(z)$ with $k$ holomorphic and $k(w)\ne0$, so $k\ne0$ near $w$ and $h$ has no zeros in a punctured disc — contradicting that $w$ is a limit of zeros. So $w\in A$. A connected space has no nonempty proper clopen subset, so $A = U$. $\square$

The Gaussian, carefully. $I(a) = \int e^{-at^2/2}\mathrm dt$ converges absolutely for $\operatorname{Re}a > 0$, locally uniformly, and each truncated integral $\int_{-T}^Te^{-at^2/2}\mathrm dt$ is entire in $a$ (differentiate under a finite integral). A locally uniform limit of holomorphic functions is holomorphic — Morera: triangle integrals pass to the limit. And $\sqrt{2\pi/a}$ with the principal branch ($\arg a\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$, so $\arg\sqrt{\cdot}\in(-\pi/4,\pi/4)$) is holomorphic on the right half-plane. The half-plane is connected, the positive reals have limit points in it. $\square$ Setting $a = \varepsilon - i\lambda$ and letting $\varepsilon\to0$ gives Fresnel's integral — but the limit needs an argument, and that argument is P6.

Where continuation meets physics. Past the line $\operatorname{Im}\omega = -\gamma/2$ the susceptibility is no longer a response to any drive; it is a function that knows where the system's free modes are. Unit 06 called those the poles; in the physics of resonances they are called quasinormal modes, and the ringdown frequencies of a perturbed black hole are found exactly this way: as the poles of a continued response function.

Uniqueness can fail round a hole. Continue $\log z$ from $z = 1$ along the upper half of the unit circle and along the lower half: at $z = -1$ you arrive with $i\pi$ and $-i\pi$. The domain $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ is connected, but no single-valued continuation exists on it. The identity theorem is not contradicted — there is no single holomorphic function on the whole domain to compare. Section 4 handles this with cuts.

Laurent series and isolated singularities

Laurent series

If $f$ is holomorphic on the annulus $r < |z - z_0| < R$, then on it

$$f(z) = \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}a_n(z - z_0)^n,$$

$a_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint f(\zeta)(\zeta - z_0)^{-n-1}\mathrm d\zeta$ round any circle in the annulus. The negative powers are the principal part.

An isolated singularity: $f$ holomorphic on $0 < |z - z_0| < R$. Three kinds:

  • removable — no principal part; happens iff $f$ is bounded near $z_0$ (Riemann);
  • pole of order $m$ — principal part ends at $a_{-m}\ne0$; iff $|f|\to\infty$;
  • essential — infinitely many, as $e^{1/z}$; $f$ then comes close to every value near $z_0$ (Casorati–Weierstrass).
the residue $$\operatorname*{Res}_{z_0}f = a_{-1} = \frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{|z - z_0| = \varepsilon}f(z)\,\mathrm dz .$$

It is the one Laurent coefficient that survives integration: every other power has a primitive on the punctured disc.

the running example

$\chi$ $= \dfrac{-1}{(\omega - \omega_+)(\omega - \omega_-)}$ has simple poles at $\omega_\pm = \pm\omega_\mathrm d - i\gamma/2$ and is holomorphic everywhere else, including at $\infty$ in the sense that $\chi(1/w)$ has a removable singularity at $w = 0$.

Physics: a pole is a free mode — its real part the ringing frequency $7.9975\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$, its imaginary part the decay rate $0.2\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$. Its residue will be that mode's amplitude in $G$.

Full proof of the Laurent expansion

Fix $z$ in the annulus and circles $C_1$ (radius $r_1$) and $C_2$ (radius $r_2$) with $r < r_1 < |z - z_0| < r_2 < R$. Cauchy's formula for the region between them (a toy contour, cut into convex pieces as described on the Cauchy slide) gives $f(z) = \frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{C_2}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta - z}\mathrm d\zeta - \frac1{2\pi i}\oint_{C_1}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta - z}\mathrm d\zeta$. On $C_2$ expand $\frac{1}{\zeta - z} = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(z - z_0)^n}{(\zeta - z_0)^{n+1}}$ (ratio $|z - z_0|/r_2 < 1$); on $C_1$ expand $-\frac{1}{\zeta - z} = \frac{1}{(z - z_0) - (\zeta - z_0)} = \sum_{m\ge1}\frac{(\zeta - z_0)^{m-1}}{(z - z_0)^m}$ (ratio $r_1/|z - z_0| < 1$). Both converge uniformly on their circles; integrate term by term. The coefficient formulas are independent of the circle because each integrand is holomorphic on the annulus and circles in it can be deformed into one another by the same cutting argument. $\square$

Riemann's removable singularity theorem. If $|f|\le M$ near $z_0$, then $|a_{-n}| = |\frac1{2\pi i}\oint f(\zeta)(\zeta - z_0)^{n-1}\mathrm d\zeta|\le M\varepsilon^n\to0$ for $n\ge1$, by ML on a circle of radius $\varepsilon\to0$. Poles. If the principal part stops at $a_{-m}\ne0$, then $f = (z - z_0)^{-m}h$ with $h(z_0) = a_{-m}\ne0$, so $|f|\to\infty$. Conversely if $|f|\to\infty$, $1/f$ has a removable singularity with a zero of some finite order $m$ (finite by the identity theorem), and $f$ has a pole of order $m$.

Casorati–Weierstrass. Suppose some disc $|w - c| < \delta$ is missed by $f$ near $z_0$. Then $g = 1/(f - c)$ is bounded by $1/\delta$, so it has a removable singularity; hence $f = c + 1/g$ has either a removable singularity or a pole — not essential. $\square$

The poles as modes. A pole at $\omega_+ = \omega_\mathrm d - i\gamma/2$ contributes to the inverse transform a term proportional to $e^{-i\omega_+t} = e^{-\gamma t/2}e^{-i\omega_\mathrm dt}$: a damped oscillation at the ringing frequency. That is why a physicist reads a spectrum by its poles, and why the ringdown of anything — a bell, a galvanometer, a black hole — is quoted as a complex frequency.

The residue theorem

the residue theorem

Let $\gamma$ be a circle, or a contour of the kinds on the Cauchy slide, traversed anticlockwise, and $f$ holomorphic on an open set containing $\gamma$ and its inside, except at finitely many points $z_1,\dots,z_k$ inside. Then

$$\oint_\gamma f\,\mathrm dz = 2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^k\operatorname*{Res}_{z_j}f .$$

Proof. Let $P_j$ be the principal part at $z_j$; it converges on $\mathbb C\setminus\{z_j\}$. Then $f - \sum P_j$ has only removable singularities, so its integral is $0$ by Cauchy. And $\oint P_j = 2\pi i\,a^{(j)}_{-1}$, term by term, by the circle computation. $\square$

computing residues

Simple pole, $f = g/h$ with $h(z_0) = 0\ne h'(z_0)$, $g$ holomorphic: $\operatorname{Res}f = g(z_0)/h'(z_0)$.

Pole of order $m$: $\operatorname{Res}f = \dfrac{1}{(m-1)!}\lim\limits_{z\to z_0}\dfrac{\mathrm d^{m-1}}{\mathrm dz^{m-1}}\big[(z - z_0)^mf(z)\big]$.

the running example's residues

$h(\omega) = \omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega$ has $h'(\omega) = -2\omega - i\gamma$, so $h'(\omega_\pm) = \mp2\omega_\mathrm d$ and

$$\operatorname*{Res}_{\omega_\pm}\chi(\omega)e^{-i\omega t} = \mp\frac{e^{-i\omega_\pm t}}{2\omega_\mathrm d} .$$

Dimensions: $\mathrm T^{-1}\cdot\mathrm T^{2}$ per pole = $\mathrm T$ — a residue of $\chi$ carries $[\chi][\omega]$ ✓.

Full proof of the two formulas

Simple pole. $h(z) = h'(z_0)(z - z_0) + O((z - z_0)^2)$, so $(z - z_0)f(z) = g(z)/\big(h'(z_0) + O(z - z_0)\big)\to g(z_0)/h'(z_0)$, and for a simple pole $(z - z_0)f\to a_{-1}$. Order $m$. $(z - z_0)^mf = a_{-m} + \dots + a_{-1}(z - z_0)^{m-1} + a_0(z - z_0)^m + \dots$, a power series; its $(m-1)$-th derivative at $z_0$ is $(m-1)!\,a_{-1}$.

The running example's arithmetic. $h'(\omega_+) = -2(\omega_\mathrm d - i\gamma/2) - i\gamma = -2\omega_\mathrm d$ and $h'(\omega_-) = -2(-\omega_\mathrm d - i\gamma/2) - i\gamma = 2\omega_\mathrm d$. With $g = e^{-i\omega t}$, entire in $\omega$ for each fixed $t$, the residues follow. With the numbers, $2\omega_\mathrm d = 15.9950\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

Where the proof is thin, said plainly. "Cauchy" for the function $f - \sum P_j$ on the inside of $\gamma$ requires that inside to be convex or cut into convex pieces. For a circle, a semicircle, a rectangle and a sector it is convex; for the keyhole and the indented contours it is the cutting argument. The theorem for an arbitrary closed curve, with winding numbers in place of "inside", is cited: Ahlfors, ch. 4 §5.1.

History. Cauchy introduced the residue — résidu, what is left over — in 1826, as the tool that unified dozens of definite integrals Euler, Laplace and Legendre had evaluated one trick at a time. The theorem in this form is in his 1831 Turin memoir.

Closing the contour: Jordan's lemma

Jordan's lemma

Let $C_R$ be the upper semicircle $z = Re^{i\theta}$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$, and $M_R = \max_{C_R}|g|\to0$ as $R\to\infty$. Then for every $a > 0$

$$\Big|\int_{C_R}g(z)e^{iaz}\,\mathrm dz\Big|\le\frac{\pi M_R}{a}\to0 .$$
proof

$|e^{iaz}| = e^{-aR\sin\theta}$ on $C_R$, so the integral is at most $M_RR\int_0^\pi e^{-aR\sin\theta}\mathrm d\theta = 2M_RR\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-aR\sin\theta}\mathrm d\theta$. Concavity gives Jordan's inequality $\sin\theta\ge2\theta/\pi$ on $[0,\pi/2]$, so this is at most $2M_RR\int_0^\infty e^{-2aR\theta/\pi}\mathrm d\theta = \pi M_R/a$. $\square$

why Jordan and not just ML

ML gives $\pi RM_R$: useless when $g\sim1/R$ — $\sin x/x$, Kramers–Kronig, any transform of a function with a jump. The exponential's decay over most of the arc is what saves you.

$|e^{-i\omega t}| = e^{t\operatorname{Im}\omega}$: it decays in the upper half-plane when $t < 0$, in the lower when $t > 0$.

For $e^{-i\omega t}$ the lemma applies with $a = -t$ above (if $t < 0$) or, reflected, with $a = t$ below (if $t > 0$). The sign of $t$ chooses the half-plane. For $\chi$, $M_R\sim1/R^2$.

Jordan's inequality. $\sin$ is concave on $[0,\pi/2]$, so its graph lies above the chord from $(0,0)$ to $(\pi/2, 1)$, which is $2\theta/\pi$. The symmetry step uses $\sin(\pi - \theta) = \sin\theta$.

The lower half-plane version. For the lower semicircle $z = Re^{-i\theta}$ and $a > 0$, $|e^{-iaz}| = e^{-aR\sin\theta}$, and the same bound holds for $\int g(z)e^{-iaz}\mathrm dz$. The lower semicircle traversed from $+R$ to $-R$ through $-iR$ closes the real axis clockwise, which costs a minus sign in the residue theorem: that minus sign is where most errors in this subject are made.

For the running example the lemma is more than needed: $|\chi(\omega)|\le1/(R^2 - \omega_0^2 - \gamma R)$ on $|\omega| = R$ once $R$ is large, so even ML gives $\pi R\cdot O(R^{-2})\to0$. Jordan's lemma is needed in section 5's theorem, where $\chi$ is only assumed to tend to zero, and for the Dirichlet integral on the next slide.

History. Camille Jordan's Cours d'analyse (second edition, 1894). The same Jordan as the Jordan normal form of unit 07 and the Jordan curve theorem.

worked example 2 — $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}\,\mathrm dx$

Step 1 — the function. Not $\sin z/z$: $\sin z$ grows like $e^{|\operatorname{Im}z|}$ in both half-planes. Take $f(z) = e^{iz}/z$: imaginary part $\sin x/x$ on the axis, decaying upwards.

Step 2 — the contour. $[-R,-\varepsilon]$, the small upper semicircle $c_\varepsilon$ clockwise round the pole at $0$, $[\varepsilon,R]$, the big semicircle $C_R$. Nothing inside, so the total is $0$.

Step 3 — the straight parts. Pair $x$ with $-x$: $\int_{\varepsilon<|x|<R}\frac{e^{ix}}{x}\mathrm dx = \int_\varepsilon^R\frac{e^{ix} - e^{-ix}}{x}\mathrm dx = 2i\int_\varepsilon^R\frac{\sin x}{x}\mathrm dx$.

Step 4 — the big arc. Jordan with $g = 1/z$, $M_R = 1/R$, $a = 1$: at most $\pi/R\to0$.

Checks. $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin ax}{x}\mathrm dx = \frac\pi2\operatorname{sgn}a$, independent of $|a|$, as $u = ax$ demands; with $x$ a time, a pure number ✓.

the indentation lemma

If $f$ has a simple pole at $x_0$ and $c_\varepsilon$ is an anticlockwise arc of angle $\alpha$ of $|z - x_0| = \varepsilon$, then $\int_{c_\varepsilon}f\to i\alpha\operatorname*{Res}_{x_0}f$. A half-circle gives half a residue.

Step 3 read differently: $\mathrm{PV}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ix}}{x}\mathrm dx = i\pi$. A pole on the contour contributes $\pm i\pi\operatorname{Res}$, the sign set by which way you step round it — Sokhotski–Plemelj, seen from the complex plane.

Convergence of the real integral. $\int_0^\infty\sin x/x$ converges as an improper integral (alternating series of decreasing areas) but not absolutely (the inversion slide's box). That is why $g\sim1/R$ and why Jordan is needed; ML would have given $\pi R\cdot(1/R) = \pi$, not $0$.

Proof of the indentation lemma. $f(z) = \frac{c}{z - x_0} + h(z)$ with $c$ the residue and $h$ holomorphic near $x_0$, hence bounded. On $z = x_0 + \varepsilon e^{i\theta}$, $\int\frac{c\,\mathrm dz}{z - x_0} = c\int i\,\mathrm d\theta = i\alpha c$ exactly, and $|\int h|\le\alpha\varepsilon\max|h|\to0$. $\square$ For a pole of order two or more the arc integral generally diverges as $\varepsilon\to0$, and there is no principal value: the lemma is really about simple poles.

Numerical check. The partial integrals $\int_0^X\sin x/x\,\mathrm dx$ oscillate about $\pi/2$ with amplitude about $1/X$; at $X = 1000$ they are within $10^{-3}$. The integral carries Dirichlet's name because his 1829 proof that Fourier series converge turns on it; its value was known before him.

What changes if the indentation goes below the pole. Then the pole is inside, the residue theorem gives $2\pi i\cdot1$ on the right, and the small arc (now anticlockwise under the pole) gives $+\pi i$: $2i\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x} + \pi i = 2\pi i$ — the same answer. Branch points and cuts

$\oint\mathrm dz/z = 2\pi i$ means no continuous logarithm exists on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$: each turn round $0$ adds $2\pi i$. Remove a ray and one does:

branch cut

On $\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0]$, the principal logarithm $\operatorname{Log}z = \ln|z| + i\operatorname{Arg}z$, $\operatorname{Arg}\in(-\pi,\pi)$, is holomorphic with derivative $1/z$. Then $z^a := e^{a\log z}$ for any $a\in\mathbb C$.

$0$ is a branch point: continuing $z^a$ once round it multiplies it by $e^{2\pi ia}$. The cut is a choice; the branch point is not.

the rule that prevents every error here

Choose the cut and the branch before writing an integral, and state the value of $\arg z$ on each edge of the cut.

The keyhole: cut along $[0,\infty)$, $\arg\in(0,2\pi)$. The two edges of the cut are the same points with different values of $z^a$.

What it buys. For $\int_0^\infty x^{a-1}R(x)\,\mathrm dx$ the two edges give $(1 - e^{2\pi ia})\int_0^\infty$; the circles vanish; the residue theorem finishes. P8: $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{a-1}}{1 + x}\mathrm dx = \frac{\pi}{\sin\pi a}$ for $0 < a < 1$.

Why $\operatorname{Log}$ is holomorphic. On the slit plane, $\operatorname{Arg}z$ is continuous (it is $2\arctan\big(y/(x + |z|)\big)$, continuous where $x + |z|\ne0$, i.e. off $(-\infty,0]$), and $e^{\operatorname{Log}z} = z$. A continuous inverse of a holomorphic function with nonzero derivative is holomorphic, with derivative the reciprocal: $\frac{\operatorname{Log}(z+h) - \operatorname{Log}z}{h} = \frac{w' - w}{e^{w'} - e^w}\to\frac1{e^w} = \frac1z$ where $w = \operatorname{Log}z$, $w' = \operatorname{Log}(z+h)\to w$ by continuity. Alternatively check Cauchy–Riemann on $u = \frac12\ln(x^2 + y^2)$, $v = \operatorname{Arg}$.

The keyhole in detail (this is P8's first rung). On the upper edge $z = x$ with $\arg z = 0$, $z^{a-1} = x^{a-1}$; on the lower edge, traversed from $R$ back to $\varepsilon$, $\arg z = 2\pi$ and $z^{a-1} = x^{a-1}e^{2\pi i(a-1)} = x^{a-1}e^{2\pi ia}$. So the two edges give $(1 - e^{2\pi ia})\int_\varepsilon^Rx^{a-1}R(x)\,\mathrm dx$. The large circle is $O(R^{a-1}\cdot R^{-1}\cdot R) = O(R^{a-1})\to0$ for $a < 1$ and $R(x)\sim1/x$; the small circle is $O(\varepsilon^a)\to0$ for $a > 0$. Those two conditions are exactly the convergence conditions of the real integral.

Where branch points meet physics. A branch point in a response function signals a continuum of modes rather than a discrete set — a string on an infinite line, a diffusing heat pulse, a radiating antenna. The transform of the diffusion kernel involves $\sqrt{-i\omega}$, and the wave equation's Green's function in even space dimensions has a cut, which is why a pulse in two dimensions leaves a wake while in three it does not (unit 24). The singularity in P10 — where Kepler's equation stops defining $E(e)$ — is a square-root branch point.

Counting zeros: the argument principle and Rouché

the argument principle

If $f$ is holomorphic inside and on a circle (or toy contour) $\gamma$ except for poles, with no zeros or poles on $\gamma$, then

$$\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,\mathrm dz = Z - P,$$

zeros minus poles inside, with multiplicity: the number of times $f(\gamma)$ winds round $0$.

Proof. Near a zero of order $m$, $f = (z - z_0)^mk$ with $k(z_0)\ne0$, so $f'/f = \frac{m}{z - z_0} + \frac{k'}{k}$: residue $m$. At a pole of order $m$, residue $-m$. Residue theorem. $\square$

Rouché's theorem

If $f, g$ are holomorphic inside and on $\gamma$ and $|g| < |f|$ on $\gamma$, then $f$ and $f + g$ have the same number of zeros inside $\gamma$.

Proof. For $s\in[0,1]$, $|f + sg|\ge|f| - |g| > 0$ on $\gamma$. So $N(s) = \frac1{2\pi i}\oint\frac{(f + sg)'}{f + sg}$ is defined, continuous in $s$, and an integer: constant. $N(0) = N(1)$. $\square$

where the roots of $z^5 + 3z + 1$ are

On $|z| = 2$: $|3z + 1|\le7 < 32 = |z^5|$, so all five roots have $|z| < 2$. On $|z| = 1$: $|z^5 + 1|\le2 < 3 = |3z|$, so exactly one has $|z| < 1$. (Computed: $-0.33199$, and four of modulus $1.2629$ and $1.3743$ ✓.)

P10 uses Rouché to show Kepler's equation has exactly one root in a disc for every $|e| < 0.6627$ — and that is the Laplace limit.

Why $N(s)$ is continuous. The integrand $(f' + sg')/(f + sg)$ is jointly continuous in $(s, z)$ on the compact $[0,1]\times\gamma$, since the denominator is bounded away from $0$ there; so it is uniformly continuous and the integral depends continuously on $s$. A continuous integer-valued function on the connected $[0,1]$ is constant.

Why "winding number". $\frac{f'}{f}\mathrm dz = \mathrm d\log f$ locally; integrating round $\gamma$ adds up the increase of $\log|f|$ (zero, since the curve closes) and $i$ times the increase of $\arg f$. So the left side is $\frac{1}{2\pi}\times$(total change of $\arg f$ along $\gamma$).

Engineering. Nyquist's stability criterion (1932) is the argument principle applied to $1 + K(s)$ along the boundary of the right half-plane: it counts the unstable poles of a feedback loop from a plot of its measured frequency response, without solving for the poles. For the galvanometer the count is immediate — $\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega$ has both zeros in the lower half-plane — but for a loop with delay there is no formula and the count is the only tool.

The roots. Numerically $z^5 + 3z + 1 = 0$ has roots $-0.33199$ and $1.00507\pm0.93726i$, $-0.83907\pm0.94385i$, of moduli $0.33199$, $1.37427$, $1.26289$: one inside the unit circle, all inside radius $2$ ✓.

The step function, explained

The example used two facts only: $\chi\to0$ at infinity, and no poles in the upper half-plane. Any response with those two properties vanishes for $t < 0$, by Step 3 verbatim. And for $t > 0$

$$G(t) = -i\sum_{\text{poles } \omega_k}\operatorname*{Res}_{\omega_k}\chi(\omega)e^{-i\omega t},$$

a sum of damped modes, one per pole — when there are finitely many poles and $\chi$ decays.

the $i0$ prescription

With no damping, $\chi = 1/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)$ has poles on the axis and the inversion integral is undefined. Causality decides: replace $\omega$ by $\omega + i0$ — poles pushed below, contour passing above — and

$$G_\mathrm{ret}(t) = H(t)\,\frac{\sin\omega_0t}{\omega_0} .$$
$LG = \delta$ does not know about time's arrow

Pass below both poles and closing upwards for $t < 0$ collects them: $G_\mathrm{adv}(t) = -H(-t)\sin(\omega_0t)/\omega_0$, which also satisfies $LG = \delta$ (its $\dot G$ jumps from $-1$ to $0$). Principal values at both poles give $\frac12(G_\mathrm{ret} + G_\mathrm{adv})$. The equation admits all of them; the choice of half-plane is the physics.

In the damped case no choice is left: $\gamma > 0$ has already moved the poles down by $\gamma/2$, and the real-axis integral is the retarded one. Dissipation and causality point the same way.

Forward: unit 24 inverts the wave operator's $1/(\omega^2/c^2 - k^2)$ with exactly this prescription, and it is how light is emitted after, not before, the charge moves.

The mode sum, justified. For $t > 0$ close downwards: if $\chi$ is rational with numerator degree at most denominator degree minus one, $|\chi|\le C/R$ on large arcs, Jordan's lemma (lower version, $a = t$) kills the arc, and the residue theorem with its clockwise sign gives $2\pi G(t) = -2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}$. For $\chi$ with infinitely many poles — a string, a cavity — the same formula holds when the arcs can be chosen to pass between poles with $\chi$ still small on them, and that is a property to be checked, not assumed.

The retarded and advanced computations. With poles at $\pm\omega_0 - i0$: closing up for $t < 0$ encloses nothing, $G = 0$; closing down for $t > 0$ gives, from the running example with $\gamma\to0$, $\sin(\omega_0t)/\omega_0$. With poles at $\pm\omega_0 + i0$: closing down for $t > 0$ encloses nothing; closing up for $t < 0$, anticlockwise, gives $G = \frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot2\pi i\big(\frac{-e^{-i\omega_0t}}{2\omega_0} + \frac{e^{i\omega_0t}}{2\omega_0}\big) = i\cdot\frac{2i\sin\omega_0t}{2\omega_0} = -\frac{\sin\omega_0t}{\omega_0}$. Both satisfy the three conditions of the jump theorem, the advanced one with the roles of $0^+$ and $0^-$ exchanged: $G_\mathrm{adv}(0^-) = 0$, $\dot G_\mathrm{adv}(0^-) = -1$, $\dot G_\mathrm{adv}(0^+) = 0$, jump $+1$ ✓. Their difference is $\sin(\omega_0t)/\omega_0$ for all $t$, a free oscillation — an element of the two-dimensional kernel of $L$ (unit 06), as it must be.

Dimensions and limits. $[G_\mathrm{ret}] = \mathrm T$ ✓. $\omega_0\to0$: $G_\mathrm{ret}\to tH(t)$, the response of a free mass to a unit kick — velocity $1$ from $t = 0$ on ✓.

History. The prescription was used by Lorentz and by the founders of quantum field theory without a name; Feynman made the $i\varepsilon$ explicit in 1949, where choosing it differently at positive and negative frequency gives his propagator. The idea that dispersion relations follow from causality alone is due to Kronig (1926) and Kramers (1927), and was made a theorem by Titchmarsh's work on Fourier integrals (1937) — the next slide.

Causality, analyticity, Kramers–Kronig: one theorem

the causality theorem

Let $G$ be continuous except possibly at $0$, with $G$, $tG$ and $\chi = \hat G$ absolutely integrable. The following are equivalent:

(a) causality: $G(t) = 0$ for all $t < 0$;

(b) analyticity: $\chi$ extends continuously to $\operatorname{Im}\omega\ge0$, holomorphic on $\operatorname{Im}\omega > 0$, with $\max_{|\omega| = R,\,\operatorname{Im}\omega\ge0}|\chi|\to0$;

(c) dispersion: for every real $\omega$,

$$\chi(\omega) = \frac{1}{i\pi}\,\mathrm{PV}\!\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(\omega')}{\omega' - \omega}\,\mathrm d\omega' .$$

The sharp form, for square-integrable $\chi$, is Titchmarsh's theorem (cited: Titchmarsh, Introduction to the Theory of Fourier Integrals, 1937, Thm 95).

(a) ⇒ (b)

$\chi(\omega) = \int_0^\infty G(t)e^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt$ and $|e^{i\omega t}| = e^{-t\operatorname{Im}\omega}\le1$ for $t\ge0$: absolutely convergent on the closed upper half-plane, continuous there, holomorphic inside (Morera). Decay: for $G$ the indicator of $[a,b]\subset[0,\infty)$, $|\chi|\le2/|\omega|$; approximate by steps. $\square$

(b) ⇒ (a)

For $t < 0$ close upwards: no singularity inside, and Jordan's lemma with $a = -t > 0$ kills the arc. So $\int_{-R}^R\chi e^{-i\omega t}\mathrm d\omega = -\int_\text{arc}\to0$, and by inversion $G(t) = 0$. $\square$

(a) ⇔ (c) is the next slide. On the running example: $G$, $tG$, $\chi$ are integrable; (a) is unit 06's construction, (b) the position of two poles, and (c) is checked with numbers two slides on.

Full proof of (a) ⇒ (b)

Continuity on the closed half-plane: for $\omega_n\to\omega$ with $\operatorname{Im}\omega_n\ge0$, the integrands $G(t)e^{i\omega_nt}$ are dominated by $|G|$ and converge pointwise: dominated convergence. Holomorphy inside: for a triangle $T$ in the open upper half-plane, $\oint_{\partial T}\chi = \int_0^\infty G(t)\big(\oint_{\partial T}e^{i\omega t}\mathrm d\omega\big)\mathrm dt = 0$ (Fubini, the double integral converging absolutely; Goursat for the entire function $e^{i\omega t}$); Morera. Decay: for $G = \mathbf 1_{[a,b]}$, $\chi = (e^{i\omega b} - e^{i\omega a})/i\omega$ and both exponentials have modulus $\le1$ when $\operatorname{Im}\omega\ge0$. Given $\varepsilon$, a step function $s$ supported in $[0,\infty)$ has $\int|G - s| < \varepsilon$, so $|\chi(\omega)|\le|\hat s(\omega)| + \varepsilon$ uniformly on the closed half-plane, and $\hat s = O(1/|\omega|)$ there. $\square$

Full proof of (b) ⇒ (a)

$\chi$ is only assumed continuous up to the real axis, so apply Cauchy to the half-disc raised by $\eta > 0$ (where $\chi$ is holomorphic on a neighbourhood) and let $\eta\to0$: on the compact closed half-disc $\chi$ is uniformly continuous, so the integrals converge. Jordan's lemma, with $g = \chi$ and $M_R\to0$ by hypothesis, disposes of the arc. Since $\chi$ is absolutely integrable, $\lim_R\int_{-R}^R$ is the inversion integral, which equals $2\pi G(t)$ at every $t\ne0$ (inversion theorem, whose proof is pointwise at continuity points). $\square$

Why the hypotheses. $G$ integrable makes $\chi$ exist; $tG$ integrable makes $\chi$ of class $C^1$, which the principal value in (c) needs; $\chi$ integrable makes the inversion integral converge. A response with a delta function at $t = 0$ (instantaneous response, $\chi\to$ const at infinity) is outside the hypotheses — apply the theorem to $\chi - \chi(\infty)$ instead, which is what optics does with $\varepsilon(\omega) - 1$.

What (b) says physically. A causal system responds finitely to every drive that was smaller in the past than it is now; the upper half-plane is the set of such drives, and holomorphy there is the statement that the response depends smoothly — in fact analytically — on the drive's growth rate.

The dispersion relations, proved

(a) ⇔ (c), in three lines

1. Causality says $G(t) = \operatorname{sgn}(t)\,G(t)$ for $t\ne0$. Transforms are injective, so (a) $\iff\chi = \widehat{\operatorname{sgn}\cdot G}$.

2. The transform of a product is $\frac1{2\pi}$ times the convolution of transforms, and $\widehat{\operatorname{sgn}} = 2i\,\mathrm{PV}\frac1\omega$:

$$\widehat{\operatorname{sgn}\cdot G}(\omega) = \frac{i}{\pi}\,\mathrm{PV}\!\!\int\frac{\chi(\omega')}{\omega - \omega'}\,\mathrm d\omega' .$$

3. That is the right side of (c). $\square$

Real and imaginary parts, $\chi = \chi' + i\chi''$:

$$\chi'(\omega) = \frac1\pi\mathrm{PV}\!\!\int\frac{\chi''(\omega')\,\mathrm d\omega'}{\omega' - \omega},$$ $$\chi''(\omega) = -\frac1\pi\mathrm{PV}\!\!\int\frac{\chi'(\omega')\,\mathrm d\omega'}{\omega' - \omega}.$$
the Kramers–Kronig relations, for real $G$

A real $G$ gives $\chi(-\omega) = \overline{\chi(\omega)}$: $\chi'$ even, $\chi''$ odd. Folding the integrals onto $\omega' > 0$:

$$\chi'(\omega) = \frac2\pi\,\mathrm{PV}\!\!\int_0^\infty\frac{\omega'\chi''(\omega')}{\omega'^2 - \omega^2}\,\mathrm d\omega',$$ $$\chi''(\omega) = -\frac{2\omega}{\pi}\,\mathrm{PV}\!\!\int_0^\infty\frac{\chi'(\omega')}{\omega'^2 - \omega^2}\,\mathrm d\omega' .$$

Read it: the in-phase (reactive, dispersive) response at one frequency is an integral of the out-of-phase (absorptive) response at all frequencies. Neither can be designed without the other.

Dimensions: $[\chi''][\omega]/[\omega] = [\chi]$ ✓. Limit: $\chi''\equiv0$ forces $\chi'\equiv0$ — the next slide but one.

Full proof of step 2

Put $s_\varepsilon(t) = \operatorname{sgn}(t)e^{-\varepsilon|t|}$, bounded and integrable, with $\hat s_\varepsilon(\nu) = \frac{2i\nu}{\nu^2 + \varepsilon^2}$ (tempered-distribution slide). For the product, write $G$ by inversion and swap (Fubini; $|\chi(\omega')||s_\varepsilon(t)|$ is integrable): $\widehat{s_\varepsilon G}(\omega) = \int s_\varepsilon(t)\frac1{2\pi}\int\chi(\omega')e^{-i\omega't}\mathrm d\omega'\,e^{i\omega t}\mathrm dt = \frac1{2\pi}\int\chi(\omega')\hat s_\varepsilon(\omega - \omega')\,\mathrm d\omega' = \frac{i}{\pi}\int\chi(\omega')\frac{\omega - \omega'}{(\omega - \omega')^2 + \varepsilon^2}\,\mathrm d\omega'$. As $\varepsilon\to0$ the left side tends to $\widehat{\operatorname{sgn}\cdot G}(\omega)$ by dominated convergence ($|s_\varepsilon G|\le|G|$). The right side tends to the principal value by the argument of the Sokhotski proof, which used only that the function paired with the kernel is $C^1$ near $\omega$ and integrable — true of $\chi$, because $tG$ is integrable. $\square$

Step 1, both directions. If (a), then $\operatorname{sgn}\cdot G$ and $G$ agree except at $t = 0$, so their transforms agree. If $\chi = \widehat{\operatorname{sgn}\cdot G}$, then $h = G - \operatorname{sgn}\cdot G = 2G\,\mathbf 1_{t<0}$ has $\hat h = 0$, so by inversion $h(t) = 0$ at each $t\ne0$: (a). $\square$

The real and imaginary parts. Multiply (c) by $i\pi$: $i\pi(\chi' + i\chi'') = \mathrm{PV}\int\frac{\chi' + i\chi''}{\omega' - \omega}$. Real parts: $-\pi\chi'' = \mathrm{PV}\int\frac{\chi'}{\omega' - \omega}$. Imaginary parts: $\pi\chi' = \mathrm{PV}\int\frac{\chi''}{\omega' - \omega}$. The folding: split at $0$, send $\omega'\to-\omega'$ on the negative half, use parity, and combine $\frac{1}{\omega' - \omega} + \frac{1}{\omega' + \omega} = \frac{2\omega'}{\omega'^2 - \omega^2}$ (for the odd $\chi''$) or $\frac{1}{\omega' - \omega} - \frac{1}{\omega' + \omega} = \frac{2\omega}{\omega'^2 - \omega^2}$ (for the even $\chi'$, with an overall sign from the odd reflection).

An operator reading. The map $\chi\mapsto\frac{1}{\pi}\mathrm{PV}\int\frac{\chi(\omega')}{\omega - \omega'}\mathrm d\omega'$ is the Hilbert transform $\mathcal H$, and step 2 says $\mathcal H\hat f = -i\,\widehat{\operatorname{sgn}f}$. Causality is then the eigenvalue statement $\mathcal H\chi = -i\chi$, and the real-imaginary form says $\chi'$ and $\chi''$ are a Hilbert-transform pair. Since $\operatorname{sgn}^2 = 1$, $\mathcal H^2 = -1$.

History. Kronig, 1926, on the dispersion of X-rays; Kramers, 1927, at the Como conference, for light in general. Both argued physically. The mathematical content — boundary values of functions holomorphic in a half-plane are Hilbert-transform pairs — was Plemelj's (1908) and Titchmarsh's.

Step 1 — the two parts. With $D(\omega) = (\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2$,

$\chi''>0$ for $\omega>0$: the oscillator absorbs.

Step 2 — at $\omega = 0$, exactly. The first relation gives

and P5's integral makes this $\frac{2\gamma}{\pi}\cdot\frac{\pi}{2\gamma\omega_0^2} = \frac1{\omega_0^2}$. That is $\chi(0) = 1/64 = 0.015625\ \mathrm{s^2}$: the static stiffness ✓.

The Laplace transform and the Bromwich contour

the Laplace transform $$F(s) = \mathcal L[f](s) = \int_0^\infty f(t)\,e^{-st}\,\mathrm dt,$$

for $f$ on $[0,\infty)$ with $|f|\le Ce^{\sigma t}$; it converges and is holomorphic on $\operatorname{Re}s > \sigma$. It is the Fourier transform of $f(t)e^{-ct}H(t)$ at $\omega$, with $s = c - i\omega$.

the Bromwich integral

For $c > \sigma$, at points where $f$ is continuous and when $F(c - i\omega)$ is integrable in $\omega$,

$$f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c - i\infty}^{c + i\infty}F(s)\,e^{st}\,\mathrm ds .$$

Proof: Fourier inversion for $f(t)e^{-ct}H(t)$, then $s = c - i\omega$, $\mathrm ds = -i\,\mathrm d\omega$. $\square$

Why bother: it takes growing functions and initial data in its stride, $\mathcal L[\dot f] = sF - f(0)$, so it solves an initial value problem in one pass.

the running example, rotated

$\mathcal L[G](s) = \dfrac{1}{s^2 + \gamma s + \omega_0^2}$, poles $s = -\gamma/2\pm i\omega_\mathrm d$ — in the left half-plane — and $\chi(\omega) = \mathcal L[G](-i\omega)$: the same picture turned a quarter-turn.

The Bromwich line runs to the right of every singularity. For $t > 0$, $e^{st}$ decays to the left: close left, collect the poles. For $t < 0$ close right: nothing there, $f = 0$. Causality, once more.

P9 uses it for the galvanometer's response to a switched-on current: the needle overshoots to $1.9244$ times its final deflection.

The substitution in full. Fourier inversion for $g(t) = f(t)e^{-ct}H(t)$: $g(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int\hat g(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}\mathrm d\omega$ with $\hat g(\omega) = \int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-(c - i\omega)t}\mathrm dt = F(c - i\omega)$. Multiply by $e^{ct}$: $f(t) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int F(c - i\omega)e^{(c - i\omega)t}\mathrm d\omega$. With $s = c - i\omega$, as $\omega$ runs from $-\infty$ to $\infty$, $s$ runs from $c + i\infty$ down to $c - i\infty$, and $\mathrm d\omega = i\,\mathrm ds$; reversing the direction absorbs a sign, giving $\frac{1}{2\pi}\cdot(-i)\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty} = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}$ ✓. The result is independent of $c > \sigma$: two Bromwich lines bound a strip with no singularity, and the horizontal ends vanish when $F\to0$ there.

The derivative rule. $\int_0^\infty\dot fe^{-st}\mathrm dt = [fe^{-st}]_0^\infty + s\int_0^\infty fe^{-st} = sF - f(0)$ for $\operatorname{Re}s > \sigma$. So $\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2x = u$ with data $x(0), \dot x(0)$ becomes $(s^2 + \gamma s + \omega_0^2)X = U + (s + \gamma)x(0) + \dot x(0)$ — the initial conditions enter as a polynomial on the right, which the transient then comes from.

The step response (P9). A current switched on at $t = 0$ gives $u = u_0H(t)$, $U = u_0/s$, so $X = u_0/[s(s^2 + \gamma s + \omega_0^2)]$: three poles, at $0$ and at $-\gamma/2\pm i\omega_\mathrm d$. Residues give $x(t) = \frac{u_0}{\omega_0^2}\big[1 - e^{-\gamma t/2}\big(\cos\omega_\mathrm dt + \frac{\gamma}{2\omega_\mathrm d}\sin\omega_\mathrm dt\big)\big]$, first maximum at $t = \pi/\omega_\mathrm d = 0.39282$ s, value $1 + e^{-\pi\gamma/2\omega_\mathrm d} = 1.92444$ times the final deflection. A $Q = 20$ galvanometer nearly doubles on the first swing, which is why unit 06 recommended damping it.

History. Laplace used the transform in probability (1812). Watson's lemma

Notation (unit 21 develops it): $f = O(g)$ if $|f|\le C|g|$ eventually; $f\sim g$ if $f/g\to1$; and $f(\lambda)\sim\sum a_n\varphi_n(\lambda)$, an asymptotic expansion, if for every $N$ the error after $N$ terms is $O(\varphi_N)$ — convergent or not.

The Gamma function: $\Gamma(\alpha) = \int_0^\infty t^{\alpha-1}e^{-t}\mathrm dt$ for $\alpha>0$; $\Gamma(n+1) = n!$ by parts, $\Gamma(\frac12) = \sqrt\pi$ by $t = u^2$.

Watson's lemma

If $|g(t)|\le Ce^{bt}$ and $g(t)\sim\sum_{n\ge0}a_nt^{\alpha_n}$ as $t\to0^+$, with $-1 < \alpha_0 < \alpha_1 < \dots\to\infty$, then as $\lambda\to+\infty$

$$\int_0^\infty g(t)e^{-\lambda t}\,\mathrm dt\sim\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{a_n\,\Gamma(\alpha_n+1)}{\lambda^{\alpha_n+1}} .$$

Only $t\lesssim1/\lambda$ matters: expand $g$ there, integrate term by term to $\infty$.

the running example: what a coil does at high frequency

From the ODE, $G$ $= t - \frac\gamma2t^2 + \frac{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}{6}t^3 + \dots$ near $0^+$ ($G(0) = 0$, $\dot G = 1$, $\ddot G = -\gamma$). Watson:

$$\mathcal L[G](s)\sim\frac1{s^2} - \frac{\gamma}{s^3} + \frac{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}{s^4} .$$

Directly, $1/(s^2 + \gamma s + \omega_0^2)$ expands in powers of $1/s$ to the same thing ✓ — and that Laurent series converges for $|s| > \omega_0$, so the expansion holds on the imaginary axis too. With $s = -i\omega$:

$$\chi(\omega) = -\frac{1}{\omega^2} + \frac{i\gamma}{\omega^3} + O(\omega^{-4}) .$$

A free mass, $\ddot x = f$, is $-1/\omega^2$: at high frequency every oscillator is pure inertia, and the coefficient is the jump $\dot G(0^+) = 1$ — the sum rule's constant.

Full proof of Watson's lemma

Fix $N$ and $\delta > 0$ small enough that $|g(t) - \sum_{n<N}a_nt^{\alpha_n}|\le Kt^{\alpha_N}$ on $(0,\delta]$. Split $\int_0^\infty = \int_0^\delta + \int_\delta^\infty$. Tail: $|\int_\delta^\infty ge^{-\lambda t}|\le C\int_\delta^\infty e^{-(\lambda - b)t} = Ce^{-(\lambda-b)\delta}/(\lambda - b)$, exponentially small, hence $O(\lambda^{-M})$ for every $M$. Head: $\int_0^\delta a_nt^{\alpha_n}e^{-\lambda t}\mathrm dt = a_n\Gamma(\alpha_n+1)\lambda^{-\alpha_n-1} - a_n\int_\delta^\infty t^{\alpha_n}e^{-\lambda t}$, the last term again exponentially small; and the remainder is at most $K\int_0^\infty t^{\alpha_N}e^{-\lambda t}\mathrm dt = K\Gamma(\alpha_N+1)\lambda^{-\alpha_N-1}$. So the error after $N$ terms is $O(\lambda^{-\alpha_N-1})$, which is the definition. $\square$ The substitution $u = \lambda t$ gives $\int_0^\infty t^\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\mathrm dt = \Gamma(\alpha+1)\lambda^{-\alpha-1}$.

The Taylor coefficients of $G$. $G(0^+) = 0$ and $\dot G(0^+) = 1$ are the jump conditions; the ODE gives $\ddot G = -\gamma\dot G - \omega_0^2G = -\gamma$ and $\dddot G = -\gamma\ddot G - \omega_0^2\dot G = \gamma^2 - \omega_0^2$ at $0^+$. Watson with $\alpha_n = n+1$: $1\cdot1!/s^2 - \frac\gamma2\cdot2!/s^3 + \frac{\gamma^2 - \omega_0^2}{6}\cdot3!/s^4$. Directly, $\frac{1}{s^2}\big(1 + \frac\gamma s + \frac{\omega_0^2}{s^2}\big)^{-1} = \frac1{s^2}\big(1 - \frac\gamma s - \frac{\omega_0^2}{s^2} + \frac{\gamma^2}{s^2} + O(s^{-3})\big)$ ✓.

Why the imaginary axis needs a word. Watson's lemma as proved is for real $\lambda\to\infty$, and $s = -i\omega$ is not real. Here no extension of the lemma is needed: $\mathcal L[G]$ is rational, holomorphic for $|s| > \omega_0$ including at $\infty$, so its Laurent series about $\infty$ converges there, and by the identity theorem it is the series Watson produced. For a general $G$ with a jump in $\dot G$, integration by parts twice gives $\chi = -\dot G(0^+)/\omega^2 + o(\omega^{-2})$ directly — the same statement.

Dimensions. $[\chi] = \mathrm T^2$ and $[1/\omega^2] = \mathrm T^2$ ✓; $[\gamma/\omega^3] = \mathrm T^2$ ✓. Limit. $\omega_0\to0$ and $\gamma\to0$ leave $\chi = -1/\omega^2$ exactly: the free mass.

History. G. N. Watson, 1918, in a paper on the harmonic functions of the parabolic cylinder. The lemma is the reason most asymptotic expansions of integrals are easy once the integral has been put in Laplace form; the next three slides are ways of putting it there.

Laplace's method

Laplace's method

Let $h$ be $C^2$ on $[a,b]$ with a unique maximum at an interior $t_0$, $h''(t_0) < 0$, and $g$ continuous with $g(t_0)\ne0$. Then as $\lambda\to\infty$

$$\int_a^bg(t)e^{\lambda h(t)}\,\mathrm dt\sim g(t_0)\,e^{\lambda h(t_0)}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\lambda|h''(t_0)|}} .$$

Why. $e^{\lambda h}$ is a spike of height $e^{\lambda h(t_0)}$ and width $\sim(\lambda|h''|)^{-1/2}$; inside the spike $h\approx h(t_0) - \frac12|h''|(t - t_0)^2$ and $g\approx g(t_0)$, and the Gaussian integral does the rest. Everything outside is exponentially smaller. Full proof in the notes.

Dimensions: with $t$ a time, $\lambda h$ dimensionless, $\sqrt{1/\lambda|h''|}$ is a time, and both sides carry $[g]\,\mathrm T$ ✓.

Stirling's formula

$n! = \Gamma(n+1) = \int_0^\infty t^ne^{-t}\mathrm dt$. Put $t = nu$:

$$n! = n^{n+1}\int_0^\infty e^{n(\ln u - u)}\,\mathrm du .$$

$h(u) = \ln u - u$: maximum at $u = 1$, $h(1) = -1$, $h''(1) = -1$. So

$$n!\sim n^{n+1}e^{-n}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{n}} = \sqrt{2\pi n}\,\Big(\frac ne\Big)^n .$$

Check at $n = 10$: $3\,598\,695.6$ against $10! = 3\,628\,800$, low by $0.83\%$. The next term multiplies by $1 + \frac1{12n}$ and gives $3\,628\,684.7$, low by $3\times10^{-5}$.

Real integrand, real maximum. The next two slides are what to do when the exponent is imaginary, or when there is no real maximum at all.

Full proof of Laplace's method

Write $h_0 = h(t_0)$, $k = |h''(t_0)|$. Given $\eta\in(0,1)$, choose $\delta$ so that on $|t - t_0|\le\delta$: $|g(t) - g(t_0)|\le\eta|g(t_0)|$ and $-\frac12k(1+\eta)(t - t_0)^2\le h(t) - h_0\le-\frac12k(1-\eta)(t - t_0)^2$ (Taylor with the continuity of $h''$). Outside the $\delta$-interval, $h\le h_0 - \mu$ for some $\mu > 0$ (unique maximum, compactness), so that part is at most $(b-a)\max|g|\,e^{\lambda(h_0 - \mu)}$ — smaller than $e^{\lambda h_0}\lambda^{-1/2}$ by a factor $e^{-\lambda\mu}\sqrt\lambda\to0$. Inside, the integral lies between $(1\mp\eta)g(t_0)e^{\lambda h_0}\int_{-\delta}^{\delta}e^{-\frac12\lambda k(1\pm\eta)u^2}\mathrm du$ (for $g(t_0) > 0$; otherwise reverse), and $\int_{-\delta}^\delta e^{-\frac12\lambda cu^2}\mathrm du = \sqrt{2\pi/\lambda c}\,(1 + o(1))$. So the ratio of the integral to the claimed form lies between $(1-\eta)/\sqrt{1+\eta}$ and $(1+\eta)/\sqrt{1-\eta}$ for large $\lambda$; $\eta$ was arbitrary. $\square$

The infinite interval. The theorem is stated on $[a,b]$; Stirling's integral runs over $[0,\infty)$. On $u\ge2$, $h'(u) = 1/u - 1\le-\frac12$, so $h(u)\le\ln2 - 2 - (u-2)/2$ and the tail is at most $\frac2ne^{n(\ln2 - 2)}$; since $\ln2 - 2 = -1.307 < -1 = h(1)$, it is exponentially smaller than the main term. On $(0,\frac12]$, $h$ is increasing, so $h\le h(\frac12) = -\ln2 - \frac12 = -1.193 < -1$ and that piece is exponentially smaller too. The theorem applies on $[\frac12, 2]$ with $g = 1$.

The Stirling numbers. $\sqrt{20\pi}\,(10/e)^{10} = 3\,598\,695.62$; ratio to $3\,628\,800$ is $0.991704$. Times $1 + 1/120$: $3\,628\,684.75$. The correction series $1 + \frac{1}{12n} + \frac{1}{288n^2} - \dots$ comes from carrying the Taylor expansion of $h$ and the Gaussian moments to higher order — a Watson's lemma computation after the substitution $\ln u - u + 1 = -\tau^2/2$; it is cited here (de Bruijn, Asymptotic Methods in Analysis, §4.5) and it is an example of a divergent asymptotic series, which is unit 21's subject.

History. Laplace, 1774, Mémoire sur la probabilité des causes par les événements, evaluating integrals of $x^p(1-x)^q$ for large $p, q$ — the beta integrals of Bayesian inference. De Moivre had $n!\approx Cn^{n+1/2}e^{-n}$ in 1730 and Stirling found $C = \sqrt{2\pi}$ the same year.

Stationary phase

the method of stationary phase

Let $g, \psi$ be smooth and real-valued ($\psi$) on $[a,b]$, with $\psi'\ne0$ except at one interior $t_0$, where $\psi''(t_0)\ne0$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}\int_a^bg\,e^{i\lambda\psi}\mathrm dt ={}& g(t_0)\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\lambda|\psi''(t_0)|}}\,e^{i\lambda\psi(t_0)\pm i\pi/4}\\ &+ O(1/\lambda),\end{aligned}$$

the sign being that of $\psi''(t_0)$.

Where $\psi'\ne0$ the integral is $O(1/\lambda)$, by one integration by parts:

$$\int ge^{i\lambda\psi} = \Big[\frac{ge^{i\lambda\psi}}{i\lambda\psi'}\Big] - \frac{1}{i\lambda}\!\int\!\Big(\frac{g}{\psi'}\Big)'e^{i\lambda\psi}.$$

Near $t_0$ the phase stands still, the contributions stop cancelling, and a Fresnel integral — $\int e^{i\lambda u^2/2}\mathrm du = \sqrt{2\pi/\lambda}\,e^{i\pi/4}$, problem P6 — gives the size.

unit 09's debt: the constants in $J_n$

Bessel's integral $J_n(x) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{i(n\theta - x\sin\theta)}\mathrm d\theta$ has phase $-\sin\theta$, stationary at $\theta = \pm\frac\pi2$ with $\psi'' = \pm1$. Two contributions, complex conjugates (P11):

$$J_n(x)\approx\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\cos\Big(x - \frac{n\pi}{2} - \frac\pi4\Big).$$

$J_0(10) = -0.245936$ against $-0.246761$; $J_0(20) = 0.167025$ against $0.166656$ ✓.

Physics: in a superposition of waves, what survives at large distance or time is the part whose phase is stationary — Kelvin's argument (1887) for the V of a ship's wake, and the next slide's argument for group velocity. Dimensions: both sides $[g]\,\mathrm T$ ✓.

Full proof

Localise. Choose a smooth cutoff $\rho$ equal to $1$ near $t_0$ and supported where $|\psi''|\ge\frac12|\psi''(t_0)|$ (built from the bump function of section 2). On the support of $1 - \rho$, $\psi'\ne0$, and the integration by parts on the slide gives $O(1/\lambda)$ (boundary terms at $a, b$ included).

Straighten the phase. Near $t_0$ with $\psi''(t_0) > 0$, put $u = \operatorname{sgn}(t - t_0)\sqrt{2(\psi(t) - \psi(t_0))}$. Since $\psi - \psi(t_0) = (t - t_0)^2q(t)$ with $q$ smooth and $q(t_0) = \psi''(t_0)/2 > 0$, $u = (t - t_0)\sqrt{2q(t)}$ is smooth with $\mathrm du/\mathrm dt = \sqrt{\psi''(t_0)}$ at $t_0$: a smooth change of variable with $\psi = \psi(t_0) + u^2/2$. The localised integral becomes $e^{i\lambda\psi(t_0)}\int k(u)e^{i\lambda u^2/2}\mathrm du$, with $k = \rho g\,\mathrm dt/\mathrm du$ smooth, compactly supported, and $k(0) = g(t_0)/\sqrt{\psi''(t_0)}$.

Evaluate. $k(u) = k(0)\rho_1(u) + u\,m(u)$ with $\rho_1$ a cutoff equal to $1$ on the support of $k$ and $m$ smooth and compactly supported. Then (i) $\int u\,m\,e^{i\lambda u^2/2}\mathrm du = \frac{1}{i\lambda}\int m\,\mathrm d(e^{i\lambda u^2/2}) = -\frac{1}{i\lambda}\int m'e^{i\lambda u^2/2} = O(1/\lambda)$; (ii) $\int\rho_1e^{i\lambda u^2/2} = \int e^{i\lambda u^2/2} - \int(1 - \rho_1)e^{i\lambda u^2/2}$, and the second piece is $O(1/\lambda)$ by integrating by parts against $u$ (the integrand vanishes near $0$). The Fresnel integral (P6, with a scaling) is $\sqrt{2\pi/\lambda}\,e^{i\pi/4}$. Assemble: $e^{i\lambda\psi(t_0)}g(t_0)\sqrt{2\pi/\lambda\psi''(t_0)}\,e^{i\pi/4} + O(1/\lambda)$. For $\psi'' < 0$ take complex conjugates. $\square$

The Bessel numbers. $\sqrt{2/10\pi}\cos(10 - \pi/4) = -0.246761$ and $J_0(10) = -0.245936$; at $20$, $0.166656$ against $0.167025$. The error is smaller than the $O(1/x)$ the theorem allows, because the integrand of Bessel's integral is periodic: there are no endpoint contributions at all, and the next term is $O(x^{-3/2})$ (cited: Watson, Treatise on the Theory of Bessel Functions, §7.21). This settles the constants unit 09 had to cite.

History. Stokes (1850, for the Airy integral) and Kelvin (1887, for water waves). The Fresnel integrals are Fresnel's, 1818, from diffraction at an edge.

Steepest descent

For $I(\lambda) = \int_Cg(z)e^{\lambda\varphi(z)}\mathrm dz$ with $g, \varphi$ holomorphic, Cauchy lets you move $C$, paying for any arcs by estimates. Where to?

saddle point, and the path through it

$|e^{\lambda\varphi}| = e^{\lambda\operatorname{Re}\varphi}$ has no local maximum (maximum modulus). The best available is a saddle, $\varphi'(z_0) = 0$. Through it, the curve on which $\operatorname{Im}\varphi = \operatorname{Im}\varphi(z_0)$ is the path of steepest descent: along it there is no oscillation and $\operatorname{Re}\varphi$ falls as fast as possible.

the method of steepest descent

On that path the integral is a Laplace integral, and

$$I(\lambda)\sim g(z_0)\,e^{\lambda\varphi(z_0)}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{-\lambda\varphi''(z_0)}},$$

the root's branch set by the direction in which the path crosses $z_0$.

Why the level curves of $\operatorname{Im}\varphi$. With $\varphi = u + iv$, Cauchy–Riemann makes the vectors of partials orthogonal: $u_xv_x + u_yv_y = u_xv_x - v_xu_x = 0$. So a level curve of $v$ runs along the direction of fastest change of $u$. Near $z_0$, $\varphi\approx\varphi(z_0) + \frac12\varphi''(z_0)(z - z_0)^2$, and the descent direction $e^{i\theta}$ makes $\varphi''(z_0)e^{2i\theta}$ negative real.

one method, three faces

Laplace: real saddle, real path. Stationary phase: saddle on the real axis, path crossing it at $45^\circ$ — the $e^{\pm i\pi/4}$. Genuinely complex saddles: exponentially small answers that real methods see only as "$O(\lambda^{-N})$ for all $N$".

where the work is

The saddle is two lines. The proof is the arc estimates that justify the move — the checkpoint's second clause.

The saddle, geometrically. $e^{\lambda\varphi}$ is holomorphic, so by the maximum modulus theorem its modulus $e^{\lambda\operatorname{Re}\varphi}$ — and hence $\operatorname{Re}\varphi$ — has no interior local maximum. Near a point where $\varphi' = 0\ne\varphi''$, $\varphi - \varphi(z_0)\approx\frac12\varphi''(z_0)(z - z_0)^2$, and $\operatorname{Re}$ of that rises in two opposite directions and falls in the two perpendicular ones: a saddle. A contour that must get from one valley to another crosses the ridge; the lowest crossing is the saddle. That is why the method is also called the saddle-point method.

Proof of the formula, given the deformation. Parametrise the steepest path near $z_0$ by $\tau$ with $\varphi(z(\tau)) = \varphi(z_0) - \tau^2/2$, $\tau$ real; this is the phase-straightening of the stationary-phase proof done in the complex plane, legitimate because $\varphi''(z_0)\ne0$ (the holomorphic inverse function theorem, cited: Ahlfors, ch. 4 §3.3). Then $I = e^{\lambda\varphi(z_0)}\int g(z(\tau))z'(\tau)e^{-\lambda\tau^2/2}\mathrm d\tau$, a real Laplace integral, and $z'(0) = (-\varphi''(z_0))^{-1/2}$ with the branch pointing along the path. Laplace's method (or Watson's lemma, for the full series) finishes. $\square$

Practice is often easier than the theory. The exact steepest path is rarely needed: any path through the saddle along which $\operatorname{Re}\varphi$ has a strict nondegenerate maximum at $z_0$ works, because Laplace's method for a complex-valued exponent with a real-part maximum is proved by the same estimates. The next slide uses a straight line.

Unit 09's last debt: how fast $I_n(ne)$ grows

Unit 09 needed the growth in $n$ of $I_n(ne) = \sum_k\frac{(ne/2)^{n+2k}}{k!(n+k)!}$, the sum of the absolute values of the terms it rearranged, and of $J_n(ne)$. Take as given data the integral $I_n(x) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}e^{x\cos\theta + in\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta$ (expand $e^{x\cos\theta}$ in powers and integrate term by term to recover the series). With $x = ne$ the exponent is $n\varphi$, $\varphi(\theta) = e\cos\theta + i\theta$, and $\varphi' = -e\sin\theta + i = 0$ at $\theta = i\beta$ with $\sinh\beta = 1/e$. The move costs nothing: the integrand is $2\pi$-periodic, so on the rectangle $-\pi, \pi, \pi + i\beta, -\pi + i\beta$ the two vertical sides cancel exactly, and Cauchy moves $[-\pi,\pi]$ to $\theta = s + i\beta$. There $\operatorname{Re}\varphi = e\cosh\beta\cos s - \beta$, with its unique maximum on $[-\pi,\pi]$ at $s = 0$, and $\varphi''(i\beta) = -e\cosh\beta = -\sqrt{1 + e^2}$ is negative real, so Laplace's method applies along the line: $$I_n(ne)\sim\frac{r(e)^n}{\sqrt{2\pi n\sqrt{1 + e^2}}},\qquad r(e) = e^{\varphi(i\beta)} = \frac{e\,e^{\sqrt{1+e^2}}}{1 + \sqrt{1 + e^2}},$$ using $e\cosh\beta = \sqrt{1+e^2}$ and $e^{\beta} = (1 + \sqrt{1+e^2})/e$. So $\sum_nI_n(ne)$ converges iff $r(e) < 1$, and $r(e) = 1$ exactly at $e = 0.6627434$: with $x_* = \sqrt{1 + \lambda^2}$ it is P10's $x_*\tanh x_* = 1$ in another form. Worked: an exponentially small integral by steepest descent

worked example 5 — $\displaystyle I(\lambda) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{i\lambda(t^3/3 + t)}\,\mathrm dt = 2\int_0^\infty\cos\big(\lambda(\tfrac{t^3}3 + t)\big)\mathrm dt$ as $\lambda\to\infty$

Step 1 — real methods fail. The phase $t^3/3 + t$ has derivative $t^2 + 1 > 0$: no stationary point. Integrating by parts repeatedly shows only $I = O(\lambda^{-N})$ for every $N$.

Step 2 — the saddles. $\varphi(z) = i(z^3/3 + z)$, $\varphi' = i(z^2 + 1) = 0$ at $z = \pm i$, with $\varphi(i) = i(-\frac i3 + i) = -\frac23$ and $\varphi(-i) = +\frac23$. Go up to $z = i$, where the integrand is small.

Step 3 — the arcs. Rectangle $-R, R, R+i, -R+i$; the integrand is entire. On the side $z = \pm R + iy$, $0\le y\le1$:

$$\operatorname{Re}\,\lambda\varphi = \lambda\big(-R^2y + \tfrac{y^3}3 - y\big)\le-\lambda R^2y,$$

so each side is at most $\int_0^1e^{-\lambda R^2y}\mathrm dy\le\frac{1}{\lambda R^2}\to0$.

Step 4 — on the line through the saddle, $z = s + i$: expanding, $i\big(\frac{(s+i)^3}3 + s + i\big) = -\frac23 - s^2 + \frac{is^3}{3}$. So, exactly,

$$I(\lambda) = e^{-2\lambda/3}\!\int e^{-\lambda s^2}\cos\tfrac{\lambda s^3}3\,\mathrm ds .$$

No cancellation left: a Gaussian times a slowly turning phase.

Step 5 — Laplace. $\cos x = 1 - \frac{x^2}2 + O(x^4)$ and $\int s^6e^{-\lambda s^2} = \frac{15}{8}\sqrt\pi\,\lambda^{-7/2}$:

$$I = \sqrt{\frac\pi\lambda}\,e^{-2\lambda/3}\Big(1 - \frac{5}{48\lambda} + O(\lambda^{-2})\Big).$$

Check at $\lambda = 5$: quadrature gives $0.0277624$; one term $0.0282775$ ($+1.9\%$); two terms $0.0276884$ ($-0.27\%$) ✓. Limit: exponentially small, consistent with Step 1.

Step 3, the algebra. With $z = R + iy$: $z^3 = R^3 + 3iR^2y - 3Ry^2 - iy^3$, so $i z^3/3 = iR^3/3 - R^2y - iRy^2 + y^3/3$ and $iz = iR - y$. Real part of $i(z^3/3 + z)$: $-R^2y + y^3/3 - y$. For $0\le y\le1$, $y^3/3 - y\le0$. Same at $-R$ ($R^2$ is even). Each side has length $1$, so the ML-type bound is $\int_0^1e^{-\lambda R^2y}\mathrm dy = (1 - e^{-\lambda R^2})/\lambda R^2$. Cauchy's theorem for the rectangle (convex) then says: integral along the real axis from $-R$ to $R$ $=$ integral along the top from $-R+i$ to $R+i$ $+$ the two sides; let $R\to\infty$. The real integral converges as an improper integral (integrate by parts once against $t^2 + 1$), so the limit is $I(\lambda)$.

Step 4, the algebra. $(s+i)^3 = s^3 + 3is^2 - 3s - i$; divide by $3$ and add $s + i$: $\frac{s^3}3 + is^2 + \frac{2i}3$. Times $i$: $\frac{is^3}3 - s^2 - \frac23$. The odd part $\sin(\lambda s^3/3)$ integrates to zero against the even Gaussian.

Step 5, with its error. $|\cos x - 1 + x^2/2|\le x^4/24$, and with $x = \lambda s^3/3$, $\int e^{-\lambda s^2}\frac{\lambda^4s^{12}}{24\cdot81}\mathrm ds = \frac{\lambda^4}{1944}\cdot\frac{10395\sqrt\pi}{64}\lambda^{-13/2} = O(\lambda^{-5/2})$, which is $O(\lambda^{-2})$ relative to $\sqrt{\pi/\lambda}$. The second term: $-\frac{\lambda^2}{18}\cdot\frac{15\sqrt\pi}{8}\lambda^{-7/2} = -\frac{5}{48\lambda}\sqrt{\frac\pi\lambda}$. All rigorous — no step was an approximation that was not bounded.

Why this line and not the exact steepest path. The level curve $\operatorname{Im}\varphi = 0$ through $i$ is tangent to the horizontal there (it is $s^3/3$ that must vanish to third order), so the straight line leaves the steepest path only at third order, and the cubic phase it picks up is what Step 5 expands. Why not $-i$: on $z = R - iy$ the real part of the exponent is $\lambda(R^2y - y^3/3 + y)$, which grows with $R$ — the sides cannot be discarded, and the saddle there has $e^{+2\lambda/3}$: it would be a huge number cancelling itself.

The numbers. The exact value is $2\pi\lambda^{-1/3}\mathrm{Ai}(\lambda^{2/3})$, where Ai is the Airy function — a name to meet later, not a fact used here; at $\lambda = 5$ the quadrature and this formula agree, $0.02776242$. $\sqrt{\pi/5}\,e^{-10/3} = 0.02827754$, and times $1 - 5/240$ it is $0.02768843$. At $\lambda = 2$ the two-term value is $0.313163$ against $0.317375$, error $-1.3\%$; the error falls like $\lambda^{-2}$, as the bound says.

20 · asymptotics

Wave packets: phase and group velocity

A wave in a dispersive medium obeys a dispersion relation $\omega = \omega(k)$, and a packet is a superposition

$$u(x,t) = \frac1{2\pi}\int A(k)\,e^{i(kx - \omega(k)t)}\,\mathrm dk .$$

Each component's crests move at the phase velocity $v_\mathrm p = \omega/k$. Where is the packet?

group velocity, by stationary phase

Look along $x = vt$ as $t\to\infty$: the phase is $t\,(kv - \omega(k))$, stationary where $\omega'(k) = v$. So

$$u(vt, t)\approx\frac{A(k_*)\,e^{it(k_*v - \omega(k_*))\mp i\pi/4}}{\sqrt{2\pi t|\omega''(k_*)|}},$$

where $\omega'(k_*) = v$.

If $A$ is concentrated near $k_0$, $u$ is small unless $x/t\approx\omega'(k_0)$: the packet moves at $v_\mathrm g = \mathrm d\omega/\mathrm dk$, and decays like $t^{-1/2}$ — it spreads.

light in BK7, at 587.6 nm

In glass $k = n(\omega)\omega/c$, so $1/v_\mathrm g = \mathrm dk/\mathrm d\omega$ gives $v_\mathrm g = c/n_\mathrm g$ with the group index $n_\mathrm g = n - \lambda\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm d\lambda}$. From the Sellmeier formula:

$n = 1.51680$, $v_\mathrm p = 0.65928\,c$;
$n_\mathrm g = 1.54136$, $v_\mathrm g = 0.64878\,c$.

A pulse travels $1.6\%$ slower than its own crests; the crests slide forward through it. Normal dispersion, $\mathrm dn/\mathrm d\lambda < 0$, is what makes $n_\mathrm g > n$.

Checks. No dispersion, $n$ constant: $n_\mathrm g = n$, $v_\mathrm g = v_\mathrm p$ ✓. Dimensions: $\mathrm d\omega/\mathrm dk$ is a speed ✓.

The stationary-phase step. Apply the theorem to $\int A(k)e^{it\psi(k)}\mathrm dk$ with $\lambda = t$ and $\psi(k) = kv - \omega(k)$: $\psi' = v - \omega'(k)$, $\psi'' = -\omega''(k)$. The contribution is $A(k_*)\sqrt{2\pi/t|\omega''(k_*)|}\,e^{it\psi(k_*) - i\frac\pi4\operatorname{sgn}\omega''(k_*)}$, divided by $2\pi$. If no $k$ in the support of $A$ has $\omega'(k) = v$, the integral is $O(1/t)$ — faster decay — so the packet's bulk is on the rays $x/t\in\omega'(\operatorname{supp}A)$.

The group index. $k(\omega) = n(\omega)\omega/c$ gives $\frac{\mathrm dk}{\mathrm d\omega} = \frac{n + \omega\,\mathrm dn/\mathrm d\omega}{c}$, and with $\lambda = 2\pi c/\omega$, $\omega\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm d\omega} = -\lambda\frac{\mathrm dn}{\mathrm d\lambda}$. The derivative of the Sellmeier $n(\lambda)$ at $0.5875618\ \mu$m is $-0.04180\ \mu\mathrm m^{-1}$, so $n_\mathrm g = 1.516800 + 0.024560 = 1.541360$; $1/n = 0.659283$ and $1/n_\mathrm g = 0.648778$.

Deep water has $\omega = \sqrt{gk}$ (a result of fluid mechanics, not derived in this course), so $v_\mathrm g = \frac12\sqrt{g/k} = \frac12v_\mathrm p$: a group of swells travels at half the speed of its crests, which appear at the back of the group, run through it and die at the front. Kelvin's ship-wake angle, $2\arcsin(1/3) = 38.94^\circ$, is stationary phase applied to this relation in two dimensions.

History. Hamilton (1839) and Stokes (1876) distinguished the two velocities; Rayleigh's Theory of Sound (1877) gave $v_\mathrm g = \mathrm d\omega/\mathrm dk$ its name and its beat-frequency derivation. Energy and information travel at $v_\mathrm g$ in normal dispersion; near an absorption line $v_\mathrm g$ can exceed $c$, and Sommerfeld and Brillouin (1914) showed — by exactly this unit's contour methods — that the front of a signal still travels at $c$, because $\chi\to0$ as $|\omega|\to\infty$ in the upper half-plane.

20 · asymptotics

Packets spread

Take a Gaussian packet, $A(k) = e^{-\sigma_0^2(k - k_0)^2/2}$, and expand the dispersion relation to second order: $\omega\approx\omega_0 + v_\mathrm g q + \frac12\beta q^2$, $q = k - k_0$. Then

$$u = \frac{e^{i(k_0x - \omega_0t)}}{2\pi}\int e^{-aq^2/2 + iq(x - v_\mathrm gt)}\,\mathrm dq,$$

a Gaussian integral with complex width $a = \sigma_0^2 + i\beta t$, $\operatorname{Re}a > 0$: by analytic continuation it is $\sqrt{2\pi/a}\,e^{-(x - v_\mathrm gt)^2/2a}$. Taking the modulus:

the spreading law $$|u|\propto e^{-(x - v_\mathrm gt)^2/2\sigma(t)^2},$$ $$\sigma(t) = \sigma_0\sqrt{1 + \big(\beta t/\sigma_0^2\big)^2} .$$

Checks. $\beta = 0$: no spreading ✓. Late times $\sigma\approx|\beta|t/\sigma_0$: the narrower the packet, the faster it spreads — the uncertainty relation, moving. $[\beta] = \mathrm L^2\mathrm T^{-1}$, so $\beta t/\sigma_0^2$ is a number ✓.

a 10 fs pulse through 1 cm of glass

In time rather than space: $k(\omega)\approx k_0 + (\omega - \omega_0)/v_\mathrm g + \frac12k''(\omega - \omega_0)^2$, and the same algebra gives a duration $\tau(z) = \tau_0\sqrt{1 + (k''z/\tau_0^2)^2}$.

BK7 at 800 nm: $k'' = 44.65\ \mathrm{fs^2\,mm^{-1}}$ from the Sellmeier formula. A pulse of intensity full width $10$ fs has $\tau_0 = 6.006$ fs; after $z = 10$ mm, $k''z = 446.5\ \mathrm{fs^2}$ and

$$\tau/\tau_0 = 12.42:\quad10\ \mathrm{fs}\to124\ \mathrm{fs}.$$

A centimetre of ordinary glass stretches the pulse twelvefold. Ultrafast optics spends much of its ingenuity undoing exactly this.

The integral with complex width. The analytic continuation slide gave $\int e^{-aq^2/2}\mathrm dq = \sqrt{2\pi/a}$ for $\operatorname{Re}a > 0$. With a linear term: for $a > 0$, $\int e^{-aq^2/2 + ibq}\mathrm dq = \sqrt{2\pi/a}\,e^{-b^2/2a}$ (worked example 1 with scaling). For fixed real $b$ both sides are holomorphic in $a$ on $\operatorname{Re}a > 0$ (Morera, as before), so the identity theorem extends it. Here $b = x - v_\mathrm gt$.

The modulus. $|e^{-b^2/2a}| = e^{-b^2\operatorname{Re}(1/a)/2}$ and $\operatorname{Re}\frac1a = \frac{\sigma_0^2}{\sigma_0^4 + \beta^2t^2}$, so $\sigma(t)^2 = \frac{\sigma_0^4 + \beta^2t^2}{\sigma_0^2}$. Also $|\sqrt{2\pi/a}| = \sqrt{2\pi}\,(\sigma_0^4 + \beta^2t^2)^{-1/4}$, decaying like $t^{-1/2}$ as the stationary-phase slide predicted, and $\int|u|^2\mathrm dx$ is constant, as it must be (Plancherel: the dispersion only changes the phases of the components).

The glass numbers. $k'' = \frac{\lambda^3}{2\pi c^2}\frac{\mathrm d^2n}{\mathrm d\lambda^2}$; at $0.8\ \mu$m the Sellmeier formula gives $k'' = 44.652\ \mathrm{fs^2\,mm^{-1}}$. The field envelope is $e^{-t^2/2\tau_0^2}$, the intensity $e^{-t^2/\tau_0^2}$, whose full width at half maximum is $2\sqrt{\ln2}\,\tau_0$; for $10$ fs that is $\tau_0 = 6.0056$ fs, $\tau_0^2 = 36.07\ \mathrm{fs^2}$. Then $k''z/\tau_0^2 = 446.52/36.067 = 12.380$, and $\sqrt{1 + 12.380^2} = 12.420$, giving $124.2$ fs. The output is also chirped — the phase of $1/a$ makes the instantaneous frequency sweep across the pulse, red first in normal dispersion — which is what a prism or grating pair then compresses.

The same law elsewhere. With $\omega = \hbar k^2/2m$ this is the spreading of a free quantum wave packet, $\beta = \hbar/m$; with a time-dependent width it is the diffraction of a Gaussian laser beam, the Rayleigh range playing the role of $\sigma_0^2/\beta$. The mathematics is one Gaussian integral with a complex parameter, legitimised by the identity theorem.

three things to carry forward
