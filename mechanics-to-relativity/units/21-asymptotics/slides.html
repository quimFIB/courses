Perturbation theory: the series that diverges and is still right

Almost nothing has an exact answer. What it has instead is an expansion in a small parameter — often divergent, usually non-uniform, and, handled correctly, accurate to ten digits.

needs 06, 02, 08~3 h slides + 6 h problemsproblems: ./mr problems 21

Unit 02 said it early and plainly: almost no equation of motion has a closed-form solution, and that is not a gap in the literature. Unit 02's answer was the qualitative picture. This unit's answer is the quantitative one. When a problem is close to one you can solve — a spring that is nearly linear, an orbit that is nearly Keplerian, a fluid that is nearly inviscid — you can expand in the smallness and get numbers. Most of the quantitative physics you will ever read is done this way, and most of it rests on three facts that are rarely said aloud:

  • the series you get usually diverges, and that does not matter, because what you want is not convergence but a controlled error at a fixed number of terms;
  • the naive expansion is usually non-uniform: correct for a while, or away from some edge, and then badly wrong — and the place where it goes wrong is where the physics is;
  • the small parameter is not handed to you: you find it by nondimensionalising, which is unit 00's dimensional habit put to work.

How to read this deck

Two calculations run through it, joined on purpose. The first is the Duffing oscillator $\ddot x + x + \varepsilon x^3 = 0$: the naive expansion produces a term $t\sin t$ that wrecks it after a time of order $1/\varepsilon$, and we learn to remove it. The second is the orbit equation $w'' + w = 1 + \varepsilon w^2$ that general relativity hands to unit 37: the same calculation produces the same kind of term — and there it must not be thrown away, because it is the advance of Mercury's perihelion. Sections 1 and 2 are about what an expansion means; 3 and 4 about secular terms; 5 about boundary layers; 6 about WKB; 7 closes the orbit.

Every number quoted was computed. Proofs are on the slides where they are short and in these notes under Full proof where they are not; anything imported is labelled cited with where its proof lives. Integral asymptotics — Laplace's method, stationary phase — belong to unit 20 and are cited here, never used as a step.

Two oscillators, one term

the Duffing oscillator · the term is removed $$\ddot x + x + \varepsilon x^3 = 0, \qquad x(0) = A,\ \dot x(0) = 0 .$$

1. Expand $x = x_0 + \varepsilon x_1 + \cdots$: $x_0 = A\cos t$, and $x_1$ is driven by $-x_0^3$, which contains $-\tfrac34A^3\cos t$ — a push at the oscillator's own frequency.

2. Resonance puts $-\tfrac38A^3\,t\sin t$ into $x_1$: unbounded, though the true $|x| \le A$ for ever.

3. That term is a frequency shift, Taylor-expanded: $A\cos\big((1 + \varepsilon\omega_1)t\big) = A\cos t - \varepsilon\omega_1At\sin t + \cdots$

4. Give the frequency its own expansion and demand periodicity: $\omega = 1 + \tfrac38\varepsilon A^2$.

the relativistic orbit · the term is kept $$w'' + w = 1 + \varepsilon w^2, \qquad w = p/r .$$

$r$ is the planet's distance from the Sun at angle $\theta$, $p$ a fixed length of its orbit, $' = \mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}\theta$.

1. At $\varepsilon = 0$, $w_0 = 1 + e\cos\theta$: unit 08's ellipse, of eccentricity $e$.

2. $w_0^2$ contains $2e\cos\theta$, again a push at the natural frequency: $w_1$ gets $e\,\theta\sin\theta$.

3. Read as in Duffing's step 3, $w \approx 1 + e\cos\big((1 - \varepsilon)\theta\big)$: perihelion comes round $2\pi\varepsilon$ late each orbit.

4. For Mercury $\varepsilon = 8.0\times10^{-8}$: $43''$ a century, observed. Here the term is the answer.

Why these two. The Duffing oscillator is the simplest nonlinear oscillator there is, and the textbook place to see a secular term; physically it is a stiffening (or, with $\varepsilon < 0$, softening) spring, and the pendulum to its first nonlinear order is one of them. The orbit equation is the reason this unit is on the course's spine: unit 37 computes the perihelion advance of Mercury from exactly this equation, and a reader who has only seen secular terms "removed" will find that calculation looks like a trick. It is not a trick, and this unit is where that is made honest.

section 1

Small parameters, and where they come from

"Small" means nothing until you say compared with what. Nondimensionalising says it.

21 · small parameters

Nondimensionalise first

A mass on a spring with a cubic stiffening: $m\ddot X + kX + \beta X^3 = 0$, released from rest at $X = a$. Six quantities, $X, t, m, k, \beta, a$, in three dimensions M, L, T.

nondimensionalisation

Choose a scale for each variable from the parameters, so that the scaled variables are of size one: here $x = X/a$ and $t' = t\sqrt{k/m}$. Divide by one coefficient. What is left over is a small parameter or it is not.

$$ka\,x'' + ka\,x + \beta a^3x^3 = 0,\ \text{ so}$$ $$x'' + x + \varepsilon x^3 = 0,\qquad \varepsilon = \frac{\beta a^2}{k}.$$

What $\varepsilon$ says. $\beta a^3/ka$: the cubic force over the linear one, at the amplitude. "Small" now has a meaning.

Buckingham, as a check

Six quantities, dimension matrix of rank 3, so three groups (unit 00): $X/a$, $t\sqrt{k/m}$ and $\beta a^2/k$. The scaled equation contains exactly those. Whatever the answer is, it is a function of $\varepsilon$ alone once time is measured in units of $\sqrt{m/k}$.

the pendulum is one of these

$\ddot\theta + \sin\theta = 0$ with $\sin\theta = \theta - \theta^3/6 + O(\theta^5)$. Put $\theta = A x$: $x'' + x - \tfrac{A^2}{6}x^3 = 0$. So Duffing with $\varepsilon = -A^2/6$, amplitude 1.

The scaled equation, line by line. With $X = a x$ and $t = \sqrt{m/k}\,t'$, the chain rule gives $\ddot X = a\,(k/m)\,x''$ where $'$ is $\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t'$. Substituting, $ka\,x'' + ka\,x + \beta a^3x^3 = 0$; divide by $ka$. The initial conditions become $x(0) = 1$, $x'(0) = 0$. In the running example we keep the amplitude $A$ visible (scale by some fixed length instead of by the amplitude) because it makes the answer's amplitude dependence easy to read: then $\varepsilon = \beta L^2/k$ for the fixed length $L$, and the combination that matters is always $\varepsilon A^2$.

Why the choice of scales matters. The scales must be the ones the solution actually has. If you scale $X$ by a length much bigger than the amplitude, every term looks small and the "small parameter" you find is an artefact. The rule: each scaled variable, and each of its derivatives that appears, should be of size one in the region you care about. That rule is also how boundary layers are found (section 5): a region where the derivatives are not of size one in the original scaling needs its own.

Dimensions. $[\beta] = \mathsf{MT^{-2}L^{-2}}$ (force over length cubed), $[k] = \mathsf{MT^{-2}}$, so $[\beta a^2/k] = 1$.

The pendulum. Unit 00 expanded the exact period to $T \approx 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\,(1 + \theta_0^2/16)$. Section 4 recovers that factor from the Duffing frequency with $\varepsilon = -A^2/6$ — the first check that the perturbation theory is right.

Regular or singular: the test

two kinds of problem

Set $\varepsilon = 0$. If the reduced problem is of the same kind — same degree, same order, all conditions still imposable — and its solution is the limit of the true one, the problem is a regular perturbation: expand in powers of $\varepsilon$ and go. If not, it is a singular perturbation.

the tell

$\varepsilon$ multiplying the highest power or derivative. Setting it to zero lowers the degree or the order: roots or boundary conditions are lost, and something must happen on a scale where the "small" term is not small.

$\varepsilon x^2 + x - 1$ for $\varepsilon = 0.2$ and $0.1$, and the line it becomes at $\varepsilon = 0$. One root stays near $1$; the other sits near $-1/\varepsilon$ and runs off to $-\infty$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$ — the root the reduced problem cannot see.

What to look for in the figure. The parabola's vertex is at $x = -1/2\varepsilon$, depth $-1 - 1/4\varepsilon$; halving $\varepsilon$ doubles the distance to the far root and the parabola flattens towards the straight line $x - 1$. Near $x = 1$ the curves are almost indistinguishable: there $\varepsilon x^2$ really is small, and the root moves smoothly with $\varepsilon$ — regular. Near the far root $\varepsilon x^2$ is as big as $x$, so dropping it is never justified there — singular. The figure is computed from the exact roots, $(-1 \pm \sqrt{1 + 4\varepsilon})/2\varepsilon$.

Why "same kind" is the right test. A quadratic has two roots, a linear equation one; a second-order differential equation takes two boundary conditions, a first-order one only one. If setting $\varepsilon = 0$ changes that count, the $\varepsilon = 0$ problem cannot be the limit of the full one everywhere, and a plain power series in $\varepsilon$ must miss something.

Where the lost root went: dominant balance

the smallest example: $\varepsilon x^2 + x - 1 = 0$

At $\varepsilon = 0$: one root, $x = 1$. But a quadratic has two. Put $x = 1 + \varepsilon x_1 + \varepsilon^2x_2$ and collect: $x_1 = -1$, $x_2 = 2$. The other root is lost — it is at $x \approx -1/\varepsilon$, where "$\varepsilon x^2$ is small" is false. Rescale $x = X/\varepsilon$: $X^2 + X - \varepsilon = 0$, regular, $X = -1 - \varepsilon + \dots$

At $\varepsilon = 0.1$: exact $0.916080$ and $-10.916080$; the series through $\varepsilon^2$, $1 - \varepsilon + 2\varepsilon^2$ and $-1/\varepsilon - 1 + \varepsilon - 2\varepsilon^2$ (notes), give $0.920$ and $-10.920$.

Finding the scale of the lost root is dominant balance: try $x = \varepsilon^{-\alpha}X$, and choose $\alpha$ so that two terms are of the same size and the rest are smaller. Here $\alpha = 1$ balances $\varepsilon x^2$ against $x$.

Duffing passes the test — $\varepsilon$ multiplies $x^3$, not $\ddot x$ — and is regular on any fixed time interval. Section 3 shows it is not regular on $[0, \infty)$: a second kind of singularity, in the domain rather than the equation.

The regular root, in full. Substitute $x = 1 + \varepsilon x_1 + \varepsilon^2 x_2 + O(\varepsilon^3)$: $\varepsilon(1 + 2\varepsilon x_1) + 1 + \varepsilon x_1 + \varepsilon^2x_2 - 1 + O(\varepsilon^3) = 0$. Order $\varepsilon$: $1 + x_1 = 0$. Order $\varepsilon^2$: $2x_1 + x_2 = 0$, so $x_2 = 2$. Hence $x = 1 - \varepsilon + 2\varepsilon^2 + O(\varepsilon^3)$. Exact: $x = (-1 + \sqrt{1 + 4\varepsilon})/2\varepsilon$, and the binomial series of $\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon}$ gives $1 - \varepsilon + 2\varepsilon^2 - 5\varepsilon^3 + \dots$ (the Catalan numbers, with signs), agreeing.

The singular root. The two roots sum to $-1/\varepsilon$, so the second is $-1/\varepsilon - 1 + \varepsilon - 2\varepsilon^2 + \dots$ — a series that starts at order $\varepsilon^{-1}$, which no power series in $\varepsilon$ starting at $\varepsilon^0$ can represent. That is what "singular" means here: the expansion needs a different gauge.

Dominant balance, spelled out. With $x = \varepsilon^{-\alpha}X$, the three terms are of sizes $\varepsilon^{1-2\alpha}$, $\varepsilon^{-\alpha}$ and $1$. The possible balances: $\varepsilon^{1-2\alpha} = \varepsilon^{-\alpha}$ gives $\alpha = 1$, with the third term $1 \ll \varepsilon^{-1}$ — consistent, the singular root. $\varepsilon^{-\alpha} = 1$ gives $\alpha = 0$, with $\varepsilon x^2 = O(\varepsilon)$ smaller — consistent, the regular root. $\varepsilon^{1-2\alpha} = 1$ gives $\alpha = 1/2$, but then the middle term is $\varepsilon^{-1/2} \gg 1$, larger than the two that balanced — inconsistent, discarded. Each consistent balance is one family of solutions. The same bookkeeping locates boundary layers in section 5.

Asymptotic series

What "$\approx$" means, precisely — and why a divergent series can deserve it.

21 · asymptotic series

Order notation

order notation

Let $f, g$ be defined near $x_0$ (possibly $x_0 = \infty$), $g \neq 0$ near $x_0$. As $x \to x_0$:

  • $f = O(g)$ if $|f/g|$ is bounded on some punctured neighbourhood of $x_0$;
  • $f = o(g)$ if $f/g \to 0$;
  • $f \sim g$, asymptotically equivalent, if $f/g \to 1$, that is, $f - g = o(g)$.
as $x \to 0$

$\sin x = x + O(x^3)$; $\ \sin x \sim x$; $\ 1 - \cos x = o(x)$; $\ x^{10} = o(x^9)$.

$e^{-1/x} = o(x^n)$ as $x \to 0^+$, for every $n$: smaller than every power.

as $x \to \infty$

$\ln x = o(x^\delta)$ for every $\delta \gt 0$; $\ x^2 + x \sim x^2$; $\ x^2 + x \sim x^2 + 1$, although their difference is unbounded.

Every statement is about a limit, never about a particular $x$. That is both the power and the trap: "$O(\varepsilon^2)$" says nothing about the constant, and nothing about $\varepsilon = 0.3$.

Proof that $e^{-1/x} = o(x^n)$ as $x \to 0^+$. Put $s = 1/x \to \infty$. Then $e^{-1/x}/x^n = s^n e^{-s}$, and $e^s \ge s^{n+1}/(n+1)!$ (one term of its series, all terms positive), so $s^ne^{-s} \le (n+1)!/s \to 0$. $\square$

Proof that $\ln x = o(x^\delta)$. Put $x = e^u$: $\ln x/x^\delta = u e^{-\delta u} \le 2/(\delta^2 u) \to 0$ by the same one-term bound $e^{\delta u} \ge (\delta u)^2/2$. $\square$

The last example. $(x^2 + x)/(x^2 + 1) \to 1$, so $x^2 + x \sim x^2 + 1$, although $(x^2 + x) - (x^2 + 1) = x - 1$ is unbounded. $\sim$ controls the relative error only. When an absolute error matters — a phase, which enters as $\cos(\text{phase})$ — you need more, and that distinction is the whole of section 3.

Notation warning. $f = O(g)$ is a one-way statement dressed as an equation: $x^3 = O(x^2)$ as $x \to 0$ does not make $O(x^2) = x^3$. Read "$=$" as "is in the class".

21 · asymptotic series

Poincaré's definition

asymptotic sequence and asymptotic expansion

$(\phi_n)_{n \ge 0}$ is an asymptotic sequence as $x \to x_0$ if $\phi_{n+1} = o(\phi_n)$ for every $n$. Then $f \sim \sum_{n\ge0} a_n\phi_n$ means

$$f - \sum_{n=0}^{N} a_n\phi_n = o(\phi_N)\ \text{ for every } N .$$

Poincaré, 1886. The series need not converge: the definition is about a fixed $N$ as $x \to x_0$, not about $N \to \infty$ at fixed $x$.

uniqueness of the coefficients

If $f$ has an expansion in $(\phi_n)$, its coefficients are $a_N = \lim_{x\to x_0}\big(f - \sum_{n\lt N} a_n\phi_n\big)/\phi_N$.

Why: divide the definition at $N$ by $\phi_N$. $\square$

but not of the function

$e^{-1/x} \sim 0 + 0\cdot x + 0\cdot x^2 + \cdots$ as $x \to 0^+$. So $f$ and $f + e^{-1/x}$ have the same expansion in powers. An expansion determines the coefficients, never the function.

Convergent is not the same as useful

convergent, and useless

$e^{-20} = \sum_n (-20)^n/n!$ converges. Its terms climb to $4.31\times10^{7}$ (at $n = 19, 20$) before they fall, and they must cancel to $2.06\times10^{-9}$.

You need terms up to $n = 74$ for $0.1\%$ accuracy. Summed in double precision, 121 terms give anything from $0.7\times10^{-9}$ to $6\times10^{-9}$, depending on how the terms are rounded — never the right answer, however many terms.

divergent, and excellent

$\displaystyle\sum_n (-1)^n n!\,x^n$ diverges for every $x \ne 0$: the ratio of terms is $(n+1)x \to \infty$.

Yet at $x = 0.04$ its first twenty-five terms give the function it expands to an error of $8.6\times10^{-11}$ — ten correct digits.

The next slide puts the two side by side, term by term.

The $e^{-20}$ numbers. The term ratio is $|t_{n+1}/t_n| = 20/(n+1)$, which exceeds 1 until $n = 19$, so the largest terms are $t_{19}$ and $t_{20}$, of size $20^{20}/20! = 4.310\times10^{7}$. In exact arithmetic the partial sums first get within $0.1\%$ of $e^{-20} = 2.0612\times10^{-9}$ at $n = 74$. In IEEE double precision each term near the peak carries a rounding error of order $10^{7}\times 10^{-16} = 10^{-9}$ — the size of the answer — so the computed sum is decided by the rounding. Three natural ways, all with $n = 0, \dots, 120$: each term $(-20)^n/n!$ correctly rounded and then summed exactly gives $7.2\times10^{-10}$; the same terms added left to right give $1.48\times10^{-9}$; terms built by the recurrence $t_n = t_{n-1}\cdot(-20/n)$ and added left to right give $6.1\times10^{-9}$. The right way is $1/e^{20}$, a series of positive terms: the problem is cancellation, not convergence.

The divergence. For $x \ne 0$ the terms $n!\,|x|^n$ do not tend to zero (their ratio tends to infinity), so the series diverges by the term test.

The general lesson is the one Poincaré drew: convergent series of celestial mechanics could need hundreds of terms, while divergent ones gave the answer in three. Physicists had known this empirically for a century; the definition on the previous slide made it mathematics.

Convergence is a statement about $N \to \infty$ at fixed $x$. Usefulness is a statement about small $N$ at the $x$ you have. Neither implies the other.

An asymptotic series is one whose first few terms are good when $x$ is near $x_0$, whatever the tail does. In the picture: what matters is how low the curve gets before it turns, not where it ends.

For the divergent series, the error after stopping at the smallest term is about half that term (Optimal truncation makes this precise), so the lowest point of the curve is the accuracy you can have.

The Stieltjes series, and its remainder

the Stieltjes function $$S(x) = \int_0^\infty \frac{e^{-t}}{1 + xt}\,\mathrm{d}t, \qquad x \ge 0 .$$

For $y \ne -1$, the finite geometric sum is exact: $\dfrac1{1+y} = \displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(-y)^n + \frac{(-y)^N}{1+y}$. Put $y = xt$, multiply by $e^{-t}$, integrate, and use $\int_0^\infty t^ne^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t = n!$:

$$S(x) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(-1)^n n!\,x^n + R_N(x),$$ $$R_N(x) = (-x)^N\!\int_0^\infty\!\frac{t^Ne^{-t}}{1+xt}\,\mathrm{d}t .$$
the error is smaller than the first omitted term

For $x \gt 0$: $R_N(x)$ has the sign of $(-1)^N$ and $0 \lt |R_N(x)| \lt N!\,x^N$.

Why: $0 \lt 1/(1+xt) \lt 1$ for $t \gt 0$, so $0 \lt \int_0^\infty t^Ne^{-t}/(1+xt) \lt N!$. $\square$

So $|R_N| \lt N!\,x^N = o(x^{N-1})$: the series is the asymptotic expansion of $S$ as $x \to 0^+$, with an explicit error bound — a rare luxury.

$S$ is not exotic: $xS(x)$ solves Euler's equation $x^2y' + y = x$, whose only formal power-series solution is $x$ times this divergent series. The problem sheet shows it.

Full proof of the remainder formula. For every real $y \ne -1$ and $N \ge 1$, $(1 + y)\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}(-y)^n = 1 - (-y)^N$ (telescoping), which rearranges to the identity on the slide. With $y = xt \ge 0$, multiply by $e^{-t}$ and integrate over $[0, \infty)$. Each term is a convergent improper integral (unit 00): $\int_0^\infty t^ne^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t = n!$ by induction, integrating by parts once, $\int t^ne^{-t} = n\int t^{n-1}e^{-t}$, with the boundary terms vanishing because $t^ne^{-t} \to 0$. The remainder integral converges because its integrand is at most $t^Ne^{-t}$. Nothing was interchanged: the sum is finite. That is the whole difference between this derivation and the illegitimate one — expand $1/(1+xt)$ in its full geometric series and integrate term by term — which fails because the geometric series diverges for $t \gt 1/x$.

Dimensions and a limit. Everything is dimensionless. At $x = 0$, $S(0) = \int_0^\infty e^{-t} = 1$, matching the first term.

Why the sign matters. The error has the sign of the first omitted term and is smaller than it. So consecutive partial sums bracket $S$ — you get a rigorous interval, not just an estimate. This is special to series of Stieltjes type; in general an asymptotic series gives no such guarantee, and the only honest error estimate is "about the size of the first omitted term", which is a heuristic.

Full proof of the exponentially small bound. For $N \ge 1$, $\ln k \le \int_k^{k+1}\ln s\,\mathrm{d}s$ for $k = 1, \dots, N-1$, so $\ln N! \le \int_1^N \ln s\,\mathrm{d}s + \ln N = N\ln N - N + 1 + \ln N$, that is, $N! \le e\,N^{N+1}e^{-N}$. With $N = \lfloor 1/x\rfloor$ we have $Nx \le 1$ and $N \gt 1/x - 1$, so

$$|R_N| \lt N!\,x^N \le e\,N\,(Nx)^N e^{-N} \le e\cdot\tfrac1x\cdot e^{-(1/x - 1)} = \tfrac{e^2}{x}e^{-1/x}.\qquad\square$$

At $x = 0.1$ this bound is $3.4\times10^{-3}$ against a true error of $1.8\times10^{-4}$: crude in the constant, right in the exponential, which is what matters.

Borel summation, named

Borel summation

Given $\sum a_nx^n$, form $B(t) = \sum a_n t^n/n!$ — often convergent where the original is not. If $B$ extends to $[0, \infty)$ and the integral exists, define

$$\text{sum} := \int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,B(xt)\,\mathrm{d}t .$$

The motive: $n! = \int_0^\infty t^ne^{-t}\,\mathrm{d}t$, so formally $\sum a_n x^n = \int_0^\infty e^{-t}\sum a_n(xt)^n/n!\,\mathrm{d}t$ — the swap being exactly the step that is not allowed.

on the Stieltjes series

$a_n = (-1)^nn!$ gives $B(t) = \sum(-t)^n = 1/(1+t)$ for $|t| \lt 1$, and the formula $1/(1+t)$ is defined on all of $[0, \infty)$. Then $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}/(1 + xt)\,\mathrm{d}t = S(x)$ — the function, recovered exactly.

named, not developed

When Borel's sum exists and when it equals "the" function is a theorem with hypotheses (Watson's, cited: Bender and Orszag, ch. 8; Hardy, Divergent Series, for the theory). This course uses optimal truncation instead, which needs nothing.

Why name it at all. Because the question it answers — can a divergent series determine its function? — has the answer "not by itself" (the $e^{-1/x}$ slide) and "yes, with extra analytic information" (Borel). Quantum field theory's perturbation series are believed to be divergent and of this factorial type, and Borel summation is the language in which their meaning is discussed. You will see the name; now you know what it is.

Why not develop it. Making "$B$ extends to $[0,\infty)$" precise wants analytic continuation, which is unit 20's complex analysis. For the Stieltjes series we side-stepped that: the formula $1/(1+t)$ is simply defined on $[0, \infty)$, and the integral is the function we started from.

A check on the definition. For a convergent geometric series $\sum x^n$ ($|x| \lt 1$), $B(t) = e^t$ and $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}e^{xt}\,\mathrm{d}t = 1/(1 - x)$, the ordinary sum. Borel summation agrees with ordinary summation where both apply — a property called regularity, which holds in general (cited: Hardy, Divergent Series).

21 · secular terms

Regular perturbation, the naive way

Treat Duffing as a regular problem: substitute

$$x(t) = x_0(t) + \varepsilon x_1(t) + \varepsilon^2x_2(t) + \cdots$$

into $\ddot x + x + \varepsilon x^3 = 0$ and into the initial conditions, and set each power of $\varepsilon$ to zero separately. This is the perturbation hierarchy:

$$\begin{aligned} \varepsilon^0:&\quad \ddot x_0 + x_0 = 0,\\ \varepsilon^1:&\quad \ddot x_1 + x_1 = -x_0^3,\\ \varepsilon^2:&\quad \ddot x_2 + x_2 = -3x_0^2x_1, \end{aligned}$$

with $x_0(0) = A$, $\dot x_0(0) = 0$, and zero data for every $x_n$, $n \ge 1$.

the structure to notice

Every order is the same linear operator $L = \mathrm{d}^2/\mathrm{d}t^2 + 1$, driven by a known right-hand side built from lower orders. One nonlinear problem has become a sequence of forced linear oscillators: unit 06, over and over.

The one identity we need, from $\cos^3 t = \big(\tfrac{e^{it} + e^{-it}}{2}\big)^3$:

$$\cos^3 t = \tfrac34\cos t + \tfrac14\cos 3t .$$

So the $\varepsilon^1$ equation is an undamped oscillator driven at its own frequency, $-\tfrac34A^3\cos t$, plus a harmless $-\tfrac14A^3\cos 3t$.

Why the hierarchy is legitimate. The solution $x(t;\varepsilon)$ depends smoothly on $\varepsilon$ for each fixed $t$, because the right-hand side is a polynomial in $(x, \varepsilon)$. Differentiating the equation in $\varepsilon$ at $\varepsilon = 0$ gives precisely the equations above, with $x_n = \frac{1}{n!}\partial_\varepsilon^n x|_{\varepsilon=0}$. So the $x_n$ are the Taylor coefficients of $x$ in $\varepsilon$ at fixed $t$. Everything about to go wrong goes wrong in the dependence on $t$, not in the existence of the coefficients. (Smooth dependence on a parameter is the one piece here not proved in unit 01, which proved continuous dependence; it is cited: Hartman, Ordinary Differential Equations, ch. V, on dependence on initial conditions and parameters. What we need is the weaker statement proved directly, by Grönwall, on "How long the naive answer is good".)

The identity. Expanding the cube, $(e^{it} + e^{-it})^3 = e^{3it} + 3e^{it} + 3e^{-it} + e^{-3it} = 2\cos 3t + 6\cos t$; divide by 8. Unit 06's complex exponential, used as algebra.

Dimensions. In scaled variables everything is dimensionless; in physical ones each order carries the same units as $X$, and $\varepsilon$ carries none — which is why the hierarchy can add them.

Step 3 — the off-resonant part. Try $c\cos 3t$: $(-9 + 1)c = -\tfrac14A^3$, so $c = \tfrac1{32}A^3$.

Step 4 — the resonant part. $c\cos t$ fails ($L\cos t = 0$): unit 06's resonance. Try $b\,t\sin t$: $L(t\sin t) = 2\cos t$, so $2b = -\tfrac34A^3$, $b = -\tfrac38A^3$.

Step 5 — the initial conditions. Add the homogeneous $d\cos t$ (the $\sin t$ part is zero since $\dot x_1(0) = 0$ already holds). $x_1(0) = \tfrac1{32}A^3 + d = 0$, so $d = -\tfrac1{32}A^3$.

The term $t\sin t$ is a secular term: it grows without bound, and it comes from forcing a linear oscillator at its own frequency.

Step 4 in full. $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(t\sin t) = \sin t + t\cos t$, and $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d}t^2}(t\sin t) = 2\cos t - t\sin t$. Adding $t\sin t$ gives $L(t\sin t) = 2\cos t$. This is exactly unit 06's "no damping, driven at $\omega_0$: the ansatz fails" slide; there the amplitude grew linearly because energy was pumped in at resonance. Here nothing pumps energy in — the system is conservative — so the growth cannot be physical. What the secular term really is, two slides on, shows what it is instead.

Step 5 in full. $\dot x_1 = -\tfrac38A^3(\sin t + t\cos t) + \tfrac1{32}A^3(-3\sin 3t + \sin t) + (\text{homogeneous})'$; every term vanishes at $t = 0$, so the homogeneous part needs no $\sin t$.

Why "secular". Latin saeculum, an age or a century. Eighteenth-century astronomers — Lagrange and Laplace above all — found terms growing like $t$ in their expansions of planetary orbits and called them secular inequalities, because they showed up over centuries rather than within a single orbit. The word has stuck to every term in a perturbation series that grows without bound in the independent variable. Section 7 returns the word to the astronomers.

What the secular term really is

$x = A\cos(\text{phase})$ is the shadow of a point going round a circle. A frequency error $\delta$ puts the true point an arc $A\delta t$ further on; Taylor-expanding moves it the same length along the tangent instead, off the circle — and its shadow leaves $|x| \le A$. Schematic, $\delta t = 1.3$.

a phase error, Taylor-expanded

Expand a slightly wrong frequency: $A\cos\big((1 + \varepsilon\omega_1)t\big) = A\cos t - \varepsilon\omega_1A\,t\sin t + O(\varepsilon^2t^2)$. With $\omega_1 = \tfrac38A^2$ that is exactly $x_0 + $ the secular part of $\varepsilon x_1$. The series is Taylor-expanding a phase error, which is fine while $\varepsilon t \ll 1$ and nonsense after.

uniformly valid

$x_0 + \varepsilon x_1$ has error $O(\varepsilon^2)$ at each fixed $t$, but not uniformly in $t \in [0, \infty)$: no single constant works for all $t$.

The figure, as algebra. Write the motion as the point $(A\cos\phi, A\sin\phi)$ with phase $\phi = (1 + \delta)t$, $\delta = \varepsilon\omega_1$; its first coordinate is $x$. (It is the phase-space point $(x, -\dot x)$ of unit 02, at leading order.) Expanding in $\delta$ at fixed $t$, $A\cos(t + \delta t) = A\cos t - \delta t\,A\sin t + O(\delta^2t^2)$, which is the first coordinate of $P + A\delta t\,(-\sin t, \cos t)$: the point $P$ at angle $t$, moved a distance $A\delta t$ along the unit tangent $(-\sin t, \cos t)$. The arc and the tangent agree to first order in $\delta t$ and part company after. In the drawing, $t = 90^\circ$ and $\delta t = 1.3$: the exact shadow is $-0.96A$, the naive one $-1.30A$, outside the band. The distance from the centre along the tangent is $A\sqrt{1 + (\delta t)^2}$, which is why the naive solution's envelope grows like $\sqrt{1 + (\tfrac38\varepsilon A^2t)^2}$.

The general point. An asymptotic expansion in $\varepsilon$ is a statement about $\varepsilon \to 0$ at fixed everything else. If "everything else" includes a variable ranging over an unbounded set, the expansion may be valid pointwise and fail uniformly. The failure occurs where $\varepsilon t$ is no longer small: the problem has a second time scale, $1/\varepsilon$, which the naive expansion cannot see.

How long the naive answer is good: a Grönwall bound

theorem

Let $\varepsilon \ge 0$ and $r = x - x_0 - \varepsilon x_1$. Then for $0 \le t \le T$,

$$|r(t)| \le \tfrac{3}{16}\,\varepsilon^2A^5\,\big(3T^2 + T\big)\,e^{3\varepsilon A^2T}.$$
proof, in outline

$r$ solves $\ddot r + r = -\varepsilon(x^3 - x_0^3)$ with zero data. Since $|x|, |x_0| \le A$, $|x^3 - x_0^3| \le 3A^2|x - x_0| \le 3A^2(\varepsilon|x_1| + |r|)$. Variation of parameters (unit 06) writes $r$ as an integral against $\sin(t - s)$, whose size is at most 1; then unit 01's Grönwall inequality closes the estimate.

full proof in the notes (N)

What it says. On any fixed interval the error is $O(\varepsilon^2)$: the naive expansion is asymptotic there. The bound is useful while $\varepsilon T \lesssim 1$ and useless for $T \gg 1/\varepsilon$, exactly where the table failed.

Check. $\varepsilon = 0.1$, $A = 1$, $T = 1$: bound $0.0101$; the true maximum error on $[0, 1]$ is $7.0\times10^{-4}$. Pessimistic, as Grönwall bounds are, but of the right order in $\varepsilon$.

Full proof. Write $u(t) = \sqrt{r^2 + \dot r^2}$. From $\ddot x + x = -\varepsilon x^3$, $\ddot x_0 + x_0 = 0$ and $\ddot x_1 + x_1 = -x_0^3$ we get $\ddot r + r = -\varepsilon x^3 + \varepsilon x_0^3 =: g(t)$, with $r(0) = \dot r(0) = 0$. Variation of parameters (unit 06) gives the unique solution $r(t) = \int_0^t \sin(t - s)\,g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ and $\dot r(t) = \int_0^t\cos(t - s)\,g(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$. So $(r, \dot r)$ is the integral of the vector $(\sin(t - s), \cos(t - s))\,g(s)$, whose length is $|g(s)|$, and the triangle inequality for integrals of vectors gives $u(t) \le \int_0^t |g(s)|\,\mathrm{d}s$.

Now bound $g$. $x^3 - x_0^3 = (x - x_0)(x^2 + xx_0 + x_0^2)$, and $|x| \le A$ (the previous slide) and $|x_0| \le A$, so $|g| \le 3\varepsilon A^2|x - x_0| \le 3\varepsilon A^2(\varepsilon|x_1| + u)$. From the worked example, $|x_1(s)| \le \tfrac38A^3s + \tfrac1{16}A^3$. So for $t \le T$,

$$u(t) \le \underbrace{3\varepsilon^2A^2\!\int_0^T\!\big(\tfrac38A^3s + \tfrac1{16}A^3\big)\mathrm{d}s}_{=\ \frac3{16}\varepsilon^2A^5(3T^2 + T)} + 3\varepsilon A^2\!\int_0^t u(s)\,\mathrm{d}s .$$

This is unit 01's Grönwall hypothesis on $[0, T]$ with constant $A_{\rm G} = \tfrac3{16}\varepsilon^2A^5(3T^2 + T)$ and $L = 3\varepsilon A^2$ (the first integral was extended from $[0,t]$ to $[0,T]$ to make it a constant). Hence $u(t) \le A_{\rm G}e^{3\varepsilon A^2 t}$ on $[0,T]$; put $t = T$, and $|r| \le u$. $\square$

Dimensions. In scaled variables $A$ is dimensionless; restoring units, $\varepsilon A^2$ is the dimensionless group $\beta a^2/k$ and the bound carries one power of length, as $r$ does.

The solvability condition

the solvability condition

Let $f$ be continuous and $2\pi$-periodic. The equation $u'' + u = f$ has a $2\pi$-periodic solution if and only if

$$\int_0^{2\pi} f(\tau)\cos\tau\,\mathrm{d}\tau = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \int_0^{2\pi} f(\tau)\sin\tau\,\mathrm{d}\tau = 0 .$$
only if, in one line

Multiply by $\cos\tau$ and integrate over a period; integrating by parts twice moves both derivatives onto $\cos\tau$ with no boundary terms (periodicity), and $\cos'' + \cos = 0$. Same for $\sin$. $\square$

If: the particular solution $u = \int_0^\tau \sin(\tau - s)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ from variation of parameters satisfies $u(\tau + 2\pi) - u(\tau) = \sin\tau\int_0^{2\pi}\!f\cos - \cos\tau\int_0^{2\pi}\!f\sin = 0$. Details in the notes.

In words: the forcing must have no component along the kernel of $\mathrm{d}^2/\mathrm{d}\tau^2 + 1$. A component along $\cos\tau$ or $\sin\tau$ is resonant, and produces $\tau\sin\tau$ or $\tau\cos\tau$.

Full proof. Only if. Let $u$ be a $2\pi$-periodic $C^2$ solution. Then $\int_0^{2\pi}u''\cos = [u'\cos]_0^{2\pi} + \int_0^{2\pi}u'\sin = 0 + [u\sin]_0^{2\pi} - \int_0^{2\pi}u\cos$, the bracketed terms vanishing by periodicity. So $\int_0^{2\pi}(u'' + u)\cos = 0$, which is $\int f\cos = 0$. The same computation with $\sin$.

If. Variation of parameters (unit 06) gives the solution with zero data at $0$: $u(\tau) = \int_0^\tau\sin(\tau - s)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$. Then $u(\tau + 2\pi) - u(\tau) = \int_\tau^{\tau+2\pi}\sin(\tau - s)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ (the integrand is $2\pi$-periodic in $s$ once $\tau$ is fixed, because $f$ and $\sin$ are), and expanding $\sin(\tau - s) = \sin\tau\cos s - \cos\tau\sin s$ turns this into $\sin\tau\int f\cos - \cos\tau\int f\sin$ over one period, which is $0$. The same argument for $u'(\tau) = \int_0^\tau\cos(\tau - s)f(s)\,\mathrm{d}s$ shows $u'$ is periodic too. A function with $u(\tau + 2\pi) = u(\tau)$ for all $\tau$ is periodic. $\square$

The linear-algebra reading. $\mathcal L = \mathrm{d}^2/\mathrm{d}\tau^2 + 1$ on $2\pi$-periodic functions is symmetric for the inner product $\int_0^{2\pi}fg$ (the integration by parts above), and its kernel is spanned by $\cos, \sin$. The condition says $f \perp \ker\mathcal L$ — the same statement as "$Ax = b$ is solvable iff $b \perp \ker A^{\top}$" for a matrix. It is called the Fredholm alternative in that generality; here it was proved by hand.

Worked: Poincaré–Lindstedt for Duffing

worked example 3 — the Poincaré–Lindstedt method on the running example

Step 1 — strain the time. The motion is periodic with an unknown frequency $\omega(\varepsilon)$. Put $\tau = \omega t$, so $x$ is $2\pi$-periodic in $\tau$, and $\omega^2x'' + x + \varepsilon x^3 = 0$.

Step 2 — expand both. $x = x_0(\tau) + \varepsilon x_1(\tau) + \cdots$, $\ \omega = 1 + \varepsilon\omega_1 + \cdots$, so $\omega^2 = 1 + 2\varepsilon\omega_1 + O(\varepsilon^2)$.

Step 3 — order $\varepsilon^0$. $x_0'' + x_0 = 0$, $x_0 = A\cos\tau$.

Step 4 — order $\varepsilon^1$.

$$\begin{aligned}x_1'' + x_1 &= -2\omega_1x_0'' - x_0^3\\ &= \big(2\omega_1A - \tfrac34A^3\big)\cos\tau - \tfrac14A^3\cos3\tau .\end{aligned}$$

Step 5 — demand periodicity. The solvability condition: the $\cos\tau$ coefficient must vanish (the $\sin\tau$ one already does).

$$\boxed{\ \omega_1 = \tfrac38A^2,\qquad \omega = 1 + \tfrac38\varepsilon A^2 + O(\varepsilon^2)\ }$$

Step 6 — the correction. $x_1 = \tfrac1{32}A^3(\cos3\tau - \cos\tau)$, fixing $x_1(0) = 0$. No secular term.

Step 7 — undo the straining. $x \approx A\cos\omega t + \tfrac{\varepsilon A^3}{32}(\cos3\omega t - \cos\omega t)$ — bounded, periodic, and uniformly valid on times of order $1/\varepsilon$.

Step 4, the right-hand side. Substituting into $\omega^2x'' + x + \varepsilon x^3 = 0$ and collecting order $\varepsilon$: $x_1'' + 2\omega_1x_0'' + x_1 + x_0^3 = 0$. With $x_0'' = -A\cos\tau$ and the cube identity, $-2\omega_1x_0'' - x_0^3 = 2\omega_1A\cos\tau - \tfrac34A^3\cos\tau - \tfrac14A^3\cos 3\tau$.

Step 6. Particular solution $\tfrac1{32}A^3\cos3\tau$ (as in worked example 2), plus $d\cos\tau + e\sin\tau$. $x_1'(0) = 0$ gives $e = 0$, $x_1(0) = 0$ gives $d = -\tfrac1{32}A^3$.

Why this works and the naive method did not. The naive method insisted the frequency was 1 at every order and then had to express a frequency shift as a polynomial in $t$ times trigonometric functions. Poincaré–Lindstedt gives the frequency its own expansion, and uses the one freedom this introduces, $\omega_1$, to satisfy the one condition — solvability — that periodicity imposes. At every order there is exactly one new unknown $\omega_n$ and one condition, which is why the method can be continued indefinitely; the problem sheet does order two and gets $\omega_2 = -\tfrac{21}{256}A^4$.

When it applies. Only when the true solution is periodic — here guaranteed by the closed energy level curves of the previous section. For a damped or driven oscillator whose amplitude drifts there is no single frequency to strain, and the method has nothing to hold on to. That is what multiple scales is for.

History. Lindstedt (1882) introduced the device for the three-body problem; Poincaré (Les méthodes nouvelles de la mécanique céleste, 1892–99) proved the resulting series are asymptotic — and that for the three-body problem they generally diverge.

21 · removing secular terms

Multiple scales

the method of multiple scales

Pretend the solution depends on two times, a fast $t$ and a slow $T = \varepsilon t$, independently: $x(t) = X(t, \varepsilon t)$. By the chain rule (unit 03)

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t} = \partial_t + \varepsilon\,\partial_T,$$ $$\frac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d}t^2} = \partial_t^2 + 2\varepsilon\,\partial_t\partial_T + \varepsilon^2\partial_T^2 .$$

Expand $X = X_0 + \varepsilon X_1 + \cdots$ and use the new freedom — how $X_0$ depends on $T$ — to kill secular terms.

On Duffing. Order 1: $X_0 = a(T)\cos\big(t + \phi(T)\big)$ — amplitude and phase are free to drift. Order $\varepsilon$:

$$\begin{aligned}\partial_t^2X_1 + X_1 = {}&2a'\sin\psi + \big(2a\phi' - \tfrac34a^3\big)\cos\psi\\ &- \tfrac14a^3\cos3\psi,\end{aligned}$$

with $\psi = t + \phi$. No secular term iff the amplitude equations hold:

$$a' = 0,\qquad \phi' = \tfrac38a^2 .$$

So $x \approx A\cos\big((1 + \tfrac38\varepsilon A^2)t\big)$ — the same answer, with no assumption of periodicity.

Order $\varepsilon$, in full. Substituting, the $\varepsilon^1$ terms are $\partial_t^2X_1 + X_1 + 2\partial_t\partial_TX_0 + X_0^3 = 0$. With $X_0 = a\cos\psi$, $\partial_tX_0 = -a\sin\psi$ and $\partial_T\partial_tX_0 = -a'\sin\psi - a\phi'\cos\psi$. So $-2\partial_t\partial_TX_0 = 2a'\sin\psi + 2a\phi'\cos\psi$, and $-X_0^3 = -\tfrac34a^3\cos\psi - \tfrac14a^3\cos3\psi$. For $X_1$ to stay bounded in $t$ at fixed $T$, the solvability condition kills the $\sin\psi$ and $\cos\psi$ coefficients: $a' = 0$ and $\phi' = \tfrac38a^2$. With $a(0) = A$, $\phi(0) = 0$: $\phi = \tfrac38A^2T = \tfrac38\varepsilon A^2t$.

Is "pretend there are two independent times" legitimate? As a derivation, no: $x$ is a function of one variable. As a way of generating an ansatz, yes — and the ansatz is then justified the way the naive one was, by a Grönwall estimate on the remainder, which now holds on intervals of length $O(1/\varepsilon)$ (cited: Kevorkian and Cole, Multiple Scale and Singular Perturbation Methods, the chapter on multiple-scale expansions for ordinary differential equations; or Verhulst, Nonlinear Differential Equations and Dynamical Systems, the chapter on averaging, where the equivalent averaging method is proved in full).

21 · removing secular terms

Secular terms are a boundary layer in time

Look at what the three methods did to Duffing:

  • naive: one time variable $t$; valid for $t = O(1)$;
  • Poincaré–Lindstedt: one strained variable $\omega t$; valid for $t = O(1/\varepsilon)$ provided the motion is periodic;
  • multiple scales: two variables $t$ and $\varepsilon t$; valid for $t = O(1/\varepsilon)$ in general.

The naive expansion is good on the "inner" time scale and fails on the "outer" one, $T = \varepsilon t = O(1)$. Multiple scales writes down an expansion that is right on both at once.

the unifying idea

Every non-uniformity has two scales, and a correct approximation respects both. Secular growth is two time scales. The next section's boundary layer is two length scales. WKB is a fast phase and a slow amplitude.

choosing a method

Periodic solution, want its frequency: Poincaré–Lindstedt, fastest. Drifting amplitude, damping, transients, slow variation of a parameter: multiple scales. Thin region of rapid change in space: matched expansions.

The analogy made exact. In section 5 we shall have an "inner" variable $X = x/\varepsilon$ near a boundary and an "outer" variable $x$. Here, the roles are: the fast time $t$ plays the inner variable, the slow time $T = \varepsilon t$ plays the outer, and the "boundary" is $t = 0$. The naive expansion is an inner expansion. The amplitude equations for $a(T)$, $\phi(T)$ are the outer equations, and multiple scales solves them simultaneously rather than matching them afterwards. Some texts do treat secular problems by matched expansions in time; the answers agree.

An honest limit. Two scales are enough for first order. At second order new secular terms can appear on the time scale $1/\varepsilon^2$, and a third time $\varepsilon^2t$ is needed. The pattern continues, and for Hamiltonian systems with more than one degree of freedom it can break down entirely — resonances between frequencies produce small divisors, which is the door into unit 17's chaos, not opened here.

21 · boundary layers

A small parameter on the highest derivative

the model problem $$\varepsilon y'' + (1 + x)\,y' + y = 0$$

on $[0, 1]$, with $y(0) = 0$ and $y(1) = 1$.

Set $\varepsilon = 0$: $(1 + x)y' + y = 0$, i.e. $\big((1 + x)y\big)' = 0$, so $y = C/(1 + x)$. A first-order equation takes one boundary condition. $y(1) = 1$ gives $C = 2$; then $y(0) = 2 \ne 0$.

The reduced problem cannot meet both conditions. Near one end, $y$ must change by $O(1)$ over a short distance, where $\varepsilon y''$ is not small: a boundary layer.

this one is exactly solvable

$\varepsilon y'' + (1 + x)y' + y = \big(\varepsilon y' + (1 + x)y\big)'$, so $\varepsilon y' + (1+x)y = K$, constant. A first-order linear equation:

$$y(x) = \frac K\varepsilon\int_0^x e^{[(1+s)^2 - (1+x)^2]/2\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}s ,$$

with $K$ fixed by $y(1) = 1$. We shall not need it to find the answer — only to check it.

Physics: Prandtl, 1904. Viscosity multiplies the highest derivative in the equation of fluid motion, and the flow past a wing is inviscid except in a thin layer at its surface — where the drag is decided.

Checking the exact solution. Integrating factor for $\varepsilon y' + (1+x)y = K$: multiply by $\varepsilon^{-1}e^{(1+x)^2/2\varepsilon}$, getting $\big(e^{(1+x)^2/2\varepsilon}y\big)' = (K/\varepsilon)e^{(1+x)^2/2\varepsilon}$; integrate from $0$ using $y(0) = 0$. That is the formula. At $\varepsilon = 0.05$, $K = 1.97433$; at $\varepsilon = 0.01$, $K = 1.99497$. Note that $K = (1+x)y + \varepsilon y'$ is exactly the combination the outer solution makes constant, and the outer value is $K = 2$: the exact constant tends to the outer one as $\varepsilon \to 0$. That is already a first matching statement.

Why the layer is not at $x = 1$. Deferred to "Where the layer goes"; the short version is that a layer at $x = 1$ would have to grow exponentially into the interior.

Prandtl. The Navier–Stokes equations of viscous flow have viscosity multiplying the second derivatives of the velocity, the highest derivatives in them. Setting viscosity to zero gives Euler's equations, which cannot satisfy no-slip at a wall and predict zero drag on any body (d'Alembert's paradox, 1752). Prandtl's 1904 paper, eight pages long, resolved the paradox with exactly this section's idea. Van Dyke later made the method systematic, and the matching rule two slides on is his.

Inner and outer solutions

outer solution

The expansion valid away from the layer, obeying the boundary condition the reduced equation can keep: $y_{\rm out} = 2/(1 + x)$.

inner solution

Stretch the variable near $x = 0$: $x = \varepsilon^\alpha X$. The terms $\varepsilon y''$, $(1+x)y'$, $y$ become of sizes $\varepsilon^{1 - 2\alpha}$, $\varepsilon^{-\alpha}$, $1$. The balance that keeps $y''$ is $\alpha = 1$ — the distinguished limit. With $Y(X) = y(\varepsilon X)$:

$$Y'' + (1 + \varepsilon X)Y' + \varepsilon Y = 0,$$

so at leading order $Y_0'' + Y_0' = 0$.

With $Y_0(0) = 0$: $Y_0 = C\,(1 - e^{-X})$. One constant is still unknown.

$\varepsilon = 0.05$: the exact solution (solid) and the composite (short dashes) climb steeply through the shaded layer, then join the outer solution $2/(1+x)$ (long dashes) — right away from $x = 0$, wrong in the layer.

The inner equation, in full. With $x = \varepsilon X$ and $Y(X) = y(\varepsilon X)$, the chain rule gives $y' = \varepsilon^{-1}Y'$ and $y'' = \varepsilon^{-2}Y''$. Substituting: $\varepsilon\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}Y'' + (1 + \varepsilon X)\varepsilon^{-1}Y' + Y = 0$; multiply by $\varepsilon$. At leading order the $\varepsilon XY'$ and $\varepsilon Y$ terms drop, leaving $Y_0'' + Y_0' = 0$ with general solution $C_1 + C_2e^{-X}$, and $Y_0(0) = 0$ forces $C_1 = -C_2$.

Dominant balance, once more. The other balances: $\alpha = 0$ ($y'$ against $y$) is the outer problem; $\varepsilon^{1-2\alpha} = 1$ gives $\alpha = \tfrac12$, but then $y'$, of size $\varepsilon^{-1/2}$, is larger than both — inconsistent. So there are exactly two regions, and the layer thickness is $\varepsilon$, not $\sqrt\varepsilon$. When the coefficient of $y'$ vanishes at the boundary the balance changes and so does the thickness; the problem sheet's problems all have a non-vanishing coefficient.

Matching: the intermediate variable and Van Dyke's rule

The inner solution must turn into the outer one somewhere in between. Matching makes that precise, in either of two ways.

through an intermediate variable

Put $x = \varepsilon^\beta\xi$ with $0 \lt \beta \lt 1$ and $\xi$ fixed, so $\varepsilon \ll x \ll 1$. Then

$$y_{\rm out} = \frac2{1 + \varepsilon^\beta\xi} \to 2,$$ $$Y_0 = C\big(1 - e^{-\varepsilon^{\beta-1}\xi}\big) \to C .$$

Both are approximations to the same $y$ in the overlap, so $C = 2$.

Van Dyke's matching rule

The $m$-term inner expansion of the $n$-term outer solution equals the $n$-term outer expansion of the $m$-term inner solution.

With $m = n = 1$: the outer $2/(1 + \varepsilon X)$ in inner variables, to one term, is $2$. The inner $C(1 - e^{-x/\varepsilon})$ in outer variables, to one term, is $C$ (the exponential is beyond all orders). So $C = 2$ again.

a rule, not a theorem

Van Dyke's rule is a procedure that works for power-law expansions and can fail when logarithms appear. The intermediate-variable argument is the one to trust when they disagree.

Why the overlap exists. The outer expansion is valid for $x \gg \varepsilon$ (away from the layer) and the inner for $x \ll 1$ (inside the region where $1 + x \approx 1$). An intermediate variable sits in both. The matching principle — that the two expansions agree there to the order both are valid — is the hypothesis of the method. It can be proved for a given problem by comparing with the exact solution or by a maximum-principle estimate, but in general it is an assumption justified afterwards. For the model problem the exact solution is available, and the next slide checks it.

Van Dyke's rule with logarithms. The standard failures involve terms like $\varepsilon\ln\varepsilon$, which do not sort cleanly into "orders"; the fix is to count $\ln\varepsilon$ with the power it multiplies. Cited: Hinch, Perturbation Methods (CUP, 1991), the chapter on matched asymptotic expansions, which gives both the rule and its failures with examples; Van Dyke, Perturbation Methods in Fluid Mechanics (1964/1975), for the original.

Higher orders. With $m = n = 2$ the rule matches the $O(\varepsilon)$ corrections as well and yields a composite with $O(\varepsilon^2)$ error. The procedure is mechanical; the bookkeeping is the whole difficulty. One order is enough to see the method.

Where the layer goes

the sign of the first-derivative term decides

For $\varepsilon y'' + a(x)y' + b(x)y = 0$ with $a$ of one sign on $[0,1]$: at a layer at $x_0$, the inner equation is $Y'' \pm a(x_0)Y' = 0$, with solutions $1$ and $e^{\mp a(x_0)X}$. The layer must decay into the interior.

  • $a \gt 0$: layer at the left end, $x = 0$.
  • $a \lt 0$: layer at the right end, $x = 1$.
  • $a$ changes sign: interior layers or turning points — a subject of its own, named here.

Test it. A layer at $x = 1$ for the model problem: $X = (1 - x)/\varepsilon$ gives $Y'' - 2Y' = 0$, solutions $1$ and $e^{2X}$. The second grows as you leave the wall — it cannot match any outer solution. No layer there.

21 · WKB

The WKB approximation

$\varepsilon^2y'' + q(x)\,y = 0$ with $q \gt 0$: an oscillator whose "stiffness" varies slowly compared with the oscillation. $\varepsilon$ is on the highest derivative — singular — but there is no layer: the solution oscillates on the short scale $\varepsilon$ everywhere.

Try $y = e^{S(x)/\varepsilon}$ with $S = S_0 + \varepsilon S_1 + \cdots$ complex. Then $S'^2 + \varepsilon S'' + q = 0$, and

$$\begin{aligned}\varepsilon^0:&\quad S_0'^2 = -q,\\ \varepsilon^1:&\quad 2S_0'S_1' + S_0'' = 0.\end{aligned}$$
the WKB approximation

$S_0 = \pm i\int\sqrt q$, $\ S_1 = -\tfrac14\ln q$, so

$$y \approx q^{-1/4}\big[C_1\cos\Phi(x) + C_2\sin\Phi(x)\big],$$ $$\Phi(x) = \frac1\varepsilon\int^x\!\sqrt{q(s)}\,\mathrm{d}s .$$

Check: $q$ constant gives $\cos(\sqrt q\,x/\varepsilon)$, the exact solution ✓. Dimensions: with $\varepsilon$ a length and $q$ dimensionless, the phase $\int\sqrt q\,\mathrm{d}x/\varepsilon$ is a pure number ✓.

Names: the eikonal equation fixes the phase, the transport equation the amplitude. If you have done unit 15, $S_0'^2 = -q$ is its eikonal again, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation of a particle whose momentum is $\sqrt q$.

The derivation in full. $y = e^{S/\varepsilon}$ gives $y' = (S'/\varepsilon)y$ and $y'' = (S''/\varepsilon + S'^2/\varepsilon^2)y$. So $\varepsilon^2y'' + qy = (\varepsilon S'' + S'^2 + q)y = 0$. With $S = S_0 + \varepsilon S_1 + O(\varepsilon^2)$: $S'^2 = S_0'^2 + 2\varepsilon S_0'S_1' + O(\varepsilon^2)$, and $\varepsilon S'' = \varepsilon S_0'' + O(\varepsilon^2)$. Order 1: $S_0'^2 + q = 0$, so $S_0' = \pm i\sqrt q$. Order $\varepsilon$: $2S_0'S_1' + S_0'' = 0$. Since $S_0' = \pm i\sqrt q$, the ratio $S_0''/S_0' = (\sqrt q)'/\sqrt q$ is real, so $S_1' = -(\sqrt q)'/2\sqrt q$ and $S_1 = -\tfrac12\ln\sqrt q + \text{const} = -\tfrac14\ln q + \text{const}$. Hence $e^{S/\varepsilon} \approx q^{-1/4}\exp(\pm\tfrac{i}{\varepsilon}\int\sqrt q)$, and the two signs combine (unit 06: the complex exponential as bookkeeping) into the real cos/sin form.

Why $q^{-1/4}$, a second way. The equation has no $y'$ term, so by Abel's identity (unit 06) the Wronskian of any two solutions is constant. For the pair $y_\pm = \mathcal A\,e^{\pm i\Phi}$ with slowly varying amplitude $\mathcal A$, the Wronskian is $y_+y_-' - y_-y_+' = -2i\mathcal A^2\Phi' + (\text{terms in }\mathcal A\mathcal A') $, and the $\mathcal A\mathcal A'$ terms cancel exactly, leaving $-2i\mathcal A^2\sqrt q/\varepsilon$. Constancy, to the accuracy with which the WKB pair solves the equation, forces $\mathcal A^2\sqrt q$ constant: amplitude squared times local wavenumber is conserved, which is $\mathcal A \propto q^{-1/4}$ — and, in time, the adiabatic invariant of the next slide.

The names. WKB is Wentzel, Kramers and Brillouin (1926), who used it on the Schrödinger equation; Jeffreys had it in 1924; Liouville and Green had it in 1837, and "Liouville–Green approximation" is the name careful mathematicians use. Unit 15 remark: writing a wave's phase as $S/\varepsilon$ and finding a Hamilton–Jacobi equation for $S$ at leading order is the "mechanics was optics all along" of unit 15, run backwards. Nothing in this unit depends on it.

21 · WKB

When WKB is valid, and an adiabatic invariant

validity condition

The expansion is consistent while the correction is small beside the leading term: $|\varepsilon S_1'| \ll |S_0'|$, that is

$$\frac{\varepsilon\,|q'|}{4\,q^{3/2}} \ll 1 .$$

In words: the wavelength $2\pi\varepsilon/\sqrt q$ changes by a small fraction of itself over one wavelength. It fails where $q \to 0$.

In time: $\ddot x + \omega(\varepsilon t)^2x = 0$ is the same equation ($s = \varepsilon t$, $q = \omega^2$). WKB says $x \approx a\cos\int\omega\,\mathrm{d}t$ with amplitude $a \propto \omega^{-1/2}$, so the energy $E \approx \tfrac12\omega^2a^2$ is proportional to $\omega$:

$$E/\omega \approx \text{const}.$$

Slowly doubling the frequency doubles the energy: the next slide shows it happen.

This adiabatic invariant is the Duffing lesson again: the multiple-scales amplitude equation for a slowly varying $\omega$ is $(\omega a^2)' = 0$ — the same law, from the same solvability condition.

The validity condition, derived. $S_0' = i\sqrt q$ and $S_1' = -q'/4q$. The ratio of the second term to the first in $S'/\varepsilon$ is $\varepsilon|S_1'|/|S_0'| = \varepsilon|q'|/4q^{3/2}$. This is a consistency condition: necessary for the expansion to make sense, not by itself a bound on the error. Rigorous error bounds for the Liouville–Green approximation, in terms of the total variation of a control function, are cited: Olver, Asymptotics and Special Functions, ch. 6.

The multiple-scales route, sketched in full. Let the fast phase $\theta$ satisfy $\dot\theta = \omega(T)$, $T = \varepsilon t$, and $x = X(\theta, T)$. Then $\ddot x = \omega^2X_{\theta\theta} + \varepsilon(2\omega X_{\theta T} + \omega'X_\theta) + O(\varepsilon^2)$, and the equation becomes $\omega^2(X_{\theta\theta} + X) + \varepsilon(2\omega X_{\theta T} + \omega'X_\theta) = 0$. Order 1: $X_0 = a(T)\cos(\theta + \phi(T))$. Order $\varepsilon$: the $\sin$ coefficient of the forcing is $2\omega a' + \omega'a$, and solvability sets it to zero: $(\omega a^2)' = 0$. The energy is $\tfrac12\omega^2a^2$, so $E/\omega = \tfrac12\omega a^2$ is constant. The same as WKB's $a\propto\omega^{-1/2}$.

History. At the 1911 Solvay conference Lorentz asked what happens to a pendulum whose string is slowly shortened; Einstein answered that $E/\nu$ stays constant. The question mattered because Planck's quanta have $E = h\nu$: only an adiabatic invariant could be quantised consistently. For a real pendulum the equation is not quite $\ddot\theta + (g/l)\theta = 0$, because shortening the string also changes the angular momentum; the problem sheet's WKB problem uses a cleaner setting.

21 · WKB

Turning points and the connection formulae

Where $q$ changes sign, at a turning point $x = a$, the approximation fails: $q^{-1/4}$ blows up and the validity ratio $\varepsilon|q'|/4q^{3/2}$ does too. On one side the solution oscillates; on the other it grows or decays like $|q|^{-1/4}e^{\pm\frac1\varepsilon\int\sqrt{|q|}}$.

Near $a$, $q \approx q'(a)(x - a)$, and the local equation is Airy's, $y'' = zy$ in a stretched variable $z$ — a boundary layer of width $\varepsilon^{2/3}$.

the connection formulae (cited)

Let $q \gt 0$ for $x \lt a$, $q \lt 0$ for $x \gt a$, with $q'(a) \ne 0$. The solution that decays for $x \gt a$,

$$y \approx |q|^{-1/4}\exp\Big(-\frac1\varepsilon\int_a^x\!\sqrt{|q|}\Big),$$

is, for $x \lt a$,

$$y \approx 2\,q^{-1/4}\cos\Big(\frac1\varepsilon\int_x^a\!\sqrt q - \frac\pi4\Big).$$

Proof: match both sides to the Airy function through its asymptotics, which need unit 20's integral methods. Bender and Orszag, ch. 10.

What is stated and what is not. The formula is cited, not proved: the proof needs the large-argument behaviour of the Airy function, which comes from its integral representation by the method of steepest descent (unit 20). What can be checked here: both sides are WKB solutions on their own side (by the previous slides), and the width of the turning-point region follows from dominant balance. With $q \approx -q_1(x - a)$, $q_1 = -q'(a) \gt 0$, put $x - a = \varepsilon^\gamma z$: the terms $\varepsilon^2y''$ and $q y$ have sizes $\varepsilon^{2 - 2\gamma}$ and $\varepsilon^\gamma$, balanced when $\gamma = 2/3$. So the inner region has width $\varepsilon^{2/3}$, and the inner equation is $y_{zz} = q_1 z\,y$, Airy's up to a rescaling of $z$.

The direction warning. The formula is safe in the direction written: from the decaying solution to the oscillatory one. Read backwards it is unstable, because a tiny error in the phase of the cosine introduces a growing exponential on the other side that swamps the decaying one.

A consequence, named. Between two turning points $a \lt b$, a solution decaying on both outer sides exists only if the two cosines agree, which forces $\frac1\varepsilon\int_a^b\sqrt q = (n + \tfrac12)\pi$. In quantum mechanics, with $\varepsilon = \hbar$ and $q = 2m(E - V)$, this is the Bohr–Sommerfeld rule with the $\tfrac12$ that the old quantum theory missed. For $V = \tfrac12x^2$ (units $m = \hbar = 1$) it gives $E = n + \tfrac12$ — exact, a coincidence special to the harmonic oscillator. Named; not used again before quantum mechanics, which this course does not reach.

Reading. Bender and Orszag ch. 10 up to (not including) the Airy-function asymptotics, which use unit 20's methods; then return after unit 20.

$\varepsilon^2y'' + q\,y = 0$ with $q = a - x$, $\varepsilon = 0.1$: exact (an Airy function) against the two WKB forms of the connection formula, decaying for $x \gt a$ and $2q^{-1/4}\cos(\cdots - \pi/4)$ for $x \lt a$ (same constant).

Away from $a$, each WKB form lies on the exact curve: oscillation where $q \gt 0$, with wavelength $2\pi\varepsilon/\sqrt q$ and amplitude $q^{-1/4}$ both growing towards $a$; decay where $q \lt 0$.

Near $a$, inside the strip of width $\sim\varepsilon^{2/3}$, both blow up like $|x - a|^{-1/4}$ while the exact solution passes smoothly through. That strip is the turning point's boundary layer.

The connection formula is the statement that the two sides belong to one solution — the factor $2$ and the phase $-\pi/4$ are what make them line up.

How the figure was computed. With $q = a - x$, the equation $\varepsilon^2y'' = (x - a)y$ is solved exactly by $y = \mathrm{Ai}\big((x - a)/\varepsilon^{2/3}\big)$, the Airy function that decays to the right, evaluated here by a library routine. The WKB curves are the two sides of the connection formula, multiplied by the one constant $\varepsilon^{1/6}/2\sqrt\pi$ that matches the decaying side to the Airy function's known large-$x$ behaviour (cited, as on the previous slide). With that single constant the oscillatory side lines up too, which is the connection formula's content, seen rather than proved. The shaded strip is $|x - a| \lt \varepsilon^{2/3} = 0.215$.

21 · the orbit

The relativistic orbit equation, nondimensionalised

Unit 08's Binet equation for the inverse-square law, with $u = 1/r$ and $h = r^2\dot\theta = L/\mu$:

$$u'' + u = \frac{GM}{h^2} .$$

General relativity (cited: unit 37 derives it from the Schwarzschild geodesics) adds one term:

$$u'' + u = \frac{GM}{h^2} + \frac{3GM}{c^2}\,u^2 .$$

Nondimensionalise with the Newtonian semi-latus rectum $p = h^2/GM$: put $w = pu$.

$$\boxed{\ w'' + w = 1 + \varepsilon w^2,\qquad \varepsilon = \frac{3GM}{c^2p}\ }$$
the size of the small parameter

$GM/c^2$ is a length: $1.476$ km for the Sun (half its Schwarzschild radius $2.953$ km). For Mercury, $p = a(1 - e^2) = 5.5460\times10^{10}$ m, so

$$\varepsilon = 7.987\times10^{-8}.$$

Classify it. $\varepsilon$ multiplies $w^2$, not $w''$: regular in the equation, like Duffing. And $\theta$ runs over $[0, \infty)$ as the planet goes round: potentially singular in the domain, like Duffing.

At $\varepsilon = 0$: $w_0 = 1 + e\cos\theta$, unit 08's ellipse, perihelion at $\theta = 0$.

The scaling, line by line. Multiply $u'' + u = GM/h^2 + (3GM/c^2)u^2$ by $p = h^2/GM$: $(pu)'' + pu = 1 + (3GM/c^2)\,p\,u^2 = 1 + \frac{3GM}{c^2p}(pu)^2$. So $w = pu$ obeys the boxed equation.

Dimensions. $[GM] = \mathsf{L^3T^{-2}}$ and $[c^2] = \mathsf{L^2T^{-2}}$, so $[GM/c^2] = \mathsf L$ and $\varepsilon$ is a ratio of lengths ✓. Limits. $c \to \infty$ gives $\varepsilon \to 0$ and Newton back ✓; a wider orbit (larger $p$) gives smaller $\varepsilon$ — relativistic effects are strongest close in ✓. Equivalently $\varepsilon = 3(GM/hc)^2$: three times the square of the orbital speed scale $GM/h$ over $c$. For Mercury $GM/hc = 1.632\times10^{-4}$.

The numbers. $GM_\odot = 1.32712440018\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$, $c = 299\,792\,458$ m/s, Mercury's $a = 5.7909050\times10^{10}$ m and $e = 0.205630$. Then $p = 5.54604\times10^{10}$ m and $\varepsilon = 3\times1476.6/5.54604\times10^{10} = 7.9875\times10^{-8}$.

What is being assumed. The equation itself. This unit's job is not to derive it — that needs the Schwarzschild metric of unit 36 — but to say, correctly, what its solution is. Everything below is unit 21's mathematics applied to a given ODE.

worked example 5 — expand the relativistic orbit naively

Step 1 — hierarchy. $w = w_0 + \varepsilon w_1 + \cdots$: $\ w_0'' + w_0 = 1$, and $w_1'' + w_1 = w_0^2$.

Step 2 — order zero. $w_0 = 1 + e\cos\theta$.

Step 3 — the forcing. With $\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 + \cos2\theta)$:

$$w_0^2 = \big(1 + \tfrac12e^2\big) + 2e\cos\theta + \tfrac12e^2\cos2\theta .$$

Step 4 — solve term by term. Constant: $1 + \tfrac12e^2$. $\cos2\theta$: $\tfrac{1}{1-4}\cdot\tfrac12e^2 = -\tfrac16e^2$. $\cos\theta$: resonant, and $L(\theta\sin\theta) = 2\cos\theta$ gives $e\,\theta\sin\theta$.

$$w_1 = 1 + \tfrac12e^2 - \tfrac16e^2\cos2\theta + \underbrace{e\,\theta\sin\theta}_{\text{secular}}$$

(plus any homogeneous solution, which only re-labels $e$ and the perihelion direction).

Step 5 — check. $L\big(e\,\theta\sin\theta\big) = 2e\cos\theta$ ✓; $L\big(-\tfrac16e^2\cos2\theta\big) = -\tfrac16e^2(-4 + 1)\cos2\theta = \tfrac12e^2\cos2\theta$ ✓.

Step 6 — the same structure as Duffing. The constant and $\cos2\theta$ pieces change the orbit's shape by a bounded, tiny amount. The secular piece is worked example 2's term again, in $\theta$ instead of $t$.

Step 1. Substituting and collecting: order 1, $w_0'' + w_0 = 1$; order $\varepsilon$, $w_1'' + w_1 = w_0^2$. The general solution of the first is $1 + e\cos(\theta - \theta_0)$; choosing the axis so that perihelion (largest $w$, smallest $r$) is at $\theta = 0$ gives $\theta_0 = 0$.

Step 3. $(1 + e\cos\theta)^2 = 1 + 2e\cos\theta + e^2\cos^2\theta = 1 + 2e\cos\theta + \tfrac12e^2 + \tfrac12e^2\cos2\theta$.

Step 4, the $\cos2\theta$ piece. Try $c\cos2\theta$: $L(c\cos2\theta) = (-4 + 1)c\cos2\theta = -3c\cos2\theta$; set equal to $\tfrac12e^2\cos2\theta$: $c = -\tfrac16e^2$.

Mercury's non-secular corrections. $\varepsilon(1 + \tfrac12e^2) = 8.16\times10^{-8}$: a fractional change in the orbit's size of that order, far below anything measurable against the uncertainty in $p$ itself. The periodic $\cos2\theta$ piece is smaller still. Only the secular piece accumulates — which is why it is the only one anybody measures.

Reading the secular term: the perihelion advances

Exactly as for Duffing, $\cos\big((1 - \varepsilon)\theta\big) = \cos\theta + \varepsilon\theta\sin\theta + O(\varepsilon^2\theta^2)$. So, to this order,

$$w \approx 1 + e\cos\big((1 - \varepsilon)\theta\big) + (\text{bounded, }O(\varepsilon)) .$$

Perihelion — largest $w$ — is where $(1 - \varepsilon)\theta = 2\pi k$, at $\theta = 2\pi k/(1 - \varepsilon)$. Each orbit the perihelion's angle $\varpi$ moves on by

$$\boxed{\ \Delta\varpi = 2\pi\varepsilon = \frac{6\pi GM}{c^2\,a(1 - e^2)}\ \text{per orbit}\ }$$

Poincaré–Lindstedt, with $\varphi = \nu\theta$, gives $\nu = 1 - \varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2)$: the same $\Delta\varpi$, uniformly valid.

Three turns of $w'' + w = 1 + \varepsilon w^2$, integrated numerically, with $e = 0.5$ and $\varepsilon = 0.05$ — exaggerated six hundred thousand times over Mercury's. Successive perihelia (1–4) step round by $\Delta\varpi$ each orbit; the Newtonian ellipse (dashed) closes.

Poincaré–Lindstedt on the orbit, in full. Put $\varphi = \nu\theta$, $\nu = 1 + \varepsilon\nu_1 + \cdots$, so $\nu^2w_{\varphi\varphi} + w = 1 + \varepsilon w^2$ with $w$ $2\pi$-periodic in $\varphi$. Order 1: $w_0 = 1 + e\cos\varphi$. Order $\varepsilon$: $w_1'' + w_1 = w_0^2 - 2\nu_1w_0'' = \dots + (2e + 2\nu_1e)\cos\varphi + \dots$ Solvability: $\nu_1 = -1$. So the radial oscillation has frequency $\nu = 1 - \varepsilon$ in $\theta$; the orbit returns to perihelion after $\Delta\theta = 2\pi/\nu = 2\pi(1 + \varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2))$.

The figure. At $\varepsilon = 0.05$ the measured step between perihelia is $0.362$ rad $= 20.7^\circ$, against $2\pi\varepsilon = 0.314$ rad: $15\%$ more, because at so large an $\varepsilon$ the next order is visible (the next slide shows that excess growing in proportion to $\varepsilon$). The advance is in the direction of motion — the planet goes round counterclockwise here, and so does the perihelion. Distances are $r/p$ with the Sun at the origin.

Mercury: 43 seconds of arc a century

Mercury

$2\pi\varepsilon = 5.019\times10^{-7}$ rad $= 0.1035''$ per orbit. With a period of $87.969$ days, $415.20$ orbits per century:

checks

Dimensionless ✓. $c \to \infty$: no advance ✓. Numerically, integrating $w'' + w = 1 + \varepsilon w^2$ at $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$, $e = 0.2$: advance per orbit $0.0062990$ against $2\pi\varepsilon = 0.0062832$ — agreement to $0.25\% \approx 2.5\varepsilon$, the size of the next order ✓.

How wrong $2\pi\varepsilon$ is: the integrated advance over $2\pi\varepsilon$, minus 1. The excess grows like $2.5\varepsilon$ — the next order; the ring is the check in the box, $0.25\%$ at $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$. For Mercury it is $2\times10^{-7}$ of the $43''$.

Mercury's numbers. $2\pi\times7.9875\times10^{-8} = 5.0187\times10^{-7}$ rad $= 0.10352''$; the sidereal period $87.9691$ d gives $36525/87.9691 = 415.203$ orbits per Julian century, and $415.203\times0.10352'' = 42.98''$.

The numerical check. Integrated from $w = 1.2$, $w' = 0$ with relative tolerance $10^{-12}$, locating successive maxima of $w$. At $\varepsilon = 10^{-2}$ the advance is $0.064461$ against $2\pi\varepsilon = 0.062832$, $2.6\%$ high — ten times the discrepancy at $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$, so the relative error is $O(\varepsilon)$ as it should be. History. Le Verrier announced the anomaly in 1859 as $38''$ per century; Newcomb's 1882 re-analysis put it at $43''$. Proposed explanations — an unseen planet (Vulcan), a dust ring, a modified inverse-square law — each failed another test. Einstein's November 1915 calculation — a first-order expansion in the same small parameter, though he evaluated the orbit integral rather than this differential equation — gave $43''$ with no adjustable parameter. He wrote that he was beside himself with joy for days.

In both, the secular term is a frequency shift written badly. "Removing" it means re-writing it correctly — never discarding it.

why it must be kept

A Newtonian bound orbit closes: $w$ is $2\pi$-periodic in $\theta$. Demand that of $w_1$ and the solvability condition requires the $\cos\theta$ coefficient of $w_0^2$, which is $2e$, to vanish — false for any non-circular orbit. The equation forbids a closed orbit. The only way to satisfy it is to let the radial frequency differ from 1, and that difference is the precession.

and the naive form is enough

The naive series fails when $\varepsilon\theta \sim 1$: for Mercury, after $2.0$ million orbits, $480\,000$ years. Observations span centuries. Over them, $\varepsilon e\,\theta\sin\theta$ is uniformly accurate — and its coefficient is the $43''$.

The distinction, stated once and carefully. The phrase "remove secular terms" is misleading and is the source of the confusion this section exists to clear up. What Poincaré–Lindstedt and multiple scales remove is the non-uniformity: the false algebraic growth that comes from Taylor-expanding $\cos((1 + \delta)t)$ in $\delta$. What they keep, and compute, is the frequency shift $\delta$ that the secular term's coefficient encodes. For Duffing that shift is interesting but nobody measures the accumulated phase; for the orbit the accumulated phase is the longitude of perihelion, and astronomers have measured it for centuries. Discarding the secular term outright — setting its coefficient to zero because "secular terms are unphysical" — would give zero precession, and it is not even consistent: the solvability argument on the slide shows the equation has no $2\pi$-periodic solution at first order unless $e = 0$.

The circular orbit. If $e = 0$ there is no secular term at all: a circular orbit has no perihelion to advance. The equation then just shifts the radius, $w = 1 + \varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2)$. ✓

How long the naive answer lasts, in numbers. $1/\varepsilon = 1.252\times10^7$ radians, which is $1.99\times10^6$ orbits, which at $87.969$ days each is $4.80\times10^5$ years. Over a century the naive first-order error is of order $(\varepsilon\theta)^2$ with $\varepsilon\theta = 7.99\times10^{-8}\times2609 = 2.1\times10^{-4}$ — invisible. So unit 37 can, honestly, use the naive expansion and read the precession off the secular coefficient, provided it says what this slide says.

The same trick, elsewhere. The binary pulsar PSR B1913+16 has $\varepsilon = 1.04\times10^{-5}$, 130 times Mercury's; the problem sheet computes its periastron advance, about $4.2^\circ$ per year.
