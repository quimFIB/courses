Schrödinger: the wave equation whose short-wavelength limit is Hamilton–Jacobi

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. On paper and without notes, you derive the Schrödinger equation by running the WKB ansatz \(\psi = Ae^{iS/\hbar}\) backwards, and recover from it the Hamilton–Jacobi equation at leading order and the continuity equation at the next; you solve the harmonic oscillator by ladder operators and by Bohr–Sommerfeld and say exactly where each one's \(\tfrac12\) comes from; and you compute the WKB tunnelling exponent for alpha decay and use it to explain why a factor of two in energy spans about 24 orders of magnitude in half-life.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about six and a half hours. Work them on paper first. The equation, and what it conserves

P1 An electron in a box the size of an atom routine

exercises: the time-independent equation as a Sturm–Liouville problem, dimensional analysis, normalisation, the Born rule, a number

An electron is confined to \(0 < x < L\) by infinitely high walls: \(V = 0\) inside, and \(\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0\).

  1. Before solving anything: show that any energy built from \(\hbar\), \(m\) and \(L\) is a pure number times \(\hbar^2/mL^2\), and evaluate \(\hbar^2/m_eL^2\) in eV for \(L = 0.1\) nm.
  2. Solve the time-independent equation. Show \(E_n = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2\), \(n = 1, 2, \dots\), with normalised eigenfunctions \(\varphi_n = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L)\). Evaluate \(E_1\) for \(L = 0.1\) nm and compare it with hydrogen's binding energy, 13.6 eV. What \(L\) would make \(E_1 = 13.6\) eV?
  3. Find the energy and wavelength of the photon emitted when the electron drops from \(n = 2\) to \(n = 1\).
  4. In the ground state, find the probability of finding the electron in the middle third of the box. What would a classical particle bouncing between the walls give, and what does \(\varphi_n\) give as \(n\to\infty\)?
  5. Show that the Hamiltonian \(-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm dx^2}\) on \(C^2\) functions vanishing at both ends is symmetric.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is unit 00's Buckingham count: three quantities, three base dimensions. Part 2 is the first row of unit 19's table — the clamped string — with the eigenvalue renamed. The rest is integrals of \(\sin^2\).

Rung 2

Inside the box the equation is \(\varphi'' = -k^2\varphi\) with \(k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2\). The condition at \(0\) kills the cosine; the condition at \(L\) quantises \(k\). For part 4 write \(\sin^2\theta = \frac12(1 - \cos2\theta)\). For part 5, integrate by parts twice and look at what is left at the ends.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\varphi = A\sin kx\), and \(\sin kL = 0\) forces \(kL = n\pi\). First line of part 4: \(\Pr = \frac2L\int_{L/3}^{2L/3}\sin^2\frac{\pi x}{L}\,\mathrm dx = \frac1L\int_{L/3}^{2L/3}\big(1 - \cos\frac{2\pi x}L\big)\mathrm dx\).

Worked solution

Idea. Dimensions give the scale before any solving; the boundary conditions then give the number in front, and the Born rule turns the eigenfunction into probabilities.

1. Seek \(\hbar^am^bL^c\) with dimensions of energy, \(\mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-2}}\). Since \([\hbar] = \mathrm{M\,L^2\,T^{-1}}\), matching T gives \(a = 2\); then M gives \(2 + b = 1\), \(b = -1\); and L gives \(4 + c = 2\), \(c = -2\). So the only energy is \(\hbar^2/mL^2\), up to a number. For \(L = 0.1\) nm, \(\hbar^2/m_eL^2 = (1.0546\times10^{-34})^2/(9.109\times10^{-31}\times10^{-20}) = 1.221\times10^{-18}\) J \(= 7.62\) eV.

2. With \(k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar\) (energies are positive: the Rayleigh quotient \(\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\int|\varphi'|^2/\int|\varphi|^2\) is), \(\varphi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx\); \(\varphi(0) = 0\) gives \(B = 0\), and \(\varphi(L) = 0\) with \(A\ne0\) gives \(kL = n\pi\), \(n\ge1\). Hence \(E_n = \hbar^2k^2/2m = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2mL^2\). Normalising, \(A^2\int_0^L\sin^2(n\pi x/L)\,\mathrm dx = A^2L/2 = 1\). For \(L = 0.1\) nm, \(E_1 = \frac{\pi^2}2\times7.62 = 37.6\) eV — the right order for atomic energies, about three times hydrogen's \(13.6\) eV, from dimensions plus one boundary condition. Setting \(E_1 = 13.6\) eV gives \(L = \pi\hbar/\sqrt{2m_e\cdot13.6\ \mathrm{eV}} = 0.166\) nm.

3. \(E_2 - E_1 = 3E_1 = 112.8\) eV, so \(\lambda = hc/\Delta E = 1239.84/112.8 = 11.0\) nm, in the extreme ultraviolet.

4. \(\Pr = \frac1L\big[x - \frac{L}{2\pi}\sin\frac{2\pi x}L\big]_{L/3}^{2L/3} = \frac13 - \frac1{2\pi}\big(\sin\frac{4\pi}3 - \sin\frac{2\pi}3\big) = \frac13 + \frac{\sqrt3}{2\pi} = 0.609\). A classical particle moves at constant speed, so its density is uniform and gives \(\frac13\). For \(\varphi_n\) the same computation gives \(\frac13 - \frac{1}{2n\pi}\big(\sin\frac{4n\pi}3 - \sin\frac{2n\pi}3\big)\), whose second term is at most \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2n\pi}\) in size and tends to \(0\): the classical \(\frac13\) returns at large \(n\), the deck's correspondence in its simplest case.

5. For \(\varphi,\psi\in C^2[0,L]\) vanishing at both ends, \(\int_0^L\bar\varphi(-\psi'') = [-\bar\varphi\psi']_0^L + \int\bar\varphi'\psi' = [-\bar\varphi\psi' + \bar\varphi'\psi]_0^L + \int(-\bar\varphi'')\psi\). The bracket vanishes because \(\varphi\) and \(\psi\) do at both ends. So \(\langle\varphi,H\psi\rangle = \langle H\varphi,\psi\rangle\). (This domain is one self-adjoint extension among several, after closure; the deck's deficiency slide says why the choice is physics.)

Check. Dimensions: \(\hbar^2/mL^2\) is \(\mathrm{J^2s^2}/(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}) = \mathrm J\) ✓. Limit: \(L\to\infty\) sends every \(E_n\to0\), the free particle's continuum ✓. Number: \(E_1\propto L^{-2}\), so at \(L = 0.166\) nm, \(E_1 = 37.6\times(0.1/0.166)^2 = 13.6\) eV ✓. And \(\varphi_n\) has \(n - 1\) interior nodes, as unit 19's oscillation theorem says ✓.

P2 How fast a free electron forgets where it is routine

exercises: free evolution by the Fourier transform, a Gaussian integral with complex width, width conventions, numbers

A free electron starts in \(\psi(x,0) = (2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/4\sigma_0^2}e^{ik_0x}\), so \(|\psi|^2\) is a normal density with mean 0 and standard deviation \(\sigma_0\).

  1. Compute \(\tilde\psi(k,0)\), evolve each component by \(e^{-i\hbar k^2t/2m}\), and invert. Show that \(|\psi(x,t)|^2\) is normal with mean \(\hbar k_0t/m\) and standard deviation \(\sigma(t) = \sigma_0\sqrt{1 + (\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2)^2}\).
  2. Unit 20 wrote \(\sigma(t) = \sigma_0\sqrt{1 + (\beta t/\sigma_0^2)^2}\) for the width of \(|u|\); call that width \(s\) here, so \(s(t) = s_0\sqrt{1 + (\beta t/s_0^2)^2}\), to keep it apart from this problem's \(\sigma\). Reconcile the two.
  3. For \(\sigma_0 = 0.1\) nm: how long until the width has doubled? What is \(\sigma\) after 1 fs and after 1 ns? How long does a proton take to double from the same start?
  4. Show that at late times \(\sigma \approx \Delta p\,t/m\) and interpret.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every step is a Gaussian integral. The only subtlety is that at \(t > 0\) the Gaussian has a complex width, which unit 20's analytic-continuation slide licenses.

Rung 2

\(\int e^{-aq^2/2 + ibq}\,\mathrm dq = \sqrt{2\pi/a}\,e^{-b^2/2a}\) for \(\mathrm{Re}\,a > 0\). Shift \(k = k_0 + q\). The modulus squared of \(e^{-b^2/2a}\) is \(e^{-b^2\mathrm{Re}(1/a)}\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\tilde\psi(k,0) = (2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-1/4}\sqrt{4\pi\sigma_0^2}\,e^{-\sigma_0^2(k - k_0)^2}\), so at time \(t\) the exponent in \(q\) is \(-\frac a2q^2\) with \(a = 2\sigma_0^2 + i\hbar t/m\).

Worked solution

Idea. Free evolution is diagonal in \(k\) (the deck's Stone slide): transform, multiply by a phase, transform back.

1. The integration variable is now \(x\): the formula of rung 2 with \(a = 1/2\sigma_0^2\) and \(b = k_0 - k\) gives \(\tilde\psi(k,0) = C\,e^{-\sigma_0^2(k - k_0)^2}\) with \(C = (2\pi\sigma_0^2)^{-1/4}\sqrt{4\pi\sigma_0^2}\). Then \[ \psi(x,t) = \frac{C}{2\pi}\int e^{-\sigma_0^2q^2 - i\hbar(k_0 + q)^2t/2m + i(k_0 + q)x}\,\mathrm dq , \] and collecting powers of \(q\): \(-\frac a2q^2 + iq(x - \hbar k_0t/m)\) times a \(q\)-independent phase, with \(a = 2\sigma_0^2 + i\hbar t/m\). So \(|\psi|^2 \propto \exp\big(-(x - vt)^2\,\mathrm{Re}(1/a)\big)\), \(v = \hbar k_0/m\), and \(\mathrm{Re}\frac1a = \frac{2\sigma_0^2}{4\sigma_0^4 + \hbar^2t^2/m^2}\). A normal density \(e^{-(x - vt)^2/2\sigma^2}\) needs \(\sigma^2 = \frac{4\sigma_0^4 + \hbar^2t^2/m^2}{4\sigma_0^2} = \sigma_0^2\big(1 + (\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2)^2\big)\). The normalisation is preserved automatically (Plancherel: only phases changed).

2. Unit 20's width (\(s\) here) is that of \(|u| \propto e^{-x^2/2s^2}\), so \(|u|^2 \propto e^{-x^2/s^2}\) has standard deviation \(s/\sqrt2\). With \(s_0 = \sqrt2\sigma_0\) and \(\beta = \omega''(k) = \hbar/m\): \(\beta t/s_0^2 = \hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2\) ✓.

3. Doubling needs \(\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2 = \sqrt3\). With \(\tau = 2m_e\sigma_0^2/\hbar = 2\times9.109\times10^{-31}\times10^{-20}/1.0546\times10^{-34} = 1.73\times10^{-16}\) s, \(t = \sqrt3\,\tau = 0.30\) fs. After 1 fs, \(\sigma = 0.1\sqrt{1 + (1/0.1728)^2} = 0.59\) nm; after 1 ns, \(\sigma \approx 0.1\ \mathrm{nm}\times10^{-9}/1.728\times10^{-16} = 5.8\times10^{5}\) nm \(= 0.58\) mm. A proton is \(1836\) times heavier, so it takes \(1836\) times as long: \(0.55\) ps.

4. For \(t\gg\tau\), \(\sigma \approx \sigma_0\cdot\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2 = \frac{\hbar}{2\sigma_0}\frac tm\), and \(\hbar/2\sigma_0 = \Delta p\) for this minimum-uncertainty packet. A cloud of classical free particles with momentum spread \(\Delta p\) spreads exactly so: the late-time spreading is classical, and only the initial spread \(\Delta p\) is quantum. Here \(\Delta p/m_e = 5.8\times10^5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

Check. Dimensions: \(\hbar t/m\sigma_0^2\) is \(\mathrm{J\,s\cdot s}/(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}) = 1\) ✓. Limits: \(t = 0\) gives \(\sigma_0\) ✓; \(m\to\infty\) gives no spreading ✓. The 1 ns figure equals \(\Delta p/m\times1\) ns \(= 5.8\times10^5\times10^{-9}\) m \(= 0.58\) mm ✓.

P3 The current, and what a complex potential does routine

exercises: the continuity equation, the probability current, conservation and its failure, a lifetime

  1. For \(\psi = e^{ikx} + Re^{-ikx}\) (an incident wave and a reflected one, \(R\in\mathbb C\)), compute \(j = \frac\hbar m\mathrm{Im}(\bar\psi\psi')\) and interpret the answer.
  2. Show that \(j = 0\) for every real \(\psi\), and hence for the oscillator's stationary states.
  3. Replace \(V\) by \(V - \frac i2\Gamma\) with \(\Gamma > 0\) constant. Show that \(\partial_t\rho + \partial_xj = -\frac\Gamma\hbar\rho\), and so \(\int|\psi|^2\) decays as \(e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}\). Evaluate the lifetime \(\hbar/\Gamma\) for \(\Gamma = 1\ \mu\)eV.
  4. Say, in terms of the deck's section 2, why this Hamiltonian cannot be self-adjoint.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a constant: compute it and read the two terms as two fluxes. Part 3 is the derivation of the continuity equation on the deck, with one term that no longer drops out.

Rung 2

In part 1 the cross terms combine into \(z - \bar z\) for some \(z\), which is purely imaginary, so they contribute nothing to the imaginary part once multiplied by the \(ik\) in front. In part 3 the extra term in \(\psi_t\) is \(-\frac{\Gamma}{2\hbar}\psi\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(\bar\psi\psi' = ik\,(e^{-ikx} + \bar Re^{ikx})(e^{ikx} - Re^{-ikx}) = ik\big(1 - |R|^2 - Re^{-2ikx} + \bar Re^{2ikx}\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The current is bilinear, so superpositions produce cross terms; whether they survive decides whether waves interfere in the flux. A non-real potential breaks the cancellation that made probability conserved.

1. With \(z = Re^{-2ikx}\), the bracket is \(1 - |R|^2 - (z - \bar z) = 1 - |R|^2 - 2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,z\), so \(\bar\psi\psi' = ik(1 - |R|^2) + 2k\,\mathrm{Im}\,z\), and \(\mathrm{Im}(\bar\psi\psi') = k(1 - |R|^2)\). Hence \[ j = \frac{\hbar k}{m}\big(1 - |R|^2\big) . \] The incident wave carries flux \(\hbar k/m\) to the right (density 1 at speed \(p/m\)); the reflected one carries \(|R|^2\hbar k/m\) to the left; the net is the difference, with no interference term, even though \(|\psi|^2\) itself has interference fringes. Conservation then says that this net flux equals the transmitted flux beyond the obstacle: \(|R|^2 + T = 1\).

2. If \(\psi\) is real so is \(\bar\psi\psi' = \psi\psi'\), whose imaginary part is zero. The oscillator's \(\varphi_n\) are real (the series has real coefficients), and the time factor \(e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\) cancels in \(\bar\psi\psi'\). So a stationary bound state carries no current: nothing flows, as befits a state in which nothing observable moves.

3. Now \(\psi_t = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\psi'' - \frac i\hbar V\psi - \frac{\Gamma}{2\hbar}\psi\). In \(\partial_t|\psi|^2 = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\bar\psi\psi_t)\) the first two terms give \(-\partial_xj\) as on the deck, and the last gives \(2\,\mathrm{Re}(-\frac\Gamma{2\hbar}|\psi|^2) = -\frac\Gamma\hbar\rho\). Integrating over \(\mathbb R\), the current term vanishes for decaying \(\psi\), so \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}N = -\frac\Gamma\hbar N\) and \(N(t) = N(0)e^{-\Gamma t/\hbar}\). For \(\Gamma = 1\ \mu\)eV, \(\hbar/\Gamma = 6.582\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm{eV\,s}/10^{-6}\ \mathrm{eV} = 6.6\times10^{-10}\) s \(= 0.66\) ns. This is how an "optical potential" models absorption, and how a decaying state is given a width \(\Gamma\).

4. \(H - \frac i2\Gamma\) is not even symmetric: \(\langle\psi,(H - \frac i2\Gamma)\psi\rangle - \langle(H - \frac i2\Gamma)\psi,\psi\rangle = -\frac i2\Gamma\lVert\psi\rVert^2 - \frac i2\Gamma\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = -i\Gamma\lVert\psi\rVert^2\ne0\) (the conjugate-linear first slot turns \(-\frac i2\Gamma\) into \(+\frac i2\Gamma\) and the sign flips on subtraction). Its evolution \(e^{-iHt/\hbar}e^{-\Gamma t/2\hbar}\) is a contraction, not unitary, and Stone's theorem says only a self-adjoint generator gives a unitary group.

Check. Dimensions: \(\Gamma/\hbar\) is \(\mathrm{J}/\mathrm{J\,s} = \mathrm s^{-1}\) ✓; \(j\) in one dimension is \(\mathrm{m^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) for a density per metre ✓. Limits: \(R = 0\) gives the free flux \(\hbar k/m\) ✓; \(|R| = 1\), total reflection, gives \(j = 0\) — a standing wave ✓; \(\Gamma\to0\) restores conservation ✓.

P4 Proof: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors — and where the box fails medium

exercises: symmetric operators, adjoints, why the domain matters, a proof

  1. Let \(A\) be symmetric on \(D(A)\). Prove: if \(A\psi = \lambda\psi\) with \(0\ne\psi\in D(A)\), then \(\lambda\in\mathbb R\); and if \(A\psi_1 = \lambda_1\psi_1\), \(A\psi_2 = \lambda_2\psi_2\) with \(\lambda_1\ne\lambda_2\), then \(\langle\psi_1,\psi_2\rangle = 0\).
  2. On \(L^2(0,L)\) let \(D = -i\,\mathrm d/\mathrm dx\) with domain the \(C^1\) functions vanishing at both ends. Show that \(D\) has no eigenvectors at all. Show that its adjoint has \(e^{x/L}\) as an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(-i/L\). Which hypothesis of part 1 fails for \(D^*\)?
  3. For \(D_\theta\) (domain \(\psi(L) = e^{i\theta}\psi(0)\)), check directly that the eigenvalues \((2\pi n + \theta)/L\) are real and that the eigenfunctions are orthogonal.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is two one-line computations, each of the form "evaluate one inner product two ways". Part 2 is an ODE whose only solution meeting the boundary conditions is zero, and then a check of what symmetry needs.

Rung 2

Remember the convention: \(\langle\lambda\psi,\chi\rangle = \bar\lambda\langle\psi,\chi\rangle\). For part 2, \(D\psi = \lambda\psi\) is \(\psi' = i\lambda\psi\). For \(D^*\), recall from the deck that its domain carries no boundary condition.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(\lambda\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = \langle\psi,A\psi\rangle = \langle A\psi,\psi\rangle = \bar\lambda\lVert\psi\rVert^2\). First line of part 2: every solution of \(\psi' = i\lambda\psi\) is \(Ce^{i\lambda x}\), which never vanishes unless \(C = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. Symmetry is exactly what lets a scalar move from one slot to the other; where symmetry fails — on a domain too large — the conclusions fail with it.

1. \(\lambda\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = \langle\psi,\lambda\psi\rangle = \langle\psi,A\psi\rangle = \langle A\psi,\psi\rangle = \langle\lambda\psi,\psi\rangle = \bar\lambda\lVert\psi\rVert^2\), and \(\lVert\psi\rVert\ne0\), so \(\lambda = \bar\lambda\). Next, \(\lambda_2\langle\psi_1,\psi_2\rangle = \langle\psi_1,A\psi_2\rangle = \langle A\psi_1,\psi_2\rangle = \bar\lambda_1\langle\psi_1,\psi_2\rangle = \lambda_1\langle\psi_1,\psi_2\rangle\), using the reality just proved; so \((\lambda_2 - \lambda_1)\langle\psi_1,\psi_2\rangle = 0\) and the inner product vanishes. \(\square\)

2. \(D\psi = \lambda\psi\) means \(-i\psi' = \lambda\psi\), i.e. \(\psi' = i\lambda\psi\), whose solutions are \(Ce^{i\lambda x}\) (unit 01: unique given \(\psi(0)\)). Then \(\psi(0) = C\), so the boundary condition forces \(C = 0\): no eigenvector, for any \(\lambda\in\mathbb C\). The adjoint acts as \(-i\,\mathrm d/\mathrm dx\) with no boundary condition, and \(D^*e^{x/L} = -\frac iLe^{x/L}\): a non-real eigenvalue. What fails is symmetry of \(D^*\): by the deck's boundary identity \(\langle\varphi,D^*\psi\rangle - \langle D^*\varphi,\psi\rangle = -i[\bar\varphi\psi]_0^L\), which for \(\varphi = \psi = e^{x/L}\) is \(-i(e^2 - 1)\ne0\). Part 1 was never applicable to \(D^*\).

3. \(D_\theta\) is self-adjoint, hence symmetric, so part 1 applies; directly, \(e_n = e^{ik_nx}\) with \(k_n = (2\pi n + \theta)/L\) is in the domain since \(e^{ik_nL} = e^{i\theta}\), and \(De_n = k_ne_n\) with \(k_n\) real. For \(n\ne m\), \(\langle e_m,e_n\rangle = \int_0^Le^{i(k_n - k_m)x}\,\mathrm dx = \frac{e^{2\pi i(n - m)} - 1}{i(k_n - k_m)} = 0\) ✓.

Check. In finite dimensions (a Hermitian matrix) symmetric and self-adjoint coincide, and part 2's pathology cannot happen: a nonzero operator on \(\mathbb C^n\) always has an eigenvector ✓. Limit \(\theta\to\theta + 2\pi\) relabels \(n\to n + 1\) and returns the same spectrum, as it must ✓. Dimensions: \(D\) has dimension \(\mathrm{L^{-1}}\), and so do \(k_n\) and \(-i/L\) ✓.

P5 Commutators from brackets: Ehrenfest's theorem medium

exercises: commutators on \(\mathcal S\), the time derivative of an expectation value, Ehrenfest's theorem, when the classical limit holds

Let \(H = P^2/2m + V(X)\) with \(V\) smooth, all its derivatives bounded by polynomials, so that \(\mathcal S\) is mapped into itself by \(X\), \(P\) and \(V(X)\).

  1. Verify \([X,P] = i\hbar\) on \(\mathcal S\), and compute \([H,X]\) and \([H,P]\).
  2. For \(\psi(t)\in\mathcal S\) solving the Schrödinger equation, prove \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle A\rangle = \frac i\hbar\langle[H,A]\rangle\) for \(A = X, P\). Deduce Ehrenfest's theorem: \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle X\rangle = \langle P\rangle/m\) and \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle P\rangle = -\langle V'(X)\rangle\). Compare with unit 14's \(\dot f = \{f,H\}\).
  3. When is this Newton's second law for the means? Show it is whenever \(V\) is at most quadratic. For \(V = \lambda x^4\) and a state whose density is normal with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\), find \(\langle V'(X)\rangle\) and the relative error of replacing it by \(V'(\langle X\rangle)\) at \(\mu = 1\), \(\sigma = 0.1\) (any units).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

All of it is the product rule. The commutator identity \([AB,C] = A[B,C] + [A,C]B\) does part 1 in a line; part 2 is the deck's Stone-slide computation with \(A\) inserted.

Rung 2

\([P^2,X] = P[P,X] + [P,X]P\). For \([V(X),P]\) act on a test function and use the product rule on \((V\psi)'\). In part 2 differentiate \(\langle\psi,A\psi\rangle\), substitute \(\dot\psi = H\psi/i\hbar\), and use the symmetry of \(H\). For part 3, the moments of a normal density: \(\langle x^3\rangle = \mu^3 + 3\mu\sigma^2\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \([V,P]\psi = V(-i\hbar\psi') + i\hbar(V\psi)' = i\hbar V'\psi\). First line of part 2: \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle\psi,A\psi\rangle = \langle\tfrac{1}{i\hbar}H\psi,A\psi\rangle + \langle\psi,A\tfrac1{i\hbar}H\psi\rangle\).

Worked solution

Idea. Dirac's rule, used where it is exact: the commutators of \(H\) with \(X\) and \(P\) are the quantum versions of Hamilton's equations, and they close on themselves exactly when \(V'\) is linear.

1. \([X,P]\psi = x(-i\hbar\psi') + i\hbar(x\psi)' = i\hbar\psi\). Then \([P^2,X] = P(-i\hbar) + (-i\hbar)P = -2i\hbar P\), so \([H,X] = -\frac{i\hbar}{m}P\) (and \([V(X),X] = 0\)). And \([H,P] = [V(X),P] = i\hbar V'(X)\), from rung 3.

2. Since the inner product is conjugate-linear in the first slot, \(\langle\frac1{i\hbar}H\psi,A\psi\rangle = -\frac1{i\hbar}\langle H\psi,A\psi\rangle = -\frac1{i\hbar}\langle\psi,HA\psi\rangle\), using symmetry of \(H\) on \(\mathcal S\). So \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle A\rangle = \frac1{i\hbar}\langle\psi,(AH - HA)\psi\rangle = \frac i\hbar\langle[H,A]\rangle\). With part 1: \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle X\rangle = \frac i\hbar\cdot(-\frac{i\hbar}m)\langle P\rangle = \langle P\rangle/m\), and \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle P\rangle = \frac i\hbar\cdot i\hbar\langle V'(X)\rangle = -\langle V'(X)\rangle\). Unit 14: \(\{x,H\} = p/m\), \(\{p,H\} = -V'(x)\); Dirac's rule \(\{f,g\}\mapsto[\hat f,\hat g]/i\hbar\) turns these into \(\frac i\hbar[H,X]\) and \(\frac i\hbar[H,P]\) exactly ✓.

3. The means obey Newton iff \(\langle V'(X)\rangle = V'(\langle X\rangle)\). If \(V\) is at most quadratic, \(V'\) is affine, \(V'(x) = \alpha + \beta x\), and \(\langle\alpha + \beta X\rangle = \alpha + \beta\langle X\rangle\) for every state: exact — the oscillator's means move classically whatever the state. Otherwise Taylor-expanding about \(\langle X\rangle\), \(\langle V'(X)\rangle = V'(\langle X\rangle) + \frac12V'''(\langle X\rangle)\Delta x^2 + \cdots\): classical when the packet is narrow compared with the scale on which the force curves. For \(V = \lambda x^4\), \(V' = 4\lambda x^3\) and \(\langle V'\rangle = 4\lambda(\mu^3 + 3\mu\sigma^2)\), against \(V'(\mu) = 4\lambda\mu^3\): relative error \(3\sigma^2/\mu^2 = 0.03\) at \(\mu = 1\), \(\sigma = 0.1\). As P2 showed, a free packet's \(\sigma\) grows, so the error grows too: Ehrenfest's classical regime does not last.

Check. Dimensions: \(\frac i\hbar[H,P]\) is \(\frac1{\mathrm{J\,s}}\cdot\mathrm{J\,s}\cdot\mathrm{N} = \mathrm N\), a force ✓. Limit \(\sigma\to0\): the correction vanishes ✓. Special case: the oscillator, \(V' = m\omega^2x\), gives \(\ddot{\langle X\rangle} = -\omega^2\langle X\rangle\), the deck's commutator slide ✓.

Rung 1

Rung 2

Rung 3

Worked solution

Idea. Uncertainty forbids a state from concentrating in both position and momentum, which puts a floor under every energy; any particular trial state puts a ceiling over the ground state.

1. \(\langle P^2\rangle = \Delta p^2 + \langle P\rangle^2\ge\Delta p^2\) and likewise \(\langle X^2\rangle\ge\Delta x^2\). So, by \(a + b\ge2\sqrt{ab}\) for \(a, b\ge0\) and Heisenberg, \(\langle H\rangle\ge\frac{\Delta p^2}{2m} + \frac{m\omega^2\Delta x^2}2\ge\omega\Delta x\Delta p\ge\frac12\hbar\omega\). Equality in the last step needs a minimum-uncertainty state, a Gaussian \(e^{-c(x - \bar x)^2 + i\bar px/\hbar}\) (the deck's Robertson slide); in the first, \(\langle X\rangle = \langle P\rangle = 0\); in the middle, \(\Delta p^2/2m = m\omega^2\Delta x^2/2\), i.e. \(\Delta x = \ell/\sqrt2\). Together: \(\psi\) is \(\varphi_0\) up to a phase. \(\square\)

2. Cauchy–Schwarz: \(\langle X^2\rangle = \int x^2|\psi|\cdot|\psi|\le(\int x^4|\psi|^2)^{1/2}(\int|\psi|^2)^{1/2} = \langle X^4\rangle^{1/2}\). 3. The trial \(\psi\) with \(|\psi|^2\) normal of variance \(s\) has \(\langle X^4\rangle = 3s^2\), and its momentum spread is \(\Delta p^2 = \hbar^2/4s\) with \(\langle P\rangle = 0\) (it saturates Heisenberg), so \(\langle H\rangle = \frac1{8s} + 3s^2\). The minimum, at \(s^3 = 1/48\), \(s = 0.2752\), is \(\frac32\cdot\frac{48^{1/3}}8 = 0.6814\). By the Rayleigh quotient (unit 19: the lowest eigenvalue is the minimum of \(\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle\) over unit vectors), \(E_0\le0.6814\). So \(0.4725\le E_0\le0.6814\): the exact \(0.6680\) is \(2.0\%\) below the upper bound and \(41\%\) above the lower.

Check. Dimensions in part 1: \(\omega\Delta x\Delta p\) is \(\mathrm{s^{-1}\cdot J\,s} = \mathrm J\) ✓. Special case: run part 3's method on the oscillator, \(\frac1{8s} + \frac s2\) (units \(\hbar = m = \omega = 1\)): minimum at \(s = \frac12\), value \(\frac14 + \frac14 = \frac12\) — exact, because the true ground state is a Gaussian ✓. Use only: \(a\) and \(a^\dagger\) map \(\mathcal S\) into itself, \(\langle\varphi,a\psi\rangle = \langle a^\dagger\varphi,\psi\rangle\) on \(\mathcal S\), and \([a,a^\dagger] = 1\).

Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

A proof by descent: lowering keeps you inside the eigenvectors, and positivity forbids going below zero. The only care needed is to show that the descent never produces the zero vector too early.

Rung 2

Three facts: \(\langle\chi,N\chi\rangle = \lVert a\chi\rVert^2\ge0\); \([N,a] = -a\); and \(\lVert a\chi\rVert^2 = \mu\lVert\chi\rVert^2\) when \(N\chi = \mu\chi\). Apply them to \(\chi = a^j\psi\).

Rung 3

By induction, \(a^j\psi\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\nu - j\) as long as it is nonzero, and \(\lVert a^{j}\psi\rVert^2 = \nu(\nu - 1)\cdots(\nu - j + 1)\lVert\psi\rVert^2\). For part 4, \(X = \frac{\ell}{\sqrt2}(a + a^\dagger)\) and \(P = \frac{i\hbar}{\sqrt2\,\ell}(a^\dagger - a)\).

Rung 4

First line of part 1: \(\nu\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = \langle\psi,N\psi\rangle = \lVert a\psi\rVert^2\ge0\), so \(\nu\ge0\). First line of part 4: \(X^2 = \frac{\ell^2}2(a^2 + a^{\dagger2} + aa^\dagger + a^\dagger a)\), and \(\langle\varphi_n,a^2\varphi_n\rangle = 0\) because \(a^2\varphi_n\) is proportional to \(\varphi_{n-2}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The spectrum of \(N\) is forced by algebra: \(a\) moves eigenvalues down by exactly one, and the only way to stop a descent that would otherwise cross zero is to land on an eigenvalue whose eigenvector \(a\) kills — which positivity pins at \(0\).

1. First, the adjoint relation also holds with the roles swapped: \(\langle\varphi,a^\dagger\psi\rangle = \overline{\langle a^\dagger\psi,\varphi\rangle} = \overline{\langle\psi,a\varphi\rangle} = \langle a\varphi,\psi\rangle\). (i) For any \(\chi\in\mathcal S\), apply it with \(\varphi = \chi\), \(\psi = a\chi\): \(\langle\chi,N\chi\rangle = \langle\chi,a^\dagger(a\chi)\rangle = \langle a\chi,a\chi\rangle\ge0\). (ii) \([N,a] = a^\dagger aa - aa^\dagger a = -(aa^\dagger - a^\dagger a)a = -a\), using \([a,a^\dagger] = 1\). (iii) If \(N\chi = \mu\chi\), then \(Na\chi = aN\chi - a\chi = (\mu - 1)a\chi\), and \(\lVert a\chi\rVert^2 = \langle\chi,N\chi\rangle = \mu\lVert\chi\rVert^2\). Now let \(N\psi = \nu\psi\), \(\psi\ne0\). By induction on \(j\), using (iii) at each step, \(Na^j\psi = (\nu - j)a^j\psi\) and \(\lVert a^j\psi\rVert^2 = \nu(\nu - 1)\cdots(\nu - j + 1)\lVert\psi\rVert^2\). Suppose \(\nu\notin\{0,1,2,\dots\}\). Then no factor vanishes, every \(a^j\psi\ne0\), and for \(j > \nu\) it is a nonzero eigenvector with eigenvalue \(\nu - j < 0\), contradicting (i): \(\langle\chi,N\chi\rangle = (\nu - j)\lVert\chi\rVert^2 < 0\). So \(\nu\) is a nonnegative integer. \(\square\)

2. In \(\xi = x/\ell\), \(a = \frac1{\sqrt2}(\xi + \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\xi})\), so \(a\varphi = 0\) is the linear ODE \(\varphi' = -\xi\varphi\), whose solutions are exactly \(Ce^{-\xi^2/2}\) (unit 01: uniqueness given \(\varphi(0)\)). All lie in \(\mathcal S\). Normalised: \(\varphi_0 = (\pi\ell^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/2\ell^2}\).

3. \([N,a^\dagger] = a^\dagger aa^\dagger - a^\dagger a^\dagger a = a^\dagger[a,a^\dagger] = a^\dagger\), so \(a^\dagger\) raises eigenvalues by one, and \(\lVert a^\dagger\chi\rVert^2 = \langle\chi,aa^\dagger\chi\rangle = \langle\chi,(N + 1)\chi\rangle = (\mu + 1)\lVert\chi\rVert^2\). From \(N\varphi_0 = 0\) and induction, \(N\varphi_n = n\varphi_n\) and \(\lVert(a^\dagger)^n\varphi_0\rVert^2 = n!\), so \(\lVert\varphi_n\rVert = 1\). Distinct \(n\) are distinct eigenvalues of the symmetric \(N\), so P4 part 1 gives orthogonality.

4. Adding and subtracting the definitions: \(a + a^\dagger = \frac{2m\omega X}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}\), so \(X = \sqrt{\frac\hbar{2m\omega}}(a + a^\dagger) = \frac\ell{\sqrt2}(a + a^\dagger)\); and \(a - a^\dagger = \frac{2iP}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}}\), so \(P = i\sqrt{\frac{m\hbar\omega}2}(a^\dagger - a) = \frac{i\hbar}{\sqrt2\ell}(a^\dagger - a)\). Then \(\langle\varphi_n,X^2\varphi_n\rangle = \frac{\ell^2}2\langle\varphi_n,(aa^\dagger + a^\dagger a)\varphi_n\rangle = \frac{\ell^2}2\big((n + 1) + n\big) = \ell^2(n + \tfrac12)\), the \(a^2\) and \(a^{\dagger2}\) terms giving \(\varphi_{n\mp2}\perp\varphi_n\). Similarly \(\langle P^2\rangle = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2\ell^2}\langle(a^\dagger - a)^2\rangle = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\ell^2}(2n + 1) = \frac{\hbar^2}{\ell^2}(n + \tfrac12)\). So \(\langle V\rangle = \frac12m\omega^2\ell^2(n + \frac12) = \frac12\hbar\omega(n + \frac12) = \frac12E_n\), and the kinetic part is the other half. Since \(\langle X\rangle = \langle P\rangle = 0\) (each is a combination of \(\varphi_{n\pm1}\)), \(\Delta x\Delta p = \ell\sqrt{n + \frac12}\cdot\frac\hbar\ell\sqrt{n + \frac12} = \hbar(n + \tfrac12)\).

Check. \(n = 0\): \(\Delta x\Delta p = \hbar/2\), the equality case of Heisenberg ✓; \(\Delta x = \ell/\sqrt2\) as on the deck ✓. The virial split \(\langle T\rangle = \langle V\rangle\) is the classical time-average result for a harmonic force, here true in every eigenstate ✓. Dimensions: \(\ell^2(n + \frac12)\) is an area ✓.

P10 Alpha decay and 24 orders of magnitude hard

exercises: the WKB tunnelling exponent, the Gamow factor, the finite-radius correction, the Geiger–Nuttall law, numbers

\(^{238}\)U emits alphas of kinetic energy \(4.20\) MeV with half-life \(4.47\times10^9\) yr, leaving \(^{234}\)Th (\(Z = 90\)); \(^{212}\)Po emits \(8.78\) MeV alphas with half-life \(0.299\ \mu\)s, leaving \(^{208}\)Pb (\(Z = 82\)). A factor of \(2.1\) in energy; a factor of how much in half-life?

Model: an alpha formed at the nuclear radius \(R\) strikes the barrier \(v/2R\) times a second, and each time tunnels with probability \(e^{-2\Gamma}\), so \(t_{1/2} = \ln2\cdot\frac{2R}{v}e^{2\Gamma}\). Use the relative-motion energy \(Q = E_\alpha A/(A - 4)\), the reduced mass \(\mu\) of alpha and daughter (daughter mass \((A - 4)\) u), and \(R = 1.2\,(A_\mathrm d^{1/3} + 4^{1/3})\) fm.

  1. With \(V = 2Zk/r\) outside \(R\) (\(k = e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0\)), derive \(\Gamma = \frac{b\sqrt{2\mu Q}}\hbar F(R/b)\) with \(b = 2Zk/Q\) and \(F(s) = \arccos\sqrt s - \sqrt{s(1 - s)}\).
  2. Show \(\Gamma\to\pi\eta\), \(\eta = 2Z\alpha c/v\), as \(R\to0\), and that then \(2\Gamma = 4\pi\alpha\sqrt{\mu c^2/2}\;Z/\sqrt Q\). Evaluate \(2\Gamma_0\) for both nuclei. How many orders of magnitude does the bare exponent predict between the half-lives? How many are measured?
  3. Now include \(R\). Evaluate \(2\Gamma\) for both, the predicted ratio with and without the prefactor, and the two absolute half-lives.
  4. Expand \(F(s)\approx\frac\pi2 - 2\sqrt s\) for small \(s\) and explain why the finite radius shortens the uranium half-life more than the polonium one. Then derive the form of the Geiger–Nuttall law, \(\log_{10}t_{1/2}\approx a\,Z/\sqrt Q + b\), and evaluate \(a\).
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a handful of numbers, each an exponent; compare exponents, not half-lives, and divide by \(\ln10\) to count orders of magnitude. Part 1 is one trigonometric substitution.

Rung 2

\(\Gamma = \frac1\hbar\int_R^b\sqrt{2\mu(2Zk/r - Q)}\,\mathrm dr = \frac{\sqrt{2\mu Q}}\hbar\int_R^b\sqrt{b/r - 1}\,\mathrm dr\). Substitute \(r = b\cos^2u\). Work in MeV and fm with \(\hbar c = 197.327\) MeV fm, so that \(\sqrt{2\mu c^2Q}/\hbar c\) is in fm\(^{-1}\).

Rung 3

\(\mu c^2 = m_\alpha c^2\cdot(A - 4)\,\mathrm u/(m_\alpha c^2 + (A - 4)\,\mathrm u)\): \(3664.7\) MeV for uranium, \(3657.0\) MeV for polonium. \(Q = 4.272\) and \(8.949\) MeV; \(b = 60.7\) and \(26.4\) fm; \(R = 9.30\) and \(9.01\) fm.

Rung 4

First line of part 1: with \(r = b\cos^2u\), \(\sqrt{b/r - 1} = \tan u\) and \(\mathrm dr = -2b\sin u\cos u\,\mathrm du\), so \(\int_R^b\sqrt{b/r - 1}\,\mathrm dr = 2b\int_0^{u_0}\sin^2u\,\mathrm du\) with \(\cos^2u_0 = R/b\).

Worked solution

Idea. The half-life is dominated by \(e^{2\Gamma}\), and \(\Gamma\propto Z/\sqrt Q\) to leading order: halving \(Q\) multiplies the exponent by \(\sqrt2\) and the exponent is of order a hundred. The finite radius trims the barrier where it is tallest, and the trim is worth more for the wider barrier.

1. From rung 4, \(2b\int_0^{u_0}\sin^2u\,\mathrm du = b(u_0 - \sin u_0\cos u_0)\). With \(s = \cos^2u_0 = R/b\): \(u_0 = \arccos\sqrt s\) and \(\sin u_0\cos u_0 = \sqrt{s(1 - s)}\). So \(\Gamma = \frac{b\sqrt{2\mu Q}}\hbar F(R/b)\).

2. \(F(0) = \frac\pi2\), so \(\Gamma_0 = \frac\pi2\frac{b\sqrt{2\mu Q}}\hbar = \frac{\pi Zk}{\hbar}\sqrt{\frac{2\mu}Q}\), and with \(v = \sqrt{2Q/\mu}\), \(\sqrt{2\mu/Q} = 2/v\): \(\Gamma_0 = \frac{2\pi Zk}{\hbar v} = \pi\eta\) since \(k/\hbar c = \alpha\). Then \(2\Gamma_0 = \frac{4\pi Z\alpha c}{v} = 4\pi\alpha\sqrt{\mu c^2/2}\,\frac{Z}{\sqrt Q}\) with \(Q\) in MeV, coefficient \(3.925\ \mathrm{MeV^{1/2}}\) for uranium (\(3.921\) for polonium). Uranium: \(2\Gamma_0 = 3.925\times90/\sqrt{4.272} = 170.9\). Polonium: \(3.921\times82/\sqrt{8.949} = 107.5\). Difference \(63.4\), i.e. \(63.4/\ln10 = 27.6\) orders of magnitude. Measured: \(t_\mathrm U = 4.47\times10^9\times3.156\times10^7 = 1.41\times10^{17}\) s, \(t_\mathrm{Po} = 2.99\times10^{-7}\) s, ratio \(10^{23.7}\). The bare exponent explains all of it and \(3.9\) orders more.

3. \(s = 9.30/60.7 = 0.153\) for uranium and \(9.01/26.4 = 0.342\) for polonium. Then \(2\Gamma = 87.96\) and \(32.32\): difference \(55.6\), i.e. \(24.2\) orders. The prefactor \(2R/v\) differs too: \(v/c = 0.0483\) and \(0.0700\), so \(v/2R = 7.78\times10^{20}\) and \(1.16\times10^{21}\ \mathrm s^{-1}\), adding \(\log_{10}(1.163/0.778) = 0.17\): predicted ratio \(10^{24.3}\), measured \(10^{23.7}\). Absolute: \(t_\mathrm U = \frac{\ln2}{7.78\times10^{20}}e^{87.96} = 1.4\times10^{17}\) s (measured \(1.41\times10^{17}\) s) and \(t_\mathrm{Po} = 6.5\times10^{-8}\) s (measured \(3.0\times10^{-7}\) s). The residual \(0.7\) orders — a factor of about 5, almost all of it in polonium — is beyond a one-body WKB model: the probability that an alpha is preformed in the nucleus, and the crude \(R\), both enter as \(O(1)\) factors.

4. \(\arccos\sqrt s = \frac\pi2 - \arcsin\sqrt s\approx\frac\pi2 - \sqrt s\) and \(\sqrt{s(1 - s)}\approx\sqrt s\), so \(F\approx\frac\pi2 - 2\sqrt s\) and \(\Gamma\approx\pi\eta - \frac2\hbar\sqrt{2\mu Q\,bR} = \pi\eta - \frac2\hbar\sqrt{4\mu ZkR}\). The correction does not depend on \(Q\) at all; it grows with \(Z\) and \(R\), which are larger for uranium, so it removes more of uranium's exponent (\(82.97\) from \(2\Gamma\), exactly) than polonium's (\(75.17\)): \(7.8\) in the difference, \(3.4\) orders. (The expansion itself is rough at polonium's \(s = 0.34\); the exact \(F\) was used above.) Hence \(\ln t_{1/2}\approx2\pi\eta - \frac4\hbar\sqrt{4\mu ZkR} + \ln\frac{2R\ln2}{v}\), and for nuclei of similar \(Z\) and \(R\) the second and third terms vary slowly: \(\log_{10}t_{1/2}\approx a\,Z/\sqrt Q + b\) with \(a = 4\pi\alpha\sqrt{\mu c^2/2}/\ln10 = 1.70\ \mathrm{MeV^{1/2}}\). Geiger and Nuttall found this straight line empirically in 1911; Gamow, and Gurney and Condon, explained it in 1928.

Check. Dimensions: \(b\sqrt{2\mu Q}/\hbar\) is (length)(momentum)/(action), a number ✓; numerically \(b\sqrt{2\mu c^2Q}/\hbar c = 60.68\times\sqrt{2\times3664.7\times4.272}/197.33 = 54.41\) for uranium, and \(F(0.1533) = \arccos(0.3915) - \sqrt{0.1533\times0.8467} = 1.1685 - 0.3602 = 0.8083\), so \(2\Gamma = 2\times54.41\times0.8083 = 87.96\) ✓. Limit \(R\to0\) recovers part 2 ✓.

P11 A rectangular barrier, and the microscope that reads atoms medium

exercises: the WKB exponent against an exact result, its validity condition, the steepness of an exponential, numbers

For a rectangular barrier of height \(V_0\) and width \(a\), and \(0 < E < V_0\), the exact transmission is \(T = \big[1 + \frac{V_0^2\sinh^2\kappa a}{4E(V_0 - E)}\big]^{-1}\) with \(\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar\) (the deck's tunnelling notes say how it is found).

  1. Show \(T\approx\frac{16E(V_0 - E)}{V_0^2}e^{-2\kappa a}\) for \(\kappa a\gg1\), and identify the WKB exponent.
  2. For an electron with \(E = 1\) eV, \(V_0 = 2\) eV: compare WKB's \(e^{-2\kappa a}\) with the exact \(T\) at \(a = 1\) nm and at \(a = 0.2\) nm. Which comparison is fair, and why?
  3. A scanning tunnelling microscope passes a current across a vacuum gap \(d\) between a tip and a surface; the barrier height is about the work function, \(W = 4.5\) eV. Taking the current \(\propto e^{-2\kappa d}\), by what factor does it change when \(d\) changes by \(0.1\) nm? by \(0.01\) nm? What change in \(d\) halves it?
  4. A proton under the same 1 eV, 1 nm barrier: estimate \(T\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything here is about an exponential: where it dominates, where it does not, and how violently it responds to its argument.

Rung 2

\(\sinh^2\kappa a\approx\frac14e^{2\kappa a}\) when \(\kappa a\gg1\), and then the 1 in the bracket is negligible. The WKB exponent for a flat barrier is \(\frac2\hbar\int_0^a\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}\,\mathrm dx = 2\kappa a\). Validity needs \(\Gamma = \kappa a\gg1\).

Rung 3

First numbers: \(\kappa = \sqrt{2m_e\times1\ \mathrm{eV}}/\hbar = 5.12\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}\); for the microscope \(\kappa = \sqrt{2m_e\times4.5\ \mathrm{eV}}/\hbar = 10.87\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}\), and the factor for a change \(\delta\) is \(e^{2\kappa\delta}\).

Worked solution

Idea. WKB gets the exponent of a thick barrier right and its prefactor right only for a smooth one; and the exponent's steepness is what makes a microscope out of a tunnel junction.

1. For \(\kappa a\gg1\), \(\frac{V_0^2\sinh^2\kappa a}{4E(V_0 - E)}\approx\frac{V_0^2e^{2\kappa a}}{16E(V_0 - E)}\gg1\), so \(T\approx\frac{16E(V_0 - E)}{V_0^2}e^{-2\kappa a}\). The exponent \(2\kappa a = \frac2\hbar\int_0^a\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}\,\mathrm dx\) is exactly the WKB \(2\Gamma\); the prefactor, \(4\) at \(E = V_0/2\), is not WKB's 1, because the corners violate the smoothness condition.

2. At \(a = 1\) nm, \(2\kappa a = 10.25\): WKB \(e^{-10.25} = 3.55\times10^{-5}\), exact \(1.419\times10^{-4}\) — the exponent is right and the ratio is the prefactor 4. At \(a = 0.2\) nm, \(2\kappa a = 2.05\): WKB \(0.129\), exact \(0.404\), and even the prefactor-corrected formula (\(0.515\)) is off, because \(\Gamma = 1.02\) is not large. Only the first comparison is within WKB's stated validity.

3. \(e^{2\kappa\times0.1\ \mathrm{nm}} = e^{2.17} = 8.8\): a tenth of a nanometre — less than an atomic radius — changes the current ninefold. For \(0.01\) nm the factor is \(e^{0.217} = 1.24\), a 24% change, easily measured; and \(d\) changes by \(\ln2/2\kappa = 0.032\) nm to halve it. Because the current falls so steeply, nearly all of it flows through the one atom of the tip nearest the surface, which is why Binnig and Rohrer's microscope (1981) resolves single atoms.

4. \(\kappa\propto\sqrt m\), so for a proton \(2\kappa a = 10.25\times\sqrt{1836} = 439\) and \(T\sim e^{-439}\approx10^{-191}\): never. Tunnelling at atomic scales is an electron's phenomenon; a proton tunnels appreciably only through barriers a few hundredths of a nanometre wide, as in some hydrogen-transfer reactions.

Check. Dimensions: \(\kappa\) is \(\sqrt{\mathrm{kg\,J}}/\mathrm{J\,s} = \mathrm m^{-1}\) ✓. Limit \(E\to V_0\): \(\kappa\to0\) and the exact \(T\to(1 + mV_0a^2/2\hbar^2)^{-1}\), no longer exponentially small, consistent with \(\Gamma\to0\) leaving WKB's regime ✓. Number: \(16\times1\times1/4 = 4\) ✓.

Unitary groups

P12 Translations on a ring and on a half-line hard

exercises: unitary groups, Stone's theorem in an example, deficiency indices, an isometry that is not unitary

  1. The ring. On \(L^2(0,L)\), extend each \(\psi\) to \(\mathbb R\) by \(\psi(x + L) = e^{i\theta}\psi(x)\), and define \((U(a)\psi)(x) = \psi(x - a)\) for \(x\in(0,L)\). Show that \(U(a)e_n = e^{-ik_na}e_n\) for \(e_n = e^{ik_nx}/\sqrt L\), \(k_n = (2\pi n + \theta)/L\); conclude that \(U\) is a unitary group, and find its generator \(i\hbar\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm da}U(a)\big|_{a=0}\). Which operator of the deck is it?
  2. The half-line. On \(L^2(0,\infty)\), for \(a\ge0\) let \((T(a)\psi)(x) = \psi(x - a)\) for \(x\ge a\) and \(0\) for \(0\le x < a\). Show that \(T(a)\) is an isometry with \(T(a)T(b) = T(a + b)\), find its adjoint, and show \(T(a)^*T(a) = I\) but \(T(a)T(a)^*\ne I\). Conclude that no unitary group \(U\) on \(L^2(0,\infty)\) has \(U(a) = T(a)\) for \(a\ge0\).
  3. Explain how part 2 and Stone's theorem together account for the deficiency indices \((1,0)\) of \(-i\,\mathrm d/\mathrm dx\) on the half-line.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A unitary operator is a bijective isometry. On the ring, translation is diagonal in the Fourier basis, so everything reduces to phases. On the half-line, translation to the right is perfectly good; ask what translation to the left would have to do.

Rung 2

The \(e_n\) are an orthonormal basis of \(L^2(0,L)\): multiply unit 19's complete system \(e^{2\pi inx/L}\) by the unimodular \(e^{i\theta x/L}\). For the adjoint on the half-line, compute \(\langle\varphi,T(a)\psi\rangle\) and substitute \(y = x - a\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: the extension of \(e_n\) is \(e^{ik_nx}/\sqrt L\) itself, since \(e^{ik_n(x + L)} = e^{ik_nL}e^{ik_nx} = e^{i\theta}e^{ik_nx}\). First line of part 2: \(\langle\varphi,T(a)\psi\rangle = \int_a^\infty\bar\varphi(x)\psi(x - a)\,\mathrm dx = \int_0^\infty\bar\varphi(y + a)\psi(y)\,\mathrm dy\).

Worked solution

Idea. By Stone, a self-adjoint momentum is the same thing as a unitary group of translations. On a ring there is one for each twist \(\theta\); on a half-line translation can only go one way, so there is none.

1. By rung 3, \(U(a)e_n = e^{ik_n(x - a)}/\sqrt L = e^{-ik_na}e_n\). For \(\psi = \sum c_ne_n\) (an orthonormal basis, rung 2), \(U(a)\psi = \sum e^{-ik_na}c_ne_n\): it multiplies coordinates by phases, so it preserves norms (Parseval) and is invertible with inverse \(U(-a)\); and \(U(a)U(b) = U(a + b)\), \(U(0) = I\), by the same formula. Continuity: \(\lVert U(a)\psi - \psi\rVert^2 = \sum|e^{-ik_na} - 1|^2|c_n|^2\to0\) as \(a\to0\), splitting the sum as on the deck's Stone slide. The generator acts on \(e_n\) as \(i\hbar\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm da}e^{-ik_na}\big|_0 = \hbar k_n\): it is \(P_\theta = \hbar D_\theta\), the self-adjoint momentum with boundary condition \(\psi(L) = e^{i\theta}\psi(0)\), and \(U(a) = e^{-iP_\theta a/\hbar}\).

2. \(\lVert T(a)\psi\rVert^2 = \int_a^\infty|\psi(x - a)|^2\,\mathrm dx = \lVert\psi\rVert^2\), and shifting by \(b\) then \(a\) is shifting by \(a + b\). From rung 3, \((T(a)^*\varphi)(y) = \varphi(y + a)\): the left shift, which discards \(\varphi\) on \([0,a)\). Then \(T(a)^*T(a)\psi = \psi\), but \(T(a)T(a)^*\varphi\) is \(\varphi\) with its values on \([0,a)\) replaced by \(0\) — not \(\varphi\) when \(\varphi\) is nonzero there. So \(T(a)\) is not surjective: its range is orthogonal to every function supported in \([0,a)\). If a unitary group had \(U(a) = T(a)\) for some \(a > 0\), \(U(a)\) would be unitary, hence surjective — a contradiction.

3. If \(-i\hbar\,\mathrm d/\mathrm dx\) had a self-adjoint extension on the half-line, Stone's theorem would give a unitary group whose generator it is, and that group would have to translate wavefunctions (its generator is the derivative); part 2 shows translations on the half-line form only a semigroup of isometries, not a group of unitaries. The deficiency indices say the same in other words: \(e^{-x}\in\ker(D^* - i)\) is square-integrable on \((0,\infty)\) but \(e^{x}\in\ker(D^* + i)\) is not, so \(n_+ = 1\ne n_- = 0\), and von Neumann's criterion forbids a self-adjoint extension. Physically: probability pushed against the wall has nowhere to go, so translating "through" it cannot conserve probability.

Check. The ring at \(\theta = 0\) is unit 19's periodic problem, \(k_n = 2\pi n/L\), with eigenvalue 0 for the constant function ✓. Dimensions: \(\hbar k_n\) is a momentum ✓. Limit \(L\to\infty\) on the ring: the spacing \(2\pi\hbar/L\to0\), approaching the line, where \(P\) has indices \((0,0)\) and translations are unitary in both directions ✓.
