One oscillator, three routes, one answer

the running example: the harmonic oscillator $$i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\,\partial_x^2\psi + \tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2\,\psi$$

2. By ladder operators: two lines of algebra and a ladder that must stop below.

Then it is compared with the classical oscillator at large $n$.

section 1

Which wave equation?

Matter has a wavelength. Unit 15 says which limit the wave theory must have; that is almost enough to find it.

21b · which wave

Quanta, and de Broglie turning them round

the Planck–Einstein relations $$E = \hbar\omega, \qquad \vec p = \hbar\vec k .$$

Light of angular frequency $\omega$ is exchanged in lumps of energy $\hbar\omega$ and momentum $\hbar k$; $\ \hbar = h/2\pi = 1.0546\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$.

Evidence, named not developed. The photoelectric effect (Einstein 1905): ejected electrons' energy depends on $\omega$, not on intensity. Compton scattering (1923): X-rays bounce off electrons with the momentum $\hbar k$.

the de Broglie relation (1924)

Turn it round: a particle of momentum $p$ is a wave of wavelength $\lambda = h/p$.

Davisson and Germer (1927). 54 eV electrons have $p = \sqrt{2mE}$ and $\lambda = 0.167$ nm. Off a nickel crystal, whose surface rows are $d = 0.215$ nm apart, they found a peak at $50^\circ$: $d\sin50^\circ = 0.165$ nm.

Neighbouring atoms' rays differ in path by $d\sin\varphi$; a strong beam needs $d\sin\varphi = \lambda$.

The numbers. $E = 54\ \mathrm{eV} = 8.65\times10^{-18}$ J, $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg, so $p = \sqrt{2m_eE} = 3.97\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$ and $\lambda = h/p = 0.1669$ nm. The measured peak gives $0.2150\sin50^\circ = 0.1647$ nm: agreement to 1.3%. The geometry is the reflection grating of elementary optics, with the surface row of atoms as the grating; the check $\arcsin(\lambda/d) = 50.9^\circ$ against the observed $50^\circ$ is the same statement.

Dimensions. $h/p$: $\mathrm{J\,s}/(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}) = \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}/(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}) = \mathrm m$ ✓. A cricket ball of $0.16$ kg at $40\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ has $\lambda = 1.0\times10^{-34}$ m, which is why nobody diffracts cricket balls: the wave nature shows only when $\lambda$ is comparable with the structure it meets.

History, briefly. Planck (1900) needed $E = h\nu$ to fit black-body radiation; Einstein (1905) took the lumps literally to explain the photoelectric effect; Compton (1923) measured the momentum. De Broglie's thesis (1924) argued from relativity that the same relations should hold for matter; Davisson and Germer at Bell Labs, and G. P. Thomson in Aberdeen, confirmed it in 1927. None of this is developed here: the unit takes $\lambda = h/p$ as the experimental input and asks what equation a wave of that wavelength obeys.

Mechanics was optics: which wave?

Unit 15's eikonal limit: put $\psi = Ae^{ik_0S}$ into Helmholtz's equation $\nabla^2\psi + k_0^2n^2\psi = 0$; at order $k_0^2$, $\lVert\nabla S\rVert^2 = n^2$. With $n^2 = 2m(E - V)$ that is the time-independent Hamilton–Jacobi equation for $W$.

De Broglie supplies the one missing constant: a phase is a pure number, an action is not, and $\hbar$ converts one into the other.

A wave of phase $W/\hbar$ climbing a potential ramp: as the particle slows, $\lambda = h/p$ grows, and the amplitude grows with it — unit 21's $q^{-1/4}$ with $q = p^2$.

Schrödinger's question (1926): which wave equation has this as its short-wavelength limit? Helmholtz with $k_0 = 1/\hbar$ already gives the stationary answer. The next slides find the time-dependent one.

Helmholtz already answers half the question. Take unit 15's substitution literally: $k_0 = 1/\hbar$ and $n^2 = 2m(E - V)$. Helmholtz's equation becomes $\nabla^2\psi + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)\psi = 0$, which is $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi = E\psi$ — the time-independent Schrödinger equation of section 3, exactly. This is close to the route of Schrödinger's first 1926 paper. What Helmholtz cannot give is the time dependence: it is an equation at one fixed $E$, and a particle whose energy is not sharp needs an equation that does not mention $E$ at all.

The figure. Computed with $\hbar = m = 1$, $V = x$, $E = 10$, phase scaled up by 3 so that several wavelengths fit: $\mathrm{Re}\,\psi = p^{-1/2}\cos\big(\int p\,\mathrm{d}x/\hbar\big)$ with $p = \sqrt{2(E - V)}$. It is the WKB form of unit 21, not an exact solution; its envelope is unit 21's transport equation, which this section is about to meet again as a conservation law.

A trap. The limit "$\hbar\to0$" means: hold the classical data ($W$, the energy, the scale of $V$) fixed and let the wavelength shrink beside them. It does not mean "set a physical constant to zero", which is meaningless; it means "the dimensionless ratio $\hbar/(\text{an action of the problem})$ is small". For an electron in an atom that ratio is of order 1, and no classical limit applies.

21b · which wave

Choosing the equation

Ask for four things:

  1. Linear — waves superpose; Davisson and Germer saw interference.
  2. First order in time — the wave at one instant determines its future (unit 01's determinism, with the wave as the state).
  3. Free plane waves solve it: $e^{i(\vec p\cdot\vec x - Et)/\hbar}$ with $E = p^2/2m$, whose phase is unit 15's free complete integral $S = \vec p\cdot\vec x - Et$.
  4. With a potential, its short-wave limit is Hamilton–Jacobi.

On a plane wave, $i\hbar\partial_t \mapsto E$ and $-i\hbar\nabla \mapsto \vec p$. So 3 says $i\hbar\partial_t\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi$; add $V$ as the simplest local term.

the Schrödinger equation $$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V(\vec x)\,\psi$$

for a complex-valued wavefunction $\psi(\vec x, t)$.

a hypothesis, motivated

This is not a derivation: requirements 1–3 pick the simplest candidate, and requirement 4 is checked on the next two slides. The equation's warrant is experiment.

Why complex: with real coefficients, first order in $t$ is the heat equation, which decays. The $i$ makes $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ oscillate.

Why not the wave equation of unit 18? $\partial_t^2\psi = c^2\nabla^2\psi$ on a plane wave gives $E^2 = c^2p^2$, a linear dispersion relation: right for light, wrong for a particle with $E = p^2/2m$. Any equation with constant coefficients turns, on plane waves, into a polynomial relation between $E$ and $\vec p$ (unit 20: differentiation becomes multiplication), and the lowest-degree polynomial with $E = p^2/2m$ as its root set is $E - p^2/2m$ itself. A second-order-in-time version, $(i\hbar\partial_t)^2\psi = (\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2)^2\psi$, admits $E = -p^2/2m$ as well, and needs $\partial_t\psi$ as extra initial data — the state would no longer be the wave alone.

Unit 18's classification. The equation is first order in $t$ and second order in $x$, like the heat equation $u_t = D\nabla^2u$, and has the same principal symbol up to a constant: it is the heat equation with the imaginary diffusion constant $D = i\hbar/2m$. That one factor of $i$ changes everything: the heat equation smooths and forgets, Schrödinger's preserves $\int|\psi|^2$ and is reversible ($\psi(t) \mapsto \overline{\psi(-t)}$ maps solutions to solutions when $V$ is real).

What "simplest local term" hides. The potential could in principle enter in other ways (for a charged particle in a magnetic field it does: $\nabla$ becomes $\nabla - iq\vec A/\hbar$). For a scalar potential, multiplication is the only choice whose short-wave limit is the right Hamilton–Jacobi equation, and that is what the next slide checks.

Worked: running WKB backwards

substitute $\psi = A\,e^{iS/\hbar}$, $A$ and $S$ real

In one dimension, to keep it short; $\nabla$ replaces $\partial_x$ at the end. Differentiate; each line is to be multiplied by $e^{iS/\hbar}$:

$$\begin{aligned}\psi_t &\to A_t + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}AS_t,\\ \psi_x &\to A_x + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}AS_x,\\ \psi_{xx} &\to A_{xx} + \tfrac{2i}{\hbar}A_xS_x + \tfrac{i}{\hbar}AS_{xx} - \tfrac{1}{\hbar^2}AS_x^2 .\end{aligned}$$

Put them into $i\hbar\psi_t = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi_{xx} + V\psi$ and cancel $e^{iS/\hbar}$:

$$\begin{aligned}i\hbar A_t - AS_t = &-\tfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}A_{xx} - \tfrac{i\hbar}{m}A_xS_x\\ &- \tfrac{i\hbar}{2m}AS_{xx} + \tfrac{1}{2m}AS_x^2 + VA.\end{aligned}$$

Separate. The real part, divided by $-A$:

$$S_t + \frac{S_x^2}{2m} + V = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{A_{xx}}{A}.$$

Imaginary part, times $2A/\hbar$:

$$2AA_t = -\tfrac1m\big(2AA_xS_x + A^2S_{xx}\big),$$

that is,

$$\partial_t(A^2) + \partial_x\Big(A^2\,\frac{S_x}{m}\Big) = 0 .$$

Nothing was neglected: the two lines are the equation, rewritten. Order by order in $\hbar$, the next slide reads them.

Each step. The product rule on $Ae^{iS/\hbar}$ gives $\psi_x$; differentiating again, $\partial_x(A_x + \frac i\hbar AS_x) = A_{xx} + \frac i\hbar(A_xS_x + AS_{xx})$, and the factor $e^{iS/\hbar}$ contributes $(A_x + \frac i\hbar AS_x)\cdot\frac i\hbar S_x = \frac i\hbar A_xS_x - \frac{1}{\hbar^2}AS_x^2$. Summing gives the three-line block, with the two $\frac i\hbar A_xS_x$ terms combining to $\frac{2i}{\hbar}A_xS_x$. Multiplying by $-\hbar^2/2m$: $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}A_{xx} - \frac{i\hbar}{m}A_xS_x - \frac{i\hbar}{2m}AS_{xx} + \frac{1}{2m}AS_x^2$. The left side is $i\hbar(A_t + \frac i\hbar AS_t) = i\hbar A_t - AS_t$.

Real parts: $-AS_t = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}A_{xx} + \frac{1}{2m}AS_x^2 + VA$. Divide by $-A$ (where $A \ne 0$) and rearrange. Imaginary parts: $\hbar A_t = -\frac\hbar mA_xS_x - \frac{\hbar}{2m}AS_{xx}$; times $2A/\hbar$, $2AA_t = -\frac1m(2AA_xS_x + A^2S_{xx})$, and the right side is $-\partial_x(A^2S_x)/m$.

In three dimensions the same computation with $\nabla$ and $\nabla\cdot$ (unit 05) gives $\partial_tS + \lVert\nabla S\rVert^2/2m + V = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2A/A$ and $\partial_t(A^2) + \nabla\cdot(A^2\nabla S/m) = 0$. This polar form is due to Madelung (1926).

Why "backwards". Unit 21 started from a wave equation and assumed $y = e^{S/\varepsilon}$ to find the eikonal (order $\varepsilon^0$) and transport (order $\varepsilon^1$) equations. Here we started from the eikonal equation we wanted — Hamilton–Jacobi — and chose the wave equation so that it comes out. The transport equation comes out too, and it is a conservation law.

A caveat. The polar form fails where $\psi = 0$ ($S$ is undefined at a node). The continuity equation on the next slide but one is derived directly from the Schrödinger equation, and holds everywhere.

21b · which wave

Hamilton–Jacobi at leading order, a conservation law at the next

the short-wavelength limit

With $A$ and $S$ held fixed as $\hbar \to 0$:

order $\hbar^0$: $\ \partial_tS + \dfrac{\lVert\nabla S\rVert^2}{2m} + V = 0$ — unit 15's Hamilton–Jacobi equation;

order $\hbar^1$: $\ \partial_t(A^2) + \nabla\cdot\Big(A^2\,\dfrac{\nabla S}{m}\Big) = 0$ — a density $A^2$ carried at the classical velocity $\nabla S/m = \vec p/m$;

order $\hbar^2$: the quantum potential $Q = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dfrac{\nabla^2A}{A}$, added to $V$.

Dimensions: $\hbar^2\nabla^2A/(mA)$ is $\mathrm{J^2s^2}/(\mathrm{kg\,m^2}) = \mathrm J$, like $\partial_tS$ ✓. Plane wave: $A$ constant, $Q = 0$, and $S = \vec p\cdot\vec x - Et$ solves the free equation exactly ✓.

check, on the oscillator's ground state

$A = e^{-x^2/2\ell^2}$, $\ S = -\tfrac12\hbar\omega t$. Then $S_x = 0$ and

$$Q = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Big(\frac{x^2}{\ell^4} - \frac1{\ell^2}\Big) = -\tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2 + \tfrac12\hbar\omega,$$

using $\ell^2 = \hbar/m\omega$. So $S_t + V + Q = -\tfrac12\hbar\omega + \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2 - \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2 + \tfrac12\hbar\omega = 0$ ✓.

where the limit does not apply

Here $Q$ cancels $V$ completely: a state "at rest everywhere", held up by $Q$. It has no classical limit, because its $A$ depends on $\hbar$ through $\ell$. Low-lying states are quantum; section 6 finds the classical ones at large $n$.

Unit 21's transport equation, recognised. For a stationary state in one dimension, the order-$\hbar$ equation says $A^2S_x = $ const, and $S_x = p(x)$, so $A \propto p^{-1/2}$. With $q = p^2$ that is unit 21's amplitude $q^{-1/4}$ — the same transport equation, read as a conservation law: the probability flux $A^2p/m$ is the same at every $x$.

The ground-state check, in full. $A = e^{-x^2/2\ell^2}$ has $A_x = -\frac{x}{\ell^2}A$ and $A_{xx} = \big(\frac{x^2}{\ell^4} - \frac1{\ell^2}\big)A$. Then $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m\ell^4} = \frac{\hbar^2m^2\omega^2}{2m\hbar^2} = \frac12m\omega^2$ and $\frac{\hbar^2}{2m\ell^2} = \frac{\hbar^2m\omega}{2m\hbar} = \frac12\hbar\omega$, which gives the slide's $Q$. The order-$\hbar$ equation holds trivially: $A^2$ does not depend on $t$ and $S_x = 0$.

The limit, said carefully. "Leading order in $\hbar$" means: for a family of solutions whose $A$ and $S$ have $\hbar$-independent limits, the equations hold in the limit. Section 6 shows that the highly excited oscillator states are such a family, locally: their amplitude is $p^{-1/2}$ and their phase $\int p\,\mathrm{d}x$, both classical objects. The ground state is not, and the warning box is the reason "$\hbar \to 0$" is never a substitute for checking which regime one is in.

History. Madelung wrote this hydrodynamic form in 1926; Bohm (1952) built an interpretation on it, in which $Q$ is a real force. Nothing in this unit depends on any interpretation: the equations are the same.

Probability, and the current that carries it

the Born rule (1926)

$|\psi(\vec x, t)|^2\,\mathrm{d}^3x$ is the probability of finding the particle in $\mathrm{d}^3x$ at time $t$, for $\psi$ with $\int|\psi|^2 = 1$.

the continuity equation

For real $V$, with $\rho = |\psi|^2$ and the probability current $\ \vec j = \frac\hbar m\,\mathrm{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi)$,

$$\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec j = 0 .$$

Why: $\partial_t\rho = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\bar\psi\psi_t)$; substitute $\psi_t$ from the equation; the $V$ term is $\frac{2}{\hbar}\mathrm{Re}(-i V|\psi|^2) = 0$, and what is left is $-\nabla\cdot\vec j$. In polar form $\vec j = A^2\nabla S/m$: the order-$\hbar$ equation of the last slide.

Global conservation. By the divergence theorem (unit 05) over a ball $B_R$,

$$\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{B_R}\!\rho = -\oint_{\partial B_R}\vec j\cdot\hat n\,\mathrm{d}A \;\to\; 0$$

as $R \to \infty$ when $\psi$ decays. Total probability is conserved — the first sign that the equation is consistent with Born's reading.

checks

Plane wave $e^{i(px - Et)/\hbar}$: $j = \rho\,p/m = \rho v$ — probability flows at the classical speed ✓. Real $\psi$ (the oscillator's stationary states): $j = 0$ ✓. Dimensions in 3D: $[\rho] = \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, $[j] = \mathrm{m^{-2}s^{-1}}$ ✓.

Full proof of the continuity equation

From the equation, $\psi_t = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\nabla^2\psi - \frac i\hbar V\psi$. Then $\partial_t|\psi|^2 = \bar\psi\psi_t + \psi\bar\psi_t = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\bar\psi\psi_t)$, and $\bar\psi\psi_t = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\bar\psi\nabla^2\psi - \frac i\hbar V|\psi|^2$. The second term is purely imaginary when $V$ is real, so its real part vanishes. For the first, $\mathrm{Re}(i z) = -\mathrm{Im}\,z$, so $2\,\mathrm{Re}\big(\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\bar\psi\nabla^2\psi\big) = -\frac\hbar m\mathrm{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla^2\psi)$. Finally $\nabla\cdot(\bar\psi\nabla\psi) = \nabla\bar\psi\cdot\nabla\psi + \bar\psi\nabla^2\psi$, and $\nabla\bar\psi\cdot\nabla\psi = \lVert\nabla\psi\rVert^2$ is real, so $\mathrm{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla^2\psi) = \nabla\cdot\mathrm{Im}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi)$. Hence $\partial_t\rho = -\nabla\cdot\vec j$. No polar form was used, so the equation holds at nodes too.

When $V$ is complex, the dropped term survives: $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec j = \frac2\hbar(\mathrm{Im}\,V)\rho$. A negative imaginary part removes probability — an "optical potential" models absorption this way. Problem P3 works it out. It is the first hint of section 2's theme: probability is conserved because the Hamiltonian is (in a sense to be made exact) self-adjoint.

On Born. Born's 1926 paper on collisions stated the rule in a footnote added in proof, correcting the text's $\psi$ to $|\psi|^2$. The rule is a postulate: no derivation of it from the equation exists, and the continuity equation only shows that it is consistent.

What the objects are

A mathematician will ask what a state is, what an observable is, and on what domain. Here are the answers, with the one place where the domain is the physics.

21b · objects

States: unit vectors in $L^2$, up to a phase

the Hilbert space of one particle

$L^2(\mathbb R^3)$: complex-valued $\psi$ with $\int|\psi|^2 < \infty$, identified when they agree almost everywhere, with

$$\langle\varphi, \psi\rangle = \int\bar\varphi\,\psi\,\mathrm{d}^3x .$$

Complete in the metric $\lVert\varphi - \psi\rVert$ (unit 19, cited there).

a state

A unit vector $\psi$, with $\psi$ and $e^{i\alpha}\psi$ the same state: a ray.

convention

Linear in the second slot, as physicists write it. Units 19 and 20 conjugated the second: $\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_{21b} = \langle\psi,\varphi\rangle_{19}$.

Why a ray. Every prediction is $|\langle\chi,\psi\rangle|^2$ or $\langle\psi, A\psi\rangle$, both blind to $\psi \mapsto e^{i\alpha}\psi$. But a relative phase is visible: $|\psi_1 + e^{i\alpha}\psi_2|^2$ depends on $\alpha$ through $2\,\mathrm{Re}(e^{i\alpha}\bar\psi_1\psi_2)$ — interference.

the oscillator's ground state, normalised $$\varphi_0(x) = (\pi\ell^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/2\ell^2},$$

since $\int e^{-x^2/\ell^2}\mathrm{d}x = \sqrt\pi\,\ell$. Its spread is $\Delta x = \ell/\sqrt2$: for CO, $3.37$ pm.

Unit 19 built this space with real scalars; complex scalars change nothing in its proofs except a bar in the right place.

Completeness. Unit 19 stated that $L^2_w$ is metrically complete (the Riesz–Fischer theorem, cited there), which is what "Hilbert space" adds to "inner-product space". The complex case is the same theorem applied to real and imaginary parts.

The spread. $|\varphi_0|^2 = (\pi\ell^2)^{-1/2}e^{-x^2/\ell^2}$ is a normal density with variance $\ell^2/2$, so $\Delta x = \ell/\sqrt2$. For CO, $\mu = 6.856$ u, $\omega = 4.087\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$ (from the spectroscopic $2169.8\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$), so $\ell = \sqrt{\hbar/\mu\omega} = 4.76$ pm and $\Delta x = 3.37$ pm.

Rays, said precisely. The set of states is the projective space of $L^2$: unit vectors modulo the circle group $U(1)$ acting by multiplication. Superposition is a statement about vectors, not rays: adding two rays is not defined until a representative of each is chosen, and the relative phase of the representatives is physical. This is the first of several places where the quantum formalism is "linear algebra, then a quotient".

Observables: position, momentum, and where they come from

position operator and momentum operator $$\begin{aligned}(X\psi)(x) &= x\,\psi(x),\\ (P\psi)(x) &= -i\hbar\,\psi'(x).\end{aligned}$$

In three dimensions, $\vec X$ and $\vec P = -i\hbar\nabla$. An expectation value is $\langle A\rangle_\psi = \langle\psi, A\psi\rangle$.

Why $X$: $\langle\psi, X\psi\rangle = \int x|\psi|^2$ is the mean of the Born density.

Why $P$: write $\psi$ as a superposition of de Broglie waves $e^{ikx}$, $k = p/\hbar$:

$$\psi(x) = \frac1{2\pi}\int\tilde\psi(k)e^{ikx}\,\mathrm{d}k,$$ $$\tilde\psi(k) = \int\psi(x)e^{-ikx}\,\mathrm{d}x .$$
the momentum density

$\Pi(p) = |\tilde\psi(p/\hbar)|^2/2\pi\hbar$ is a probability density, and its mean is $\langle\psi, P\psi\rangle$.

Why: Plancherel (unit 20) gives $\int\Pi = \int|\psi|^2 = 1$; and $\widetilde{\psi'} = ik\tilde\psi$, so the polarised Plancherel identity turns $\int\bar\psi(-i\hbar\psi')$ into $\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\hbar k\,|\tilde\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}k = \int p\,\Pi(p)\,\mathrm{d}p$.

sign

In space the transform carries $e^{-ikx}$, so that $e^{ik_0x}$ has its spectrum at $+k_0$. Unit 20's $\hat f(\omega) = \int fe^{i\omega t}$ was chosen for $e^{-i\omega t}$ in time: $\tilde\psi(k) = \hat\psi(-k)$. Plancherel is unchanged.

On $\varphi_0$: $\tilde\varphi_0$ is again a Gaussian, $\Pi$ has $\Delta p = \hbar/\sqrt2\,\ell$, and $\langle X\rangle = \langle P\rangle = 0$.

Full proof of the momentum mean

Take $\psi$ in the Schwartz class $\mathcal S$ (unit 20). Plancherel in polarised form, with this slide's sign, reads $\int\bar f g\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac1{2\pi}\int\bar{\tilde f}\tilde g\,\mathrm{d}k$ — the substitution $k\mapsto-k$ in unit 20's version. Apply it with $f = \psi$, $g = -i\hbar\psi'$, whose transform is $-i\hbar\cdot ik\tilde\psi = \hbar k\tilde\psi$ (integrate by parts once; the boundary terms vanish for Schwartz functions). Then $\langle\psi, P\psi\rangle = \frac1{2\pi}\int\hbar k|\tilde\psi(k)|^2\,\mathrm{d}k$, and the change of variable $p = \hbar k$ gives $\int p\,\Pi(p)\,\mathrm{d}p$. The same argument with $P^n$ gives every moment of $\Pi$: momentum is to $\tilde\psi$ what position is to $\psi$.

The ground state's momentum density. $\tilde\varphi_0(k) = (\pi\ell^2)^{-1/4}\sqrt{2\pi}\,\ell\,e^{-k^2\ell^2/2}$ (the Gaussian integral of unit 20's analytic-continuation slide), so $|\tilde\varphi_0|^2 \propto e^{-k^2\ell^2}$, with variance $1/2\ell^2$ in $k$ and $\hbar^2/2\ell^2$ in $p$: $\Delta p = \hbar/\sqrt2\,\ell$. Unbounded operators need domains

operators, symmetric, adjoint, self-adjoint

An operator is a linear map $A : D(A) \to \mathcal H$ from a dense subspace, its domain.

Symmetric: $\langle\varphi, A\psi\rangle = \langle A\varphi, \psi\rangle$ for all $\varphi, \psi \in D(A)$.

The adjoint: $D(A^*)$ is the set of $\varphi$ for which $\psi \mapsto \langle\varphi, A\psi\rangle$ is bounded on $D(A)$; then $A^*\varphi$ is the unique $\chi$ with $\langle\varphi, A\psi\rangle = \langle\chi, \psi\rangle$ for all $\psi \in D(A)$.

Self-adjoint: $A = A^*$, domains included.

Symmetric means exactly $A \subset A^*$: the adjoint agrees with $A$ on $D(A)$ and may be defined on more. Self-adjoint means it is defined on nothing more.

why there is no escape (cited)

Hellinger–Toeplitz: a symmetric operator defined on all of $\mathcal H$ is bounded.

And $P$ is unbounded: on a Gaussian of width $s$, $\lVert P\psi\rVert = \hbar/\sqrt2\,s$, which is as large as you like. So $P$ (and $X$, and $H$) live on proper dense subspaces, and which subspace is part of the definition.

In finite dimensions the three notions coincide, and the spectral theorem of linear algebra is the whole story.

The adjoint is well defined. If $\psi\mapsto\langle\varphi, A\psi\rangle$ is bounded on the dense $D(A)$, it extends uniquely to a bounded linear functional on $\mathcal H$, and the Riesz representation theorem (cited: every bounded linear functional on a Hilbert space is $\langle\chi,\cdot\rangle$ for a unique $\chi$ — proved by projecting onto the orthogonal complement of its kernel, Reed and Simon vol. I, §II.2) supplies $\chi$. Density of $D(A)$ is what makes $\chi$ unique; that is why domains are required to be dense.

Symmetric iff $A \subset A^*$. If $A$ is symmetric and $\varphi \in D(A)$, then $\psi\mapsto\langle\varphi, A\psi\rangle = \langle A\varphi,\psi\rangle$ is bounded (by $\lVert A\varphi\rVert$), so $\varphi\in D(A^*)$ and $A^*\varphi = A\varphi$. The converse is the same line read backwards.

The Gaussian estimate. For $\psi = (\pi s^2)^{-1/4}e^{-x^2/2s^2}$, $\psi' = -\frac{x}{s^2}\psi$, so $\lVert P\psi\rVert^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{s^4}\int x^2|\psi|^2 = \frac{\hbar^2}{s^4}\cdot\frac{s^2}2$, and $\lVert P\psi\rVert = \hbar/\sqrt2\,s$ with $\lVert\psi\rVert = 1$. As $s\to0$ this is unbounded.

Hellinger–Toeplitz is cited (Reed and Simon vol. I, §III.5): its proof is the closed graph theorem. Its moral is the slide's: the operators physics needs cannot be everywhere defined, and so a choice of domain is unavoidable. The next slide shows the choice changing the answer.

Momentum in a box: symmetric is not enough

On $L^2(0, L)$, write $P = \hbar D$ with $D = -i\,\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}x$. Integration by parts, for $C^1$ functions:

$$\langle\varphi, D\psi\rangle = \langle D\varphi, \psi\rangle - i\big[\bar\varphi\psi\big]_0^L .$$

Domain 1: $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$. Then $D$ is symmetric. But the boundary term vanishes for every $\varphi$, so $D^*$ is $-i\,\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}x$ with no boundary condition at all — and

$$D^*e^{\kappa x} = -i\kappa\,e^{\kappa x}\quad\text{for every }\kappa\in\mathbb C .$$

The adjoint has every complex number as an eigenvalue. Not self-adjoint.

the self-adjoint extensions $D_\theta$

Domain $\theta$: $\psi(L) = e^{i\theta}\psi(0)$. The boundary term is $\psi(0)\big(e^{i\theta}\bar\varphi(L) - \bar\varphi(0)\big)$, which vanishes for all such $\psi$ iff $\varphi$ obeys the same condition. So $D_\theta^* = D_\theta$.

Eigenfunctions $e^{ikx}$ with $e^{ikL} = e^{i\theta}$:

$$p_n = \frac{\hbar(2\pi n + \theta)}{L},\qquad n\in\mathbb Z .$$

Two choices of boundary condition, two different momentum observables.

Full proof of the boundary identity

$\langle\varphi, D\psi\rangle = \int_0^L\bar\varphi(-i\psi')\,\mathrm{d}x = -i[\bar\varphi\psi]_0^L + i\int_0^L\bar\varphi'\psi\,\mathrm{d}x$, and $i\bar\varphi' = \overline{-i\varphi'} = \overline{D\varphi}$, so the last integral is $\langle D\varphi,\psi\rangle$.

The extension condition. For $\psi(L) = e^{i\theta}\psi(0)$ the boundary term is $\bar\varphi(L)e^{i\theta}\psi(0) - \bar\varphi(0)\psi(0)$. Since $\psi(0)$ can be any complex number, it vanishes for all admissible $\psi$ iff $e^{i\theta}\bar\varphi(L) = \bar\varphi(0)$, that is $\varphi(L) = e^{i\theta}\varphi(0)$ — the same condition. So the adjoint's domain is the operator's own.

What "$C^1$" stands in for. The honest domains are not $C^1$ functions but the absolutely continuous $\psi$ with $\psi'\in L^2$ (the Sobolev space $H^1(0,L)$): with $C^1$ the operator is symmetric but not closed, and $D^*$ lands in $H^1$. Every statement on the slide is true with $C^1$ replaced by $H^1$; the proofs are the same integration by parts (Hall, Quantum Theory for Mathematicians, ch. 9, works this example in full).

Physics of $\theta$. $\theta = 0$ is periodic: a particle on a ring of circumference $L$. $\theta \ne 0$ is a ring threaded by a magnetic flux (the Aharonov–Bohm phase), or a crystal electron at a fixed Bloch momentum. The spectra differ, and so the physics does: the boundary condition is not a technicality, it is part of the system.

Why self-adjoint: Stone's theorem

Evolution $\psi(t) = U(t)\psi(0)$ should be linear, conserve probability, compose, and depend continuously on $t$: a unitary group,

$$U(t + s) = U(t)U(s),\quad U(0) = I,$$

each $U(t)$ unitary, and $t \mapsto U(t)\psi$ continuous for each $\psi$.

Stone's theorem (cited)

Such groups correspond exactly to self-adjoint operators $H$, via $U(t) = e^{-iHt/\hbar}$ and $H\psi = i\hbar\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}U(t)\psi\big|_{t=0}$ on the $\psi$ where the derivative exists.

So: the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial_t\psi = H\psi$ is a probability-conserving dynamics for every initial state iff $H$ is self-adjoint. A merely symmetric $H$ gives none (indices unequal) or many (one per extension): the dynamics is not yet defined.

The easy half: if $\psi(t) \in D(H)$ solves the equation and $H$ is symmetric, $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\big(\langle\psi, H\psi\rangle - \langle H\psi, \psi\rangle\big) = 0$.

the free particle, explicitly

In Fourier space $H = P^2/2m$ is multiplication by $\hbar^2k^2/2m$, so

$$\widetilde{U(t)\psi}(k) = e^{-i\hbar k^2t/2m}\,\tilde\psi(k),$$

unitary by Plancherel, since the factor has modulus 1.

Stone's theorem is cited: Reed and Simon vol. I, §VIII.4; Hall, ch. 10. One direction needs the spectral theorem for unbounded self-adjoint operators (to define $e^{-iHt/\hbar}$); the other shows the generator of a strongly continuous unitary group is densely defined, symmetric, and has no deficiency, by a resolvent computation. Neither proof is long once the spectral theorem is in hand, and neither is attempted here.

The easy half, in full. $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\psi,\psi\rangle = \langle\dot\psi,\psi\rangle + \langle\psi,\dot\psi\rangle$ with $\dot\psi = \frac{1}{i\hbar}H\psi$. The inner product is conjugate-linear in the first slot, so $\langle\frac{1}{i\hbar}H\psi,\psi\rangle = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle H\psi,\psi\rangle$, and the sum is $\frac{1}{i\hbar}(\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle - \langle H\psi,\psi\rangle) = 0$ by symmetry. What this does not show is that a solution exists for every $\psi(0)$, or that it is unique; that is the hard half, and it is exactly where self-adjointness is spent.

Continuity of the free group. $\lVert U(t)\psi - \psi\rVert^2 = \frac1{2\pi}\int|e^{-i\hbar k^2t/2m} - 1|^2|\tilde\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}k$. Split at $|k| = K$. The tail is at most $\frac{4}{2\pi}\int_{|k|>K}|\tilde\psi|^2$, small for large $K$; on $|k|\le K$ use $|e^{i\theta} - 1|\le|\theta|$ to bound the rest by $(\hbar K^2t/2m)^2\lVert\psi\rVert^2$, which tends to 0 with $t$. Section 3 solves this group explicitly on a Gaussian.

Spectrum, and what a measurement returns

the spectrum

$\sigma(A)$ is the set of $\lambda\in\mathbb C$ for which $A - \lambda : D(A) \to \mathcal H$ is not a bijection with bounded inverse. For self-adjoint $A$, $\sigma(A) \subset \mathbb R$.

the measurement postulate

Measuring $A$ returns a value in $\sigma(A)$. If $A$ has an orthonormal eigenbasis $e_n$ with $Ae_n = a_ne_n$,

$$\Pr(a_n) = |\langle e_n, \psi\rangle|^2,$$ $$\langle A\rangle_\psi = \sum_n a_n|\langle e_n,\psi\rangle|^2 .$$

The probabilities sum to $\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = 1$: Parseval (unit 19).

Continuous spectrum. $X$ and $P$ on $L^2(\mathbb R)$ have no eigenvectors at all: $X\psi = x_0\psi$ forces $(x - x_0)\psi = 0$, so $\psi = 0$ almost everywhere. Their "eigenvectors" $\delta_{x_0}$ and $e^{ip_0x/\hbar}$ are distributions (unit 20), and $\sigma(X) = \sigma(P) = \mathbb R$. Their outcomes come with densities instead: $|\psi|^2$ and $\Pi$.

the oscillator

Its Hamiltonian has a pure point spectrum $\{\hbar\omega(n + \frac12)\}$ with an orthonormal eigenbasis — sections 3 and 4 prove both. A measurement of energy returns one of those numbers and nothing else.

The real-eigenvalue half of "$\sigma(A)\subset\mathbb R$" is one line: $\lambda\lVert e\rVert^2 = \langle e, Ae\rangle = \langle Ae, e\rangle = \bar\lambda\lVert e\rVert^2$. Problem P4.

"$\sigma(A)\subset\mathbb R$" in full is cited (Reed and Simon vol. I, §VIII.1); the eigenvalue case is the one line on the slide. The general case: for $\lambda = \mu + i\nu$ with $\nu \ne 0$, $\lVert(A - \lambda)\psi\rVert^2 = \lVert(A - \mu)\psi\rVert^2 + \nu^2\lVert\psi\rVert^2 \ge \nu^2\lVert\psi\rVert^2$ (expand, using symmetry to kill the cross term), so $A - \lambda$ is injective with a bounded inverse on its range; self-adjointness is what makes the range everything. For a merely symmetric operator the range can be a proper closed subspace — its orthogonal complement is exactly a deficiency subspace.

The postulate, honestly. The measurement postulate is a postulate: what a measurement is physically, and why outcomes are random, is the subject of a literature this unit does not enter. The mathematical content is that a self-adjoint operator and a unit vector determine a probability measure on $\sigma(A)$ — the spectral measure, which for the discrete case is the list $|\langle e_n,\psi\rangle|^2$ and for $X$ is $|\psi|^2\,\mathrm{d}x$.

Why distributions are the right home for $e^{ipx/\hbar}$. Unit 20 computed $\hat1 = 2\pi\delta$: the plane waves are orthonormal "to a delta", $\langle e^{ipx/\hbar}, e^{ip'x/\hbar}\rangle = 2\pi\hbar\,\delta(p - p')$, which is the continuous version of $\langle e_n, e_m\rangle = \delta_{nm}$, and the factor $2\pi\hbar$ is the one in the momentum density $\Pi$.

The commutator replaces the bracket

the canonical commutation relation

On the Schwartz class $\mathcal S$, which $X$ and $P$ both map into itself,

$$[X, P] = XP - PX = i\hbar .$$

Proof: $[X,P]\psi = x(-i\hbar\psi') + i\hbar(x\psi)' = i\hbar\psi$. $\square$

Unit 14: $\{x, p\} = 1$, and $\dot f = \{f, H\}$. Dirac's rule (1925) replaces the bracket by a commutator over $i\hbar$:

$$\{f, g\} \;\longmapsto\; \frac{1}{i\hbar}\,[\hat f, \hat g] .$$

It reproduces $[X,P] = i\hbar$, and on the oscillator gives $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle X\rangle = \langle P\rangle/m$, $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle P\rangle = -m\omega^2\langle X\rangle$: Newton, for the means.

Groenewold's theorem (1946, cited)

There is no linear map $f \mapsto \hat f$ from polynomials in $(x, p)$ to symmetric operators with $\hat 1 = I$, $\hat x = X$, $\hat p = P$ (irreducibly) and $\widehat{\{f,g\}} = [\hat f, \hat g]/i\hbar$ for all $f, g$.

where it breaks: degree four

Classically $x^2p^2 = \frac19\{x^3, p^3\} = \frac13\{x^2p, xp^2\}$. Quantised with symmetric ordering, the two routes differ by $\frac13\hbar^2$.

Dirac's rule is a heuristic, exact up to quadratic polynomials, wrong at order $\hbar^2$ beyond. Quantisation is not a functor.

The oscillator's means. The time derivative of an expectation value is $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\psi, A\psi\rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H, A]\rangle$ (differentiate, substitute $\dot\psi = H\psi/i\hbar$, and use the symmetry of $H$ as in the Stone slide's easy half). With $H = P^2/2m + \frac12m\omega^2X^2$: $[H, X] = \frac{1}{2m}[P^2, X] = \frac{1}{2m}(P[P,X] + [P,X]P) = -\frac{i\hbar}{m}P$, and $[H, P] = \frac12m\omega^2[X^2, P] = i\hbar m\omega^2X$. So $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle X\rangle = \langle P\rangle/m$ and $\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle P\rangle = -m\omega^2\langle X\rangle$ — unit 06's oscillator, exactly, for the means. For a general $V$ one gets $-\langle V'(X)\rangle$, which is not $-V'(\langle X\rangle)$ unless $V'$ is linear; problem P5 is Ehrenfest's theorem and its limits.

The degree-four computation

Classically, $\{x^3, p^3\} = 3x^2\cdot3p^2 = 9x^2p^2$ and $\{x^2p, xp^2\} = (2xp)(2xp) - x^2p^2 = 3x^2p^2$. Groenewold's argument first shows the rule forces $\widehat{x^3} = X^3$, $\widehat{p^3} = P^3$, $\widehat{x^2p} = \frac12(X^2P + PX^2)$ and $\widehat{xp^2} = \frac12(XP^2 + P^2X)$ (from brackets with $x$, $p$ and the quadratics, plus irreducibility). Acting on a test function $f(x)$, one computes $\frac{1}{9i\hbar}[X^3, P^3]f = -\hbar^2\big(x^2f'' + 2xf' + \frac23f\big)$ and $\frac{1}{3i\hbar}[\widehat{x^2p}, \widehat{xp^2}]f = -\hbar^2\big(x^2f'' + 2xf' + \frac13f\big)$. They differ by $-\frac13\hbar^2f$, so no assignment of $\widehat{x^2p^2}$ satisfies both. (Checked symbolically.)

Where to read it. Groenewold, Physica 12 (1946); van Hove (1951) for the general form; Hall, ch. 13, for a textbook proof; Gotay, J. Math. Phys. 40 (1999), for the clean statement. The lesson is the one on the slide: quantisation is an act of choice, made consistent by experiment, and the classical limit is an asymptotic statement in $\hbar$, not an identity.

Robertson's inequality

Robertson's inequality (1929)

For symmetric $A, B$ and a unit vector $\psi$ in the domains of $AB$ and $BA$, with $\Delta A = \lVert(A - \langle A\rangle)\psi\rVert$,

$$\Delta A\,\Delta B \;\ge\; \tfrac12\,\big|\langle[A, B]\rangle_\psi\big| .$$
with $[X, P] = i\hbar$: the Heisenberg uncertainty relation $$\Delta x\,\Delta p \;\ge\; \frac\hbar2 .$$
proof, by Cauchy–Schwarz

Put $A' = A - \langle A\rangle$, $B' = B - \langle B\rangle$; they are symmetric and $[A', B'] = [A, B]$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}\langle\psi, [A, B]\psi\rangle &= \langle A'\psi, B'\psi\rangle - \langle B'\psi, A'\psi\rangle\\ &= 2i\,\mathrm{Im}\langle A'\psi, B'\psi\rangle .\end{aligned}$$

So $|\langle[A,B]\rangle| \le 2|\langle A'\psi, B'\psi\rangle| \le 2\lVert A'\psi\rVert\,\lVert B'\psi\rVert = 2\,\Delta A\,\Delta B$. $\square$

Dimensions: $\mathrm m\cdot\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{J\,s}$ ✓. Equality needs $B'\psi$ parallel to $A'\psi$ with a purely imaginary ratio. For $X, P$ that is $\psi' = -c\,x\psi$, $c > 0$: a Gaussian — $\varphi_0$, with $\Delta x\,\Delta p = \frac{\ell}{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{\sqrt2\,\ell} = \frac\hbar2$.

Full proof, the steps the slide compresses

Symmetry of $A'$: $\langle\varphi, A'\psi\rangle = \langle\varphi, A\psi\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle$, and $\langle A\rangle$ is real because $A$ is symmetric, so this equals $\langle A'\varphi,\psi\rangle$. The commutator: the constants commute with everything. Then $\langle\psi, A'B'\psi\rangle = \langle A'\psi, B'\psi\rangle$ and $\langle\psi, B'A'\psi\rangle = \langle B'\psi, A'\psi\rangle = \overline{\langle A'\psi, B'\psi\rangle}$, and $z - \bar z = 2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,z$. Finally $|\mathrm{Im}\,z| \le |z|$ and Cauchy–Schwarz in $L^2$.

The equality case. Both inequalities must be equalities: Cauchy–Schwarz forces $B'\psi = \lambda A'\psi$, and $|\mathrm{Im}\,z| = |z|$ forces $\langle A'\psi, B'\psi\rangle = \lambda\lVert A'\psi\rVert^2$ to be imaginary, so $\lambda = -i\mu$ with $\mu$ real. For $A = X$, $B = P$ with means removed: $-i\hbar\psi' = -i\mu x\psi$, so $\psi' = -(\mu/\hbar)x\psi$, and square-integrability needs $\mu > 0$. The solution, unique by unit 01, is a Gaussian.

History. Heisenberg (1927) gave the relation as a heuristic from a thought experiment; Kennard (1927) proved $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$; Robertson (1929) proved the general form. The relation is a theorem about the state, not about disturbance by a measuring apparatus — a distinction Heisenberg's original paper blurred.

Separating time

Try $\psi(x,t) = \varphi(x)\,T(t)$ (unit 19's separation of variables). Then $i\hbar\dot T/T = H\varphi/\varphi = E$, a constant, so $T = e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ and

the time-independent Schrödinger equation $$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\varphi'' + V\varphi = E\varphi .$$

A Sturm–Liouville problem with $p = \hbar^2/2m$, $q = V$, $w = 1$, eigenvalue $E$.

A stationary state $\varphi e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ has $|\psi|^2$ independent of $t$: nothing observable moves.

unit 19's theorems, doing the work

On an interval with separated conditions, or on $\mathbb R$ with $V \to +\infty$ at both ends (cited):

  • real, simple eigenvalues $E_0 < E_1 < \cdots \to \infty$: energy quantisation;
  • orthogonal eigenfunctions, complete in $L^2$ (cited in unit 19);
  • $\varphi_n$ has exactly $n$ zeros (oscillation theorem);
  • $E_0 = \min$ of the Rayleigh quotient $> \min V$.

Then $\psi(t) = \sum_n c_n\varphi_ne^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ with $c_n = \langle\varphi_n, \psi(0)\rangle$, and $\Pr(E_n) = |c_n|^2$ never changes. Nothing was quantised by hand: the boundary conditions did it.

Free evolution, solved by Fourier

The oscillator, nondimensionalised

The oscillator's stationary equation:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\varphi'' + \tfrac12m\omega^2x^2\varphi = E\varphi .$$

From $\hbar$, $m$, $\omega$ there is one length, $\ell = \sqrt{\hbar/m\omega}$, and one energy, $\hbar\omega$ (unit 00's Buckingham count: three quantities, three dimensions, no free number). Put $\xi = x/\ell$, $\varepsilon = 2E/\hbar\omega$:

the scaled equation $$\varphi'' + (\varepsilon - \xi^2)\,\varphi = 0,\qquad {}' = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi} .$$

The ends, by dominant balance (unit 21). For large $|\xi|$ the equation is $\varphi'' \approx \xi^2\varphi$, and $e^{\pm\xi^2/2}$ balance it:

$$\big(e^{\pm\xi^2/2}\big)'' = (\xi^2 \pm 1)\,e^{\pm\xi^2/2}.$$

Only $e^{-\xi^2/2}$ is square-integrable — and with the lower sign that line is exact at $\varepsilon = 1$. So peel it off:

$$\varphi = h(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}.$$
checks

$[\ell] = \sqrt{\mathrm{J\,s}/(\mathrm{kg\,s^{-1}})} = \mathrm m$ ✓. $\varepsilon = 1$: $E = \frac12\hbar\omega$, the floor found by inequalities on The same theorem as unit 20's ✓.

The scaling, in full. With $x = \ell\xi$, $\frac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d}x^2} = \ell^{-2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2}{\mathrm{d}\xi^2}$, so the kinetic term is $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m\ell^2}\varphi'' = -\frac{\hbar\omega}{2}\varphi''$ and the potential term is $\frac12m\omega^2\ell^2\xi^2\varphi = \frac{\hbar\omega}2\xi^2\varphi$. Divide the equation by $\hbar\omega/2$: $-\varphi'' + \xi^2\varphi = \varepsilon\varphi$.

Dominant balance. Try $\varphi = e^{S(\xi)}$: $S'' + S'^2 = \xi^2 - \varepsilon$. For large $\xi$, the balance $S'^2 \sim \xi^2$ gives $S \sim \pm\xi^2/2$, and the correction $S''$ is then $\pm1$, of the same order as $\varepsilon$ — so the next term is a power, $\varphi \sim \xi^{(\varepsilon - 1)/2}e^{-\xi^2/2}$ for the decaying one. The power is exactly what the polynomial $h$ supplies below.

CO, for scale. With $\hbar\omega = 0.269$ eV and $\ell = 4.76$ pm, one unit of $\xi$ is a few picometres and one unit of $\varepsilon$ is $0.135$ eV. The equation itself has no parameters left: every harmonic oscillator in nature is this one equation.

Worked: the series, and why it must stop

route one: a power series that must stop

1. The equation for $h$. With $\varphi = he^{-\xi^2/2}$, $\ \varphi'' = (h'' - 2\xi h' + (\xi^2 - 1)h)e^{-\xi^2/2}$, so $h$ obeys Hermite's equation:

$$h'' - 2\xi h' + (\varepsilon - 1)h = 0 .$$

2. The series. $\xi = 0$ is an ordinary point and the coefficients are polynomials, so $h = \sum a_k\xi^k$ converges for every $\xi$ (unit 09). Matching $\xi^k$:

$$(k + 2)(k + 1)a_{k+2} - 2k\,a_k + (\varepsilon - 1)a_k = 0,$$ $$a_{k+2} = \frac{2k + 1 - \varepsilon}{(k + 1)(k + 2)}\;a_k .$$

Even and odd series separate, started by $a_0$ and $a_1$.

3. If it does not stop, $a_{k+2}/a_k \sim 2/k$. The series of $e^{\xi^2} = \sum\xi^{2j}/j!$ has ratio $\frac{1}{j+1} = \frac{2}{k + 2}$ at $k = 2j$: the same. So $h$ grows like $e^{\xi^2}$ and $\varphi$ like $e^{\xi^2/2}$ — not in $L^2$.

4. It stops iff $2n + 1 - \varepsilon = 0$ for some $n$, with the other parity's series switched off ($a_1 = 0$ for even $n$, $a_0 = 0$ for odd). So

$$\varepsilon = 2n + 1,\qquad E_n = \hbar\omega\big(n + \tfrac12\big).$$

5. The first three. $\varepsilon = 1$: $h = 1$. $\ \varepsilon = 3$: $h = \xi$. $\ \varepsilon = 5$: $a_2 = \frac{1 - 5}{2}a_0$, $h = 1 - 2\xi^2$.

Step 1. $\varphi' = (h' - \xi h)e^{-\xi^2/2}$ and $\varphi'' = (h'' - h - \xi h' - \xi(h' - \xi h))e^{-\xi^2/2} = (h'' - 2\xi h' + (\xi^2 - 1)h)e^{-\xi^2/2}$. Adding $(\varepsilon - \xi^2)\varphi$ cancels $\xi^2h$ and leaves $h'' - 2\xi h' + (\varepsilon - 1)h$.

Step 2. $h'' = \sum(k + 2)(k + 1)a_{k+2}\xi^k$ and $\xi h' = \sum ka_k\xi^k$. Unit 09's theorem at an ordinary point gives a radius of convergence at least the distance to the nearest singularity of the coefficients, and polynomial coefficients have none.

Full proof of step 3

Suppose the even series does not terminate (the odd one is the same). Fix $\delta\in(0,1)$. Since $\frac{2k + 1 - \varepsilon}{k + 1}\to 2$, there is an even $K$ beyond which $\frac{a_{k+2}}{a_k} \ge \frac{2(1 - \delta)}{k + 2} > 0$; so the $a_k$ with $k\ge K$ all have the sign of $a_K$, say positive. With $k = K + 2j$ and $J = K/2$, $a_{K + 2j} \ge a_K\prod_{i=0}^{j-1}\frac{1 - \delta}{J + i + 1} = a_K\,(1 - \delta)^j\frac{J!}{(J + j)!}$. Hence for $\xi > 0$, $\sum_{j\ge0}a_{K+2j}\xi^{K+2j} \ge a_KJ!\,\xi^{K}\big((1 - \delta)\xi^2\big)^{-J}\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{((1 - \delta)\xi^2)^{J+j}}{(J + j)!}$, and the last sum is $e^{(1-\delta)\xi^2}$ minus a polynomial. The finitely many terms with $k < K$ are a polynomial. So $h(\xi) \ge c\,e^{(1-\delta)\xi^2}$ for large $\xi$ and some $c > 0$, and $\varphi \ge c\,e^{(1/2 - \delta)\xi^2}$, not square-integrable for $\delta < \frac12$. $\square$

Step 4, the other parity. At $\varepsilon = 2n + 1$ with $n$ even, the odd series has $a_{k+2}/a_k = (2k - 2n)/((k+1)(k+2))$ with $k$ odd, never zero, so it does not terminate and must be absent: $a_1 = 0$. The eigenfunction is purely even. Check against unit 19: one eigenfunction per eigenvalue — simple, as the theory promised.

The even solution of $\varphi'' = (\xi^2 - \varepsilon)\varphi$ with $\varphi(0) = 1$, $\varphi'(0) = 0$, integrated numerically at $\varepsilon = 0.9$, $1$ and $1.1$.

physics

A particle in a well cannot have any energy because a wave in a well must fit: decaying on both sides, matched in the middle. That is all quantisation is.

This is the "shooting method", and it is how eigenvalues of problems with no series solution are found in practice.

Hermite functions

Hermite functions $$\varphi_n(x) = \frac{H_n(x/\ell)\,e^{-x^2/2\ell^2}}{\sqrt{2^nn!\sqrt\pi\,\ell}}$$

$H_0 = 1$, $H_1 = 2\xi$, $H_2 = 4\xi^2 - 2$, $H_3 = 8\xi^3 - 12\xi$: the terminating series, scaled to leading coefficient $2^n$.

Orthogonal: different eigenvalues of one Sturm–Liouville problem with $w = 1$ (unit 19; the Gaussian kills the boundary term). $n$ nodes, by the oscillation theorem. Complete in $L^2(\mathbb R)$: proof in the notes, from unit 20.

Hermite's equation itself is unit 19's "fourth row": $p = w = e^{-\xi^2}$ on $\mathbb R$.

Full proof of completeness, from unit 20

Suppose $f\in L^2(\mathbb R)$ is orthogonal to every $\varphi_n$. The span of $\varphi_0,\dots,\varphi_n$ is the span of $\xi^ke^{-\xi^2/2}$, $k\le n$, so $\int f(\xi)\xi^ke^{-\xi^2/2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 0$ for every $k$. Let $F(z) = \int f(\xi)e^{-\xi^2/2}e^{-iz\xi}\,\mathrm{d}\xi$. The Gaussian makes the integral converge for every complex $z$, and $F$ is entire (Morera, as in unit 20's analytic-continuation slide). Differentiating under the integral, $F^{(k)}(0) = \int f(\xi)(-i\xi)^ke^{-\xi^2/2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi = 0$ for every $k$. An entire function with all derivatives zero at a point is zero (it equals its Taylor series). So the Fourier transform of $fe^{-\xi^2/2}$ vanishes on $\mathbb R$, and by Plancherel $fe^{-\xi^2/2} = 0$ in $L^2$, hence $f = 0$. So the only vector orthogonal to all $\varphi_n$ is $0$, which in the complete space $L^2$ is completeness (unit 19's remark on the converse). $\square$

21b · ladder

Ladder operators

ladder operators $$a = \frac{m\omega X + iP}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}},\qquad a^\dagger = \frac{m\omega X - iP}{\sqrt{2m\hbar\omega}} .$$

In $\xi$: $a = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\xi + \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi})$, $a^\dagger = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\xi - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi})$. On $\mathcal S$, $\langle\varphi, a\psi\rangle = \langle a^\dagger\varphi, \psi\rangle$.

Where they come from. Classically $\alpha = (m\omega x + ip)/\sqrt{2m\omega}$ obeys $\dot\alpha = -i\omega\alpha$ and $H = \omega|\alpha|^2$: $|\alpha|^2$ is unit 15's action $I$, $\arg\alpha$ minus its angle. $a$ is the quantised complex amplitude, over $\sqrt\hbar$.

two lines

$[m\omega X + iP,\ m\omega X - iP] = -im\omega[X, P] + im\omega[P, X] = 2m\omega\hbar$, so

$$[a, a^\dagger] = 1 .$$

$(m\omega X - iP)(m\omega X + iP) = m^2\omega^2X^2 + P^2 + im\omega[X, P] = 2mH - m\omega\hbar$, so

$$H = \hbar\omega\big(a^\dagger a + \tfrac12\big) .$$

Call $N = a^\dagger a$, the number operator. The spectrum of $H$ is $\hbar\omega(\sigma(N) + \frac12)$, and the next slide finds $\sigma(N)$ without solving anything.

The adjoint. $X$ and $P$ are symmetric on $\mathcal S$ and $\mathcal S$ is mapped into itself by both, so $\langle\varphi, (m\omega X + iP)\psi\rangle = \langle(m\omega X - iP)\varphi,\psi\rangle$ — the $i$ is conjugated as it moves to the first slot. Strictly, $a^\dagger$ restricted to $\mathcal S$ is the restriction of the adjoint $a^*$; the dagger is the physicists' name.

The classical amplitude. Hamilton's equations $\dot x = p/m$, $\dot p = -m\omega^2x$ give $\dot\alpha = (m\omega\dot x + i\dot p)/\sqrt{2m\omega} = (\omega p - im\omega^2x)/\sqrt{2m\omega} = -i\omega\alpha$, and $\omega|\alpha|^2 = \frac{m^2\omega^2x^2 + p^2}{2m} = H$. In unit 15's action–angle variables $\alpha = \sqrt{I}e^{-iw}$: the action is the squared modulus, the angle is the phase. Quantising, $N + \frac12 = a^\dagger a + \frac12$ is the action counted in units of $\hbar$ — the Bohr–Sommerfeld slide will reach the same statement, $I = \hbar(n + \frac12)$, from the other side.

The ordering term. $(m\omega X - iP)(m\omega X + iP)$ expands to $m^2\omega^2X^2 + im\omega XP - im\omega PX + P^2$, and the middle pair is $im\omega[X,P] = im\omega\cdot i\hbar = -m\omega\hbar$. Dividing by $2m\hbar\omega$: $a^\dagger a = \frac{H}{\hbar\omega} - \frac12$. Classically the cross terms cancel and $|\alpha|^2 = H/\omega$ exactly: the $\frac12$ is the price of not commuting.

21b · ladder · example

Worked: the spectrum from the algebra alone

route two: four facts, and a ladder that must stop below

1. $N \ge 0$. $\langle\psi, N\psi\rangle = \langle a\psi, a\psi\rangle = \lVert a\psi\rVert^2 \ge 0$.

2. $a$ lowers, $a^\dagger$ raises. $[N, a] = [a^\dagger, a]a = -a$ and $[N, a^\dagger] = a^\dagger$. So if $N\psi = \nu\psi$,

$$\begin{aligned}N(a\psi) &= (\nu - 1)\,a\psi,\\ N(a^\dagger\psi) &= (\nu + 1)\,a^\dagger\psi .\end{aligned}$$

3. Norms. $\lVert a\psi\rVert^2 = \nu\lVert\psi\rVert^2$ and $\lVert a^\dagger\psi\rVert^2 = \langle\psi, aa^\dagger\psi\rangle = (\nu + 1)\lVert\psi\rVert^2$.

4. The ground state. $a\varphi_0 = 0$ is $(\xi + \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi})\varphi_0 = 0$: one solution up to scale, $\varphi_0 = Ce^{-\xi^2/2}$ (unit 01).

5. The ladder stops. Let $N\psi = \nu\psi$, $\psi \ne 0$. Lower $k$ times: $a^k\psi$ has eigenvalue $\nu - k$ and, by 3,

$$\lVert a^k\psi\rVert^2 = \nu(\nu - 1)\cdots(\nu - k + 1)\,\lVert\psi\rVert^2 .$$

If $\nu \notin \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, no factor vanishes, so every $a^k\psi \ne 0$ — and for $k > \nu$ it is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\nu - k < 0$, against 1. Contradiction.

So $\nu = n$, and the descent stops exactly when it reaches $a^n\psi$, a multiple of $\varphi_0$ by 4. Raising from $\varphi_0$ never stops, by 3. Hence

$$\sigma(N) = \{0, 1, 2, \dots\},\ \text{each once};$$ $$E_n = \hbar\omega\big(n + \tfrac12\big).$$

Fact 2. $[N, a] = a^\dagger aa - aa^\dagger a = (a^\dagger a - aa^\dagger)a = -[a, a^\dagger]a = -a$. Then $Na\psi = (aN + [N,a])\psi = a\nu\psi - a\psi$. Likewise $[N, a^\dagger] = a^\dagger aa^\dagger - a^\dagger a^\dagger a = a^\dagger[a, a^\dagger] = a^\dagger$.

Fact 3. $\lVert a\psi\rVert^2 = \langle\psi, a^\dagger a\psi\rangle = \nu\lVert\psi\rVert^2$, and $aa^\dagger = a^\dagger a + 1 = N + 1$.

Why the descent stops exactly at $\varphi_0$. With $\nu = n$, the norm formula gives $\lVert a^n\psi\rVert^2 = n!\lVert\psi\rVert^2 \ne 0$ and $\lVert a^{n+1}\psi\rVert^2 = 0$. So $a^n\psi$ is a nonzero solution of $a\chi = 0$, hence a multiple of $\varphi_0$. Running the argument upwards, $\psi$ is a multiple of $(a^\dagger)^n\varphi_0$ (apply $a^\dagger$ $n$ times to $a^n\psi$ and use $a^\dagger a = N$ on each eigenspace), so each eigenvalue is simple.

Domains. Every vector in sight is in $\mathcal S$, which $a$ and $a^\dagger$ preserve, so every step is legitimate there. That every $L^2$ eigenvector of $H$ lies in $\mathcal S$, and that $N$ is self-adjoint on its natural domain, is cited (Hall, ch. 11); the series route of section 3 already showed the eigenvectors are Hermite functions, which settles it for this operator. Problem P7 asks for this proof with every line justified.

matching route one

$\sqrt2\,a^\dagger = \xi - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi}$ sends $H_ne^{-\xi^2/2}$ to $(2\xi H_n - H_n')e^{-\xi^2/2}$. From $H_0 = 1$: $2\xi$, then $4\xi^2 - 2$, then $8\xi^3 - 12\xi$ — the series' polynomials, leading coefficient $2^n$ ✓.

The recurrence. $(\xi - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi})(H_ne^{-\xi^2/2}) = \xi H_ne^{-\xi^2/2} - H_n'e^{-\xi^2/2} + \xi H_ne^{-\xi^2/2} = (2\xi H_n - H_n')e^{-\xi^2/2}$. Iterating the operator is Rodrigues' formula $H_n = (-1)^ne^{\xi^2}\frac{\mathrm{d}^n}{\mathrm{d}\xi^n}e^{-\xi^2}$, as unit 10's Legendre polynomials had theirs.

The normalisation. By fact 3, $\lVert a^\dagger\varphi_n\rVert^2 = (n + 1)\lVert\varphi_n\rVert^2$, so $\lVert(a^\dagger)^n\varphi_0\rVert^2 = n!$. With $\varphi_0 = \pi^{-1/4}e^{-\xi^2/2}$ (in $\xi$, $\ell = 1$) and $(a^\dagger)^n = 2^{-n/2}(\xi - \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\xi})^n$: $(a^\dagger)^n\varphi_0 = 2^{-n/2}\pi^{-1/4}H_ne^{-\xi^2/2}$, whose squared norm is $2^{-n}\pi^{-1/2}\int H_n^2e^{-\xi^2}$. Setting that equal to $n!$ gives $\int H_n^2e^{-\xi^2} = 2^nn!\sqrt\pi$.

Where this route's $\frac12$ came from: the ordering. $H = \hbar\omega(a^\dagger a + \frac12)$, and the $\frac12$ is $\frac12[a, a^\dagger]$; classically $H = \omega|\alpha|^2$ has none. The floor is the commutator.

Beyond the oscillator. The same algebra — an operator, its adjoint, a commutator of 1 — describes a mode of the electromagnetic field, where $n$ counts photons. That is field quantisation, and this coda stops before it.

section 5

WKB revisited: quantisation and tunnelling

Unit 21's ansatz with $\varepsilon = \hbar$. Route three to the oscillator, and why alpha decay spans 24 orders of magnitude.

21b · WKB

The Schrödinger equation is unit 21's equation

Multiply the stationary equation by $-2m$:

$$\hbar^2\varphi'' + p(x)^2\,\varphi = 0,$$ $$p(x) = \sqrt{2m(E - V(x))} .$$

This is unit 21's $\varepsilon^2y'' + q\,y = 0$ with $\varepsilon = \hbar$ and $q = p^2$: the local classical momentum squared.

WKB, read physically

Allowed ($E > V$):

$$\varphi \approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{p}}\cos\Big(\frac1\hbar\!\int^x\!\! p + \phi\Big).$$

Forbidden ($E < V$): $\ \varphi \approx \dfrac{C_\pm}{\sqrt{|p|}}\,e^{\pm\frac1\hbar\int^x|p|\,\mathrm{d}x'}$.

The amplitude is classical. $|\varphi|^2$, averaged over a wavelength, is $\propto 1/p \propto 1/v$: the particle is found where it moves slowly, in proportion to the time it spends there. Section 6 makes this exact.

validity, from unit 21

$\varepsilon|q'|/4q^{3/2} \ll 1$ becomes, with $\bar\lambda = \hbar/p$,

$$\Big|\frac{\mathrm{d}\bar\lambda}{\mathrm{d}x}\Big| \ll 2 :$$

the reduced de Broglie wavelength changes little over one wavelength. It fails at turning points, $p = 0$, where unit 21's connection formulae take over.

The validity condition, translated. With $q = p^2$, $q' = 2pp'$ and $q^{3/2} = p^3$, so $\frac{\hbar|q'|}{4q^{3/2}} = \frac{\hbar|p'|}{2p^2} = \frac12\big|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\frac\hbar p\big|$. The condition $\ll1$ is the slide's.

The amplitude and the time. A classical particle of energy $E$ moves at $v = p/m$; the time it spends in $\mathrm{d}x$ is $\mathrm{d}x/v$. The WKB density $C^2\cos^2(\cdots)/p$ averages to $C^2/2p$ over a wavelength: proportional to $1/v$. The same statement is the conservation of probability flux of the "running WKB backwards" slide: $A^2p/m$ constant.

"Short wavelength" means large $n$. For a bound state the number of half-wavelengths between the turning points is about $n$, so the condition improves as $n$ grows. That is why the next slides' rule is exact for the oscillator only by a coincidence, and good for every well at large $n$.

Bohr–Sommerfeld, with its ½

A well with turning points $a < b$. A bound state must decay beyond both. Unit 21's connection formula at $b$, and its mirror image at $a$, give two forms inside:

$$\varphi \approx \frac{2C_b}{\sqrt p}\cos\Big(\theta_b(x) - \frac\pi4\Big),\quad \theta_b = \frac1\hbar\!\int_x^b\!p,$$ $$\varphi \approx \frac{2C_a}{\sqrt p}\cos\Big(\theta_a(x) - \frac\pi4\Big),\quad \theta_a = \frac1\hbar\!\int_a^x\!p .$$

With $\Theta = \theta_a + \theta_b = \frac1\hbar\int_a^bp$, the first cosine is $\cos\big((\theta_a - \frac\pi4) - (\Theta - \frac\pi2)\big)$. It is a multiple of the second for all $x$ iff $\Theta - \frac\pi2 = n\pi$.

the Bohr–Sommerfeld rule $$\oint p\,\mathrm{d}q = 2\!\int_a^b\!p\,\mathrm{d}x = 2\pi\hbar\Big(n + \frac12\Big),$$

with $C_a = (-1)^nC_b$ — the sign of a function with $n$ nodes ✓.

the ½ is not decoration

Each smooth turning point shifts the phase by $\pi/4$; two of them make $\pi/2$, which is the $\frac12$. The count of quarter-phases, here $2$, is the Maslov index $\mu$: in general $\oint p\,\mathrm{d}q = 2\pi\hbar(n + \mu/4)$.

The old quantum theory (Bohr 1913, Sommerfeld 1916) had $\oint p\,\mathrm{d}q = nh$: no $\frac12$, and a ground state of zero energy.

Full proof of the matching

The connection formula is unit 21's, cited there (its proof needs the Airy function's asymptotics). In unit 21's form — oscillating for $x < a$, decaying for $x > a$ — it applies at $b$ directly. At $a$, apply it to $\tilde\varphi(x) = \varphi(-x)$, whose well lies to the left of $-a$: that gives the second form. Now $\theta_b(x) = \Theta - \theta_a(x)$, so $\theta_b - \frac\pi4 = -\big((\theta_a - \frac\pi4) - (\Theta - \frac\pi2)\big)$, and cosine is even. Write $u = \theta_a - \frac\pi4$, which runs over an interval of length $\Theta > 0$ as $x$ crosses the well, and $\gamma = \Theta - \frac\pi2$. Then $\cos(u - \gamma) = \cos\gamma\cos u + \sin\gamma\sin u$ is a constant multiple of $\cos u$ on an interval iff $\sin\gamma = 0$, i.e. $\gamma = n\pi$, and the multiple is $\cos n\pi = (-1)^n$. $\square$

Which $n$. $\Theta = (n + \frac12)\pi$ with $n\ge0$ because $\Theta>0$. The cosine $\cos(\theta_a - \frac\pi4)$ then has its zeros at $\theta_a = \frac{3\pi}4, \frac{7\pi}4,\dots$ below $\Theta$: exactly $n$ of them, matching the oscillation theorem.

The Maslov index is named here, not developed (Maslov, 1965; Keller had it for billiards in 1958): in higher dimensions it counts how the invariant torus of unit 15 folds over configuration space, and the rule becomes the Einstein–Brillouin–Keller condition on each of unit 15's actions.

Worked: the oscillator by Bohr–Sommerfeld

route three

1. The orbit. At energy $E$, $\frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac12m\omega^2x^2 = E$ is an ellipse in the $(x, p)$ plane with semi-axes

$$x_{\max} = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\omega^2}},\qquad p_{\max} = \sqrt{2mE} .$$

2. Its area. $\oint p\,\mathrm{d}q = \pi x_{\max}p_{\max} = \pi\sqrt{\frac{2E}{m\omega^2}\cdot2mE} = \frac{2\pi E}{\omega}$.

3. Quantise. $\frac{2\pi E}{\omega} = 2\pi\hbar(n + \frac12)$, so

$$E_n = \hbar\omega\big(n + \tfrac12\big)\quad\text{exactly, for every }n.$$

4. Checks. $[\oint p\,\mathrm{d}q] = \mathrm{J\,s} = [\hbar]$ ✓. Unit 15's action is $I = E/\omega = \hbar(n + \frac12)$, and its frequency theorem $\mathrm{d}E/\mathrm{d}I = \omega$ is the level spacing over $\hbar$ ✓.

Scaled phase plane: circles of radius $\sqrt{2n + 1}$. The first disc has area $\pi$, each ring after it $2\pi$ — one state per $2\pi\hbar$ of phase-space area.

The area of an ellipse with semi-axes $a$, $b$ is $\pi ab$ (unit 05: the Jacobian of $(x,p) = (ar\cos t, br\sin t)$ is $abr$). And $\frac{2E}{m\omega^2}\cdot 2mE = \frac{4E^2}{\omega^2}$, whose square root is $\frac{2E}\omega$.

The scaled picture. In $\xi = x/\ell$ and $\pi_\xi = p\ell/\hbar$ the Hamiltonian is $\frac{\hbar\omega}2(\xi^2 + \pi_\xi^2)$, the orbits are circles, and areas are measured in units of $\hbar$. The quantised orbits have $\xi^2 + \pi_\xi^2 = 2n + 1$; consecutive circles enclose rings of area $\pi(2n + 3) - \pi(2n + 1) = 2\pi$. "One state per $2\pi\hbar$ of phase space" is the counting rule that statistical mechanics uses, and Weyl's law makes it a theorem for large energies in any dimension.

Unit 21's Solvay story, finished. Unit 21's notes told of Lorentz asking, in 1911, what happens to a slowly shortened pendulum, and Einstein answering that $E/\nu$ is invariant. Here is why it mattered: Bohr–Sommerfeld quantises the action $I = E/\omega$, and only an adiabatic invariant can be quantised consistently — a slow change of the system must not move it between allowed values $\hbar(n + \frac12)$. Unit 15's adiabatic theorem guarantees it does not.

Why exact? For a quadratic potential every higher-order WKB correction to the quantisation condition vanishes (Dunham, 1932; Bender and Orszag, ch. 10, problems on higher-order WKB). For every other potential the rule has an error that falls as $n$ grows, which the next slide measures.

Tunnelling

A barrier with $V > E$ on $(a, b)$. Inside, WKB gives $|p|^{-1/2}e^{\pm\int|p|/\hbar}$, and the transmitted wave must be the decaying one, so the amplitude falls by $e^{-\Gamma}$ across the barrier:

tunnelling: the WKB transmission $$T \approx e^{-2\Gamma},$$ $$\Gamma = \frac1\hbar\int_a^b\!\sqrt{2m\,(V - E)}\;\mathrm{d}x .$$

Valid when $\Gamma \gg 1$ and $V$ is smooth on the scale of $\hbar/|p|$ near $a$ and $b$; the prefactor is then 1 to leading order (cited).

Checks. $\Gamma$ is (action)/$\hbar$, a number ✓. $\hbar\to0$: $T\to0$, no classical leak ✓.

Exact solution for a rectangular barrier, $E = V_0/2$, $\kappa a = 2$: $T = 0.0707$ against $e^{-2\kappa a} = 0.0183$.

The rectangle, exactly: $T \approx \frac{16E(V_0 - E)}{V_0^2}e^{-2\kappa a}$ for $\kappa a \gg 1$. The exponent is WKB's; the prefactor, here 4, is not — sharp corners break the smoothness condition. An electron $1$ eV under a $1$ nm barrier: $e^{-2\kappa a} = 3.5\times10^{-5}$, exact $1.42\times10^{-4}$.

The exponent, argued. Away from the turning points the forbidden-region solutions are $|p|^{-1/2}e^{\pm\frac1\hbar\int_a^x|p|}$. On the far side only an outgoing wave exists. Connecting it back through $b$ reads unit 21's formula against the safe direction of its warning, but harmlessly: the outgoing wave fixes the coefficient of the solution that grows towards $a$, which dominates everywhere inside the barrier, and only the size of that coefficient is needed. The warning is about the subdominant piece, which is lost and does not matter at leading order. So the amplitude at $a$ exceeds that at $b$ by $e^{\Gamma}$. Transmission is a ratio of fluxes, $|\text{amplitude}|^2p/m$, and the $p^{-1/2}$ prefactors cancel in it, so $T \approx e^{-2\Gamma}$ up to a factor of order one that a careful connection at both turning points fixes as $1 + O(e^{-2\Gamma})$ (cited: Landau and Lifshitz, Quantum Mechanics, ch. VII; Hall, ch. 15).

The rectangular barrier. Solving piecewise with $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$, $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}/\hbar$, and matching $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at both edges, gives the exact $T = \big[1 + \frac{V_0^2\sinh^2\kappa a}{4E(V_0 - E)}\big]^{-1}$; for $\kappa a\gg1$, $\sinh^2\kappa a \approx \frac14e^{2\kappa a}$, which gives the slide's form. The figure solves the same matching numerically and plots $\psi$: interference of incident and reflected waves on the left, exponential decay inside, a small steady wave on the right.

The electron numbers. $\kappa = \sqrt{2m_e\cdot1\,\mathrm{eV}}/\hbar = 5.12\ \mathrm{nm^{-1}}$, $2\kappa a = 10.25$, $e^{-10.25} = 3.55\times10^{-5}$; the exact formula at $E = 1$ eV, $V_0 = 2$ eV gives $1.419\times10^{-4}$, four times larger — the prefactor $16E(V_0 - E)/V_0^2 = 4$. Problem P11 uses the exponent's steepness to explain the scanning tunnelling microscope.

Alpha decay: the Gamow factor

An alpha particle formed inside a nucleus of radius $R$ meets the daughter's Coulomb wall $V = 2Z\,e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0r$. It escapes by tunnelling to $b = 2Ze^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0Q$.

the Gamow factor (1928) $$\Gamma = \frac{b\sqrt{2\mu Q}}{\hbar}\,F\Big(\frac Rb\Big),$$ $$F(s) = \arccos\sqrt{s} - \sqrt{s(1 - s)} .$$

$F(0) = \frac\pi2$, so $\Gamma \to \pi\eta$ as $R\to0$, where $\eta = 2Z\alpha c/v$.

Half-life $\approx \ln2\cdot\frac{2R}{v}\,e^{2\Gamma}$.

The barrier, to scale. Half the energy, more than twice the width — and the width is in an exponent.

$^{238}$U ($Q = 4.27$ MeV, $4.5\times10^{9}$ yr) against $^{212}$Po ($8.95$ MeV, $0.30\ \mu$s): 23.7 orders of magnitude apart. The bare exponent $2\pi\eta$ predicts 27.6 orders; with the finite $R$, 24.2. Problem P10.

The integral. $\Gamma = \frac1\hbar\int_R^b\sqrt{2\mu(\frac{Qb}{r} - Q)}\,\mathrm{d}r = \frac{\sqrt{2\mu Q}}{\hbar}\int_R^b\sqrt{\frac br - 1}\,\mathrm{d}r$. Substitute $r = b\cos^2u$: $\sqrt{b/r - 1} = \tan u$ and $\mathrm{d}r = -2b\cos u\sin u\,\mathrm{d}u$, so the integral is $2b\int_0^{u_0}\sin^2u\,\mathrm{d}u = b\big[u_0 - \sin u_0\cos u_0\big]$ with $\cos u_0 = \sqrt{R/b}$, which is $b\,F(R/b)$ — the slide's $F$, since $\sin u_0\cos u_0 = \sqrt{s(1 - s)}$ with $s = \cos^2u_0$. At $R = 0$, $u_0 = \pi/2$ and $\Gamma_0 = \frac{\pi}{2}\frac{\sqrt{2\mu Q}}{\hbar}b$; with $b = 2Zk/Q$ ($k = e^2/4\pi\varepsilon_0$) and $\sqrt{2\mu/Q} = 2/v$ this is $\pi\cdot\frac{2Zk}{\hbar v} = \pi\eta$, since $k/\hbar c = \alpha$. $\mu$ is the reduced mass of alpha and daughter, $Q = E_\alpha A/(A - 4)$ the energy of relative motion.

The numbers (computed, with $R = 1.2(A_\mathrm d^{1/3} + 4^{1/3})$ fm, the touching distance): U: $Q = 4.272$ MeV, $b = 60.7$ fm, $R = 9.30$ fm, $2\Gamma_0 = 170.9$, $2\Gamma = 87.96$. Po: $Q = 8.949$ MeV, $b = 26.4$ fm, $R = 9.01$ fm, $2\Gamma_0 = 107.5$, $2\Gamma = 32.32$. Differences: bare $63.4$, i.e. $63.4/\ln10 = 27.6$ orders; with $R$, $55.6$, i.e. $24.2$ orders; with the assault frequency $v/2R$ too, $24.3$. Measured: $\log_{10}(4.468\times10^9\ \mathrm{yr}/0.299\ \mu\mathrm s) = 23.7$. The absolute predictions are $1.4\times10^{17}$ s for uranium (measured $1.41\times10^{17}$) and $6.5\times10^{-8}$ s for polonium (measured $3.0\times10^{-7}$).

History. Gamow, and independently Gurney and Condon, explained alpha decay this way in 1928 — the first application of quantum mechanics to the nucleus, and the explanation of the Geiger–Nuttall law of 1911, which problem P10 derives.

the correspondence principle, as a theorem

For every bounded continuous $g$,

$$\begin{aligned}&\int g\Big(\frac{x}{A_n}\Big)|\varphi_n(x)|^2\,\mathrm{d}x\\ &\quad\to \int_{-1}^1\frac{g(u)\,\mathrm{d}u}{\pi\sqrt{1 - u^2}}\end{aligned}$$

as $n\to\infty$, $A_n = \sqrt{2n + 1}\,\ell$.

Why: WKB holds uniformly away from the turning points (unit 21); there $|\varphi_n|^2 = \frac{N^2}{2p}(1 + \sin 2\theta_a)$, and the oscillating term integrates to zero in the limit by Riemann–Lebesgue (unit 19).

The limit is weak, not pointwise: averages converge, values do not. The correspondence principle is unit 21's asymptotics, not a philosophy.

Proof sketch, with what is cited

Rescale $u = x/A_n$. On $|u| \le 1 - \delta$, the WKB approximation with Bohr–Sommerfeld phase holds with relative error $O(1/n)$ uniformly (cited: Olver, Asymptotics and Special Functions, ch. 11, which gives uniform error bounds for Hermite functions; or Szegő, Orthogonal Polynomials, §8.22). There, $|\varphi_n|^2\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{1 + \sin2\theta_a}{\pi\sqrt{1 - u^2}}\,\mathrm{d}u\,(1 + O(1/n))$, using $\cos^2(\theta - \frac\pi4) = \frac12(1 + \sin2\theta)$. The phase $2\theta_a$ is $n$ times a fixed smooth function of $u$ with nonvanishing derivative on $|u|<1$, so $\int g(u)\sin(2\theta_a)\,\frac{\mathrm{d}u}{\sqrt{1 - u^2}}\to0$ — the Riemann–Lebesgue lemma of unit 19 after the change of variable to the phase. The regions $1 - \delta < |u|$ carry probability $O(\sqrt\delta)$ classically, and the quantum leak beyond $|u| = 1$ tends to zero (the table's $0.157 \to 0.048$, falling like $n^{-1/3}$, cited from the Airy asymptotics). Let $n\to\infty$, then $\delta\to0$.

What correspondence is not. It is not "quantum mechanics becomes classical mechanics as $\hbar\to0$" for every state: the ground state never does (the warning on the Hamilton–Jacobi slide), and superpositions of macroscopically different states have interference terms that no limit of this kind removes. It is the statement above: for highly excited states, local averages are classical.

The ground state is the Gaussian $e^{-x^2/2\ell^2}$: the kernel of $a$, the end of the series, the minimiser of uncertainty, and — seen from far off — a wave with a quarter-wave of slack at each turning point.

The connection formulae of unit 21 were cited, not proved. They are the only route whose $\frac12$ is a phase, and they get it right. That is the cleanest available evidence that they are not decoration.

The series' $\frac12$, said once more. In the recursion $a_{k+2} \propto (2k + 1 - \varepsilon)a_k$, the termination value at $k = n$ is $\varepsilon = 2n + 1$, so $E = \frac{\hbar\omega}{2}(2n + 1)$. The "$+1$" traces back to step 1 of the worked example, where differentiating $e^{-\xi^2/2}$ twice produced $(\xi^2 - 1)$: without the $-1$, the recursion would read $2k - \varepsilon$, terminating at $\varepsilon = 2n$, and the floor would be zero. So the $\frac12$ is the ground state's curvature, and the ground state is what it is because it must decay.

Why all agree. The four are not independent: the ground state that the series finds is the one that $a$ kills, which is the one that saturates Robertson's inequality. Bohr–Sommerfeld is different in kind — an asymptotic formula that happens to be exact — and its agreement is the nontrivial check. Had unit 21's connection formula carried a phase other than $\pi/4$, route three would disagree with the other two at every $n$, which would be a contradiction.

The running example, closed. The oscillator has now been solved as a differential equation, as an algebra and as an asymptotic problem, and compared with its classical self. Every number in its hover card has been derived.
