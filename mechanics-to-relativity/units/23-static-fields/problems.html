the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. On paper and without notes, you can solve a spherical boundary-value problem by Legendre expansion and independently by images, and show the two agree coefficient by coefficient; produce a vector potential for a given divergence-free \(\vec B\) in a stated gauge, verifying its curl and the gauge condition; and state the Helmholtz theorem with its hypotheses, prove its uniqueness half, and use it to say how many field equations electromagnetism must have, and what condition its sources must satisfy, before seeing them.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Thirteen problems, about five hours. SI units throughout. \(\varphi\) is the electrostatic potential in volts, \(\vec E = -\nabla\varphi\); the fundamental solutions are \(\Gamma_3 = 1/4\pi r\) and \(\Gamma_2 = -\frac1{2\pi}\log s\), so that a charge \(q\) has potential \(q\Gamma_3/\varepsilon_0\). Cylindrical coordinates are \((s,\phi,z)\), spherical \((r,\theta,\phi)\). "Grounded" means held at potential \(0\).

Green's functions and images

P1 The dimension in the Green's function routine

exercises: the radial Laplacian in \(\mathbb R^n\), flux normalisation, the logarithmic potential

  1. Show that on \(\mathbb R^n\setminus\{0\}\) a radial function \(f(r)\) has \(\nabla^2f = r^{1-n}(r^{n-1}f')'\), and find every radial harmonic function for \(n = 2, 3, 4\).
  2. In \(\mathbb R^4\) the unit sphere \(S^3\) has area \(2\pi^2\) (take this as given). Normalise the fundamental solution \(\Gamma_4\) so that the outward flux of \(-\nabla\Gamma_4\) through every sphere about \(0\) is \(1\). How does the field of a point charge fall off in four space dimensions?
  3. A coaxial cable has an inner conductor of radius \(a = 0.50\) mm carrying \(\lambda = 10\ \mathrm{nC\,m^{-1}}\), inside a coaxial conducting shell of inner radius \(b = 2.5\) mm carrying \(-\lambda\). Using \(\Gamma_2\), find the potential difference between the conductors and the field at the surface of each.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a chain-rule identity followed by a first-order ODE for \(f'\); part 2 is one constant; part 3 is a difference of two values of a logarithm, and two numbers of the form \(\lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0s\).

Rung 2

Use \(\partial_ir = x_i/r\). Harmonic means \(r^{n-1}f'\) is constant. For the cable, only the region \(a \lt s \lt b\) matters, the problem is \(z\)-independent, and the field there is that of the inner line charge alone (Gauss on a cylinder).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(-\Gamma_4'(r)\cdot2\pi^2r^3 = 1\), so \(\Gamma_4' = -1/(2\pi^2r^3)\). First line of part 3: \(\varphi(s) = \frac{\lambda}{\varepsilon_0}\Gamma_2(s) + C = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\log s + C\) for \(a \le s \le b\).

Worked solution

Idea. Flux conservation through spheres of area \(\propto r^{n-1}\) fixes the field as \(r^{1-n}\); the only freedom is the constant, fixed by unit flux.

1. \(\partial_if = f'x_i/r\), and \(\partial_i(f'x_i/r) = f''x_i^2/r^2 + f'(1/r - x_i^2/r^3)\). Summing with \(\sum x_i^2 = r^2\): \(\nabla^2f = f'' + (n-1)f'/r = r^{1-n}(r^{n-1}f')'\). So harmonic iff \(r^{n-1}f' = \beta\), i.e. \(f' = \beta r^{1-n}\):

  • \(n = 2\): \(f = \alpha + \beta\log r\);
  • \(n = 3\): \(f = \alpha - \beta/r\);
  • \(n = 4\): \(f = \alpha - \beta/(2r^2)\).

2. \(-\Gamma_4' \cdot 2\pi^2r^3 = 1\) gives \(\Gamma_4' = -\frac{1}{2\pi^2r^3}\), so \[ \Gamma_4 = \frac{1}{4\pi^2r^2} . \] The field of a point charge is \(-\nabla\Gamma_4 \propto r^{-3}\): in four dimensions Coulomb's law would be an inverse-cube law.

3. Between the conductors the field is radial and \(z\)-independent, and by Gauss on a coaxial cylinder of radius \(s\) it is that of the enclosed line charge: \(\varphi = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\log s + C\). The potential difference is \[ \varphi(a) - \varphi(b) = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\log\frac ba = \frac{10^{-8}\times\log5}{2\pi\times8.8542\times10^{-12}} = \mathbf{289.3\ V}. \] The field \(E = \lambda/2\pi\varepsilon_0s\) is \(3.595\times10^5\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) at the inner conductor and \(7.190\times10^4\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\) at the outer; outside the shell the enclosed charge is zero and the field vanishes.

Check. Dimensions: \([\lambda/\varepsilon_0] = \mathrm{C\,m^{-1}}/\mathrm{F\,m^{-1}} = \mathrm V\) ✓. Limit \(b\to a\): the gap closes and the potential difference \(\to0\) ✓. The two fields are in ratio \(b/a = 5\) ✓. And \(\Gamma_4\) fits the pattern \(\Gamma_n \propto r^{2-n}\) with constant \(1/((n-2)\omega_{n-1}) = 1/(2\cdot2\pi^2)\) ✓. The logarithm's argument is the ratio \(b/a\): the arbitrary scale inside \(\log s\) cancels, which is the only way a two-dimensional potential difference can be meaningful.

P2 A charge above a grounded plane routine

exercises: images, the jump condition, the energy of an image system

A point charge \(q\) sits at height \(d\) above the grounded conducting plane \(z = 0\).

  1. Find \(\varphi\) in \(z \gt 0\) and justify that it is the answer.
  2. Find the induced surface charge \(\sigma(\rho)\), where \(\rho\) is distance from the foot of the perpendicular, and show that the total induced charge is \(-q\). What fraction of it lies within \(\rho \lt d\)?
  3. Find the force on \(q\), and the work needed to pull it slowly from \(d\) to infinity. (Careful: it is not the potential energy of \(q\) and \(-q\) at distance \(2d\).)
  4. An electron is \(1.00\) nm from a metal surface. How deep, in electronvolts, is its image potential well there?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A potential in the half-space, a surface density that integrates to a charge, a force, an integral of the force, then one number. Uniqueness is the justification in part 1.

Rung 2

Image \(-q\) at depth \(d\). Below the plane is conductor, so \(\vec E = 0\) there and \(\sigma = \varepsilon_0E_z(z = 0^+)\). For the work, integrate the force as the charge moves — the image moves with it.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: on the plane both charges are at distance \(\sqrt{\rho^2+d^2}\), and their \(z\)-fields add: \(E_z = -2\cdot\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{d}{(\rho^2+d^2)^{3/2}}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The image makes the plane an equipotential; uniqueness makes the image potential correct above the plane; everything else is reading it off.

1. \(\varphi = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\Big(\frac{1}{|\vec x - d\hat z|} - \frac1{|\vec x + d\hat z|}\Big)\). In \(z \gt 0\) it is harmonic except at the charge, where it has the right singularity; it vanishes on \(z = 0\) (equal distances) and tends to \(0\) at infinity. By the uniqueness theorem (Dirichlet data on the plane, decay at infinity, applied to the difference of two solutions, which is harmonic everywhere in \(z \gt 0\)) it is the answer. The half-space is neither bounded nor the exterior of a bounded set, but the deck's proof of case 1 works on it verbatim: apply the maximum principle on the half-ball \(\{z \gt 0\}\cap B_R\) and let \(R\to\infty\).

2. From rung 3, \[ \sigma(\rho) = \varepsilon_0E_z = -\frac{qd}{2\pi(\rho^2+d^2)^{3/2}} . \] Total: \(\int_0^\infty\sigma\,2\pi\rho\,\mathrm d\rho = -qd\int_0^\infty\frac{\rho\,\mathrm d\rho}{(\rho^2+d^2)^{3/2}} = -qd\Big[-\frac1{\sqrt{\rho^2+d^2}}\Big]_0^\infty = -q\). Within \(\rho \lt d\) the same integral up to \(d\) gives \(-q\big(1 - 1/\sqrt2\big)\): \(\mathbf{29.3\,\%}\) of the induced charge.

3. The field at \(q\) is that of the image alone: \(F = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0(2d)^2} = \frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0d^2}\), towards the plane. The work to pull it from \(d\) to infinity is \(\int_d^\infty\frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0z^2}\mathrm dz = \frac{q^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0d}\) — half of \(q^2/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\cdot2d)\), because the image is not a fixed charge: it recedes as \(q\) does, and only the region \(z \gt 0\) holds real field energy.

4. \(U = -\frac{e^2}{16\pi\varepsilon_0d} = -\frac{(1.602\times10^{-19})^2}{16\pi\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times10^{-9}}\ \mathrm J = -5.77\times10^{-20}\ \mathrm J = \mathbf{-0.360\ eV}\). The force there is \(5.77\times10^{-11}\) N.

Check. Dimensions of \(\sigma\): \([q d/\rho^3] = \mathrm{C\,m^{-2}}\) ✓. Limit \(d\to0\): \(\sigma\) concentrates at \(\rho = 0\) with total \(-q\), cancelling the charge ✓. Limit \(\rho\gg d\): \(\sigma\sim-qd/2\pi\rho^3\), the charge density that a dipole \(2qd\) (charge plus image) would induce ✓. The \(-0.36\) eV is a fraction of a typical metal work function of 4–5 eV, the right size for the correction it is known to be.

P3 A charge outside a sphere, grounded and not medium

exercises: the Kelvin image, Legendre reconciliation, conductor data, the plane limit

A point charge \(q\) is at distance \(R\) from the centre of a conducting sphere of radius \(a \lt R\).

  1. The sphere is grounded. Solve by images, and independently by Legendre expansion, and show the two give the same coefficient of \(r^{-\ell-1}P_\ell(\cos\theta)\) for every \(\ell\). What is the total induced charge?
  2. The sphere is instead isolated and neutral. Modify the solution, and justify it by the uniqueness theorem for conductor data.
  3. With \(q = 1.00\) nC, \(a = 10.0\) cm, \(R = 20.0\) cm, find the force on \(q\) in both cases.
  4. Show that as \(a, R\to\infty\) with \(R - a = d\) fixed, part 1 becomes P2.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

An image charge and position; two series whose coefficients you compare; a second image that restores neutrality without spoiling the equipotential; two numbers; a limit.

Rung 2

Kelvin: \(q' = -qa/R\) at \(a^2/R\). The source's potential for \(r \lt R\) is \(\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum r^\ell R^{-\ell-1}P_\ell\). For the neutral sphere you need a charge that keeps the sphere an equipotential — where can you put \(+qa/R\)?

Rung 3

First line of the reconciliation: the image's potential for \(r \gt a^2/R\) is \(\frac{q'}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\sum_\ell\frac{(a^2/R)^\ell}{r^{\ell+1}}P_\ell(\cos\theta)\), and \(q'(a^2/R)^\ell = -q\,a^{2\ell+1}/R^{\ell+1}\).

Worked solution

Idea. One image makes the sphere a zero equipotential; a second, at the centre, changes the sphere's potential without changing its shape as an equipotential, and fixes its charge.

1. Images. Put the axis through \(q\). The deck's Apollonius computation shows that \(q\) at \(R\hat z\) and \(q' = -qa/R\) at \(b\hat z\), \(b = a^2/R\), give \(\varphi = 0\) on \(r = a\). Outside the sphere the pair is harmonic except at \(q\), and decays; by uniqueness it is the answer there.

Legendre. In \(a \lt r \lt R\), \(\varphi\) is axisymmetric and harmonic, so \(4\pi\varepsilon_0\varphi = \sum(A_\ell r^\ell + B_\ell r^{-\ell-1})P_\ell\). The growing part is the source's: \(A_\ell = q/R^{\ell+1}\). The sphere demands \(A_\ell a^\ell + B_\ell a^{-\ell-1} = 0\), so \(B_\ell = -qa^{2\ell+1}/R^{\ell+1}\).

Reconciliation. The image's coefficients are \(q'b^\ell = -\frac{qa}{R}\frac{a^{2\ell}}{R^\ell} = -\frac{qa^{2\ell+1}}{R^{\ell+1}}\), equal to \(B_\ell\) for every \(\ell\). The total induced charge is the \(\ell = 0\) coefficient, \(B_0 = -qa/R = q'\) (Gauss on a sphere just outside \(r = a\)).

2. Add \(q'' = +qa/R\) at the centre. It adds the constant \(q''/4\pi\varepsilon_0a\) on the sphere, which stays an equipotential, and it makes the total charge inside zero. The configuration has the right singularity at \(q\), makes the sphere a conductor with total charge \(0\), and decays. The uniqueness theorem for conductor data (the deck's proof 2) then says its field is the field. The sphere's potential is \(\frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0R}\) — exactly the potential \(q\) alone would produce at the centre, which is the mean value property in disguise.

3. Here \(q' = -0.500\) nC at \(b = 5.00\) cm, so \(R - b = 0.150\) m. Grounded: \[ F = \frac{kqq'}{(R-b)^2} = \frac{8.98755\times10^9\times10^{-9}\times(-0.5\times10^{-9})}{0.150^2} = \mathbf{-2.00\times10^{-7}\ N}, \] attractive. Neutral: add \(kqq''/R^2 = 8.98755\times10^9\times10^{-9}\times0.5\times10^{-9}/0.04 = 1.12\times10^{-7}\) N of repulsion: total \(\mathbf{-8.74\times10^{-8}\ N}\), still attractive — a neutral sphere always attracts a charge, because the near side's induced charge is closer. (For a neutral conductor of general shape this is not a theorem: suitably shaped conductors can repel a nearby charge — cited: Levin and Johnson, Am. J. Phys. 79 (2011) 843.)

4. \(q' = -qa/(a+d)\to-q\). The image's distance from the surface is \(a - a^2/R = ad/R \to d\). So: image \(-q\) at depth \(d\) behind a surface that has become flat — P2 ✓.

Check. Dimensions: \([q'] = [q]\), \([b] = \mathrm L\) ✓. Limit \(R\to\infty\) at fixed \(q\): induced charge \(-qa/R\to0\) ✓. Limit \(R\to a^+\): \(q'\to-q\) and \(b\to a\), the charge and image meet on the surface ✓. Large-\(R\) force on the neutral sphere: expanding, \(F \approx -2kq^2a^3/R^5\) — a charge polarising a sphere of polarisability \(4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3\) and attracting the induced dipole, which is the running example's \(p = 4\pi\varepsilon_0a^3E\) with \(E = kq/R^2\) ✓.

P4 Images and their limits: the 120° corner hard

exercises: images by reflection groups, separation in a wedge, a proof that a method cannot work

Two grounded conducting half-planes meet along the \(z\)-axis at an angle \(\alpha\), so the region is the wedge \(0 \lt \phi \lt \alpha\) (for all \(s \gt 0\), all \(z\)). A line charge \(\lambda \gt 0\) parallel to the edge passes through \((s_0, \phi_0)\) inside the wedge. The potential \(\varphi\) is harmonic in the wedge away from the charge, where \(\varphi + \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\log|\vec x - \vec x_0|\) is harmonic; it is continuous up to the walls and zero on them, and \(\varphi\to0\) as \(s\to\infty\).

  1. For \(\alpha = \pi/2\) and \(\alpha = \pi/3\), give a finite set of image line charges that solves the problem.
  2. Show that near the edge, for \(s \lt s_0\), \(\varphi = \sum_{k\ge1}c_k\,s^{k\pi/\alpha}\sin(k\pi\phi/\alpha)\), and that \(c_1 \gt 0\).
  3. Prove that for \(\alpha = 2\pi/3\) no finite set of line charges parallel to the edge and lying outside the open wedge reproduces \(\varphi\) in the wedge.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a picture: reflect in the walls repeatedly. Part 2 is a Fourier sine series in \(\phi\) whose coefficients obey an ODE in \(s\), plus a sign argument. Part 3 is a proof by contradiction comparing which powers of \(s\) can appear near the edge.

Rung 2

A finite set of line charges not on the edge gives a potential harmonic in a whole disc about the edge, and a harmonic function on a disc has a Fourier series in \(\phi\) with coefficients \(a_js^j\), \(j\) an integer. For \(\alpha = 2\pi/3\), \(\pi/\alpha = 3/2\). For \(c_1 \gt 0\): show \(\varphi \gt 0\) in the wedge by the minimum principle.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: set \(b_k(s) = \frac2\alpha\int_0^\alpha\varphi(s,\phi)\sin\frac{k\pi\phi}{\alpha}\,\mathrm d\phi\) and use \(\frac1s(s\varphi_s)_s + \frac{1}{s^2}\varphi_{\phi\phi} = 0\), integrating the \(\phi\)-term by parts twice (the walls kill the boundary terms), to get \(s^2b_k'' + sb_k' - (k\pi/\alpha)^2b_k = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. Near a corner of angle \(\alpha\) the true potential behaves like \(s^{\pi/\alpha}\). Point sources away from the corner can only produce integer powers of \(s\). When \(\pi/\alpha\) is not an integer the two are incompatible.

1. Reflection in a grounded wall sends a charge to minus itself on the other side. The group generated by reflections in the two walls is dihedral of order \(2\pi/\alpha\) when \(\pi/\alpha\) is an integer \(n\); its \(2n - 1\) non-identity elements give the images, with charge \(-\lambda\) for reflections and \(+\lambda\) for rotations. \(\alpha = \pi/2\): \(-\lambda\) at \((s_0, -\phi_0)\) and at \((s_0, \pi - \phi_0)\), and \(+\lambda\) at \((s_0, \pi + \phi_0)\) — three images. \(\alpha = \pi/3\): \(-\lambda\) at \(\phi = -\phi_0,\ 2\pi/3 - \phi_0,\ 4\pi/3 - \phi_0\) and \(+\lambda\) at \(\phi = 2\pi/3 + \phi_0,\ 4\pi/3 + \phi_0\) — five images, all at radius \(s_0\), all outside the wedge. Each wall is a mirror line of the whole configuration with charges reversed, so \(\varphi = 0\) on it; decay holds because the total charge is zero; by uniqueness this is the solution.

2. On \(0 \lt s \lt s_0\) the potential is harmonic. Differentiating \(b_k\) under the integral (Leibniz) and using Laplace's equation in polar form, \(s(sb_k')' = -\frac2\alpha\int_0^\alpha\varphi_{\phi\phi}\sin\frac{k\pi\phi}\alpha\,\mathrm d\phi\). Two integrations by parts, with \(\varphi = 0\) and \(\sin = 0\) at \(\phi = 0, \alpha\), turn this into \((k\pi/\alpha)^2b_k\). So \(b_k = c_ks^{k\pi/\alpha} + d_ks^{-k\pi/\alpha}\); \(\varphi\) is bounded near the edge (it is continuous and zero there), so \(d_k = 0\). Completeness of the sine series on \((0,\alpha)\) (unit 19) gives the expansion.

Sign. Unit 05's maximum principle was proved in three dimensions; it applies here because a \(z\)-independent function harmonic in the plane is harmonic on the three-dimensional region wedge \(\times\,\mathbb R\), and an interior extremum in the plane is an interior extremum there. \(\varphi \ge 0\): on the region between a small circle round the charge (where \(\varphi\) is large and positive, the logarithm dominating), the walls (\(\varphi = 0\)) and a large arc (\(|\varphi|\) small), the minimum principle bounds \(\varphi\) below by the boundary minimum, which tends to \(0\) as the circle shrinks and the arc grows. So \(\varphi \ge 0\), and \(\varphi \gt 0\) inside, since a harmonic function attaining an interior minimum is constant. Then \(b_1(s) = c_1s^{\pi/\alpha}\) is the integral of a positive function against \(\sin(\pi\phi/\alpha) \gt 0\): \(c_1 \gt 0\).

3. Suppose finitely many line charges \(\mu_i\) at points \(\vec x_i\) outside the open wedge reproduce \(\varphi\) there. Then \(\varphi = -\frac{1}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\big(\lambda\log|\vec x-\vec x_0| + \sum\mu_i\log|\vec x-\vec x_i|\big) + C\) in the wedge. Split off any charge \(\mu_*\) sitting on the edge itself: the rest, \(h\), is harmonic on a disc \(|\vec x| \lt \delta\). A harmonic function on that disc has \(h = \sum_{j\ge0}s^j(a_j\cos j\phi + \beta_j\sin j\phi)\) with \(|a_j|, |\beta_j| \le 2M\delta^{-j}\) (the full-circle version of part 2's argument; \(M = \max|h|\) on the circle of radius \(\delta\)), so the series converges uniformly for \(s \le \delta/2\) and may be integrated term by term. Hence, for small \(s\), \[ b_1(s) = -\frac{\mu_*}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}\gamma\log s + \sum_{j\ge0}\kappa_js^j , \qquad \gamma = \frac2\alpha\int_0^\alpha\sin\frac{\pi\phi}\alpha\,\mathrm d\phi = \frac4\pi , \] a logarithm plus a convergent power series in integer powers of \(s\).

But part 2 says \(b_1(s) = c_1s^{3/2}\) with \(c_1 \gt 0\). As \(s\to0\), \(c_1s^{3/2}\to0\) while \(\log s\to-\infty\), so \(\mu_* = 0\). Then \(c_1s^{3/2} = \sum\kappa_js^j\), a function that is infinitely differentiable at \(s = 0\). Differentiate twice: the right side stays bounded as \(s\to0^+\), the left is \(\tfrac34c_1s^{-1/2}\to\infty\). Contradiction. So no finite image set exists for \(\alpha = 2\pi/3\). \(\square\)

Check. The argument must fail when it should: for \(\alpha = \pi/n\), \(\pi/\alpha = n\) is an integer and \(s^n\sin n\phi\) is a harmonic polynomial — no contradiction, and indeed part 1 found images ✓. Dimensionally \(c_k\) carries \(\mathrm V\,\mathrm m^{-k\pi/\alpha}\), a non-integer power of length for the \(120°\) corner, which is already a hint that no finite arrangement of charges at definite distances produces it. Physically, the field goes as \(s^{\pi/\alpha - 1}\): at an opening of \(120° \lt 180°\) that is \(s^{1/2}\), which vanishes at the edge, as in any hollow corner ✓; for \(\alpha \gt \pi\) (a protruding edge) the exponent is negative and the field diverges — why sharp points spark first.

Another way

A look ahead, not needed for anything above: unit 20's complex analysis maps the wedge onto a half-plane by \(w = \zeta^{\pi/\alpha}\), \(\zeta = x + \mathrm iy\), and solves the problem there with one image. Pulled back, the solution involves \(\zeta^{3/2}\), which is not single-valued round the edge — the same obstruction, seen as a branch point.

Boundary-value problems by separation

P5 Two hemispheres at opposite potentials medium

exercises: Legendre coefficients of discontinuous data, interior and exterior expansions, convergence

A hollow sphere of radius \(a\) is split along its equator into two insulated conducting hemispheres, the upper held at \(+V_0\) and the lower at \(-V_0\).

  1. Find \(\varphi\) inside and outside as Legendre series, giving the first three nonzero coefficients exactly.
  2. Find the field at the centre and the dipole moment seen from far away.
  3. With \(V_0 = 100\) V and \(a = 10.0\) cm, evaluate the field at the centre, and evaluate \(\varphi\) on the axis at \(z = a/2\) from the first one, two, three and four nonzero terms. Compare with the exact on-axis value \(\varphi(z) = V_0\Big[\frac az - \frac{a^2 - z^2}{z\sqrt{a^2+z^2}}\Big]\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Inside, only \(r^\ell\) survives; outside, only \(r^{-\ell-1}\). Both series share the Legendre coefficients \(V_\ell\) of the boundary data \(V_0\operatorname{sgn}(\cos\theta)\).

Rung 2

The data are odd in \(x = \cos\theta\), so only odd \(\ell\) appear, and \(V_\ell = (2\ell+1)V_0\int_0^1P_\ell(x)\,\mathrm dx\). Near the centre, \(\varphi \approx V_1r\cos\theta/a = V_1z/a\).

Rung 3

First line: \(V_1 = 3V_0\int_0^1x\,\mathrm dx = \tfrac32V_0\), and \(V_3 = 7V_0\int_0^1\tfrac12(5x^3 - 3x)\,\mathrm dx = 7V_0\big(\tfrac58 - \tfrac34\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Expand the boundary data in \(P_\ell\); attach \((r/a)^\ell\) inside and \((a/r)^{\ell+1}\) outside so that each term matches at \(r = a\) and behaves at the centre and at infinity.

1. By the expansion theorem, inside \(\varphi = \sum A_\ell r^\ell P_\ell\) (boundedness at the centre kills \(r^{-\ell-1}\)) and outside \(\varphi = \sum B_\ell r^{-\ell-1}P_\ell\) (decay kills \(r^\ell\)). At \(r = a\) both must equal \(\sum V_\ell P_\ell\), so \[ \varphi_{\text{in}} = \sum_\ell V_\ell\Big(\frac ra\Big)^\ell P_\ell(\cos\theta),\qquad \varphi_{\text{out}} = \sum_\ell V_\ell\Big(\frac ar\Big)^{\ell+1}P_\ell(\cos\theta). \] Oddness leaves odd \(\ell\) only. \(V_1 = \tfrac32V_0\); \(V_3 = 7V_0(\tfrac58 - \tfrac34) = -\tfrac78V_0\); \(V_5 = 11V_0\int_0^1\tfrac18(63x^5 - 70x^3 + 15x)\,\mathrm dx = 11V_0\cdot\tfrac18\big(\tfrac{63}6 - \tfrac{70}4 + \tfrac{15}2\big) = \tfrac{11}{16}V_0\).

2. Near the centre \(\varphi = \frac{3V_0}{2a}z + O(r^3)\), so \(\vec E(0) = -\frac{3V_0}{2a}\hat z\): from the positive hemisphere towards the negative one. Far away \(\varphi \approx \frac32V_0\frac{a^2}{r^2}\cos\theta = \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\) with \(p = 6\pi\varepsilon_0a^2V_0\).

A proof about the difference \(w\) of two solutions: show \(\int_D|\nabla w|^2 = 0\). Then two small counterexamples with one sphere.

Rung 2

Rung 3

First line: \(\int_D|\nabla w|^2\,\mathrm dV = \oint_{S_0}w\,\partial_nw\,\mathrm dA + \sum_{i=1}^mW_i\oint_{S_i}\partial_nw\,\mathrm dA\), and \(\oint_{S_i}\partial_nw\,\mathrm dA = \pm Q_i^{(w)}/\varepsilon_0\) by Gauss.

Idea. The energy of the difference field is a sum over conductors of (potential difference) × (charge difference), and every term has one factor zero.

2. A single conducting sphere of radius \(a\) centred inside a large grounded sphere of radius \(b\), no other charge. 3. The same geometry with nothing prescribed on the inner sphere: every \(Q\) gives a solution, a one-parameter family. Under-determined.

Check. The count is one number per conductor, which matches the physics: a conductor has one unknown constant (its potential) and one unknown integral (its charge), related by the capacitance, so exactly one of them is free data. Let \(\vec B = g\,\vec x/r^3\) on \(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}\) — the field a magnetic monopole would have.

  1. Show \(\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0\) there, and that no \(C^1\) field \(\vec A\) on \(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}\) has \(\nabla\times\vec A = \vec B\).
  2. Show that \(\vec A_N = g\,\dfrac{(-y,\ x,\ 0)}{r(r+z)}\) is \(C^1\) on \(\mathbb R^3\) minus the half-line \(\{x = y = 0,\ z \le 0\}\), and that \(\nabla\times\vec A_N = \vec B\) there.
  3. With \(\vec A_S = -g\,\dfrac{(-y,\ x,\ 0)}{r(r-z)}\) on the complement of the other half-line, compute \(\vec A_N - \vec A_S\) on the overlap, and identify it.
  4. Explain in one paragraph how parts 1–3 fit together.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1: a flux, and Stokes on two hemispheres. Part 2: a direct Cartesian curl with \(f = g/(r(r+z))\). Part 3: an algebraic simplification with \(s^2 = (r - z)(r + z)\).

Rung 2

\(\nabla\times\big(f(-y,x,0)\big) = \big(-xf_z,\ -yf_z,\ 2f + xf_x + yf_y\big)\). And \(\partial_i(r^2 + rz)\) is \(2x_i + zx_i/r\) for \(i = x, y\) and \((r+z)^2/r\) for \(i = z\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(f_z = -g\frac{(r+z)^2/r}{r^2(r+z)^2} = -\frac{g}{r^3}\), so the first two components of the curl are \(gx/r^3\) and \(gy/r^3\).

Worked solution

Idea. A sphere round the origin cannot be shrunk in the domain, and the flux through it is the obstruction; remove a half-line to make the domain star-shaped (about any point of the remaining half-axis), and the obstruction reappears on the overlap as unit 04's vortex.

1. \(\nabla\cdot(\vec x/r^3) = 3/r^3 - 3r^2/r^5 = 0\) (unit 05). Flux through the unit sphere: \(g\cdot4\pi\). If \(\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A\) with \(\vec A\in C^1\), Stokes on the northern hemisphere gives \(\oint_{\text{equator}}\vec A\cdot\mathrm d\vec r\), anticlockwise; on the southern hemisphere, whose outward normal induces the opposite orientation of the equator, it gives minus that. The total flux is \(0 \ne 4\pi g\). Contradiction.

2. \(r + z = 0\) exactly when \(x = y = 0\), \(z \le 0\); elsewhere \(f = g/(r(r+z))\) is \(C^\infty\), hence so is \(\vec A_N\). With \(r_i = x_i/r\): \(\partial_x(r^2 + rz) = 2x + zx/r = x(2r+z)/r\), similarly for \(y\), and \(\partial_z(r^2 + rz) = 2z + r + z^2/r = (r+z)^2/r\). So \(f_z = -g/r^3\) and \(xf_x + yf_y = -g\frac{s^2(2r+z)}{r^3(r+z)^2} = -g\frac{(r-z)(2r+z)}{r^3(r+z)}\). Then \[ 2f + xf_x + yf_y = \frac{g\big[2r^2 - (r-z)(2r+z)\big]}{r^3(r+z)} = \frac{g\big[rz + z^2\big]}{r^3(r+z)} = \frac{gz}{r^3}. \] With the first two components \(-xf_z = gx/r^3\), \(-yf_z = gy/r^3\): \(\nabla\times\vec A_N = g\vec x/r^3\) ✓.

3. \(\vec A_S\) is \(\vec A_N\) under \(z\to-z\) with an extra sign, and the same computation gives \(\nabla\times\vec A_S = \vec B\). On the overlap (off the whole \(z\)-axis), \[ \vec A_N - \vec A_S = g(-y,x,0)\Big[\frac1{r(r+z)} + \frac1{r(r-z)}\Big] = g(-y,x,0)\frac{2r}{r(r^2 - z^2)} = 2g\,\frac{(-y,\ x,\ 0)}{x^2+y^2} = 2g\,\vec F_{\mathrm V}, \] unit 04's vortex: closed (as it must be — both have curl \(\vec B\)) and not exact, circulation \(4\pi g\) round the axis. Locally it is \(\nabla(2g\phi)\).

4. The monopole is a closed 2-form-like field (zero divergence) that is not a curl on \(\mathbb R^3\setminus\{0\}\), the degree-two failure of the Poincaré lemma. Removing a half-line makes a region on which a vector potential exists (\(\vec A_N\) or \(\vec A_S\)). The two potentials differ on the overlap by a closed field that is not a gradient, the degree-one failure; and the circulation \(4\pi g\) of that difference is exactly the flux that part 1 said could not be produced by a single potential. The obstruction has not disappeared; it has moved down a degree, onto the overlap. Unit 34 turns this into the statement that \(H^2\) of the sphere is built from \(H^1\) of the overlap of its two hemispheres.

Check. Dimensions: \([g] = \mathrm{T\,m^2}\) (flux), \([A] = [g]/\mathrm m\) ✓. On the upper axis \(z \gt 0\), \(r(r+z)\approx2z^2\) and \(\vec A_N\to0\) smoothly as \(x, y\to0\) ✓; on the lower axis it blows up as \(1/s\) — the "Dirac string", carrying the flux \(4\pi g\) back up to the origin. Physically: if a monopole existed, \(\vec B\) would be the whole field and the string an artefact of the gauge; Dirac showed in 1931 that quantum mechanics hides it only if \(qg\) is quantised.

exercises: stating a theorem with its hypotheses, a uniqueness proof, using a structure theorem to predict

  1. State the Helmholtz theorem precisely, with its smoothness and decay hypotheses.
  2. Prove its uniqueness half, citing exactly which earlier results you use.
  3. Give an example showing that the decay hypothesis cannot be dropped, and one showing that divergence and curl cannot be prescribed independently.
  4. Knowing only that electromagnetism has two vector fields \(\vec E\), \(\vec B\), decaying away from bounded sources, and that its sources are a charge density \(\rho\) and a current density \(\vec J\): predict how many field equations it must have and what they must specify. Then predict a condition the sources must satisfy in statics, and what must happen when they don't.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A statement; a proof by showing a difference field is harmonic and small; two one-line counterexamples; a counting argument ending in \(\nabla\cdot\vec J = 0\).

Rung 2

The identity \(\nabla\times\nabla\times\vec W = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec W) - \nabla^2\vec W\), and the maximum principle on large balls. For the constraint: what is the divergence of a curl?

Rung 3

First line of part 2: let \(\vec W = \vec F_1 - \vec F_2\); then \(\nabla\cdot\vec W = 0\) and \(\nabla\times\vec W = \vec 0\), so \(\nabla^2\vec W = \vec 0\) componentwise.

Worked solution

Idea. A decaying field with no divergence and no curl is harmonic and decaying, hence zero. Everything else follows from applying that to \(\vec E\) and \(\vec B\).

1. Let \(\vec F\in C^2(\mathbb R^3)\) with \(\vec F(\vec x)\to\vec 0\) as \(|\vec x|\to\infty\), and suppose \(D = \nabla\cdot\vec F\) and \(\vec C = \nabla\times\vec F\) are \(C^1\) with compact support. Then \(\vec F = -\nabla\Phi + \nabla\times\vec A\) with \(\Phi = \frac1{4\pi}\int\frac{D(\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|}\mathrm dV\), \(\vec A = \frac1{4\pi}\int\frac{\vec C(\vec y)}{|\vec x-\vec y|}\mathrm dV\), and \(\vec F\) is the only field tending to \(\vec 0\) with divergence \(D\) and curl \(\vec C\).

2. Let \(\vec F_1, \vec F_2\) both qualify; \(\vec W = \vec F_1 - \vec F_2\) is \(C^2\), tends to \(\vec 0\), and has zero divergence and curl. By the curl-curl identity (proved in the deck's Biot–Savart notes by Clairaut), \(\nabla^2W_i = 0\) for each Cartesian component. By the maximum principle (unit 05) on the ball \(B_R\), \(|W_i(\vec x)| \le \max_{|\vec y| = R}|W_i(\vec y)|\) for \(|\vec x| \le R\) (apply it to \(W_i\) and \(-W_i\)). Fix \(\vec x\) and let \(R\to\infty\): the right side tends to \(0\). So \(\vec W = \vec 0\). \(\square\)

3. Decay: \(\vec F = \hat z\) has zero divergence and curl and is not \(\vec 0\); so is \(\nabla h\) for any harmonic polynomial \(h\), e.g. \(\nabla(xy) = (y, x, 0)\). Without decay, the divergence and curl determine \(\vec F\) only up to such fields. Independence: the curl of anything has zero divergence, so a prescribed \(\vec C\) with \(\nabla\cdot\vec C \ne 0\) — say \(\vec C = (x,0,0)\eta(\vec x)\) with \(\eta\) a bump equal to \(1\) near the origin — is the curl of no field at all.

4. By part 2 each of \(\vec E\), \(\vec B\) is fixed by its divergence and curl, and nothing less fixes it (part 3's non-uniqueness returns if one is dropped: add \(\nabla\times\vec G\) or \(\nabla h\) with decay). So the theory must specify exactly four things, \(\nabla\cdot\vec E\), \(\nabla\times\vec E\), \(\nabla\cdot\vec B\), \(\nabla\times\vec B\), in terms of the sources: four equations. Count: the divergences are one function each, the curls — divergence-free by necessity — two functions' worth each, so \(3 + 3 = 6\) functions determined, matching the six components. In statics these are Gauss (\(\rho/\varepsilon_0\)), \(\vec 0\), \(0\), and \(\mu_0\vec J\). The constraint of part 3 applied to \(\nabla\times\vec B = \mu_0\vec J\) forces \[ \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0 , \] steady currents. When charge accumulates, continuity gives \(\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial_t\rho \ne 0\), the static curl equation becomes inconsistent, and the right side must gain a term whose divergence is \(\mu_0\partial_t\rho\). Since \(\partial_t\rho = \varepsilon_0\partial_t\nabla\cdot\vec E\), the term \(\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E\) does it — Maxwell's displacement current, which unit 24 derives exactly this way.

Check. Test the statement on the running example: the total field \(-\nabla\varphi\) does not decay and Helmholtz does not apply to it; the induced part does, and is fixed by \(\sigma = 3\varepsilon_0E_0\cos\theta\) alone — the deck's third derivation ✓. Dimensions of the predicted term: \([\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t E] = \mathrm{s^2m^{-2}}\cdot\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}s^{-1}} = \mathrm{T\,m^{-1}} = [\nabla\times\vec B]\) ✓, using \(\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1/c^2\) and \(\mathrm V = \mathrm{T\,m^2\,s^{-1}}\).
