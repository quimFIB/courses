The collapse, and why ħ stops it

Unit 24's failure, quantified, and a two-line repair whose answer is exact for a reason worth knowing.

24b · the failure

The atom unit 24 could not keep

A classical electron on a circular orbit of radius $r$ about a proton has $E = -k/2r$ and acceleration $a_\mathrm{acc} = k/m_er^2$. Larmor (unit 24) makes it radiate $P = \tfrac23 k\,a_\mathrm{acc}^2/c^3$, so $\dot E = -P$ gives

$$\dot r = -\frac{4}{3}\frac{r_e^2\,c}{r^2},$$

with $r_e = k/m_ec^2 = 2.818\times10^{-15}$ m, the classical electron radius.

the collapse time

Integrating from $r = a_0$ to $r = 0$: $$t_\mathrm{collapse} = \frac{a_0^3}{4r_e^2c} = 1.56\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm s ,$$ after about $1/4\pi\alpha^3 = 2.05\times10^5$ orbits.

Dimensions: $\mathrm{m^3}/(\mathrm{m^2\cdot m\,s^{-1}}) = \mathrm s$ ✓. Limit: $c\to\infty$ switches radiation off and $t\to\infty$ ✓.

$r(t) = a_0(1 - t/\tau)^{1/3}$ with $\tau = 1.56\times10^{-11}$ s, computed. Half the radius is lost in the last eighth of the time; the plunge at the end is where the circular-orbit assumption finally fails, by which point it no longer matters.

The inspiral, in full (problem P1 does it again with every step checked). For a circular orbit, force balance $m_ev^2/r = k/r^2$ gives $T = \tfrac12m_ev^2 = k/2r$ and $E = T - k/r = -k/2r$. The acceleration is $k/m_er^2$, so Larmor's $P = q^2a_\mathrm{acc}^2/6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3 = \tfrac23k\,a_\mathrm{acc}^2/c^3$ is $\tfrac23k^3/m_e^2c^3r^4$. Then $\dot E = (k/2r^2)\,\dot r = -P$ gives $\dot r = -\tfrac43k^2/(m_e^2c^3r^2) = -\tfrac43r_e^2c/r^2$, since $k^2/m_e^2c^3 = r_e^2c$. So $r^2\,\mathrm dr = -\tfrac43r_e^2c\,\mathrm dt$, $r^3 = a_0^3 - 4r_e^2c\,t$, and the radius reaches zero at $\tau = a_0^3/4r_e^2c = 1.556\times10^{-11}$ s.

Is the circular-orbit assumption self-consistent? The energy radiated in one orbit at $a_0$ is $P\cdot T_\mathrm{orb} = (4.67\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm W)(1.52\times10^{-16}\ \mathrm s) = 7.10\times10^{-24}$ J, a fraction $\tfrac{8\pi}3\alpha^3 = 3.3\times10^{-6}$ of the binding energy. So the orbit is very nearly circular at each turn, and the electron makes $\int\mathrm dt/T_\mathrm{orb} = 1/4\pi\alpha^3 = 2.05\times10^5$ turns before it arrives; the integral is done in P1. Here $\alpha = k/\hbar c$, about to be defined — it is used only as shorthand.

Why the failure is decisive. It is not a small discrepancy. Every classical atom collapses, and nothing in classical physics fixes its size: the next slide shows there is no length to be built from $m_e$ and $k$.

24b · the scale

No length without ħ

Classically the atom is fixed by $m_e$ and $k$, with $[m_e] = \mathrm{kg}$ and $[k] = \mathrm{J\,m} = \mathrm{kg\,m^3\,s^{-2}}$. No product $m_e^ak^b$ is a length: the seconds force $b = 0$, and then the metres vanish too. A classical atom has no preferred size — every orbit radius is allowed, and radiation runs down through all of them.

The dimensional argument in full — unit 00's method. A length $m_e^ak^b\hbar^c$ needs, in kg, m and s: $a + b + c = 0$, $3b + 2c = 1$, $-2b - c = 0$. The last gives $c = -2b$, then $3b - 4b = 1$ so $b = -1$, $c = 2$, $a = -1$: the unique answer is $\hbar^2/m_ek$. Without $\hbar$ ($c = 0$) the last equation forces $b = 0$ and the second then reads $0 = 1$: no length exists. The same computation for an energy ($a + b + c = 1$, $3b + 2c = 2$, $-2b - c = -2$) gives $b = 2$, $c = -2$, $a = 1$: $m_ek^2/\hbar^2$. What $\alpha$ measures. The electron's speed on the first orbit is $\alpha c$, so $\alpha$ is how relativistic hydrogen is. Corrections of relative size $(v/c)^2 = \alpha^2 = 5.3\times10^{-5}$ are the fine structure of the last section, where unit 27's relativity returns. And $r_e = \alpha^2a_0$: the length unit 24's collapse was built from is smaller than the atom by $18\,779$.

Worked: the uncertainty estimate

the ground state, way 1 · hydrogen

Step 1 — the trade. An electron confined within radius $r$ has position spread $\sim r$, so by unit 21b's uncertainty relation its momentum is at least of order $\hbar/r$, and its kinetic energy at least $\hbar^2/2m_er^2$.

Step 2 — the energy. $$E(r) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m_er^2} - \frac{k}{r} .$$

Step 3 — minimise. $E'(r) = -\hbar^2/m_er^3 + k/r^2 = 0$ at $r = \hbar^2/m_ek = a_0$.

Step 4 — evaluate. $E(a_0) = \dfrac{m_ek^2}{2\hbar^2} - \dfrac{m_ek^2}{\hbar^2} = -\dfrac{m_ek^2}{2\hbar^2} = -13.6\ \mathrm{eV}$.

Check. $E''(a_0) = 3\hbar^2/m_ea_0^4 - 2k/a_0^3 = k/a_0^3 > 0$: a minimum ✓. In units of Ry and $a_0$, $E = 1/x^2 - 2/x$ with minimum $-1$ at $x = 1$ ✓.

Computed: the kinetic cost grows as $1/r^2$, the attraction only as $1/r$, so at small $r$ the cost wins and $E$ is bounded below. Classically the first curve is absent and the second runs to $-\infty$.

Why this is the whole repair, in one sentence. The kinetic term grows as $r^{-2}$ while the Coulomb term grows only as $r^{-1}$, so squeezing the electron costs more than it gains below some radius. An attraction more singular than $1/r^2$ at the origin — or a $-c/r^2$ with $c$ larger than Hardy's $\hbar^2/8m_e$ — would beat the uncertainty principle and collapse again; the Coulomb potential is far from either.

The second derivative. $E'' = 3\hbar^2/m_er^4 - 2k/r^3$. At $r = a_0$, $\hbar^2/m_e = ka_0$, so $E'' = 3k/a_0^3 - 2k/a_0^3 = k/a_0^3 > 0$.

Reading the figure. Energies in Ry and radii in $a_0$, so the three curves are $1/x^2$, $-2/x$ and their sum $1/x^2 - 2/x$, which crosses zero at $x = \tfrac12$ and has its minimum $-1$ at $x = 1$. The kinetic curve is plotted only where it is below $2$ Ry, the potential only where it is above $-1.5$ Ry.

History. Bohr's 1913 model got $a_0$ and $-13.6$ eV by postulating that angular momentum comes in units of $\hbar$ on circular orbits — right numbers, partly wrong reasons: his ground state has $L = \hbar$, and the true one has $L = 0$, as section 3 shows.

24b · the scale

Exact by luck in the constant, not in the scaling

The estimate lands on the exact answer of section 3. That is luck. Taking the momentum to be $\hbar/2r$ instead — the bound $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ read literally — gives

$$E(r) = \frac{\hbar^2}{8m_er^2} - \frac kr$$ $$\Rightarrow\quad r = \tfrac14a_0,\qquad E = -4\,\mathrm{Ry} = -54.4\ \mathrm{eV} .$$

What is not luck is the scaling: any such estimate gives $r\propto\hbar^2/m_ek$ and $E\propto -m_ek^2/\hbar^2$, because those are the only length and energy available. The heuristic fixes the form; only an exact solution fixes the factor.

the energy is bounded below · a theorem

Hardy's inequality: for every smooth $\psi$ of compact support in $\mathbb R^3$, $$\int\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^2\,\mathrm d^3x\ \ge\ \frac14\int\frac{\lvert\psi\rvert^2}{r^2}\,\mathrm d^3x .$$ Hence, for every normalised such $\psi$, $\ \langle\psi, H\psi\rangle\ \ge\ -4\,\mathrm{Ry} = -54.4\ \mathrm{eV}$.

full proof in the notes (N)

so the repair is not a heuristic

No state of hydrogen lies below $-54.4$ eV. The bound is a factor of four from the truth, and it is exactly the careless estimate on the left — Hardy's constant $\tfrac14$ is what $\hbar/2r$ was guessing at.

Full proof of Hardy's inequality

Let $\psi$ be smooth with compact support, and $\vec F = \vec x/r^2$, which has $\nabla\cdot\vec F = 3/r^2 + \vec x\cdot\nabla(r^{-2}) = 3/r^2 - 2/r^2 = 1/r^2$ away from the origin. For real $t$, $$0\le\int\big\lvert\nabla\psi + t\,\vec F\psi\big\rvert^2 = \int\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^2 + t^2\int\frac{\lvert\psi\rvert^2}{r^2} + t\int\vec F\cdot\nabla\lvert\psi\rvert^2 ,$$ using $\nabla\lvert\psi\rvert^2 = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\bar\psi\nabla\psi)$. The last integral is $-\int\lvert\psi\rvert^2\,\nabla\cdot\vec F = -\int\lvert\psi\rvert^2/r^2$ by the divergence theorem (unit 05), applied outside a small ball about the origin: the boundary term on the sphere of radius $\epsilon$ is at most $4\pi\epsilon^2\cdot\epsilon^{-1}\max\lvert\psi\rvert^2\to0$. So $0\le\int\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^2 + (t^2 - t)\int\lvert\psi\rvert^2/r^2$, and $t = \tfrac12$ gives the inequality. $\square$

Full proof of the bound on the energy

For normalised $\psi$, $\langle T\rangle = (\hbar^2/2m_e)\int\lvert\nabla\psi\rvert^2\ge(\hbar^2/8m_e)\langle r^{-2}\rangle$ by Hardy, and $\langle r^{-1}\rangle^2\le\langle r^{-2}\rangle\langle1\rangle = \langle r^{-2}\rangle$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. With $s = \langle r^{-1}\rangle$, $\langle H\rangle\ge(\hbar^2/8m_e)s^2 - ks$, a parabola in $s$ with minimum at $s = 4m_ek/\hbar^2$ and minimum value $-2m_ek^2/\hbar^2 = -4\,\mathrm{Ry} = -54.42$ eV. $\square$ Problem P3 asks for both proofs and for a function that comes close to equality in Hardy's inequality.

Why bother. The uncertainty relation of unit 21b bounds $\Delta x\,\Delta p$, which says nothing about how $\langle 1/r\rangle$ compares with $\langle p^2\rangle$; a state could, as far as Robertson's inequality knows, be concentrated near the nucleus with its spread carried by a far-away tail. Hardy's inequality is the local uncertainty principle that closes that gap, and it is what makes "the uncertainty principle stabilises the atom" a theorem rather than a slogan.

24b · angular momentum

Angular momentum as an operator

orbital angular momentum

On $L^2(\mathbb R^3)$, with $p_k = -i\hbar\,\partial_k$ and $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ unit 14's Levi-Civita symbol, $$L_i = \sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{ijk}\,x_jp_k ,\qquad\text{e.g. } L_z = -i\hbar\,(x\partial_y - y\partial_x) .$$

No ordering question arises: $\varepsilon_{ijk}\ne0$ needs $j\ne k$, and $x_j$ commutes with $p_k$ then. Each $L_i$ is symmetric.

the commutators

From $[x_j, p_k] = i\hbar\,\delta_{jk}$ (unit 21b) alone, summing over repeated indices: $$[L_i, x_j] = i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{ijk}x_k,\qquad [L_i, p_j] = i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{ijk}p_k,$$ $$[L_i, L_j] = i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{ijk}L_k .$$

proof in the notes (N)

Dirac's rule, exact for once

Unit 14 found the Poisson brackets $\{L_i, L_j\} = \varepsilon_{ijk}L_k$ for Kepler. Replace $\{\,,\}$ by $[\,,]/i\hbar$ and you get the third commutator exactly. Groenewold's theorem (unit 21b) says this cannot work for all observables; for those at most quadratic in $x$ and $p$, it does.

checks

Dimensions: $[L] = \mathrm{m\cdot kg\,m\,s^{-1}} = \mathrm{J\,s}$, so both sides of $[L_i,L_j] = i\hbar L_k$ are $(\mathrm{J\,s})^2$ ✓. Special case: $[L_z, x] = [-y\,p_x, x] = -y(-i\hbar) = i\hbar\,y$, and $\varepsilon_{31k}x_k = \varepsilon_{312}\,y = y$ ✓.

Full proof of the commutators

Repeated indices are summed. Use the Leibniz rule $[A, BC] = [A,B]C + B[A,C]$ and $[x_j, p_k] = i\hbar\delta_{jk}$, $[x_j,x_k] = [p_j,p_k] = 0$.

With $x$. $[L_i, x_m] = \varepsilon_{ijk}[x_jp_k, x_m] = \varepsilon_{ijk}x_j[p_k, x_m] = -i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{ijm}x_j = i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{imj}x_j$.

With $p$. $[L_i, p_m] = \varepsilon_{ijk}[x_j, p_m]p_k = i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{imk}p_k$.

With itself. $[L_i, L_j] = \varepsilon_{jab}[L_i, x_ap_b] = \varepsilon_{jab}([L_i,x_a]p_b + x_a[L_i,p_b]) = i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{jab}(\varepsilon_{iac}x_cp_b + \varepsilon_{ibc}x_ap_c)$. The identity $\varepsilon_{jab}\varepsilon_{iac} = \delta_{ji}\delta_{bc} - \delta_{jc}\delta_{bi}$ turns the first term into $\delta_{ij}\,\vec x\cdot\vec p - x_jp_i$, and similarly the second is $x_ip_j - \delta_{ij}\,\vec x\cdot\vec p$. The sum is $i\hbar(x_ip_j - x_jp_i)$, and $x_ip_j - x_jp_i = \varepsilon_{ijk}L_k$ (for $(i,j) = (1,2)$ it is $xp_y - yp_x = L_z$; the rest by cyclic relabelling and antisymmetry). $\square$

The identity used. $\varepsilon_{jab}\varepsilon_{iac} = \delta_{ji}\delta_{bc} - \delta_{jc}\delta_{bi}$ is the component form of BAC–CAB (unit 08); unit 14 used the same contraction.

Symmetry, and the domain. $L_i$ is a sum of products $x_jp_k$ of commuting symmetric operators, so it is symmetric on smooth rapidly decaying functions. Its self-adjointness follows from the next slide: it generates a unitary group, and Stone's theorem (unit 21b) then says its closure is self-adjoint.

$\vec L$ generates rotations

the rotation operator

For $R\in SO(3)$ (unit 16) put $(U(R)\psi)(\vec x) = \psi(R^{-1}\vec x)$: the wavefunction carried round rigidly. $U(R)$ is unitary, since $\det R = 1$.

theorem

For rotation by $\theta$ about the unit vector $\hat n$, $$U(R_{\hat n}(\theta)) = \exp\!\big(-i\theta\,\hat n\cdot\vec L/\hbar\big),$$ as translation by $\vec a$ is $\exp(-i\,\vec a\cdot\vec p/\hbar)$ (the same argument; notes).

Why: $R^{-1}\vec x = \vec x - \theta\,\hat n\times\vec x + O(\theta^2)$, so $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\theta}U\psi\big|_0 = -(\hat n\times\vec x)\cdot\nabla\psi = -\hat n\cdot(\vec x\times\nabla\psi) = -\tfrac{i}{\hbar}\,\hat n\cdot\vec L\,\psi$.

So $\ell_k = -iL_k/\hbar$ obey $[\ell_i,\ell_j] = \varepsilon_{ijk}\ell_k$: unit 16's $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, now acting on wavefunctions.

$U(R)\psi$ takes at $\vec x$ the value $\psi$ had at $R^{-1}\vec x$, so its picture is $\psi$'s picture turned forward by $R$. The inverse is what makes $U(R_1)U(R_2) = U(R_1R_2)$.

Full proof

The derivative. Rodrigues' formula (unit 16) gives $R_{\hat n}(\theta)\vec x = \vec x + \sin\theta\,\hat n\times\vec x + (1-\cos\theta)\,\hat n\times(\hat n\times\vec x)$, and $R^{-1} = R_{\hat n}(-\theta)$, so $R^{-1}\vec x = \vec x - \theta\,\hat n\times\vec x + O(\theta^2)$. By the chain rule (unit 03) $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\theta}\psi(R^{-1}\vec x)\big|_{\theta=0} = -(\hat n\times\vec x)\cdot\nabla\psi$. The triple product rearranges it to $-\hat n\cdot(\vec x\times\nabla\psi)$, and $\vec x\times\nabla = (i/\hbar)\,\vec x\times\vec p = (i/\hbar)\vec L$.

From the derivative to the exponential. Rotations about one axis form a one-parameter group, $R(\theta+\phi) = R(\theta)R(\phi)$, and $U(R_1R_2) = U(R_1)U(R_2)$ (check: $(U(R_1)U(R_2)\psi)(\vec x) = (U(R_2)\psi)(R_1^{-1}\vec x) = \psi(R_2^{-1}R_1^{-1}\vec x)$). So $\theta\mapsto U(R(\theta))$ is a strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group; by Stone's theorem (unit 21b) it is $\exp(-i\theta G/\hbar)$ for a unique self-adjoint $G$, and the derivative just computed identifies $G = \hat n\cdot\vec L$ on smooth functions.

The Lie algebra. $[\ell_i,\ell_j] = (-i/\hbar)^2[L_i,L_j] = -\hbar^{-2}\,i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{ijk}L_k = \varepsilon_{ijk}(-iL_k/\hbar)$. Unit 16's basis of $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ — the antisymmetric matrices $\hat e_k$ — obeys the same relations, so $\hat e_k\mapsto\ell_k$ is a Lie algebra representation: the derivative at the identity of the group representation $R\mapsto U(R)$.

The parallel with momentum is exact, and proved the same way. $(T(\vec a)\psi)(\vec x) = \psi(\vec x - \vec a)$ is unitary, $T(s\hat e)T(t\hat e) = T((s+t)\hat e)$ along any unit vector $\hat e$, and $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\psi(\vec x - s\hat e)\big|_0 = -\hat e\cdot\nabla\psi = -(i/\hbar)\,\hat e\cdot\vec p\,\psi$. Stone's theorem then gives $T(s\hat e) = \exp(-is\,\hat e\cdot\vec p/\hbar)$. Momentum is to translations what angular momentum is to rotations; unit 21b's half-line, where leftward translation is not unitary and the momentum has no self-adjoint extension, is the case where the parallel breaks.

Rotational symmetry is conservation: Noether, quantum version

lemma · change of an expectation

If $i\hbar\,\dot\psi = H\psi$ and $A$ does not depend on $t$, then $$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle\psi, A\psi\rangle = \frac i\hbar\,\langle\psi, [H,A]\,\psi\rangle .$$

Why: $\langle\dot\psi,A\psi\rangle + \langle\psi,A\dot\psi\rangle = \frac i\hbar\langle H\psi,A\psi\rangle - \frac i\hbar\langle\psi,AH\psi\rangle$, and $H$ is self-adjoint.

theorem · central potentials

For $H = p^2/2\mu + V(r)$: $\ [H, L_i] = 0$ for each $i$. So every $\langle L_i\rangle$ is constant, and each eigenspace of $H$ is mapped into itself by $\vec L$.

Why: $[L_i, p^2] = i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{ijk}(p_kp_j + p_jp_k) = 0$ by antisymmetry, and $L_if(r) = -i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{ijk}x_jx_kf'(r)/r = 0$ likewise.

unit 13, quantum

Classically, Noether's theorem turned rotation invariance of the Lagrangian into conservation of $\vec L$, and unit 13 warned that the converse fails. Here it holds both ways: $[H, G] = 0$ for self-adjoint $G$ iff $H$ commutes with every $e^{-isG/\hbar}$ — the symmetry is the conserved quantity, by Stone.

on hydrogen

$V = -k/r$ is central, so $H$, $L^2$ and $L_z$ can be diagonalised together: the states will be labelled $(n, l, m)$. If $H\psi = E\psi$ then $H(L_i\psi) = L_iH\psi = E\,L_i\psi$: rotation cannot change the energy.

Full proof of the lemma

The inner product is antilinear in its first slot, and $\dot\psi = -(i/\hbar)H\psi$. So $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\langle\psi,A\psi\rangle = \langle-\tfrac i\hbar H\psi, A\psi\rangle + \langle\psi, A(-\tfrac i\hbar)H\psi\rangle = \tfrac i\hbar\langle H\psi,A\psi\rangle - \tfrac i\hbar\langle\psi,AH\psi\rangle$, and self-adjointness moves $H$ across: $\langle H\psi, A\psi\rangle = \langle\psi, HA\psi\rangle$. (Domains: assume $\psi(t)$ stays in a common domain of $H$, $A$, $HA$ and $AH$, as it does for the smooth rapidly decaying states used here.) This is the Ehrenfest computation of unit 21b's problem sheet, with $A$ general.

Full proof of the theorem

$[L_i, p^2] = [L_i,p_j]p_j + p_j[L_i,p_j] = i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{ijk}(p_kp_j + p_jp_k)$, which vanishes because $p_kp_j + p_jp_k$ is symmetric in $j,k$ and $\varepsilon_{ijk}$ antisymmetric. For a radial function, $\partial_kf(r) = f'(r)x_k/r$, so $L_if = -i\hbar\,\varepsilon_{ijk}x_jx_kf'/r = 0$ by the same antisymmetry, and hence $[L_i, f]\psi = L_i(f\psi) - fL_i\psi = (L_if)\psi = 0$ (the operator $L_i$ is a first-order derivation). $\square$

The converse, and the contrast with unit 13. If $[H, G] = 0$ then $H$ commutes with every function of $G$, in particular with $e^{-isG/\hbar}$, so the one-parameter group generated by $G$ maps solutions to solutions. Conversely, differentiating $e^{isG/\hbar}He^{-isG/\hbar} = H$ at $s = 0$ gives $[G,H] = 0$. (Making "commutes" precise for unbounded operators means commuting spectral projections; cited, Reed and Simon, vol. I, ch. VIII.) In unit 13 the converse failed because a conserved quantity's flow need not act on configuration space; unit 14 rescued it in phase space; here every self-adjoint operator generates a flow, and the rescue is automatic.

24b · angular momentum

Ladder operators

the ladder operators

$$L_\pm = L_x\pm iL_y,\qquad L^2 = L_x^2 + L_y^2 + L_z^2 .$$ $(L_\pm)^\dagger = L_\mp$ on the common domain, since $L_x$, $L_y$ are symmetric.

their algebra

$$[L_z, L_\pm] = \pm\hbar L_\pm,\qquad [L_+, L_-] = 2\hbar L_z,\qquad [L^2, L_i] = 0,$$ $$L_\mp L_\pm = L^2 - L_z^2\mp\hbar L_z .$$

Why the first: $[L_z, L_x\pm iL_y] = i\hbar L_y\pm i(-i\hbar L_x) = \hbar(\pm L_x + iL_y) = \pm\hbar L_\pm$.

what they do

If $L_z\psi = \hbar m\psi$, then $L_z(L_\pm\psi) = (L_\pm L_z\pm\hbar L_\pm)\psi = \hbar(m\pm1)\,L_\pm\psi$. And $L^2$ commutes with $L_\pm$, so $L_\pm\psi$ keeps the eigenvalue of $L^2$. The ladder moves $m$ by one and leaves the rest alone.

This is unit 21b's oscillator ladder $a$, $a^\dagger$ again, with one difference that decides everything: there, $N = a^\dagger a$ was bounded below and not above; here $L_z^2\le L^2$, so the ladder is bounded both ways.

$L^2$ commutes with all of $\mathfrak{so}(3)$: it is the Casimir operator of the algebra, the one quadratic combination every rotation leaves alone.

Full proofs

$[L_+, L_-]$. $[L_x + iL_y, L_x - iL_y] = -i[L_x,L_y] + i[L_y,L_x] = -2i[L_x,L_y] = -2i\cdot i\hbar L_z = 2\hbar L_z$.

$[L^2, L_z]$. $[L_x^2, L_z] = L_x[L_x,L_z] + [L_x,L_z]L_x = -i\hbar(L_xL_y + L_yL_x)$ and $[L_y^2, L_z] = L_y[L_y,L_z] + [L_y,L_z]L_y = i\hbar(L_yL_x + L_xL_y)$; they cancel, and $[L_z^2, L_z] = 0$. The other components follow by cyclic relabelling, which the commutation relations respect.

The product identity. $L_\mp L_\pm = (L_x\mp iL_y)(L_x\pm iL_y) = L_x^2 + L_y^2\pm i[L_x,L_y] = L_x^2 + L_y^2\mp\hbar L_z = L^2 - L_z^2\mp\hbar L_z$.

"Bounded both ways". For a normalised $\psi$ in the domain, $\langle\psi, L^2\psi\rangle - \langle\psi,L_z^2\psi\rangle = \lVert L_x\psi\rVert^2 + \lVert L_y\psi\rVert^2\ge0$. So on a joint eigenvector, $m^2\hbar^2\le$ the eigenvalue of $L^2$.

The word Casimir. An element built from the generators that commutes with all of them; for $\mathfrak{so}(3)$, $L^2$ is the only quadratic one up to a factor. Named after Hendrik Casimir, who introduced the general notion in 1931.

The spectrum of $L^2$ and $L_z$, from the algebra alone

theorem

Let $\psi\ne0$ satisfy $L^2\psi = \hbar^2\lambda\,\psi$, $L_z\psi = \hbar m\,\psi$, with $\psi$ in a domain the $L_i$ preserve. Then $$\lambda = l(l+1),\qquad l\in\{0, \tfrac12, 1, \tfrac32, \dots\},$$ $$m\in\{-l, -l+1, \dots, l\}.$$ Each $l$ comes with $2l+1$ values of $m$, joined by $L_\pm$: a multiplet.

the key line

$\lVert L_\pm\psi\rVert^2 = \langle\psi, L_\mp L_\pm\psi\rangle = \hbar^2\big(\lambda - m(m\pm1)\big)\lVert\psi\rVert^2\ \ge\ 0$.
So $m(m+1)\le\lambda$ and $m(m-1)\le\lambda$: the ladder cannot climb or fall for ever.

full proof in the notes (N)

The $l = 2$ multiplet. $\lVert L_+\psi\rVert^2 = \hbar^2(6 - m(m+1))$ is $0$ exactly at $m = 2$, and would be $-6\hbar^2$ at $m = 3$: the rung above the top cannot exist.

Full proof

Step 1, the norms. $\langle\psi, L_\mp L_\pm\psi\rangle = \langle L_\pm\psi, L_\pm\psi\rangle$ since $L_\mp = L_\pm^\dagger$, and by the product identity of the previous slide this equals $\langle\psi,(L^2 - L_z^2\mp\hbar L_z)\psi\rangle = \hbar^2(\lambda - m^2\mp m)\lVert\psi\rVert^2$.

Step 2, the ladder stops at the top. Apply $L_+$ repeatedly. While $L_+^j\psi\ne0$ it is a joint eigenvector with the same $\lambda$ and $m + j$ (previous slide). Step 1 forces $(m+j)(m+j+1)\le\lambda$, which fails for large $j$; so some $L_+^{j}\psi\ne0$ has $L_+^{j+1}\psi = 0$. Call its $m$-value $m_\mathrm{top}$; step 1 with norm zero gives $\lambda = m_\mathrm{top}(m_\mathrm{top}+1)$.

Step 3, and at the bottom. The same with $L_-$ gives $m_\mathrm{bot}$ with $\lambda = m_\mathrm{bot}(m_\mathrm{bot} - 1)$, and $m_\mathrm{top} - m_\mathrm{bot}$ is a non-negative integer, since both were reached from $m$ in whole steps.

Step 4, solve. $m_\mathrm{top}^2 + m_\mathrm{top} = m_\mathrm{bot}^2 - m_\mathrm{bot}$ rearranges to $(m_\mathrm{top} + m_\mathrm{bot})(m_\mathrm{top} - m_\mathrm{bot} + 1) = 0$. The second factor is at least $1$, so $m_\mathrm{bot} = -m_\mathrm{top}$. Write $l = m_\mathrm{top}$: then $2l = m_\mathrm{top} - m_\mathrm{bot}$ is a non-negative integer, $\lambda = l(l+1)$, and $m$ lies among $-l, \dots, l$. $\square$

Checks. Dimensions: $\hbar^2l(l+1)$ is an angular momentum squared ✓. Special case $l = 0$: one state, killed by all three $L_i$ — the rotation-invariant functions ✓. Classical limit: for large $l$, $\sqrt{l(l+1)} = l + \tfrac12 + O(1/l)$, and at $m = l$ the transverse part $\sqrt{L^2 - L_z^2} = \hbar\sqrt l$ is negligible relative to $\hbar l$: the vector can never point exactly along $z$, but nearly.

Where the domain hypothesis is used. Step 2 needs $L_+^j\psi$ to stay in a space where the identities hold. For smooth functions on the sphere (the case that matters) this is automatic.

Integers only: single-valuedness, and the half-integers that are spin

In spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\phi)$ about $z$, the chain rule (unit 03) gives

$$L_z = -i\hbar\,(x\partial_y - y\partial_x) = -i\hbar\,\partial_\phi .$$

So $L_z\psi = \hbar m\psi$ means $\psi = e^{im\phi}g(r,\theta)$. A function on $\mathbb R^3$ takes one value at each point, and $\phi$ and $\phi + 2\pi$ are the same point:

orbital angular momentum is integral

$e^{2\pi im} = 1$ forces $m\in\mathbb Z$; since $l - m$ is an integer, $l\in\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$.

the algebra allowed more

The theorem permitted $l = \tfrac12, \tfrac32, \dots$, and nothing in the commutators excludes them. They are excluded for functions on space. Matter uses them anyway: the electron carries an internal angular momentum with quantum number $\tfrac12$, its spin, which is not of the form $\vec x\times\vec p$.

how it was seen

The Stern–Gerlach experiment (1922): a beam of silver atoms through a non-uniform magnetic field splits into two — an even number, $2l+1 = 2$, impossible for any integer $l$. This course names spin and goes no further with it.

$L_z = -i\hbar\,\partial_\phi$. With $x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi$, $z = r\cos\theta$, the chain rule gives $\partial_\phi = (\partial x/\partial\phi)\partial_x + (\partial y/\partial\phi)\partial_y = -y\,\partial_x + x\,\partial_y$. So $-i\hbar\,\partial_\phi = -i\hbar(x\partial_y - y\partial_x) = L_z$.

The rigorous version. On $L^2(S^1)$, $-i\hbar\,\mathrm d/\mathrm d\phi$ with the periodic domain is self-adjoint and its spectrum is $\hbar\mathbb Z$ — unit 19's complex Fourier series diagonalises it. A twisted condition $\psi(2\pi) = e^{i\beta}\psi(0)$ is also self-adjoint (unit 21b's deficiency indices) and gives $m\in\mathbb Z + \beta/2\pi$; it is excluded because a wavefunction is a function on space, single-valued, and nothing physical distinguishes $\phi = 0$.

History. Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach, Frankfurt, 1922. They interpreted the two spots as the "space quantisation" of the Bohr–Sommerfeld model; it took Uhlenbeck and Goudsmit's proposal of electron spin (1925) to see that silver's two spots are the two states of a single unpaired electron's spin, with the orbital angular momentum of the atom zero. The mathematics of $l = \tfrac12$ — spinors, and the double cover $SU(2)\to SO(3)$ — is outside this course, which excludes spinors throughout.

Spherical harmonics are the joint eigenfunctions

$L^2$ is the angular Laplacian

$$L^2 = r^2p^2 - (\vec x\cdot\vec p)^2 + i\hbar\,\vec x\cdot\vec p\quad\Longrightarrow\quad L^2 = -\hbar^2\Delta_{S^2},$$ with $\Delta_{S^2} = \frac1{\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta) + \frac1{\sin^2\theta}\partial_\phi^2$, the angular part of unit 19's Laplacian.

proof in the notes (N)

So the joint eigenfunctions of $L^2$ and $L_z$ on the sphere are unit 19's spherical harmonics, $$Y_l^m(\theta,\phi) = N_{lm}\,P_l^m(\cos\theta)\,e^{im\phi},$$ built from unit 10's Legendre polynomials: $P_l^m(x) = (1-x^2)^{m/2}\frac{\mathrm d^m}{\mathrm dx^m}P_l(x)$ for $m\ge0$. The algebra and the differential equation agree: $l(l+1)$, and $2l+1$ of them.

Full proof of the identity

Summing repeated indices, $L^2 = \varepsilon_{ijk}\varepsilon_{ilm}x_jp_kx_lp_m = (\delta_{jl}\delta_{km} - \delta_{jm}\delta_{kl})x_jp_kx_lp_m = x_jp_kx_jp_k - x_jp_kx_kp_j$. First term: $p_kx_j = x_jp_k - i\hbar\delta_{jk}$, so it is $r^2p^2 - i\hbar\,\vec x\cdot\vec p$. Second term: $p_kx_k = \vec x\cdot\vec p - 3i\hbar$, so it is $x_j(\vec x\cdot\vec p)p_j - 3i\hbar\,\vec x\cdot\vec p$; and $(\vec x\cdot\vec p)^2 = x_jp_jx_kp_k = x_jx_kp_jp_k - i\hbar\,\vec x\cdot\vec p$, so $x_j(\vec x\cdot\vec p)p_j = x_jx_kp_kp_j = (\vec x\cdot\vec p)^2 + i\hbar\,\vec x\cdot\vec p$. Altogether $L^2 = r^2p^2 - i\hbar\,\vec x\cdot\vec p - (\vec x\cdot\vec p)^2 - i\hbar\,\vec x\cdot\vec p + 3i\hbar\,\vec x\cdot\vec p$, which is the identity.

Into spherical coordinates. $\vec x\cdot\vec p = -i\hbar\,r\partial_r$ (the chain rule: $\vec x\cdot\nabla = r\partial_r$), and $p^2 = -\hbar^2\Delta$ with $r^2\Delta = \partial_r(r^2\partial_r) + \Delta_{S^2} = r^2\partial_r^2 + 2r\partial_r + \Delta_{S^2}$ (unit 19). Two bodies become one: the reduced mass

Electron at $\vec x_e$, proton at $\vec x_p$: $\ H = \dfrac{p_e^2}{2m_e} + \dfrac{p_p^2}{2m_p} - \dfrac{k}{\lvert\vec x_e - \vec x_p\rvert}$ on $L^2(\mathbb R^6)$.

Unit 08's change of variables, now of operators: $M = m_e + m_p$, $\vec R = (m_e\vec x_e + m_p\vec x_p)/M$, $\vec x = \vec x_e - \vec x_p$, and $$\vec P = \vec p_e + \vec p_p,\qquad\vec p = \frac{m_p\vec p_e - m_e\vec p_p}{M}.$$

separation

$(\vec R,\vec P)$ and $(\vec x,\vec p)$ obey the canonical commutators and commute with each other, and $$H = \frac{P^2}{2M} + \frac{p^2}{2\mu} - \frac kr,\qquad \mu = \frac{m_em_p}{m_e + m_p}.$$

So $\Psi = e^{i\vec K\cdot\vec R}\,\psi(\vec x)$, the centre of mass a free plane wave and $\psi$ the eigenfunction of a one-body problem with the reduced mass $\mu$.

for hydrogen

$\mu = m_e/(1 + m_e/m_p) = m_e/1.000544617$. Every $m_e$ in the atomic scale becomes $\mu$: $a = \hbar^2/\mu k = 1.000545\,a_0$ and $\mathrm{Ry}_\mathrm H = 13.6057/1.000545 = 13.5983$ eV.

Limit: $m_p\to\infty$ gives $\mu\to m_e$, a fixed nucleus ✓. Dimensions: $\mu$ is a mass ✓. Five parts in $10^4$ is far below what the uncertainty estimate can see — and is exactly what section 6 will need.

Full proof of the separation

Commutators. Componentwise, $[R_a, P_b] = (m_e[x_{e,a},p_{e,b}] + m_p[x_{p,a},p_{p,b}])/M = i\hbar\delta_{ab}$; $[x_a,p_b] = (m_p[x_{e,a},p_{e,b}] + m_e[x_{p,a},p_{p,b}])/M = i\hbar\delta_{ab}$, the minus signs cancelling in the second; $[R_a,p_b] = (m_em_p - m_pm_e)\,i\hbar\delta_{ab}/M^2 = 0$; $[x_a,P_b] = i\hbar\delta_{ab} - i\hbar\delta_{ab} = 0$.

The kinetic energy. Invert: $\vec p_e = (m_e/M)\vec P + \vec p$ and $\vec p_p = (m_p/M)\vec P - \vec p$. Then $\frac{p_e^2}{2m_e} + \frac{p_p^2}{2m_p} = \frac{m_e P^2}{2M^2} + \frac{\vec P\cdot\vec p}{M} + \frac{p^2}{2m_e} + \frac{m_pP^2}{2M^2} - \frac{\vec P\cdot\vec p}{M} + \frac{p^2}{2m_p}$ $= \frac{P^2}{2M} + \frac{p^2}{2\mu}$. The cross terms cancel exactly as in unit 08 (and $\vec P$, $\vec p$ commute, so there is no ordering issue). The potential depends on $\vec x$ alone.

Why the plane wave is allowed. $e^{i\vec K\cdot\vec R}$ is not normalisable, so strictly the centre-of-mass motion has continuous spectrum; one works in the centre-of-mass frame, $\vec K = 0$, or with a wave packet in $\vec R$ whose spreading (unit 21b) does not affect the internal state at all. Only the internal $\psi$ matters for the spectrum.

The numbers. $m_e/m_p = 1/1836.15267 = 5.44617\times10^{-4}$, so $\mu/m_e = 0.999455679$ and $\mathrm{Ry}_\mathrm H = 13.60569\times0.999456 = 13.59829$ eV, which is hydrogen's measured ionisation energy (13.598 eV) to the accuracy that fine structure allows.

The two ends: what Frobenius and the far field allow

Measure lengths in $a = \hbar^2/\mu k$ and energy in $\mathrm{Ry}_\mu = \mu k^2/2\hbar^2$, with $\rho = r/a$ and $E = -\kappa^2\,\mathrm{Ry}_\mu$ ($\kappa > 0$ for a bound state):

$$u'' = \Big[\frac{l(l+1)}{\rho^2} - \frac2\rho + \kappa^2\Big]u .$$
near $\rho = 0$ · unit 09

A regular singular point. $u\sim\rho^s$ gives the indicial equation $s(s-1) = l(l+1)$, roots $s = l+1$ and $s = -l$.

near $\rho = \infty$

The equation tends to $u'' = \kappa^2u$: solutions behave like $e^{\pm\kappa\rho}$, times powers.

two conditions, one free constant

At 0, keep $s = l+1$, so $u(0) = 0$. The root $s = -l$ is not square-integrable for $l\ge1$; for $l = 0$ it gives $\psi\sim1/r$, and $\Delta(1/r) = -4\pi\delta$ (unit 23): not a solution at the origin.

At $\infty$, keep $e^{-\kappa\rho}$. Fixing the solution at 0 leaves nothing to choose at $\infty$, so for most $\kappa$ the growing exponential appears. Bound states are the $\kappa$ for which it does not.

This is a singular Sturm–Liouville problem (unit 19) on $(0,\infty)$, and the eigenvalue condition is the same shape as the drum's: regularity at one end, a condition at the other.

The dimensionless form. Put $r = a\rho$. Then $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a^2}u_{\rho\rho} + \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a^2}\frac{l(l+1)}{\rho^2}u - \frac ka\frac1\rho u = Eu$. With $a = \hbar^2/\mu k$: $\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu a^2} = \frac{\mu k^2}{2\hbar^2} = \mathrm{Ry}_\mu$ and $\frac ka = \frac{\mu k^2}{\hbar^2} = 2\,\mathrm{Ry}_\mu$. Divide by $\mathrm{Ry}_\mu$ and set $E/\mathrm{Ry}_\mu = -\kappa^2$.

The indicial equation. Multiply by $\rho^2$: $\rho^2u'' = [l(l+1) - 2\rho + \kappa^2\rho^2]u$. The coefficient of $u$ is analytic at $0$ with value $l(l+1)$ there, so $\rho = 0$ is a regular singular point (unit 09), and $u = \rho^s(1 + \dots)$ requires $s(s-1) = l(l+1)$, i.e. $(s - l - 1)(s + l) = 0$.

Why $s = -l$ fails for $l = 0$. Then $u\to$ const and $\psi\sim c/r$ near the origin, which is square-integrable. But $-\frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}\Delta(c/r) = \frac{\hbar^2}{2\mu}4\pi c\,\delta$ (unit 23's Green's function), so $H\psi$ contains a delta function that $E\psi$ does not: $\psi$ is not in the domain of the self-adjoint $H$. This is the domain lesson of unit 21b in a concrete case.

That $H$ is self-adjoint at all — used here, on the Noether slide and in the variational principle — is cited: Kato's theorem (1951), Reed and Simon, vol. II, Theorem X.15. $H = p^2/2\mu - k/r$ is self-adjoint on the domain of $p^2$ alone. The proof is short once the machinery is in hand, and its engine is Hardy's inequality again: it makes $k/r$ small relative to $p^2$, so the Coulomb term cannot change the domain.

The far field. For large $\rho$ the $1/\rho$ and $1/\rho^2$ terms are small against $\kappa^2$. The precise statement — that solutions are $e^{\pm\kappa\rho}\rho^{\pm1/\kappa}(1 + O(1/\rho))$ — is unit 21's WKB, and is not needed: the next slide's series argument settles the behaviour directly.

The series, and why it must stop

Peel off both ends: $u = \rho^{l+1}e^{-\kappa\rho}f(\rho)$. Then $$\rho f'' + 2(l + 1 - \kappa\rho)f' + 2\big(1 - \kappa(l+1)\big)f = 0,$$ and $f = \sum_j c_j\rho^j$ (unit 09) gives the recurrence

$$c_{j+1} = \frac{2\big[\kappa(j + l + 1) - 1\big]}{(j+1)(j + 2l + 2)}\,c_j .$$
theorem · termination is quantisation

If no $c_j$ vanishes, $u$ grows at least like $e^{\kappa\rho/2}$ and is not square-integrable. So the series must stop: $c_{N+1} = 0$ for some $N\ge0$, which happens iff $$\kappa = \frac1{N + l + 1}.$$

full proof in the notes (N)

the idea

For large $j$, $c_{j+1}/c_j\approx2\kappa/j$ — the ratio of consecutive coefficients of $e^{2\kappa\rho}$. A series that never stops is eventually $e^{2\kappa\rho}$ in disguise, and then $u\sim e^{-\kappa\rho}e^{2\kappa\rho} = e^{+\kappa\rho}$: the growing solution, which the far field had warned of.

If it stops, $f$ is a polynomial of degree $N$ and $u = \rho^{l+1}e^{-\kappa\rho}\times$ polynomial decays: a genuine eigenfunction. These polynomials are the associated Laguerre polynomials, $f\propto L^{(2l+1)}_N(2\rho/n)$ — named, not needed.

Unit 21b's oscillator did the same: a Hermite series that must stop to stay in $L^2$. Twice now, the spectrum is where a series stops.

Full proof of the theorem

The equation for $f$. With $s = l+1$, $u = \rho^se^{-\kappa\rho}f$ has $u'' = \rho^se^{-\kappa\rho}[f'' + 2(s/\rho - \kappa)f' + ((s/\rho - \kappa)^2 - s/\rho^2)f]$. Equating to $[s(s-1)/\rho^2 - 2/\rho + \kappa^2]u$, the $f$-terms leave $(2/\rho)(1 - s\kappa)f$; multiply by $\rho$.

The recurrence. Substituting $f = \sum c_j\rho^j$, the coefficient of $\rho^j$ is $(j+1)j\,c_{j+1} + 2s(j+1)c_{j+1} - 2\kappa j\,c_j + 2(1 - s\kappa)c_j = 0$, i.e. $(j+1)(j + 2s)c_{j+1} = 2[\kappa(j + s) - 1]c_j$. The series converges for every $\rho$ (ratio test: $c_{j+1}/c_j\to0$), as unit 09 promised for an equation with no other finite singular point.

Non-termination forces growth. Suppose no $c_j$ is zero. Since $(j+1)\,c_{j+1}/c_j\to2\kappa$, there is $J$ with $c_{j+1}/c_j\ge\kappa/(j+1)$ for all $j\ge J$ — all of one sign, say positive. Then $c_j\ge c_J\,J!\,\kappa^{j-J}/j!$ for $j\ge J$, and $f(\rho)\ge c_J\,J!\,\kappa^{-J}\big(e^{\kappa\rho} - \sum_{j<J}(\kappa\rho)^j/j!\big) - \lvert p(\rho)\rvert$, with $p$ the polynomial $\sum_{j<J}c_j\rho^j$. The exponential wins, so $f(\rho)\ge Ce^{\kappa\rho}$ for large $\rho$, and $u\ge C\rho^{l+1}e^{-\kappa\rho}e^{\kappa\rho}$ — in fact, choosing the threshold $2\kappa(1-\delta)$ instead of $\kappa$, $u\gtrsim e^{\kappa(1 - 2\delta)\rho}$. Either way $\int u^2 = \infty$.

Termination. $c_{N+1} = 0\ne c_N$ iff $\kappa(N + l + 1) = 1$; then every later coefficient vanishes too. Since $c_0\ne0$ and the numerators are $2[\kappa(j+l+1) - 1]$, which for $\kappa = 1/(N+l+1)$ vanish only at $j = N$, the polynomial has degree exactly $N$. $\square$ Problem P7 asks for this proof with the constants made explicit.

What happens when the series does not stop

Fix $l = 0$ and the regular start $u\approx\rho$ at the origin, and integrate outward for three energies:

  • $E = -1.0\,\mathrm{Ry}$: $\kappa = 1$, the series stops at once, $u = \rho e^{-\rho}$ decays — the ground state;
  • $E = -1.2\,\mathrm{Ry}$: too deep, $u$ sags but never reaches zero, and runs off to $+\infty$;
  • $E = -0.8\,\mathrm{Ry}$: too shallow, $u$ overshoots, crosses zero at $\rho = 2.82$ and runs off to $-\infty$.
the picture behind the theorem

The sign of the runaway flips as $E$ passes the eigenvalue. Bound states are the energies at which the solution is balanced between the two runaways — and, as unit 19's node count predicts (notes), the $n$-th has $N = n - l - 1$ zeros.

Start instead from $\rho_0 + c_1\rho_0^2$ with $f$'s coefficient $c_1 = \kappa - 1$ in place of $u$'s $d_1$ — an error of $10^{-8}$ at $\rho_0 = 10^{-4}$ — and the value at $\rho = 12$ comes out doubled, $1.48\times10^{-4}$. That is why a numerical search for eigenvalues integrates inward from the far side as well.

Unit 19's oscillation theorem. On a regular Sturm–Liouville problem the $k$-th eigenfunction has $k - 1$ interior zeros. The radial problem is singular at both ends, so the theorem is cited rather than applied, but the count it predicts holds: the polynomial $f$ of degree $N$ has $N$ positive real zeros (cited: Laguerre polynomials have only simple positive zeros), so the $(N+1)$-th state of given $l$ has $N$ nodes. The $E = -0.8$ curve, between the first and second $l = 0$ levels ($-1$ and $-0.25$), has already acquired one zero, as Sturm comparison says it must.

The variational principle

Unit 19's Rayleigh quotient, promoted: every guess is an upper bound, and the error is second order.

24b · variational

Every trial function is an upper bound

the variational principle · Rayleigh–Ritz

Let $H$ be self-adjoint with eigenvalues $E_0\le E_1\le\cdots$ and an orthonormal eigenbasis $\{\phi_j\}$ that is complete. Then for every nonzero $\psi$ in its domain $$\frac{\langle\psi, H\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi,\psi\rangle}\ \ge\ E_0 ,$$ with equality iff $H\psi = E_0\psi$.

proof

Expand $\psi = \sum_jc_j\phi_j$. Then $\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle = \sum_jE_j\lvert c_j\rvert^2\ge E_0\sum_j\lvert c_j\rvert^2 = E_0\langle\psi,\psi\rangle$, with equality iff $c_j = 0$ whenever $E_j > E_0$. $\square$

second-order accuracy

If $\psi = \phi_0 + \varepsilon\chi$ with $\chi\perp\phi_0$, the quotient is $E_0 + \varepsilon^2\langle\chi,(H - E_0)\chi\rangle/(1 + \varepsilon^2\lVert\chi\rVert^2)$: a trial function wrong at order $\varepsilon$ gives an energy wrong at order $\varepsilon^2$.

hydrogen is not quite in the hypothesis

Above $E = 0$ hydrogen's spectrum is continuous: there is no complete set of eigenfunctions. The conclusion survives — for any self-adjoint $H$ bounded below, $\inf\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle/\lVert\psi\rVert^2$ is the bottom of the spectrum (cited: the min–max principle, Reed and Simon, vol. IV, §XIII.1). Hardy's bound made hydrogen's $H$ bounded below.

Full proof, with the domain made honest

Completeness gives $\psi = \sum c_j\phi_j$ with $c_j = \langle\phi_j,\psi\rangle$ and $\sum\lvert c_j\rvert^2 = \lVert\psi\rVert^2$ (Parseval, unit 19). For $\psi$ in the domain, $\langle\phi_j, H\psi\rangle = \langle H\phi_j,\psi\rangle = E_jc_j$ by self-adjointness, so $H\psi$ has coefficients $E_jc_j$ and $\langle\psi, H\psi\rangle = \sum_j\bar c_j\,E_jc_j$ by Parseval's identity in its polarised form. The rest is the line on the slide.

The second-order statement. With $\lVert\phi_0\rVert = 1$, $\chi\perp\phi_0$ and $H\phi_0 = E_0\phi_0$: $\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle = E_0 + \varepsilon^2\langle\chi,H\chi\rangle$ (the cross terms are $\varepsilon E_0\langle\phi_0,\chi\rangle + $ c.c. $= 0$), and $\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = 1 + \varepsilon^2\lVert\chi\rVert^2$. Subtract $E_0$. This is why a crude wavefunction gives a decent energy, and why a good energy says little about the wavefunction.

Unit 19, and unit 11. The Rayleigh quotient $\int(pu'^2 + qu^2)/\int wu^2$ of unit 19 is this with $H$ a Sturm–Liouville operator, after integrating by parts; the drum's fundamental was bounded the same way. And the stationarity of the quotient at an eigenfunction is unit 11's first variation: the Schrödinger equation is the Euler–Lagrange equation of $\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle$ with $\lVert\psi\rVert = 1$ as a constraint.

The general statement, cited: for self-adjoint $H$ bounded below, $\inf\{\langle\psi,H\psi\rangle : \lVert\psi\rVert = 1\} = \inf\sigma(H)$, and the min–max values give the eigenvalues below the essential spectrum in order. For hydrogen those are exactly the $E_n$, and $\inf\sigma(H) = E_1$.

The degeneracy rotation cannot explain

Unit 08's Runge–Lenz vector, as an operator, and unit 14's $\mathfrak{so}(4)$ on each level.

24b · hidden symmetry

$n^2$ states on every level

Level $n$ holds $l = 0, \dots, n-1$, each with $2l+1$ values of $m$:

$$\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1) = 2\cdot\frac{(n-1)n}2 + n = n^2 .$$
accidental degeneracy

Rotational symmetry explains the $2l+1$: $\vec L$ commutes with $H$ and moves $m$ within a level. It explains nothing about different $l$ sharing an energy: $2s$ and $2p$ live in different effective potentials. For a generic central $V$ — the screened potential in sodium, say — they split.

"Accidental" is the name for a degeneracy one has not explained. Here the explanation is a symmetry that is not a rotation of space.

Each dot is one state $(n, l, m)$. Rows are energies, exactly level across the columns: that levelness is the accidental degeneracy.

The sum. $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l + 1) = 2\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}l + n = (n-1)n + n = n^2$. With the electron's spin, each state doubles, and level $n$ holds $2n^2$: 2, 8, 18, 32 — the lengths of the rows of the periodic table, which is where this number shows up in chemistry (the order in which the rows fill is a many-electron effect, outside this unit).

Why a generic central potential splits the levels. For $V$ not exactly $-k/r$, the radial equations for different $l$ are different Sturm–Liouville problems, and there is no reason for their eigenvalues to coincide. In sodium the outer electron sees the nucleus screened by ten inner electrons far out and unscreened close in; $s$ states, which reach the nucleus (the right figure of the radial densities), are bound more tightly than $p$ states of the same $n$, and the yellow sodium D lines at 589 nm are $3p\to3s$. Hydrogen is special exactly as the Kepler problem was special among central forces in unit 08 — Bertrand's theorem cited there, its quantum shadow here.

Classical counterpart. In the Kepler problem the energy depends on the semi-major axis only, not on the eccentricity (unit 08: $E = -k/2a$). Orbits of one energy and different angular momentum — different eccentricity — are mapped into each other by the Runge–Lenz flow (unit 14). States of one $n$ and different $l$ are the quantum version.

The algebra of $[H, \vec A] = 0$

Summation over repeated indices; $\hat x_i = x_i/r$. Three facts: $[f(\vec x), p_j] = i\hbar\,\partial_jf$; $[p^2, f] = -i\hbar(p_j\,\partial_jf + \partial_jf\,p_j)$; and $\vec L$ commutes with $r$, with $p^2$ (the Noether slide) and hence with $H$.

The kinetic part with the cross products. $p^2$ commutes with every $p_j$ and every $L_j$, so $[p^2, \vec p\times\vec L - \vec L\times\vec p] = 0$.

The potential part with the cross products. $[-k/r, \vec p\times\vec L - \vec L\times\vec p] = -k([1/r,\vec p]\times\vec L - \vec L\times[1/r,\vec p])$, and $[1/r, p_j] = i\hbar\,\partial_j(1/r) = -i\hbar\,x_j/r^3$. So this half contributes $\tfrac12\cdot i\hbar k(\vec x\times\vec L - \vec L\times\vec x)/r^3$. Expanding with $\varepsilon\varepsilon = \delta\delta - \delta\delta$ and $[x_j,p_k] = i\hbar\delta_{jk}$: $(\vec x\times\vec L)_i = x_i(\vec x\cdot\vec p) - r^2p_i$ and $(\vec L\times\vec x)_i = r^2p_i - x_i(\vec x\cdot\vec p) + 2i\hbar\,x_i$. So it contributes $\tfrac{i\hbar k}{r^3}\big(x_i(\vec x\cdot\vec p) - r^2p_i - i\hbar x_i\big)$.

The kinetic part with $\hat x$. $[p^2/2\mu, -\mu k\hat x_i] = -\tfrac k2[p^2,\hat x_i] = \tfrac{i\hbar k}2(p_jT_{ij} + T_{ij}p_j)$ with $T_{ij} = \partial_j\hat x_i = (\delta_{ij} - \hat x_i\hat x_j)/r$. Move $p_j$ to the right: $p_jT_{ij} = T_{ij}p_j - i\hbar\,\partial_jT_{ij}$, and $\partial_jT_{ij} = -2x_i/r^3$ (differentiate; the trace $\delta_{jj} = 3$ enters). So it contributes $i\hbar k\big(T_{ij}p_j + i\hbar x_i/r^3\big) = \tfrac{i\hbar k}{r^3}\big(r^2p_i - x_i(\vec x\cdot\vec p) + i\hbar x_i\big)$.

The potential part with $\hat x$ is $[-k/r, \hat x_i] = 0$. Adding the four: the second and third cancel term by term, so $[H, A_i] = 0$. $\square$ Classically the same cancellation is unit 08's $\dot{\vec A} = 0$; the $i\hbar x_i/r^3$ terms are the quantum corrections, and the symmetrisation is exactly what makes them cancel. Problem P10 uses the cited identities.

Cited. W. Pauli, "Über das Wasserstoffspektrum vom Standpunkt der neuen Quantenmechanik", Z. Physik 36, 336 (1926); the full algebra is also in M. Bander and C. Itzykson, Rev. Mod. Phys. 38, 330 (1966). The vector identity $\vec L\times\vec p = -\vec p\times\vec L + 2i\hbar\vec p$ is a two-line exercise in the same style: $(\vec L\times\vec p)_i + (\vec p\times\vec L)_i = \varepsilon_{ijk}[L_j, p_k] = \varepsilon_{ijk}i\hbar\varepsilon_{jkl}p_l = 2i\hbar\,p_i$.

24b · hidden symmetry

$\mathfrak{so}(4)$ on each level, and $n^2$ out of it

On the eigenspace $\mathcal H_E$ of a bound energy $E < 0$ — which $\vec L$ and $\vec A$ preserve, since both commute with $H$ — put $\vec M = \vec A/\sqrt{-2\mu E}$:

$$[L_i,L_j] = i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}L_k,\qquad [L_i,M_j] = i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}M_k,$$ $$[M_i,M_j] = i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}L_k :$$

unit 14's $\mathfrak{so}(4)$. Split it as unit 14 did, $\vec J^\pm = \tfrac12(\vec L\pm\vec M)$: two commuting copies of the angular-momentum algebra.

Pauli's derivation, in structure

$\vec L\cdot\vec M = 0$ gives $(J^+)^2 = (J^-)^2 = \hbar^2j(j+1)$, one $j$ for both. Then $A^2$ gives $\hbar^2(2j+1)^2 = -\mu k^2/2E$: $$E = -\frac{\mu k^2}{2\hbar^2(2j+1)^2},\qquad\dim\mathcal H_E = (2j+1)^2 .$$ With $n = 2j+1$: the spectrum and the $n^2$, without solving a differential equation.

the two steps

1. $(J^\pm)^2 = \tfrac14(L^2 + M^2\pm2\vec L\cdot\vec M) = \tfrac14(L^2 + M^2)$: equal.

2. $M^2 = A^2/(-2\mu E) = -\mu k^2/2E - L^2 - \hbar^2$, so $L^2 + M^2 = -\mu k^2/2E - \hbar^2$. And $L^2 + M^2 = 4\hbar^2j(j+1)$, so $-\mu k^2/2E = \hbar^2(4j^2 + 4j + 1)$.

Each copy contributes $2j+1$ states (the multiplet theorem), and here $j$ may be half-integral: $\vec J^\pm$ are not orbital, so single-valuedness does not apply to them. $j = 0, \tfrac12, 1, \dots$ gives $n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$ — all of them.

Checks: $[\vec M] = [\vec A]/\sqrt{[\mu E]} = \mathrm{J\,s}$, an angular momentum ✓. $j = 0$: $E = -\mu k^2/2\hbar^2$, the ground state ✓.

The commutators of $\vec M$. On $\mathcal H_E$, $H$ acts as the number $E$, so $[A_i,A_j] = -2i\hbar\mu E\,\varepsilon_{ijk}L_k$ there and $[M_i, M_j] = [A_i,A_j]/(-2\mu E) = i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}L_k$. The mixed relation is linear in $\vec A$. This is unit 14's rescaling, word for word, with brackets replaced by commutators over $i\hbar$.

Why $\vec J^\pm$ commute and close. $[J^+_i, J^-_j] = \tfrac14([L_i,L_j] - [L_i,M_j] + [M_i,L_j] - [M_i,M_j]) = \tfrac{i\hbar}4\varepsilon_{ijk}(L_k - M_k + M_k - L_k) = 0$, using $[M_i,L_j] = -[L_j,M_i] = -i\hbar\varepsilon_{jik}M_k = i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}M_k$. Similarly $[J^\pm_i, J^\pm_j] = i\hbar\varepsilon_{ijk}J^\pm_k$.

Step 2 in full. On $\mathcal H_E$, $A^2 = \mu^2k^2 + 2\mu E(L^2 + \hbar^2)$, so $M^2 = A^2/(-2\mu E) = -\mu k^2/2E - L^2 - \hbar^2$. With $L^2 + M^2 = 2[(J^+)^2 + (J^-)^2] = 4\hbar^2j(j+1)$: $4\hbar^2j(j+1) + \hbar^2 = -\mu k^2/2E$, and $4j^2 + 4j + 1 = (2j+1)^2$.

What is assumed. That $\mathcal H_E$ is finite-dimensional and is a single irreducible piece $j^+ = j^- = j$, so that its dimension is $(2j+1)^2$ rather than a multiple. The algebra alone gives "a multiple of $(2j+1)^2$"; that the multiple is one is confirmed by section 3's count, which found exactly $n^2$ states. Pauli's 1926 paper is cited for the original argument, which appeared within weeks of Schrödinger's and independently of it. V. Fock (Z. Physik 98, 145, 1935; cited) made the $SO(4)$ geometric: in momentum space, stereographic projection carries the bound states onto spherical harmonics on the three-sphere $S^3\subset\mathbb R^4$, and $n^2$ is the number of degree-$(n-1)$ harmonics there.

24b · hidden symmetry

How $n^2$ splits into $l = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$

$L_z = J^+_z + J^-_z$, so a state's $m$ is $m_+ + m_-$: constant along the dotted anti-diagonals, which hold $1, 2, 3, 2, 1$ states for $m = 2, \dots, -2$.

the decomposition

$\vec L = \vec J^+ + \vec J^-$ is an angular momentum, so $\mathcal H_E$ is a sum of $L$-multiplets. Peel them off from the top: $m = 2j$ occurs once, so one $l = 2j$ multiplet; remove it, and $m = 2j - 1$ is left once, so one $l = 2j - 1$; and so on down to $l = 0$.

So level $n$ holds $l = n-1, n-2, \dots, 0$, once each — section 3's answer, recovered from symmetry.

the thesis, closed

Unit 08: $\vec A$ conserved, so every Kepler ellipse closes. Unit 14: $\vec L$ and $\vec A$ close into $\mathfrak{so}(4)$. Here: the same algebra, made of operators, makes $2s$ and $2p$ — and every $l < n$ — share one energy. Same $1/r$, same hidden symmetry, two theories.

The multiplicity count in general. In the $(2j+1)^2$-dimensional space, the number of states with $L_z = \hbar m$ is the number of pairs $(m_+, m_-)$ in $\{-j,\dots,j\}^2$ with $m_+ + m_- = m$, which is $2j + 1 - \lvert m\rvert$. A multiplet of $L$ with quantum number $l$ contributes one state to each $m$ with $\lvert m\rvert\le l$. If $l$-multiplets occur with multiplicities $c_l$, then the number of states with $L_z = \hbar m$ is $\sum_{l\ge\lvert m\rvert}c_l$, and differencing in $m$ gives $c_l = (2j+1-l) - (2j+1-(l+1)) = 1$ for $0\le l\le 2j$ (and $0$ beyond). That the space is a sum of $L$-multiplets at all follows from the multiplet theorem applied to the finite-dimensional space, on which $L^2$ and $L_z$ can be diagonalised together (they commute and are symmetric).

This is the Clebsch–Gordan rule $j\otimes j = 0\oplus1\oplus\cdots\oplus2j$ for adding two angular momenta, here proved by counting. Its general form, with the coefficients, is not needed and is cited: any text on angular momentum, e.g. Sakurai, Modern Quantum Mechanics, §3.8.

The spec's question, answered. $n^2$ is the dimension of the irreducible representation of $\mathfrak{so}(4)\cong\mathfrak{so}(3)\oplus\mathfrak{so}(3)$ with labels $(j, j)$, and each bound level carries one.

How deuterium was found

A nucleus twice as heavy changes nothing in $H$ but $\mu$. The isotope shift of a line is therefore

$$\frac{\lambda_\mathrm H - \lambda_\mathrm D}{\lambda}\approx\frac{m_e}{m_p} - \frac{m_e}{m_d}\approx\frac{m_e}{2m_p} = 2.72\times10^{-4}.$$
the deuterium shift of H-alpha

With $m_d/m_e = 3670.483$: $\lambda_\mathrm D = 656.112\times(1 + 1/3670.483) = 656.291$ nm in vacuum, $656.109$ nm in air. The shift: $$\lambda_\mathrm H - \lambda_\mathrm D = 0.1786\ \mathrm{nm},$$ deuterium to the blue. Resolving it needs $\lambda/\Delta\lambda\approx3700$ — easy for a good grating.

To scale, $800$ px per nm. In natural hydrogen the D line is about $1/6400$ as strong; the D-alpha line was seen only in an enriched sample.

history where the history is the argument

Urey, Brickwedde and Murphy (1932) computed exactly this shift, distilled liquid hydrogen to concentrate any heavy isotope in the residue, and found faint companions on the blue side of H-alpha, H-beta, H-gamma and H-delta, at the predicted places and strengthening with enrichment. The reduced mass — unit 08's two-body reduction — discovered an isotope.

Where this stops

spin

The electron's internal angular momentum $\tfrac12$, seen by Stern and Gerlach. It doubles every level to $2n^2$ and couples to the orbital motion. Not developed: the course excludes spinors.

fine structure · where unit 27 returns

With $v/c\sim\alpha$, relativistic kinetic energy and the spin–orbit coupling shift levels by relative amounts of order $\alpha^2 = 5.3\times10^{-5}$ and split each $n$ by $j$. For $n = 2$ the splitting is $R_\infty\alpha^2/16 = 0.365\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ (Dirac's formula, cited). The Dirac equation itself stays excluded.

the stability of matter, in bulk

This unit proved one atom has a ground state. That a mole of atoms has energy proportional to the number of particles — so that two stones together hold no more binding per atom than one — needs the exclusion principle: no two electrons in the same state. With it, bulk matter is stable (Dyson and Lenard 1967; a short proof by Lieb and Thirring 1975 — both cited). Without it, Dyson showed, it would not be.

what was repaired

Unit 24's single-atom collapse: $1.56\times10^{-11}$ s became a ground state at $-13.6$ eV that no state undercuts by more than a factor of four — Hardy's bound — and that the exact solution reaches.

The Lamb shift, named for completeness: the $2s_{1/2}$ and $2p_{1/2}$ levels, degenerate even in Dirac's theory, differ by about 1058 MHz (Lamb and Retherford, 1947), an effect of the quantised electromagnetic field — the first triumph of quantum electrodynamics, and two theories beyond this coda.

The stability of matter, cited precisely. F. J. Dyson and A. Lenard, J. Math. Phys. 8, 423 (1967) and 9, 698 (1968): for $N$ electrons obeying the exclusion principle and any number of nuclei, the ground-state energy is bounded below by $-CN$. E. H. Lieb and W. E. Thirring, Phys. Rev. Lett. 35, 687 (1975): a far shorter proof, with a realistic constant, from a many-particle version of the kinetic-energy inequality — a descendant of Hardy's inequality above. F. J. Dyson, J. Math. Phys. 8, 1538 (1967): for bosonic "electrons" the binding energy grows faster than $N$ (as $N^{7/5}$), so that bringing two such lumps together would release energy on the scale of an explosion. Exclusion is what turns the stability of one atom into the stability of a table.

Why the course stops here. Spin, exclusion and relativistic wave equations are each the start of a second course of quantum mechanics. General relativity, where this course is going, is a classical field theory; units 25–40 use none of this unit, and the main line resumes at unit 25 with Maxwell's equations and the Galilean boost.
