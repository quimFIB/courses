Minkowski space: relativity as the geometry of a quadratic form

Homogeneity, isotropy and the group axioms leave one free constant, a speed; fix it, and every paradox of special relativity becomes a statement about one indefinite bilinear form.

needs 03, 14~2½ h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./mr problems 26

Part C ended with a crisis: Maxwell's equations are not invariant under the Galilean change of frame that unit 03 used without naming it. This unit is the resolution, and it is presented the way a mathematician would want it: not as two postulates followed by a page of algebra, but as a classification. We ask which groups of coordinate changes between inertial frames are possible at all, given only that space and time have no preferred point, space has no preferred direction, and changes of frame compose. The answer is a one-parameter family of groups, indexed by one constant $K$. $K = 0$ is Galileo. $K > 0$ is Lorentz, with an invariant speed $c = K^{-1/2}$. $K < 0$ is excluded because it lets a clock run backwards. Nature chose $K > 0$, and experiment fixes the value.

Once the group is in hand, the rest of the unit is geometry of the quadratic form it preserves. Relativity of simultaneity is the real content; time dilation and length contraction are corollaries of it. The causal structure — which parts of "before" survive a change of frame — is the geometry of the light cone. And the twin paradox, the most argued-about result in the subject, is a triangle inequality with the inequality reversed.

26 · overview

One trip, three ways to see the same six years

the running example

One twin stays on Earth. The other flies at $0.8c$ to a star $4$ light-years away and straight back. Earth's clock reads $10$ years at the reunion; the traveller's reads $6$. Each thinks the other's clock runs slow, so why is the traveller the younger?

three answers, which must agree

1 Integrate proper time along each worldline: $10$ and $6$. 2 Count the one-a-year radio signals each twin receives: $10$ and $6$, and see when the Doppler shift flips. 3 Draw the traveller's lines of simultaneity on a correctly scaled diagram and watch them jump $6.4$ years at the turn.

the claim of the unit

Answer 1 is a theorem about the quadratic form $-c^2t^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2$, where time enters with the opposite sign to space: for it, the straight path between two events is the longest, not the shortest. The twin paradox is the reversed triangle inequality.

getting around

A dotted underline is a glossary term: click it, or press G. N opens the notes, where the longer proofs live. A solid underline, like the twins, shows the running example on hover.

Units. Throughout the running example, time is in years and distance in light-years, so $c = 1$ ly/yr and a light ray is a line at $45°$. Elsewhere $c$ is kept explicit and the time coordinate is $x^0 = ct$, a length, so all four coordinates have the same dimension.

The numbers. At $\beta = v/c = 0.8$: $\gamma = (1 - 0.64)^{-1/2} = 1/0.6 = 5/3$. Earth time for each leg $4/0.8 = 5$ yr; traveller time for each leg $5/\gamma = 3$ yr. The rapidity is $\operatorname{artanh} 0.8 = \tfrac12\ln\frac{1.8}{0.2} = \ln 3 = 1.0986$, and the Doppler factor $\sqrt{1.8/0.2} = 3$. Every one of these is derived in the deck before it is used; the list is here so you can check them as they arrive.

Why this example. It is the one result of special relativity that looks like a contradiction, which makes it the best test of whether the geometry has been understood. The usual textbook escape — "the traveller accelerates, so the situation is not symmetric" — is true but explains nothing, and section 5 shows why: the acceleration can be confined to an arbitrarily short stretch whose contribution to the traveller's age tends to zero, while the age difference stays at four years. What breaks the symmetry is that the traveller's worldline is not straight, and the geometry of the form says what that costs.

What this unit assumes of you. Linear algebra (bilinear forms, change of basis, determinants), the chain rule of unit 03, the line-integral habits of unit 04, and unit 11's second variation, which is used once in section 5. Nothing about four-vectors, momenta or energy: that is unit 27.

26 · the group

Events, frames, and the question

definitions

An event is a point of spacetime: a place at an instant. An inertial frame is a coordinate system $(t, x, y, z)$ on the set of events in which every free particle moves on a straight line at constant speed. A worldline is the set of events a particle occupies.

the principle of relativity

No experiment distinguishes one inertial frame from another. So the coordinate changes between inertial frames form a group $G$, and the change to a frame moving at velocity $\vec v$ depends on $\vec v$ alone.

The question. What can $G$ be? Unit 03 answered, without saying so, with the Galilean boost $t' = t$, $x' = x - vt$. Unit 25 showed that Maxwell's equations are not invariant under it. So assume less, and see what is forced.

three structural assumptions

homogeneity: no event is special. isotropy: no spatial direction is special. group: frame changes compose and invert, depend continuously on $\vec v$, and $\vec v = 0$ is the identity.

Einstein's second postulate — the speed of light is independent of its source — is not among them. It will be needed for exactly one thing.

Why a group. If $f_{12}$ changes coordinates from frame 1 to frame 2 and $f_{23}$ from 2 to 3, then $f_{23}\circ f_{12}$ changes from 1 to 3, and frame 3 is inertial, so the composite is again an allowed change; inverses exist because frame 1 is inertial as seen from frame 2 as much as the other way round. This is the principle of relativity doing its first job.

Why "depends on $\vec v$ alone". Two frames with the same velocity relative to $S$ differ only by a translation and a rotation of their axes. Having set those aside by choosing coinciding origins and aligned axes, the only datum left distinguishing $S'$ from $S$ is its velocity.

History. The derivation in this section goes back to Ignatowski (1910) and Frank and Rothe (1911), who noticed that the relativity principle alone almost determines the transformation; it was rediscovered many times, most cleanly by Berzi and Gorini (J. Math. Phys. 10, 1969, 1518) and by Lévy-Leblond (Am. J. Phys. 44, 1976, 271, "One more derivation of the Lorentz transformation"). Einstein's 1905 paper used both postulates from the start.

A warning on the word "inertial". The definition is circular in a familiar way: free particles are those that move straight in inertial frames. Unit 03 met the same circle and broke it operationally — a frame is inertial if no Coriolis or centrifugal terms are needed. Unit 30 returns to the question in earnest, because gravity is exactly what makes it hard.

26 · the group

Homogeneity gives linearity

homogeneity, made precise

Let $f: \mathbb R^4 \to \mathbb R^4$ send an event's $S$-coordinates to its $S'$-coordinates. Homogeneity: the $S'$-separation of two events depends only on their $S$-separation, $$f(X + A) - f(X) = g(A)\quad\text{for all } X, A.$$

lemma

If $f$ is continuous and homogeneous, then $f(X) = f(0) + \Lambda X$ with $\Lambda$ linear.

proof

$g(A + B) = f(X + A + B) - f(X + A) + f(X + A) - f(X) = g(B) + g(A)$: $g$ is additive. Additive gives $g(qA) = q\,g(A)$ for rational $q$, and continuity extends it to real $q$. So $g$ is linear, and $f(X) = f(0) + g(X)$.

Choose the origins so the two frames' spatial origins coincide at $t = t' = 0$; then $f(0) = 0$ and $f = \Lambda$ is a $4\times 4$ matrix. Physically: straight worldlines go to straight worldlines, as they must if both frames are inertial.

Full proof

Additivity: set $X$ arbitrary and compute $g(A+B) = f(X + A + B) - f(X)$, then insert $\pm f(X + A)$: the first difference is $g(B)$ evaluated at base point $X + A$, the second is $g(A)$. So $g(A + B) = g(A) + g(B)$, and $g(0) = 0$.

Rational scaling: by induction $g(nA) = n\,g(A)$ for $n \in \mathbb N$; $g(-A) = -g(A)$ from $g(A) + g(-A) = g(0) = 0$; and $n\,g(A/n) = g(A)$ gives $g(A/n) = g(A)/n$. So $g(qA) = q\,g(A)$ for every $q \in \mathbb Q$.

Real scaling: $g$ is continuous because $f$ is. For real $\lambda$ take rationals $q_k \to \lambda$; then $g(q_kA) \to g(\lambda A)$ and $q_k g(A) \to \lambda g(A)$. So $g$ is $\mathbb R$-linear.

Is continuity needed? Yes, as a matter of mathematics: there are discontinuous additive maps $\mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ (built from a Hamel basis, so with the axiom of choice), and they are not linear. It is not needed as a matter of physics, where nobody proposes a coordinate change with a graph dense in the plane, and measurability would already do. Section 2 cites a theorem that removes even the assumption of homogeneity, at the price of assuming the causal order is preserved.

Why "straight lines go to straight lines" is the same thing. An affine map sends lines to lines. Conversely, a bijection of $\mathbb R^n$ ($n \ge 2$) sending lines to lines is affine — the fundamental theorem of affine geometry, cited (e.g. Berger, Geometry I, §2.6). Either route gives linearity; the route on the slide is shorter and uses the physical assumption directly.

26 · the group

Isotropy reduces it to two dimensions

Rotate both frames' axes so $\vec v = v\,\hat x$. Isotropy then forces the transverse coordinates to be left alone:

$$y' = y,\qquad z' = z,$$

and the $(t, x)$ block to decouple from them. What remains is a $2\times 2$ matrix $L(v)$ acting on $(t, x)$.

The origin of $S'$ moves along $x = vt$, and $x' = 0$ there, so the second row is fixed up to a factor:

$$x' = \gamma(v)\,(x - vt),\qquad t' = a(v)\,t + b(v)\,x.$$
two facts from isotropy

Mirror. Reflecting $x \mapsto -x$ in both frames turns a boost by $v$ into a boost by $-v$: $L(-v) = PL(v)P$, $P = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1)$. So $a$ and $\gamma$ are even in $v$, and $b$ is odd.

Reciprocity. $L(v)^{-1} = L(-v)$: if $S'$ moves at $v$ in $S$, then $S$ moves at $-v$ in $S'$. This one needs a proof, in the notes; it forces $a = \gamma$.

Full proof that $y' = y$, $z' = z$

A rotation by $\pi$ about the $x$-axis, $(y,z)\mapsto(-y,-z)$, applied to both frames, leaves the boost along $x$ unchanged (isotropy: nothing distinguishes the rotated picture). So $\Lambda$ commutes with $\operatorname{diag}(1,1,-1,-1)$, and a matrix commuting with that is block diagonal: $(t,x)$ and $(y,z)$ do not mix. The same argument with any rotation about $x$ shows the $(y,z)$ block commutes with $SO(2)$, and the reflection $y\mapsto -y$ (also a symmetry of the boost) makes it diagonal, so it is $k(v)\,I_2$. Reciprocity (below) gives $k(v)k(-v) = 1$, the mirror gives $k(-v) = k(v)$, so $k^2 = 1$, and $k(0) = 1$ with continuity gives $k \equiv 1$.

Full proof of reciprocity

$L(v)^{-1}$ is an allowed change of frame, so it is $L(\varphi(v))$ for some velocity $\varphi(v)$ — the velocity of $S$ as seen from $S'$. Directly from the matrix, the worldline $x = 0$ has $x' = -\gamma vt$ and $t' = at$, so $\varphi(v) = -\gamma v/a$: continuous, and $\varphi(v) < 0$ for small $v > 0$ because $\gamma, a \to 1$ as $v \to 0$. Three properties:

  • $\varphi(\varphi(v)) = v$, because inverting twice is the identity;
  • $\varphi(-v) = -\varphi(v)$: invert the mirror relation, $L(-v)^{-1} = PL(v)^{-1}P = PL(\varphi(v))P = L(-\varphi(v))$;
  • $\varphi$ is injective (it has an inverse, itself), so being continuous it is monotone.

If $\varphi$ were increasing, an increasing involution is the identity (if $\varphi(v) > v$ then $v = \varphi(\varphi(v)) > \varphi(v)$, a contradiction; similarly for $<$), contradicting $\varphi(v) < 0$ for small $v > 0$. So $\varphi$ is decreasing, and $\psi = -\varphi$ is increasing with $\psi(\psi(v)) = \varphi(\varphi(v)) = v$ by oddness. So $\psi$ is the identity and $\varphi(v) = -v$. Then $-\gamma v/a = -v$ gives $a = \gamma$.

This argument is Berzi and Gorini's, tidied. Many textbooks simply assert reciprocity; it is a theorem, and it uses isotropy essentially.

26 · the group

Only one constant is left free

theorem (Ignatowski, 1910)

Under homogeneity, isotropy and the group axioms, there is a constant $K$, the same for every $v$, with

$$t' = \gamma\,(t - Kvx),\qquad x' = \gamma\,(x - vt),\qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - Kv^2}}.$$
proof

With $a = \gamma$ and $K(v) := -b/(\gamma v)$, $L(v) = \gamma\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1 & -Kv\\ -v & 1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$. Reciprocity, $L(v)^{-1} = L(-v)$, forces $\det L(v) = \gamma^2(1 - Kv^2) = 1$. The product $L(v)L(u)$ has diagonal entries $\gamma_v\gamma_u(1 + K(v)vu)$ and $\gamma_v\gamma_u(1 + K(u)uv)$; it is a boost, so they are equal, and $K(v) = K(u)$.

Check. $[Kvx] = [t]$ needs $[K] = \mathrm{s^2\,m^{-2}}$: $K^{-1/2}$ is a speed ✓. $v \to 0$: $\gamma \to 1$, identity ✓.

The structure leaves exactly one number undetermined. $K > 0$ gives the Lorentz transformation, with $c := K^{-1/2}$; the sign of $K$ decides which world we are in — drawn on the next slide.

Full proof

From the last slide, $L(v) = \begin{pmatrix}\gamma & b\\ -\gamma v & \gamma\end{pmatrix}$ with $\gamma$ even and $b$ odd. For $v \ne 0$ define $K(v) = -b/(\gamma v)$, which is even, so $L(v) = \gamma\begin{pmatrix}1 & -Kv\\ -v & 1\end{pmatrix}$. Its inverse is $\frac{1}{\det}\,\gamma\begin{pmatrix}1 & Kv\\ v & 1\end{pmatrix}$ with $\det = \gamma^2(1 - Kv^2)$, and $L(-v) = \gamma\begin{pmatrix}1 & Kv\\ v & 1\end{pmatrix}$. Reciprocity therefore says $\det = 1$, i.e. $\gamma^2 = 1/(1 - K(v)v^2)$.

Composition. $L(v)L(u) = \gamma_v\gamma_u\begin{pmatrix}1 + K_vvu & -K_uu - K_vv\\ -v - u & 1 + K_uuv\end{pmatrix}$. Every boost has equal diagonal entries, and the product of two boosts along $x$ is a boost along $x$ (it is an allowed change of frame; it keeps the transverse coordinates fixed; so it is some $L(w)$). Equal diagonal entries give $K_vvu = K_uuv$, so $K(v) = K(u)$ whenever $uv \ne 0$. $K$ is one number for the whole group.

Where the dimension check sits. $K$ has dimension $\mathrm{time}^2/\mathrm{length}^2$ because $Kvx$ is added to $t$; so $1/\sqrt K$ is a speed. Nothing in the three assumptions supplies a speed, which is why its value is a fact about the world, to be measured.

26 · the group

Three worlds, one per sign of $K$

Where a moving clock's first tick, $t' = 1$, lands in $S$ for several $v$ (dots, each on that frame's time axis $x = vt$). The boosts slide it along the curve $t^2 - Kx^2 = 1$: a line, a hyperbola inside the light lines, or a circle — and only on the circle can composing boosts carry it to $t < 0$ (dashed).

$K = 0$: Galileo, $t' = t$, $x' = x - vt$. $K > 0$: Lorentz, $c = K^{-1/2}$. $K < 0$: rotations of the $(t, x)$ plane — two boosts can turn a clock's time backwards. Excluded.

Why the curve. In $S$ the tick is at $(x, t) = (\gamma v, \gamma)$, by the inverse boost at $x' = 0$, $t' = 1$. So $t^2 - Kx^2 = \gamma^2(1 - Kv^2) = 1$ for every $v$: one curve per world.

What to look for. Each thin line is the time axis of a frame moving at $v$, and the dot on it is that frame's clock reading $1$. For Galileo the dots lie on the horizontal line $t = 1$ — absolute time — and $v$ is unbounded. For $K > 0$ ($K = 1$ drawn, so $c = 1$) they lie on the hyperbola $t^2 - x^2 = 1$, which approaches the light lines $x = \pm t$ but never reaches them: no frame moves at $c$. For $K < 0$ ($K = -1$ drawn) they lie on the circle $t^2 + x^2 = 1$; a single boost covers only the upper half, but boosts compose like rotations, and the lower half is reached.

Why $K < 0$ is excluded. Write $K = -1/k^2$. Then $L(v)$ is $\gamma\begin{pmatrix}1 & v/k^2\\ -v & 1\end{pmatrix}$; in the coordinates $(kt, x)$ it is the rotation by $\theta$ with $\tan\theta = v/k$. Rotations compose by adding angles, so four boosts at $v = k$ (each $\theta = \pi/4$) give a rotation by $\pi$: $t' = -t$ on the worldline $x = 0$. A clock at rest in $S$ would be seen, from an inertial frame, to run backwards; its successive ticks — timelike-related events on one worldline, each a cause of the next — would be reordered. Two boosts at $v = k$ already send the time axis to the space axis. The problems page works this world out.

Why the group closes for $K > 0$. The composite velocity is read off the bottom-left entry: $w = (u + v)/(1 + Kuv)$, and $\gamma_w = \gamma_u\gamma_v(1 + Kuv)$ consistently. If $|u|, |v| < c$ then $|w| < c$ (section 2 proves this cleanly with rapidity). So the set $|v| < c$ is closed, and it is the whole group: $\gamma$ is real only there.

The invariant speed, and the work each postulate does

the invariant speed

For $K = 1/c^2$ the composite velocity is $w = \dfrac{u + v}{1 + uv/c^2}$. Put $u = c$: $w = c$ for every $v$. Something moving at $c$ in one frame moves at $c$ in all of them. Proof: one line of algebra.

the turn, in the outbound frame

$c = 1$, $v = 0.8$, $\gamma = 5/3$. $T = (t, x) = (5, 4)$: $t' = \tfrac53(5 - 0.8\cdot 4) = 3$, $x' = \tfrac53(4 - 0.8\cdot 5) = 0$. At the traveller's own position, after three years ✓.

Proof of invariance. $w = (c + v)/(1 + v/c) = c(c + v)/(c + v) = c$, valid for $v \ne -c$. The limit $uv \ll c^2$ returns Galileo's $w = u + v$, which is the special case every boxed result in this unit will be checked against.

What the second postulate really says. Einstein's wording (1905): light in empty space propagates with a definite speed $c$ independent of the state of motion of the emitting body. Combined with the first postulate this makes $c$ the same in every frame; the structural derivation shows that the whole content of that combination is "choose $K > 0$, and the invariant speed is light's". De Sitter's binary-star argument (unit 25) is the evidence that light's speed does not depend on its source.

On credit, honestly. If you skipped unit 25, the statement "$K > 0$ and $c$ is the speed of light" is here an experimental input, not a derivation. The course does not pretend otherwise. Unit 25's argument is that the one-dimensional wave operator changes form under a Galilean boost and keeps it under exactly the transformations of this slide with $K = 1/c^2$, $c$ being the constant in the wave equation. The example. The turnaround event $T$ is where the traveller is, so in the traveller's outbound frame it must have $x' = 0$; the computation confirms it, and gives the traveller's clock reading at the turn, $3$ years. 26 · the form

The interval is invariant

theorem

With $x^0 = ct$, every Lorentz transformation preserves the interval

$$s^2 = -(x^0)^2 + x^2 + y^2 + z^2 .$$
proof

$x^{0\prime} = \gamma(x^0 - \beta x)$, $x' = \gamma(x - \beta x^0)$ with $\beta = v/c$. Then $x'^2 - x^{0\prime 2} = \gamma^2(1 - \beta^2)(x^2 - x^{0\,2}) = x^2 - x^{0\,2}$, and $y, z$ are untouched.

So the physics — the group — has an invariant, and it is a quadratic form. Turn the logic round and define the setting by the form:

the programme

Spacetime is a 4-dimensional real vector space with a symmetric bilinear form of a certain signature. The Lorentz group is the group of that form. Every kinematic effect is a property of the form.

Check. $s^2$ is a length squared in every term ✓. On the turn: $-25 + 16 = -9$ in $S$ and $-9 + 0 = -9$ in $S'$ ✓.

The proof, expanded. $x'^2 - (x^{0\prime})^2 = \gamma^2\big[(x - \beta x^0)^2 - (x^0 - \beta x)^2\big] = \gamma^2\big[x^2 - 2\beta xx^0 + \beta^2x^{0\,2} - x^{0\,2} + 2\beta xx^0 - \beta^2x^2\big] = \gamma^2(1 - \beta^2)(x^2 - x^{0\,2})$, and $\gamma^2(1 - \beta^2) = 1$. The cross terms cancel exactly; that is the reason the time coordinate had to be mixed with $x$ in the particular proportion $K v = v/c^2$.

Not just boosts. Rotations of the spatial axes preserve $x^2 + y^2 + z^2$ and leave $x^0$ alone, so they preserve $s^2$ too; and a general change between inertial frames with coinciding origins is a rotation, then a boost along $x$, then a rotation, so it preserves $s^2$.

History. Poincaré noticed the invariance of $x^2 + y^2 + z^2 - c^2t^2$ in 1905–06 and even wrote it with an imaginary fourth coordinate $ict$ to make it look Euclidean. Minkowski's 1908 Cologne lecture, "Raum und Zeit", made the form the primary object: "Henceforth space by itself, and time by itself, are doomed to fade away into mere shadows." The $ict$ trick survived in textbooks into the 1970s; it hides exactly the sign this unit is about, and this course does not use it.

The sign convention. This course uses $(-,+,+,+)$, as Misner–Thorne–Wheeler, Wald, Carroll and Schutz do; particle physicists usually use $(+,-,-,-)$. Nothing physical depends on the choice, but every formula with a sign in it does — see the warning on the classification slide.

26 · the form

Minkowski space, defined

Minkowski space

A 4-dimensional real vector space $V$ with a symmetric bilinear form $\eta: V\times V\to\mathbb R$ of signature $(-,+,+,+)$: some basis $(e_0, e_1, e_2, e_3)$ has $\eta(e_\mu, e_\nu) = \eta_{\mu\nu}$, $\eta_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(-1, 1, 1, 1)$. Such a basis is a Lorentz orthonormal basis: an inertial frame.

The Minkowski form $\eta$ is the object; its matrix is what it looks like in one basis. Events form an affine space over $V$; differences of events are vectors.

summation convention

For $X = X^\mu e_\mu$, $Y = Y^\nu e_\nu$: $\ \eta(X, Y) = \eta_{\mu\nu}X^\mu Y^\nu$, a repeated upper-lower index summed over $0..3$. Notation, nothing more.

theorem: components change like this

If $e'_\alpha = \Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\, e_\mu$ is another basis, then $\eta'_{\alpha\beta} = \Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta\,\eta_{\mu\nu}$, i.e. $\eta' = \Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda$.

Proof: $\eta'_{\alpha\beta} = \eta(e'_\alpha, e'_\beta) = \eta(\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha e_\mu, \Lambda^\nu{}_\beta e_\nu)$; expand by bilinearity.

Definition first, transformation law second. The transformation law is a consequence of bilinearity, not a definition. This is the habit the course keeps for every tensor to come: unit 27's four-vectors, unit 28's field tensor, unit 32's metric. Here the only object is a bilinear form on one vector space, and that is linear algebra you already have. What unit 32 adds is a bilinear form on each tangent space of a manifold, varying from point to point; that is the metric, and this $\eta$ is its constant, flat case.

Signature is well defined. That the numbers of $-1$, $+1$ and $0$ entries in a diagonal matrix of $\eta$ do not depend on the diagonalising basis is Sylvester's law of inertia (cited: any linear algebra text, e.g. Lang, Linear Algebra, ch. V, or Axler's chapter on bilinear forms). So "signature $(-,+,+,+)$" is a property of $\eta$, not of a basis. In particular $\eta$ is non-degenerate: $\eta(X, Y) = 0$ for all $Y$ forces $X = 0$.

Affine versus linear. Spacetime itself has no origin; "the event where the traveller left" and "the turnaround" are points, and their difference, $(5, 4)$ in years and light-years, is a vector of $V$. An inertial frame is a choice of origin together with a Lorentz orthonormal basis. Section 1's homogeneity is precisely the statement that nothing depends on the origin.

Index positions. Components of vectors carry upper indices; the form's carry lower ones. This bookkeeping is the vector/covector distinction of unit 04's gradient slide, and unit 28 is where it stops being optional; here it is just the convention that makes the summation rule self-checking: an index summed over must appear once up and once down.

The proof, written out. $\eta(\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha e_\mu, \Lambda^\nu{}_\beta e_\nu) = \sum_{\mu,\nu}\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta\,\eta(e_\mu, e_\nu)$, which in matrices is $(\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda)_{\alpha\beta}$.

26 · the form

Timelike, null, spacelike: the light cone

classification of a nonzero $X \in V$

timelike if $\eta(X,X) < 0$; null if $\eta(X,X) = 0$; spacelike if $\eta(X,X) > 0$. The null vectors form the light cone. A timelike or null $X$ is future-directed if $X^0 > 0$.

Because $\eta$ is invariant, the classification is the same in every inertial frame: it is a property of the pair of events, not of the observer.

the twins

Out-leg $X = (5, 4)$: $-25 + 16 = -9$, timelike. A light ray $(4, 4)$: null. The separation from Earth-at-$t{=}5$ to $T$, $(0, 4)$: $+16$, spacelike.

The plane $(x, x^0)$, years and light-years. The cone $x^0 = \pm x$ splits it; each of the twins' three vectors, components $(x^0, x)$, ends where its $\eta(X, X)$ puts it.

Physical reading. Timelike separation: some inertial observer passes through both events (move at $\vec v = c\,\vec x/x^0$, which is slower than $c$). Null: a light signal can pass through both. Spacelike: no signal slower than or equal to light connects them; section 4 shows their time order is then not an invariant.

Reading the figure. Only the $x$ direction is drawn; with $y$ and $z$ restored, the two dashed lines are the cross-section of a double cone, and "spacelike" is the connected region outside it. The Lorentz group and its four pieces

the Lorentz group $O(1,3)$

$\{\Lambda \in GL(V) : \eta(\Lambda X, \Lambda Y) = \eta(X, Y)\ \forall X, Y\}$. In an orthonormal basis: $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$.

Two continuous invariants: $\det\Lambda = \pm1$ (take determinants: $\det(\Lambda)^2 = 1$), and whether $\Lambda$ maps the future cone to itself, which happens iff $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$; otherwise $\Lambda^0{}_0 \le -1$.

$\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$$\Lambda^0{}_0 \le -1$
$\det = +1$$SO^+(1,3)$: boosts, rotations$PT$
$\det = -1$$P = \operatorname{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$$T = \operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$
theorem

$O(1,3)$ has exactly four connected components, the cells of the table. The identity's component, the proper orthochronous group $SO^+(1,3)$, is a normal subgroup; the others are its cosets $P\,SO^+$, $T\,SO^+$, $PT\,SO^+$.

$|\Lambda^0{}_0| \ge 1$. The $00$ entry of $\Lambda^{\mathsf T}\eta\Lambda = \eta$ reads $-(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 + \sum_i(\Lambda^i{}_0)^2 = -1$, so $(\Lambda^0{}_0)^2 = 1 + \sum_i(\Lambda^i{}_0)^2 \ge 1$.

Orthochronous means $\Lambda^0{}_0 \ge 1$. $\Lambda e_0$ is timelike (the form is preserved), and its time component is $\Lambda^0{}_0$. The future timelike cone is convex, hence connected, and $\Lambda$ maps it continuously into the timelike vectors, which are the disjoint union of the future and past cones; so its image lies in one of them, the one containing $\Lambda e_0$. Write $C$ for the future cone. Maps sending $C$ into itself are closed under composition; they are closed under inverses too, because if $\Lambda^{-1}$ sent $C$ into $-C$ then $\Lambda$ would send $-C$ into $C$ and hence, being linear, $C$ into $-C$, which it does not. So the orthochronous transformations form a subgroup — the problems page asks for the argument in full.

Full proof of the four components

At least four. $\det$ and $\operatorname{sign}\Lambda^0{}_0$ are continuous functions on $O(1,3)$ with values in $\{\pm1\}$, so they are constant on connected components; the four cells are therefore unions of components, and each is nonempty ($I, P, T, PT$).

At most four: $SO^+(1,3)$ is path-connected. Let $\Lambda \in SO^+$ and $u = \Lambda e_0$, a future unit timelike vector, $u = (\gamma, \gamma\vec\beta)$ with $|\vec\beta| < 1$. Let $B$ be the boost with velocity $\vec\beta$ (rotate $\vec\beta$ onto the $x$-axis, boost, rotate back): $Be_0 = u$. Then $R = B^{-1}\Lambda$ fixes $e_0$, so it preserves $e_0^\perp = \operatorname{span}(e_1,e_2,e_3)$ with its positive-definite restriction of $\eta$; it is an orthogonal $3\times3$ block with $\det = 1$, a rotation. So $\Lambda = BR$. Rotations are connected to $I$: a rotation $R$ of $\mathbb R^3$ has an axis, because $\det(R - I) = \det(R^{\mathsf T})\det(R - I) = \det(I - R^{\mathsf T}) = \det(I - R) = -\det(R - I)$ in odd dimension, so $\det(R - I) = 0$; on the plane orthogonal to the axis $R$ is a planar rotation by some $\theta$, and shrinking $\theta$ to $0$ is a path to $I$. Boosts are connected to $I$ by shrinking $\vec\beta$. So a path runs from $I$ to $\Lambda$. The other cells are $P\,SO^+$ etc., images of a connected set under a homeomorphism.

Normal. The identity component of any topological group is a normal subgroup (conjugation is continuous and fixes $I$). Physically only $SO^+(1,3)$ is a symmetry of everything: parity is violated by the weak interaction (Wu, 1957), and time reversal too — Christenson, Cronin, Fitch and Turlay found $CP$ violation in neutral-kaon decay in 1964, which is $T$ violation given $CPT$, and CPLEAR saw $T$ violation directly in 1998.

Boosts are hyperbolic rotations

rapidity

For $|\beta| < 1$ put $\varphi = \operatorname{artanh}\beta$. Then $\gamma = \cosh\varphi$, $\gamma\beta = \sinh\varphi$, and

$$B(\varphi) = \begin{pmatrix}\cosh\varphi & -\sinh\varphi\\ -\sinh\varphi & \cosh\varphi\end{pmatrix}.$$
theorem

$B(\varphi)B(\psi) = B(\varphi + \psi)$. The boosts along one axis form a one-parameter group isomorphic to $(\mathbb R, +)$.

Proof: multiply, and use $\cosh(\varphi+\psi) = \cosh\varphi\cosh\psi + \sinh\varphi\sinh\psi$ and its partner for $\sinh$.

A boost slides points along the hyperbolae $x^2 - (x^0)^2 = $ const, as a rotation slides them along circles (arrows: $B(\varphi)$, $\varphi > 0$). Dots: $e_0$ moved by $\varphi = 0, \pm0.5, \pm1$. The light lines are only rescaled.

Why $\gamma = \cosh\varphi$. $\cosh^2 - \sinh^2 = 1$ and $\tanh = \sinh/\cosh$ give $\cosh\varphi = 1/\sqrt{1 - \tanh^2\varphi} = 1/\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} = \gamma$, and $\sinh\varphi = \tanh\varphi\cosh\varphi = \beta\gamma$. So the boost of section 1, $x^{0\prime} = \gamma(x^0 - \beta x)$, $x' = \gamma(x - \beta x^0)$, is the matrix on the slide.

The product. $B(\varphi)B(\psi)$ has diagonal entries $\cosh\varphi\cosh\psi + \sinh\varphi\sinh\psi = \cosh(\varphi+\psi)$ and off-diagonal entries $-(\cosh\varphi\sinh\psi + \sinh\varphi\cosh\psi) = -\sinh(\varphi+\psi)$. Both identities follow from $\cosh a = (e^a + e^{-a})/2$, $\sinh a = (e^a - e^{-a})/2$ by expanding. The velocity formula is then $\tanh(\varphi+\psi) = (\tanh\varphi + \tanh\psi)/(1 + \tanh\varphi\tanh\psi)$, which is the $\sinh$ formula over the $\cosh$ formula divided through by $\cosh\varphi\cosh\psi$.

Reading the figure. The hyperbolae $x^2 - (x^0)^2 = \text{const}$ are the boosts' orbits, as circles are the rotations': a boost slides each point along its own hyperbola, the timelike ones (top and bottom) and the spacelike ones (left and right) alike. The light lines are the eigenvectors: $B(\varphi)(1, 1) = (\cosh\varphi - \sinh\varphi)(1, 1) = e^{-\varphi}(1, 1)$ and $B(\varphi)(1, -1) = e^{\varphi}(1, -1)$, components $(x^0, x)$. So a boost is a squeeze: it shrinks one light direction by $e^{-\varphi}$ and stretches the other by $e^{\varphi}$, keeping their product, which is $-\eta$ in null coordinates. Section 3's Doppler factor is this $e^{\varphi}$.

Only collinear boosts add. Two boosts in different directions do not compose to a boost. Proof: a pure boost is a symmetric matrix — its $0i$ and $i0$ entries are both $-\gamma\beta_i$, as the $2\times2$ case above shows — whereas the product $B_xB_y$ of a boost along $y$ and then one along $x$ has $(B_xB_y)^0{}_1 = -\gamma_x\beta_x$ and $(B_xB_y)^1{}_0 = -\gamma_x\gamma_y\beta_x$, which differ because $\gamma_y > 1$. By the decomposition $\Lambda = BR$ of "The Lorentz group and its four pieces" the product is therefore a boost followed by a non-trivial rotation — the Wigner rotation, whose accumulation along a curved path is Thomas precession. So rapidity is additive along one line, not in general; the set of all boosts is not a subgroup. Unit 27 meets the consequence.

A generator. Differentiating, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varphi}B(\varphi) = K_xB(\varphi)$ with $K_x = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ and $B(0) = I$: the boosts are the flow of a linear vector field, a one-parameter group in unit 02's sense, and the group property is uniqueness of solutions (unit 01). $K_x$ is the generator of boosts (the conventional letter; it has nothing to do with section 1's constant $K$); it is symmetric where a rotation generator is antisymmetric, which is the sign of $\eta$ showing up in the Lie algebra: $K^{\mathsf T}\eta + \eta K = 0$.

Causality alone forces the group

the Alexandrov–Zeeman theorem (cited)

Write $p \prec q$ if $q - p$ is a nonzero future-directed timelike or null vector. Let $f:\mathbb R^4\to\mathbb R^4$ be any bijection with $p \prec q \iff f(p) \prec f(q)$. Then $f$ is a composition of an orthochronous Lorentz transformation, a translation and a dilation $X \mapsto \lambda X$, $\lambda > 0$.

Zeeman, "Causality implies the Lorentz group", J. Math. Phys. 5 (1964) 490–493; Alexandrov proved the version for light-cone-preserving maps in the 1950s.

What it removes. No linearity, no continuity, no homogeneity: section 1's lemma is not needed. Preserving "could have caused" is already enough to force the group, up to the scale of the units.

needs three or more dimensions

In one space and one time dimension it is false: with $u = x^0 + x$, $w = x^0 - x$, any $(u, w)\mapsto(F(u), H(w))$ with $F, H$ increasing bijections preserves $\prec$. A problem on the sheet.

Why cited. Zeeman's proof is three pages of elementary but intricate geometry: he shows that $f$ maps light rays to light rays (a light ray is characterised order-theoretically: a chain along which no two points are chronologically related), then that it maps null planes to null planes, then that it is affine, and finally that an affine map preserving the cone is a Lorentz transformation times a dilation. The argument is worth reading once and is self-contained; it does not fit in speaker notes.

Why the dilation survives. $X\mapsto\lambda X$ scales every interval by $\lambda^2$ and keeps every sign, so it preserves $\prec$. Causal structure fixes the form only up to a positive multiple: it knows which way the cones point, not how long a second is. Clocks fix the scale.

Why 1+1 dimensions fail. In the null coordinates $u = x^0 + x$, $w = x^0 - x$, $q - p$ is future causal iff $\Delta u \ge 0$ and $\Delta w \ge 0$ (and not both zero): the cone is a quadrant. Any map applying increasing bijections to $u$ and $w$ separately preserves that, and $F(u) = u^3$ is not affine. In higher dimensions there are infinitely many null directions, and they are tied together by the rotations; that rigidity is what Zeeman's proof exploits.

The moral for this course. Special relativity can be founded on causal order alone. General relativity keeps the causal structure locally and drops everything global; unit 40's discussion of horizons is causal structure with the flatness removed.

The relativity of simultaneity

the relativity of simultaneity

Two events with $\Delta t = 0$ and $\Delta x \ne 0$ in $S$ have, in $S'$,

$$\Delta t' = -\gamma\,\frac{v\,\Delta x}{c^2} \ne 0 .$$

In $S'$, the event further along $+x$ happens first.

The lines of constant $t'$ are $t - vx/c^2 = \text{const}$: in an $(x, ct)$ diagram they tilt up with slope $\beta$. They are exactly the vectors $\eta$-orthogonal to the observer's worldline.

$\beta = 0.5$, $c = 1$. $A$ and $B$ share $S$'s horizontal "now". $B$ is on the $x'$ axis, $t' = 0$; the line of constant $t'$ through $A$ (dashed) meets the $ct'$ axis at $t' = \gamma = 1.15$. So $\Delta t'_{B-A} = -\gamma v\Delta x/c^2 = -1.15$.

Proof. $t' = \gamma(t - vx/c^2)$, so $\Delta t' = \gamma(\Delta t - v\Delta x/c^2)$, and $\Delta t = 0$ leaves the formula. Checks: dimensions $[v\Delta x/c^2] = \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}\cdot m}/\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}} = \mathrm{s}$ ✓; $c\to\infty$ gives $\Delta t' = 0$, Galilean absolute simultaneity ✓; $\Delta x = 0$ gives $\Delta t' = 0$ — coincident events are simultaneous for everyone, which is why a collision is a frame-independent fact.

Reading the figure. $S'$ moves at $\beta = 0.5$, so its $ct'$ axis is the line $x = 0.5\,ct$ and its $x'$ axis, the set $t' = 0$, is $ct = 0.5\,x$: both tilt towards the light line by the same angle. Einstein's train (1917, Relativity): lightning strikes the two ends of a train. The embankment observer, midway, sees the flashes together; the train's midpoint passenger, moving towards the front flash, sees it first and, knowing that light travels at $c$ in her frame and that she is midway, concludes that the front strike happened first. Einstein synchronisation: simultaneity by radar

Einstein synchronisation

A clock at rest sends light at $t_1$ to a distant event $E$, and gets the reflection back at $t_2$. It defines $E$ to have happened at $t_E = \tfrac12(t_1 + t_2)$, at distance $\tfrac12 c(t_2 - t_1)$.

Apply that definition in each frame and you get each frame's own simultaneity, and they differ. No experiment picks one: the choice is a convention, and the group says all choices are equally good.

For a moving observer, the radar "now" is the tilted line of the previous slide: light out and back at $45°$ makes the triangle symmetric about it.

An observer at $\beta = 0.5$ times the echo from $E$: out at $\tau_1$, back at $\tau_2$, so $E$ is assigned the clock reading $\tfrac12(\tau_1 + \tau_2)$. That event and $E$ lie on a line of slope $\beta$ — the observer's now — not on $S$'s horizontal one.

Reading the figure. The observer's proper times are $\tau_1 = 0.8$, $\tau_2 = 3.2$, so $E$ is assigned $\tau = 2.0$ and distance $1.2$ (with $c = 1$). $E$ was built as $2u + 1.2\,n$, with $u = \gamma(1, \beta)$ the observer's unit time direction and $n = \gamma(\beta, 1)$ the unit spacelike vector $\eta$-orthogonal to it, components $(ct, x)$: radar recovers exactly those two numbers. $S$'s own clocks call $E$ simultaneous with $ct = 3.0$ at $x = 0$, a different "now".

Why radar simultaneity is $\eta$-orthogonality. Let the observer have unit future timelike direction $u$ (so $\eta(u,u) = -1$) through the origin, and let $E$ be an event. The outgoing and returning light rays meet the worldline at $\tau_1 u$ and $\tau_2 u$ (times in units with $c = 1$), with $E - \tau_1u$ and $E - \tau_2u$ null. Expand, using $\eta(u,u) = -1$: $0 = \eta(E - \tau u, E - \tau u) = \eta(E,E) - 2\tau\,\eta(E,u) - \tau^2$, a quadratic in $\tau$ whose two roots are $\tau_1, \tau_2$. Their sum is $-2\eta(E,u)$. So $\tfrac12(\tau_1 + \tau_2) = -\eta(E, u)$, and $E$ is radar-simultaneous with the origin iff $\eta(E, u) = 0$. The problems page asks for this proof in full, and for the radar distance $\sqrt{\eta(E,E)}$ of such an $E$.

Is it only a convention? Reichenbach and Grünbaum argued that $\tfrac12$ could be any $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ without observable consequence, since only round-trip light speeds are measurable. Malament (1977) proved that Einstein's $\varepsilon = \tfrac12$ is the only simultaneity relation definable from the causal structure and the observer's worldline alone. Either way, what is not a convention is that two frames' Einstein simultaneities differ.

Time dilation and length contraction, as corollaries

time dilation

A clock at rest in $S'$ ticks at $\Delta x' = 0$. By the inverse boost, $\Delta t = \gamma(\Delta t' + v\Delta x'/c^2) = \gamma\,\Delta t'$: the moving clock's ticks are $\gamma$ times further apart in $S$.

evidence

Muons in the CERN storage ring at $\gamma = 29.3$: rest lifetime $2.197\ \mu$s, so $\gamma\tau = 64.4\ \mu$s predicted and seen to about $0.1\%$ (Bailey et al., Nature 268, 1977).

length contraction

A rod at rest in $S'$ has ends at $x' = 0$ and $x' = L_0$. Its length in $S$ is the separation of its ends at one $S$-instant. $x' = \gamma(x - vt)$ at fixed $t$ gives $\Delta x = L_0/\gamma$.

Both follow in one line from the boost. What they mean is clearer drawn, once diagrams are drawn honestly: "Length contraction is a slice of a strip", two slides on, cuts a rod's history two ways.

Checks. Dimensions: $\gamma$ is a pure number ✓. $v\to0$: $\gamma\to1$, no effect ✓. $v\to c$: $\gamma\to\infty$, the moving clock stops and the rod flattens — neither is reached, since $|v| < c$ for every frame.

The symmetry that looks like a contradiction. $S$ says $S'$'s clocks run slow; $S'$ says $S$'s clocks run slow. There is no contradiction because each statement compares one clock of one frame with a succession of clocks of the other, synchronised by the other's convention. The two comparisons are different experiments. The twins break the symmetry by bringing the same two clocks together twice: then a frame-independent comparison exists, and section 5 computes it.

The numbers. The muon lifetime is $2.1969811\ \mu$s. $29.3\times2.19698 = 64.37\ \mu$s. The ring had radius about $7$ m, so the muons' acceleration in the lab was $\gamma^2v^2/r \approx 1.1\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, about $10^{18}\,g$. That the lifetime still came out as $\gamma\tau$ is the evidence for the clock hypothesis of section 5: acceleration itself does not affect an ideal clock.

Contraction does not mean looks contracted. What an eye or camera records is light that left the object's parts at different times, arriving together. Penrose and Terrell (both 1959) showed a moving sphere still photographs as a circular outline: contraction plus light-travel delays combine into an apparent rotation. Length contraction is a statement about simultaneous positions, not about images.

Time dilation, read off. The $S$-simultaneity line through the $1'$ tick on the $ct'$ axis is horizontal at $ct = \gamma = 1.25$: the moving clock reads $1$ when $S$'s clocks read $1.25$. The $S'$-simultaneity line through $S$'s tick $(0, 1)$ — an event with $ct' = \gamma(1 - 0) = 1.25$ — hits the $ct'$ axis above $1'$, at $ct' = 1.25$: $S$'s clock reads $1$ when $S'$ says $1.25$ has passed, the same effect seen from $S'$. Both at once, with no contradiction, because the two lines are different lines.

If the back door stays shut. Then the pole's front hits it, and the pole stops or breaks. The collision is an event, frame-independent. Doppler: the rapidity, exponentiated

collinear Doppler factor

A source recedes at $\beta$ and emits pulses a proper time $T_0$ apart. The receiver's pulses are $T$ apart, with

$$\frac{T}{T_0} = \sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}} = e^{\varphi}.$$

Approaching: $e^{-\varphi}$. Frequencies go the other way.

Proof. Receiver at the origin; source on $x = vt$, emitting at $t = 0$ and $t = \gamma T_0$. The second pulse starts $v\gamma T_0$ further away, so arrives $\gamma T_0(1 + \beta)$ after the first. $\gamma(1+\beta) = \sqrt{(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)}$.

Check. $\beta\to0$: $e^\varphi \approx 1 + \beta$, the classical Doppler shift ✓. $\beta\to1$: infinite stretch, redshift to nothing ✓.

The proof, drawn for the traveller: $\beta = 0.8$, $T_0 = 1$ yr, $c = 1$. Ticks at $t = \gamma T_0 = 5/3$ and $10/3$; each extra $\beta\gamma T_0$ of distance adds that much light-time. Arrivals at $3$ and $6$: $T = \gamma(1 + \beta)T_0 = 3T_0$.

The proof, in full. Work in the receiver's frame, receiver at $x = 0$, source on $x = vt$, $v > 0$ (receding). The source's clock runs slow by $\gamma$, so pulses separated by proper time $T_0$ are emitted at $t = 0$ (at $x = 0$) and $t = \gamma T_0$ (at $x = v\gamma T_0$). The second pulse needs an extra $v\gamma T_0/c$ to reach the receiver, so the arrival times are $0$ and $\gamma T_0 + \gamma\beta T_0$. Hence $T = \gamma(1 + \beta)T_0 = \frac{1 + \beta}{\sqrt{(1-\beta)(1+\beta)}}T_0 = \sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}}\,T_0$. And $e^{2\varphi} = (1+\beta)/(1-\beta)$ from the rapidity slide.

Oblique direction. If the source moves at $\beta$ and the line from source to receiver makes angle $\theta$ with its velocity (in the receiver's frame), the same argument gives $T = \gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta)T_0$: between emissions the source closes the distance by $v\gamma T_0\cos\theta$ to first order in the ratio of that step to the distance, which is negligible for a distant source. At $\theta = \pi/2$: $T = \gamma T_0$, the transverse Doppler effect, which is pure time dilation and has no classical counterpart. Ives and Stilwell (1938) saw it in moving hydrogen ions (unit 25).

Aberration and the headlight effect

aberration

A source moving at $\beta$ along $x$ emits light at angle $\theta'$ to $x$ in its own frame. In the lab the angle is $\theta$ with

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\cos\theta' + \beta}{1 + \beta\cos\theta'}.$$

Proof. The ray's direction is a null vector, $(1, \cos\theta', \sin\theta', 0)$ in the source frame. Boost by $-\beta$: $(\gamma(1 + \beta\cos\theta'),\ \gamma(\cos\theta' + \beta),\ \sin\theta',\ 0)$. It is still null, and $\cos\theta$ is its $x$ part over its time part.

Stellar aberration. The Earth's orbital $\beta \approx 29.8/299\,792 = 9.9\times10^{-5}$ shifts starlight by up to $\beta$ radians: $20.5''$, which Bradley measured in 1728.

Check. $\beta = 0$: $\theta = \theta'$ ✓. $\theta' = 0$: $\theta = 0$ ✓.

What the formula does to a whole sky of rays is the headlight effect, drawn on the next slide.

Why the boost is by $-\beta$. The lab sees the source moving at $+\beta$, so the lab's coordinates are obtained from the source's by the inverse boost, $x^0 = \gamma(x^{0\prime} + \beta x')$, $x = \gamma(x' + \beta x^{0\prime})$. Applied to $(1, \cos\theta', \sin\theta', 0)$ this gives the vector on the slide. Null-ness is preserved because $\eta$ is: check, $\gamma^2(\cos\theta' + \beta)^2 + \sin^2\theta' - \gamma^2(1 + \beta\cos\theta')^2 = \gamma^2(1 - \beta^2)(\cos^2\theta' - 1) + \sin^2\theta' = 0$ ✓.

The Doppler factor is in the same vector. Scaling the null direction by the frequency $\omega'$, its time component in the lab is $\gamma(1 + \beta\cos\theta')\omega'$: the observed frequency. It agrees with the oblique Doppler formula in the notes of "Doppler: the rapidity, exponentiated", $\omega = \omega'/(\gamma(1 - \beta\cos\theta))$, once the aberration formula is used to convert $\theta'$ to $\theta$ — a two-line identity the problems page asks for.

Stellar aberration. For a star at angle $\theta'$ in the Sun's frame, $\theta - \theta' \approx -\beta\sin\theta'$ to first order (a sign that depends on which way the observer, rather than the source, moves); the amplitude is $\beta$ radians. With the mean orbital speed $29.78\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$: $\beta = 9.934\times10^{-5}$ rad $= 20.49''$. Bradley found it while looking for stellar parallax, announced in 1729. First order in $\beta$ is also what the ether theories could reproduce; unit 25 describes why they could not reproduce it together with Fizeau's experiment.

26 · causality

What survives of "before"

theorem

(a) If $q - p$ is timelike or null, every orthochronous Lorentz transformation preserves the sign of $\Delta t$.

(b) If $q - p$ is spacelike, there are inertial frames in which $\Delta t' > 0$, $= 0$ and $< 0$.

proof

(a) Orthochronous maps send the future cone to itself (the cone's closure too, by continuity). (b) Align $x$ with $\Delta\vec x$, so $|\Delta x| > c|\Delta t|$. Then $\Delta t' = \gamma(\Delta t - v\Delta x/c^2)$, and $v$ ranges over $(-c, c)$, so $v\Delta x/c^2$ takes every value in $(-|\Delta x|/c,\ |\Delta x|/c) \ni \Delta t$.

the causal future and the elsewhere

$J^+(p) = \{q : q - p \text{ future causal or } 0\}$, and $J^-(p)$ likewise. The elsewhere of $p$ is everything outside $J^+(p)\cup J^-(p)$: events whose order relative to $p$ is a matter of frame.

$q$ is spacelike to $p$. Through $p$, three frames' lines of simultaneity put $q$ after $p$, level with it, or before it. No such line can pass above an event in $J^+(p)$.

Proof of (a), with null separation. For timelike $q - p$ this is the definition of orthochronous (the future timelike cone maps to itself). A future null vector is a limit of future timelike vectors; its image is a limit of future timelike vectors, so it has $\Delta t' \ge 0$, and $\Delta t' = 0$ would make it (being null) the zero vector, which it is not.

Proof of (b), in full. Rotate so that $\Delta\vec x = (\Delta x, 0, 0)$ with $\Delta x > 0$; spacelike means $\Delta x > c|\Delta t|$. The boost with $v = c^2\Delta t/\Delta x$ has $|v| < c$ and gives $\Delta t' = 0$; any larger $v < c$ gives $\Delta t' < 0$, and any smaller $v > -c$ gives $\Delta t' > 0$. The frame in which the two events are simultaneous is unique up to transverse motion, and in it their distance is the proper distance $\sqrt{\eta(q - p, q - p)}$.

Reading the figure. With $c = 1$, $p$ at the origin and $q = (x, ct) = (2, 0.6)$, spacelike since $2 > 0.6$. The frame moving at $v$ has simultaneity lines $ct = (v/c)\,x$ through $p$: for $v = -0.5c$ the line is below $q$ ($\Delta t' > 0$), for $v = c^2\Delta t/\Delta x = 0.3c$ it passes through $q$, for $v = 0.7c$ it is above ($\Delta t' < 0$). For an event inside the shaded cones every such line, having slope between $-1$ and $1$, leaves it on the same side: part (a).

Chronological versus causal. Some books write $I^+(p)$ for the open cone (timelike only) and $J^+(p)$ for the closed one; in Minkowski space $J^+(p)$ is the closure of $I^+(p)$. The distinction matters in unit 40, where in curved spacetimes the two can behave differently.

No superluminal signalling

theorem

Suppose signals could be sent at some speed $u > c$, in every inertial frame (relativity). Then some sequence of two such signals arrives at its sender's worldline before it was sent.

proof

Emission $p$ at the origin, reception $q = (\Delta t, u\Delta t)$: spacelike. In the frame with $c^2/u < v < c$, $\Delta t' = \gamma\Delta t(1 - uv/c^2) < 0$: the signal runs backwards in time. The receiver, at rest in that frame, replies with its own speed-$u$ signal backwards to the sender, arriving in the sender's past (details in the notes).

$c = 1$, $u = 2$, Bob at $v = 0.9$. Bob's "now" through $q$ passes below $p$, so for him the question arrives before it is sent. His reply (red), at $u$ in his frame, reaches Alice at $t = -0.45$.

Full proof: the tachyonic antitelephone

Units $c = 1$. Alice at rest at $x = 0$ sends a signal at speed $u > 1$ at $t = 0$, received by Bob at $q = (t_1, ut_1)$, $t_1 > 0$. Let Bob move at $v$ with $1/u < v < 1$. In Bob's frame $t'_q = \gamma t_1(1 - uv) < 0 = t'_p$: the signal arrived before it left. Bob replies at once, with a speed-$u$ signal aimed back along $-x$ in his frame. By the principle of relativity this is as allowed for Bob as Alice's was for her. In Bob's frame it travels forward in his time, from $q$ to Alice's worldline. Where does it land? Its Alice-frame velocity is, by velocity addition, $w = (-u + v)/(1 - uv)$; since $1 - uv < 0$ and $v - u < 0$, $w > 0$ — in Alice's frame the reply moves in the $+x$ direction while going backward in $t$, i.e. it travels from $q$ towards $x = 0$ with $\mathrm dt/\mathrm dx = 1/w$ and decreasing $x$ means decreasing $t$. It covers the distance $ut_1$ in Alice-time $-ut_1/w$, arriving at $t_1 - ut_1/w = t_1(1 - u/w)$. Now $w < u$ exactly when $v > 2u/(1 + u^2)$, a number between $1/u$ and $1$ (strictly less than $1$ because $2u < 1 + u^2$); for such $v$, $u/w > 1$ and the reply arrives at negative $t$: before Alice sent the question. That is the loop.

The figure's numbers. $u = 2$, $t_1 = 1$, so $q = (t, x) = (1, 2)$. Bob moves at $v = 0.9$, above the loop threshold $2u/(1 + u^2) = 0.8$. His line of simultaneity through $q$ is $t - 1 = 0.9(x - 2)$, which meets Alice's worldline at $t = -0.8$, below $p$: in his frame $p$ comes after $q$. The reply's Alice-frame velocity is $w = (-2 + 0.9)/(1 - 1.8) = 1.375$, and it arrives at $t = 1 - 2/1.375 = -0.455$.

26 · causality

What the antitelephone proves, and what it does not

what exactly is proved

One backwards-in-time signal is odd but not contradictory. The contradiction is the loop: a reply that reaches the sender before the question left, who then does not send it. Relativity plus "no causal loops" forbids signals faster than $c$. Neither alone does.

why it fails for timelike separation

If $u < c$, then $uv/c^2 < 1$ for every $|v| < c$, so $\Delta t'$ has the sign of $\Delta t$ in every frame — part (a) of the theorem on "What survives of 'before'".

Put together: if relativity holds, and nothing can arrive at its own past to stop itself being sent, then nothing travels faster than $c$. The light cone is the boundary of every possible influence.

A number. $u = 2c$: the first signal runs backwards for observers with $v > c/2$, and the loop closes for Bob's speeds $v > 2u/(1 + u^2) = 4/5$ of $c$.

History and status. The name is Benford, Book and Newcomb's (Phys. Rev. D 2, 1970); the argument goes back to Einstein (1907) and Tolman (1917). Quantum mechanics sharpens the theorem into the no-signalling theorem for entanglement: correlations between spacelike-separated measurements are allowed, controllable signals are not. General relativity reopens the question — some exact solutions contain closed timelike curves — and the question of whether physics forbids them is open (Hawking's chronology protection conjecture).

Proper time: the length of a worldline

proper time

For a future-directed timelike curve $\lambda\mapsto X(\lambda)$, piecewise $C^1$, $\lambda\in[a, b]$:

$$\tau[X] = \frac1c\int_a^b\sqrt{-\eta_{\mu\nu}\,\dot X^\mu\dot X^\nu}\ \mathrm d\lambda .$$

Parametrised by the frame's $t$, with $\vec x(t)$: $\ \tau = \displaystyle\int\sqrt{1 - |\dot{\vec x}|^2/c^2}\ \mathrm dt$.

It is invariant twice over: under reparametrisation (as unit 03's arc length is), and under Lorentz transformations (the integrand is built from $\eta$).

the clock hypothesis (physics, not mathematics)

An ideal clock carried along a worldline reads its proper time, whatever its acceleration. The storage-ring muons, at about $10^{18}\,g$, obeyed it.

Check. $[\eta\,\dot X\dot X] = \mathrm m^2$ per unit of $\lambda$ squared, so the integral is a length; over $c$, a time ✓. At rest, $\tau = \Delta t$ ✓. Constant speed, $\tau = \Delta t/\gamma$: time dilation ✓.

Why timelike. $-\eta(\dot X, \dot X) > 0$ exactly when the tangent is timelike, so the square root is real. Future-directed timelike curves are the possible worldlines of massive particles: at every point the velocity is below $c$.

Reparametrisation invariance. If $\lambda = \phi(\sigma)$ with $\phi' > 0$, then $\mathrm dX/\mathrm d\sigma = \phi'(\sigma)\,\mathrm dX/\mathrm d\lambda$, the square root scales by $\phi'$, and the substitution rule absorbs it: $\int\sqrt{-\eta(X_\sigma, X_\sigma)}\,\mathrm d\sigma = \int\sqrt{-\eta(X_\lambda, X_\lambda)}\,\phi'\,\mathrm d\sigma = \int\sqrt{-\eta(X_\lambda, X_\lambda)}\,\mathrm d\lambda$. The same computation as unit 03's arc length, with $-\eta$ in place of the dot product.

The $t$-parametrisation. Along a future timelike curve $\mathrm dt/\mathrm d\lambda > 0$, so $t$ is a legitimate parameter. With $X = (ct, \vec x(t))$: $-\eta(\dot X, \dot X) = c^2 - |\dot{\vec x}|^2$, and dividing by $c$ gives the slide's formula.

The clock hypothesis is an assumption. Nothing in the Lorentz group says how a clock behaves when accelerated; that is a statement about clocks. It holds for clocks whose internal time scales are short compared with the time over which their velocity changes appreciably — atoms, muons — and fails for clocks that the acceleration damages (a pendulum clock on a centrifuge). Its evidence is the storage ring: proper acceleration $\gamma^2v^2/r$ at $\gamma = 29.3$, $r \approx 7$ m is $1.1\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}} \approx 1.1\times10^{18}\,g$, and the lifetime was still $\gamma\tau$.

Unit 27 will take $-mc^2\tau$ as the action of a free particle, and the Euler–Lagrange equations of unit 11 applied to it give relativistic momentum. The next two slides say why that action has a maximum, not a minimum.

It is not the acceleration

"it is because the traveller accelerates"

Replace the instant turn by one lasting Earth-time $\delta$. Its contribution to $\tau_T$ is at most $\delta$, since the integrand is at most $1$. As $\delta\to0$ it vanishes, and the $4$-year difference does not. Acceleration marks which twin's worldline is bent; it is not where the time goes.

The age difference is accumulated during the coasting legs, uniformly, because the bent path is shorter in the sense of $\eta$. The next slide proves that bent is always shorter.

The instant turn replaced by one lasting $\delta = 1$ or $2$ Earth years, speed going linearly from $+0.8c$ to $-0.8c$. The traveller's proper time grows only by $0.28\,\delta$; as $\delta\to0$ it returns to $6$, and the $4$-year gap stays.

The warning, quantitatively. A smooth turnaround in which the speed goes from $+0.8$ to $-0.8$ linearly in Earth-time over $[5 - \delta/2,\ 5 + \delta/2]$ contributes $\int\sqrt{1 - \beta(t)^2}\,\mathrm dt = \frac{\delta}{1.6}\int_{-0.8}^{0.8}\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}\,\mathrm d\beta = \frac{\delta}{1.6}\,(0.8\cdot0.6 + \arcsin0.8) = 0.880\,\delta$, compared with the $\delta$ that Earth ages meanwhile. (The trip's turning point moves a little, which changes the coasting legs by $O(\delta)$ too.) Every effect of the turn is $O(\delta)$; the age difference is $4$ years at $\delta = 0$. The proper acceleration during the turn is of order $c\cdot1.6/\delta$, unbounded as $\delta\to0$ — large acceleration, negligible time.

The figure's numbers. Coasting legs: $2\times0.6\times(5 - \delta/2) = 6 - 0.6\,\delta$. Turn: $0.880\,\delta$. Total $6 + 0.28\,\delta$: $6.28$ at $\delta = 1$, $6.56$ at $\delta = 2$. The far point moves in from $x = 4$ to $4 - 0.2\,\delta$. A long turn makes the traveller slightly older, because the slow part of the turn is spent near rest; after it, each curve rejoins the instant-turn worldline.

Cauchy–Schwarz and the triangle inequality, reversed

reversed Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

If $X$ is timelike and $Y$ is any vector, $\ \eta(X, Y)^2 \ge \eta(X, X)\,\eta(Y, Y)$, with equality iff $Y$ is a multiple of $X$.

proof

Write $Y = aX + Z$ with $\eta(X, Z) = 0$. $Z$ is $0$ or spacelike (lemma of section 2). Then $\eta(X,Y)^2 = a^2\eta(X,X)^2$ and $\eta(X,X)\eta(Y,Y) = a^2\eta(X,X)^2 + \eta(X,X)\eta(Z,Z)$. The last term is $\le 0$, and $= 0$ iff $Z = 0$.

reversed triangle inequality

With $\lVert X\rVert := \sqrt{-\eta(X, X)}$, for future-directed timelike $X, Y$:

$$\lVert X + Y\rVert \ \ge\ \lVert X\rVert + \lVert Y\rVert ,$$

with equality iff $X, Y$ are parallel.

proof

$\lVert X + Y\rVert^2 = \lVert X\rVert^2 + \lVert Y\rVert^2 - 2\eta(X, Y)$. By the section 2 lemma $\eta(X, Y) < 0$, and by reversed Cauchy–Schwarz $-\eta(X, Y) \ge \lVert X\rVert\,\lVert Y\rVert$. So the right side is $\ge (\lVert X\rVert + \lVert Y\rVert)^2$.

The decomposition. Put $a = \eta(X, Y)/\eta(X, X)$, legitimate since $\eta(X, X) \ne 0$, and $Z = Y - aX$; then $\eta(X, Z) = \eta(X, Y) - a\,\eta(X, X) = 0$. The lemma that $X^\perp$ is spacelike was proved in the notes of "Two future timelike vectors pair negatively" by counting dimensions: a 2-plane on which $\eta \le 0$ would have to meet the positive-definite spatial 3-plane.

The inequality, in words. In Euclidean space $|x\cdot y| \le |x||y|$. Here, for timelike $X$, the inequality goes the other way — because $\eta(X, X) < 0$ multiplies $\eta(Z, Z) \ge 0$ and so helps rather than hurts. For two spacelike vectors neither direction holds in general. Take $X = (0, 1, 0, 0)$. With $Y = (0.5, 1, 0, 0)$, $\eta(X,Y)^2 = 1 > 0.75 = \eta(X,X)\eta(Y,Y)$, against the Euclidean direction; with $Y = (0, 0, 1, 0)$, $\eta(X,Y)^2 = 0 < 1$, against the reversed one. Indefinite forms only give inequalities on cones.

On the twins. $X = (5, 4)$, $Y = (5, -4)$: $\eta(X, Y) = -25 - 16 = -41$, $\lVert X\rVert\lVert Y\rVert = 9$, and indeed $41 \ge 9$. $\lVert X + Y\rVert^2 = 9 + 9 + 82 = 100$, so $\lVert X + Y\rVert = 10 \ge 3 + 3$.

Equality. Equality in the triangle inequality needs equality in reversed Cauchy–Schwarz, so $Y = aX$, and $a > 0$ since both are future-directed. Then the broken path is not broken.

Induction. For a polygon of future timelike steps $X_1, \dots, X_n$ from $p$ to $q$: $\lVert q - p\rVert \ge \sum\lVert X_i\rVert$, since the sum of future timelike vectors is future timelike (convexity of the cone). Proper time along a smooth curve is a limit of such sums, which gives the next slide's theorem a second proof.

The straight worldline is the longest

theorem: the twin paradox

Let $q - p$ be future timelike. Among all future-directed timelike piecewise-$C^1$ curves from $p$ to $q$, the straight segment has the greatest proper time, $\lVert q - p\rVert/c$, and it is the only one that does.

proof

Boost and translate so $p = 0$ and $q = (cT, \vec 0)$. Parametrise by $t\in[0, T]$. Then $\tau = \int_0^T\sqrt{1 - |\dot{\vec x}|^2/c^2}\,\mathrm dt \le T$, with equality iff $\dot{\vec x} \equiv 0$.

and there is no shortest

Zig-zag at speed $\beta c$ between $p$ and $q$: $\tau = T\sqrt{1 - \beta^2} \to 0$ as $\beta \to 1$. The infimum is $0$ and is not attained.

From $p$ to $q$, $10$ years apart on the straight clock. The twins' path at $0.8c$: $6$. A zig-zag at $0.95c$, never more than $1.2$ light-years from the straight line: $3.1$. Faster zig-zags push it towards $0$.

Full proof

The frame. $q - p$ is future timelike, $= (c\Delta t, \Delta\vec x)$ with $|\Delta\vec x| < c\Delta t$. Rotate $\Delta\vec x$ onto the $x$-axis, then boost with $\beta = |\Delta\vec x|/c\Delta t < 1$: in the new frame $q - p = (cT, \vec 0)$ with $cT = \lVert q - p\rVert$ (the interval is invariant). Proper time is invariant, so it may be computed in this frame.

The parameter. Along a future timelike curve $t$ is strictly increasing, so it runs from $0$ to $T$ and parametrises the curve; $\vec x(t)$ is piecewise $C^1$ with $\vec x(0) = \vec x(T) = 0$.

The inequality. $0 < \sqrt{1 - |\dot{\vec x}|^2/c^2} \le 1$ pointwise, with equality iff $\dot{\vec x}(t) = 0$. Integrating gives $\tau \le T$; if $\tau = T$, the continuous non-negative function $1 - \sqrt{\cdots}$ integrates to zero on each piece, so vanishes, so $\dot{\vec x} \equiv 0$, $\vec x \equiv 0$, and the curve is the straight segment.

The figure. Four out-and-back zig-zags at $\beta = 0.95$ over $T = 10$: each leg lasts $10/8$ years and reaches $0.95\times10/8 = 1.19$ light-years out, and $\tau = 10\sqrt{1 - 0.9025} = 3.12$. The twins' single zig at $0.8$ gives $10\times0.6 = 6$.

The zig-zag. Go out at $+\beta c$ for time $T/2n$, back at $-\beta c$ for $T/2n$, $n$ times. Each piece has $\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}$ times its coordinate time, so $\tau = T\sqrt{1-\beta^2}$, arbitrarily small, while the curve stays within $\beta cT/2n$ of the straight one: arbitrarily close in space, arbitrarily far in proper time.

Checks. Dimensions: $\lVert q - p\rVert/c$ is a time ✓. Limit $c\to\infty$: the integrand tends to $1$ for every curve, and every path takes the same time — Newton's absolute time, with no twin effect ✓.

The twins, as a corollary. Earth's worldline is the straight segment from departure to reunion; the traveller's is not; so the traveller's proper time is strictly less. Whose clock "really" ran slow is not a well-posed question; which worldline is longer is.

A maximum principle — worth ten uncomfortable minutes

Unit 11 asked whether a stationary curve is a minimum. For $\tau$ the Lagrangian in $\dot x$ is $F = \sqrt{1 - \dot x^2/c^2}$:

$$F_{\dot x\dot x} = -\frac{1}{c^2\,(1 - \dot x^2/c^2)^{3/2}} < 0 .$$

Unit 11's Legendre condition for a minimum, $F_{\dot x\dot x} \ge 0$, fails everywhere; the one for a maximum holds everywhere. The sign of $\eta$ has turned the variational problem upside down.

The two integrands against $\dot x$ ($c = 1$). Arc length's is convex: a zig-zag at $\pm0.8$ averages $1.28 > 1$, so straight is shortest. Proper time's is concave: the zig-zag averages $0.6 < 1$, so straight is longest.

The second derivative. $F = (1 - u^2/c^2)^{1/2}$ with $u = \dot x$; $F' = -(u/c^2)(1 - u^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$; $F'' = -\frac{1}{c^2}(1 - u^2/c^2)^{-1/2} - \frac{u^2}{c^4}(1 - u^2/c^2)^{-3/2} = -\frac{1}{c^2}(1 - u^2/c^2)^{-3/2}$. The Euler–Lagrange equation of $\int F\,\mathrm dt$ is $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}F' = 0$, so $F'$ and hence $\dot x$ are constant: stationary curves are straight, as the theorem already said.

Reading the figure. For a path from $p$ to $q$ parametrised by $t$, $\dot x$ averages to $0$ (the path returns). A concave $F$ lies below its tangent line at $0$: $F(\dot x) \le F(0) + F'(0)\,\dot x$. Average over the trip; the last term averages to zero, so $\frac1T\int F(\dot x)\,\mathrm dt \le F(0)$: the time-average of the integrand is at most its value on the straight path, with equality only for $\dot x \equiv 0$ when $F$ is strictly concave. For a convex $F$ every inequality turns round. (Here $F'(0) = 0$ anyway; the tangent argument is what survives in curved spacetime, where only the local, second-order version holds.) The Legendre condition is the infinitesimal version of the same convexity: the sign of $F_{\dot x\dot x}$. The numbers: $\sqrt{1 + 0.64} = 1.28$, $\sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 0.6$.

Why the maximum matters later

why it matters later

Unit 32 says free fall follows geodesics, and unit 27 takes $-mc^2\tau$ as the free action. In Lorentzian signature a timelike geodesic is a local maximum of proper time: a freely falling clock ages the most.

the discomfort

Every intuition about "shortest paths" is backwards here, and only for timelike curves. For spacelike curves $\eta$ is indefinite on the variations and a straight line is neither maximum nor minimum. In curved spacetime the maximum is only local, up to unit 11's conjugate points.

Why ten minutes. In a Riemannian space, "geodesic" means "locally shortest", and every intuition about geodesics is shaped by that. The same word in relativity means "locally longest in proper time" for timelike curves. The equations — unit 32's geodesic equation — are identical in form, because they only express stationarity; the nature of the extremum is what changes. Section 5's theorem is the flat-space version: straight is longest, globally, and no shortest exists.

Conjugate points. In curved spacetime a timelike geodesic maximises proper time only until the first conjugate point along it, exactly as a great-circle arc on a sphere minimises length only until the antipode (unit 11's problem). A thrown ball and a clock in circular orbit can both leave and return to the same satellite; both are geodesics, they age differently, and only one is the maximum. Unit 37 meets this.

The sign of the action. With $S = -mc^2\tau$ the free particle minimises $S$ locally, and in the Newtonian limit $-mc^2\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2} \approx -mc^2 + \tfrac12mv^2$, recovering unit 12's $L = T$ up to a constant. The minus sign is there so that the non-relativistic limit is the familiar one, not because anything is minimised.
