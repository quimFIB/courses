The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: Maxwell's action,
\(S[A] = \frac1c\int_\Omega(-\frac{1}{4\mu_0}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} + A_\mu J^\mu)\,\mathrm d^4x\).
Varying \(A\) gives \(\partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0J^\mu\); gauge invariance
forces \(\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0\); translations give the canonical tensor
\(\eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L + \mu_0^{-1}F^{\mu\alpha}\partial^\nu A_\alpha\), which is
neither symmetric nor gauge invariant and gives the energy density
\(-4.427\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\) in a uniform \(1.00\ \mathrm{MV\,m^{-1}}\) field;
varying the metric gives
\(\mu_0^{-1}(F^{\mu\alpha}F^\nu{}_\alpha - \frac14\eta^{\mu\nu}F^2)\), which is
symmetric, gauge invariant, traceless and gives \(+4.427\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\).
The two differ by \(\partial_\lambda B^{\lambda\mu\nu}\) with
\(B^{\lambda\mu\nu} = \mu_0^{-1}F^{\mu\lambda}A^\nu\) antisymmetric in
\(\lambda\mu\), worth \(-8.854\ \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\) in that field.

* Fields: mechanics with a continuous index

- classical field :: A mechanical system whose coordinates are labelled by the
  points of space rather than by an index: \(n\) functions \(\varphi_a(x)\) on
  spacetime. The index \(i\) of \(q_i(t)\) has become the event \(x\), and the
  sum over \(i\) has become an integral. /Slide: A field is a mechanical system whose index is a point./
- Lagrangian density :: \(\mathcal L(\varphi_a, \partial_\mu\varphi_a, x)\), a
  number attached to each event, built from the fields and their /first/
  derivatives there and nothing else. Locality is its whole content. In SI
  units an energy density, \(\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\). Unit 18 met it in one space
  dimension.
- action over a spacetime region :: \(S[\varphi] = \frac1c\int_\Omega\mathcal
  L\,\mathrm d^4x\) with \(\mathrm d^4x = c\,\mathrm dt\,\mathrm d^3x\) and
  \(\Omega\) a bounded region with piecewise smooth boundary. The factor
  \(1/c\) makes it \(\mathrm{J\,s}\) and changes no field equation.
- first variation :: \(\delta S = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varepsilon}
  S[\varphi + \varepsilon\eta]\big|_{\varepsilon = 0}\), unit 11's Gateaux
  derivative with a four-dimensional integral. It splits into a bulk term and a
  boundary term. /Slide: The first variation, and where the boundary term goes./
- field Euler–Lagrange equations :: \(\partial_\mu\big(\partial\mathcal
  L/\partial(\partial_\mu\varphi_a)\big) = \partial\mathcal L/\partial\varphi_a\),
  one second-order PDE per field component. Obtained from stationarity against
  every variation of compact support. /Slide: The field Euler–Lagrange equations./
- fundamental lemma in four dimensions :: If \(f\) is continuous and \(\int
  f\eta\,\mathrm d^4x = 0\) for every \(\eta \in C_c^\infty\), then \(f \equiv
  0\). Unit 11's proof with a four-dimensional bump function; it uses the
  Euclidean geometry of \(\mathbb R^4\), not \(\eta_{\mu\nu}\).
- functional derivative :: \(\delta S/\delta\varphi_a(x)\), the unique
  continuous density with \(\delta S = \int (\delta S/\delta\varphi_a)\,\eta_a\,
  \mathrm d^4x\). The analogue of \(\partial S/\partial q_i\) with the index
  replaced by an event. The field equations are \(\delta S/\delta\varphi_a = 0\).
- boundary term :: \(\frac1c\oint_{\partial\Omega}\pi^\mu{}_a\eta_a\,\mathrm
  d\Sigma_\mu\), what is left of the first variation after integrating by parts.
  Killed by fixing the field on \(\partial\Omega\), by a natural condition
  \(\pi^\mu{}_an_\mu = 0\), or by decay. For the Einstein–Hilbert action it is
  killed by none of these, and unit 35 adds the Gibbons–Hawking–York integral to
  cancel it. /Slide: What the boundary term is for./
- Klein–Gordon equation :: \(\Box\varphi + \kappa^2\varphi = 0\) with
  \(\kappa = mc/\hbar\), the Euler–Lagrange equation of \(\mathcal L =
  -\frac12(\partial_\mu\varphi\partial^\mu\varphi + \kappa^2\varphi^2)\). Its
  dispersion relation \(\omega^2 = c^2k^2 + (mc^2/\hbar)^2\) is unit 27's mass
  shell; the mass term is exactly what makes the wave equation dispersive.
  /Slide: Worked: the Klein–Gordon field, and what mass does to a wave./
- reduced Compton wavelength :: \(\kappa^{-1} = \hbar/mc\), the length the mass
  term puts into the field equation, and the range of the static solution
  \(e^{-\kappa r}/r\). For the charged pion, \(\hbar c/mc^2 = 197.327\
  \mathrm{MeV\,fm}/139.570\ \mathrm{MeV} = 1.4138\) fm, which is Yukawa's range
  for the nuclear force. The Compton wavelength proper is \(h/mc\), larger by
  \(2\pi\); it is \(\hbar/mc\) that appears in the field equation, so that is
  the one named here.
- Maxwell Lagrangian :: \(\mathcal L = -\frac{1}{4\mu_0}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} +
  A_\mu J^\mu = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}|\vec E|^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu_0}|\vec B|^2 -
  \rho\varphi + \vec A\cdot\vec J\). The unique Lorentz-invariant,
  gauge-invariant, parity-even functional of \(A\) quadratic in first
  derivatives. In \((+,-,-,-)\) books the coupling is written \(-A_\mu J^\mu\).
  /Slide: The running example: Maxwell's action./

* Maxwell from an action

- Bianchi identity :: \(\partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda}
  + \partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu} = 0\), true for every \(A\) whatsoever because
  \(F = \partial A\) and mixed partials commute. It /is/ \(\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0\)
  and Faraday's law, so those two Maxwell equations are identities and not field
  equations. /Slide: Why the potential and not the field./

* Noether, for fields

- internal symmetry :: A symmetry that mixes the field components at a fixed
  event, \(\delta\varphi_a = \varepsilon M_{ab}\varphi_b\), as against a
  spacetime symmetry, which moves the event. The phase rotation of a complex
  field is the example; its charge is electric charge. /Slide: What a symmetry of a field action is./
- Noether current :: \(j^\mu = \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu
  \varphi_a)}X_a - K^\mu\), attached to a variation \(\delta\varphi_a =
  \varepsilon X_a\) satisfying \(\delta\mathcal L = \varepsilon\partial_\mu K^\mu\)
  /off shell/. On shell \(\partial_\mu j^\mu = 0\). Unit 13's theorem with a
  four-divergence in place of a time derivative. Determined only up to
  \(\partial_\lambda B^{\lambda\mu}\) with \(B\) antisymmetric.
  /Slide: Noether's theorem for fields./
- conserved charge :: \(Q = \frac1c\int j^0\,\mathrm d^3x\) over a
  constant-time slice, with \(\mathrm dQ/\mathrm dt = -\oint\vec\jmath\cdot
  \mathrm d\vec A\). Constant when the flux at infinity vanishes; the local law
  \(\partial_\mu j^\mu = 0\) is strictly stronger than the global one.
- canonical energy–momentum tensor :: \(T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{can}} =
  \eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal L - \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu
  \varphi_a)}\partial^\nu\varphi_a\), the Noether current of spacetime
  translation: the current of a translation by \(a\) is \(j^\mu = a_\nu
  T^{\mu\nu}\). \(T^{00}\) is the energy density, \(cT^{i0}\) the energy flux,
  \(T^{0j}/c\) the momentum density, \(T^{ij}\) the momentum flux. For a scalar
  it is symmetric; for Maxwell it is neither symmetric nor gauge invariant.
  /Slide: Translations, and the canonical energy–momentum tensor./
- improvement term :: \(\partial_\lambda B^{\lambda\mu\nu}\) with
  \(B^{\lambda\mu\nu} = -B^{\mu\lambda\nu}\). Identically conserved, and it
  changes no total charge, so conservation alone cannot determine
  \(T^{\mu\nu}\). \(B\) itself is called a superpotential.
  /Slide: What you are allowed to add: improvement./
- Belinfante–Rosenfeld tensor :: The canonical tensor with the improvement term
  \(B^{\lambda\mu\nu} = \mu_0^{-1}F^{\mu\lambda}A^\nu\) subtracted, which for
  Maxwell leaves \(\mu_0^{-1}(F^{\mu\alpha}F^\nu{}_\alpha - \frac14\eta^{\mu\nu}
  F^2)\): symmetric, gauge invariant and with the right sign. Belinfante (1939)
  and Rosenfeld (1940) built \(B\) from the spin current, for any
  Lorentz-invariant theory. /Slide: Belinfante and Rosenfeld: the repair, for Maxwell./
- spin current :: \(S^{\lambda\mu\nu}\), the part of the Noether current of
  Lorentz invariance coming from the rotation of the field's own indices rather
  than from its position. It is why the canonical tensor of a field carrying
  spacetime indices is asymmetric, and it is measurable: circularly polarised
  light carries angular momentum with no orbital motion, as Beth measured in 1936.

* The definition general relativity uses

- invariant volume element :: \(\sqrt{-g}\,\mathrm d^4x\), unchanged by any
  change of coordinates, because \(\det g' = (\det\Lambda)^2\det g\) cancels the
  Jacobian of unit 05's change-of-variables theorem. In inertial coordinates
  \(\det\Lambda = \pm1\) and the distinction is invisible, which is why it
  appears only now. /Slide: The move: let the metric be general, in flat space./
- tensor density :: A quantity whose components transform as a tensor's do,
  times \((\det\Lambda)^w\) for some weight \(w\). \(\sqrt{-g}\) has weight 1;
  the Levi-Civita symbol of unit 28, with entries \(\pm1\) in every coordinate
  system, has weight \(-1\), which is why \(\sqrt{-g}\,\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho
  \sigma}\) is the honest tensor.
- Jacobi's formula :: \(\delta\det M = \det M\operatorname{tr}(M^{-1}\delta M)\)
  for invertible \(M\), proved from \(\det(I+\varepsilon A) = 1 +
  \varepsilon\operatorname{tr}A + O(\varepsilon^2)\). Applied to the metric it
  gives \(\delta\sqrt{-g} = \frac12\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\nu}\delta g_{\mu\nu} =
  -\frac12\sqrt{-g}\,g_{\mu\nu}\delta g^{\mu\nu}\), which supplies half of
  \(T^{00}\). /Slide: Differentiating a determinant./
- metric stress–energy tensor :: The unique symmetric tensor with \(\delta
  S_{\text m} = -\frac{1}{2c}\int\sqrt{-g}\,T_{\mu\nu}\,\delta g^{\mu\nu}\,
  \mathrm d^4x\); equivalently \(T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{-g}}\,\delta(\sqrt{-g}
  \mathcal L_{\text m})/\delta g_{\mu\nu}\). Symmetric because \(g\) is, gauge
  invariant because \(S\) is, and conserved on shell by coordinate invariance.
  This is the right-hand side of Einstein's equation. Also called the Hilbert
  stress–energy tensor. /Slide: The definition, and why its two properties are free./
- angular-momentum current :: \(M^{\lambda\mu\nu} = x^\mu T^{\lambda\nu} -
  x^\nu T^{\lambda\mu}\), with \(\partial_\lambda M^{\lambda\mu\nu} = T^{\mu\nu}
  - T^{\nu\mu}\): conserved if and only if \(T\) is symmetric. Its six charges
  are the three angular momenta and the three boost charges of the Lorentz
  group. /Slide: Why symmetry is angular momentum./
