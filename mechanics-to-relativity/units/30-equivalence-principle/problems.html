The equivalence principle, and why flat spacetime cannot hold gravity

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You derive the gravitational redshift from the equivalence principle with no geometry and match Pound–Rebka within its error bar; you state the weak, Einstein and strong equivalence principles with an experiment that distinguishes them; and you exhibit a non-constant metric on flat spacetime — thereby proving that non-constant metric components alone are not gravity.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about five hours. Conventions as in the deck: signature \((-,+,+,+)\), \(x^0 = ct\), Greek indices \(0\) to \(3\) and Latin \(1\) to \(3\). \(\Phi\) is the Newtonian potential with \(\vec g = -\nabla\Phi\), so \(\Phi\) is negative near a mass and \(\Phi\to0\) at infinity. A fractional frequency shift is \(\Delta\nu/\nu = (\nu_{\text{received}} - \nu_{\text{emitted}})/\nu_{\text{emitted}}\), so a redshift is negative.

Constants: \(c = 299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(GM_\oplus = 3.986004418\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\), \(R_\oplus = 6.378137\times10^{6}\) m, \(\omega_\oplus = 7.292115\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\), \(GM_\odot = 1.32712440018\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\), \(R_\odot = 6.957\times10^{8}\) m; one light year is \(9.4607\times10^{15}\) m, one radian is \(206\,264.8''\), one day is \(86\,400\) s.

Consequences of the principle

P1 Six redshifts, six scales routine

exercises: the equivalence-principle redshift, potential differences, orders of magnitude, a number

Compute the fractional frequency shift \(\Delta\nu/\nu\) for light received above its emitter in each of the following, and say in one line which formula you used and why.

  1. A strontium lattice clock, over the \(1\) mm height of the atom cloud (JILA, 2022).
  2. Two aluminium-ion clocks, \(33\) cm apart vertically (NIST, 2010).
  3. The \(22.5\) m Harvard tower (Pound and Rebka, 1960).
  4. A GPS satellite at \(a = 26\,561\) km, against a clock on the Earth's surface — the potential term only.
  5. Light escaping from the Sun's surface to infinity.
  6. Light escaping from the white dwarf Sirius B, of mass \(1.018\,M_\odot\) and radius \(0.0084\,R_\odot\), to infinity. Express the answer as an equivalent Doppler velocity in \(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) and compare it with the measured \(80.65\pm0.77\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Six dimensionless numbers, one of which is then converted to a speed. Each is a ratio of a potential difference to \(c^2\); the only decision is whether the field may be treated as uniform over the height in question.

Rung 2

Uniform field, \(\Delta\Phi = gh\), is legitimate when \(h \ll R\): parts 1–3. For parts 4–6 the height is comparable with the source's radius, so use \(\Phi = -GM/r\) directly. "To infinity" means \(\Phi_{\text{receive}} = 0\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(\Delta\nu/\nu = -gh/c^2 = -(9.80665)(10^{-3})/(8.987552\times10^{16})\). First line of part 6: \(\Delta\nu/\nu = -GM/(Rc^2)\) with \(GM = 1.018\times1.32712\times10^{20}\) and \(R = 0.0084\times6.957\times10^8\) m; the equivalent velocity is \(c\,|\Delta\nu/\nu|\).

Worked solution

Idea. One formula, \(\Delta\nu/\nu = \Delta\Phi/c^2\) with \(\Delta\Phi = \Phi_{\text{emit}} - \Phi_{\text{receive}}\), applied six times. The whole exercise is in noticing that \(gh\) is a special case of it, valid when the field does not change appreciably over \(h\), and that the range of the answer is thirteen orders of magnitude.

1–3, uniform field. \(\Delta\nu/\nu = -gh/c^2\) with \(c^2 = 8.987552\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\):

  • \(h = 10^{-3}\) m: \(-1.091\times10^{-19}\).
  • \(h = 0.33\) m: \(-3.601\times10^{-17}\).
  • \(h = 22.5\) m: \(-2.455\times10^{-15}\).

4, GPS. Emitter on the ground, receiver in orbit: \[\frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu} = \frac{1}{c^2}\Big(-\frac{GM_\oplus}{R_\oplus} + \frac{GM_\oplus}{a}\Big) = -\frac{GM_\oplus}{c^2}\Big(\frac1{R_\oplus} - \frac1a\Big).\] With \(1/R_\oplus = 1.567855\times10^{-7}\) and \(1/a = 3.764919\times10^{-8}\), the difference is \(1.191363\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\); times \(GM_\oplus\) that is \(4.74858\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\), so \(\Delta\nu/\nu = -5.284\times10^{-10}\). (Read the other way: the satellite clock gains at that rate, which is P9.)

5, the Sun. \(\Delta\nu/\nu = -GM_\odot/(R_\odot c^2) = -1.32712\times10^{20}/(6.957\times10^{8}\times8.987552\times10^{16}) = -2.123\times10^{-6}\), an equivalent \(636\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). It is measured, and it is a nuisance: it must be separated from convective Doppler shifts in solar spectra, which is why the solar redshift was not a clean test.

6, Sirius B. \(GM = 1.018\times1.32712\times10^{20} = 1.35101\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\); \(R = 0.0084\times6.957\times10^{8} = 5.844\times10^{6}\) m. Then \[\Big|\frac{\Delta\nu}{\nu}\Big| = \frac{1.35101\times10^{20}}{5.844\times10^{6}\times8.987552\times10^{16}} = 2.572\times10^{-4},\] an equivalent \(c\times2.572\times10^{-4} = 77.1\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\), against the measured \(80.65\pm0.77\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\). The \(4\%\) discrepancy is not a failure of the physics: it is the uncertainty in the star's mass and radius, and the measurement is nowadays used backwards, assuming the redshift and solving for \(M/R\).

Check. Dimensions: \(GM/Rc^2\) is \(\mathrm{(m^3s^{-2})/(m\cdot m^2s^{-2})}\) — dimensionless ✓. Limit: part 4's formula with \(a\to\infty\) gives \(-GM_\oplus/R_\oplus c^2 = -6.95\times10^{-10}\), the Earth's own surface redshift, and the orbital answer is correctly smaller ✓. Consistency: part 3's uniform-field answer against the exact \(GM_\oplus h/(R_\oplus(R_\oplus+h)c^2) = 2.453\times10^{-15}\) — a \(0.09\%\) difference, which is the size of \(g - GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^2\) and confirms the uniform-field approximation ✓. Ordering: \(10^{-19}\) to \(10^{-4}\), monotone in \(\Delta\Phi\) ✓.

Another way

Parts 5 and 6 can be quoted as \(z = \Delta\lambda/\lambda = +GM/Rc^2\) to first order, since a fractional frequency drop is a fractional wavelength rise of the same size. Astronomers publish \(z\), and often as \(cz\) in \(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\), which is where the \(80.65\) comes from: it is not a velocity and nothing is moving.

P2 How local is "local"? routine

exercises: unit 05's tidal tensor, the error bar on the equivalence principle, a number

A laboratory falls freely near the Earth's surface. Unit 05 gives the tidal tensor of a point mass as \(T_{ij} = (GM/r^3)(\delta_{ij} - 3n_in_j)\), with eigenvalues \((-2, 1, 1)\,GM/r^3\), the \(-2\) along \(\hat r\).

  1. Evaluate \(GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3\) and say what its dimensions are.
  2. Two balls are released \(1\) m apart, first vertically and then horizontally. Give the relative acceleration in each case, with its sign, and say which pair separates.
  3. Take the vertical pair. How far apart are they after \(1\) s, over and above their initial metre? Compare with how far they have both fallen.
  4. An interferometer resolves a change in their separation of \(10^{-15}\) m. How long after release does it see the laboratory is not in deep space?
  5. How small must the laboratory be for the tidal acceleration across it to stay below \(10^{-9}g\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Five numbers, of which parts 2 and 3 differ only by a factor of two and a sign. The equation you need is unit 05's deviation equation \(\ddot\xi_i = -T_{ij}\xi_j\); with \(\xi\) along an eigenvector it is just \(\ddot\xi = -\lambda\xi\).

Rung 2

Vertical means \(\xi\) along \(\hat r\), where \(\lambda = -2GM/r^3\), so \(\ddot\xi = +2(GM/r^3)\xi \gt 0\): the pair separates. Horizontal means \(\lambda = +GM/r^3\), so the pair converges at half the rate. Part 3 is then one application of \(\tfrac12at^2\), since the acceleration barely changes over a second.

Rung 3

First line: \(GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3 = 3.986004\times10^{14}/(6.378137\times10^6)^3\). For part 5, "tidal acceleration across the lab" means \(2(GM/r^3)L\), and you are solving \(2(GM/r^3)L \le 10^{-9}g\) for \(L\).

Worked solution

Idea. The equivalence principle is not an identity but an inequality: it holds to the accuracy with which the tidal term is invisible. Putting numbers on that term turns "locally" from a hedge into a specification.

1. \(R_\oplus^3 = (6.378137\times10^6)^3 = 2.5946\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3}\), so \[\frac{GM_\oplus}{R_\oplus^3} = \frac{3.986004\times10^{14}}{2.5946\times10^{20}} = 1.5362\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}} .\] Dimensions: \(\mathrm{(m^3s^{-2})/m^3 = s^{-2}}\) — the square of a frequency, as unit 05 noted. The corresponding period \(2\pi/\sqrt{GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3} = 5070\ \mathrm s = 84.5\) minutes is unit 05's tunnel time, and it is not a coincidence.

2. Vertical: \(\ddot\xi = 2(GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3)\xi = +3.072\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) for \(\xi = 1\) m — separating, because the lower ball is nearer the centre and falls harder. Horizontal: \(\ddot\xi = -1.536\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) — converging, because the two balls fall along radii that meet at the centre. The magnitudes are in the ratio \(2:1\) and the signs are opposite, which is the eigenvalue pattern \((-2,1,1)\) and the trace-free condition.

3. Over \(1\) s the acceleration is constant to a part in \(10^{6}\), so the extra separation is \[\tfrac12(3.072\times10^{-6})(1)^2 = 1.536\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm m = 1.54\ \mu\mathrm m ,\] while both balls have fallen \(\tfrac12g(1)^2 = 4.903\) m. The tidal residue is \(1.536\times10^{-6}/4.903 = 3.13\times10^{-7}\) of the fall.

4. \(\tfrac12(3.072\times10^{-6})t^2 = 10^{-15}\) gives \[t = \sqrt{\frac{2\times10^{-15}}{3.072\times10^{-6}}} = 2.55\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm s = 26\ \mu\mathrm s .\] Twenty-six microseconds. The elevator's cover is not hard to blow.

5. \(2(1.5362\times10^{-6})L \le 10^{-9}\times9.80665\) gives \(L \le 3.19\times10^{-3}\) m: about three millimetres. To keep the equivalence principle good to a part in \(10^{9}\) on the Earth's surface you need a laboratory the size of a pea.

Check. Dimensions throughout: \(\mathrm{s^{-2}}\) times a length is an acceleration ✓. Limit: \(r\to\infty\) sends \(GM/r^3\to0\) and every answer to the deep-space one ✓. Sanity: the vertical relative acceleration \(3.07\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) should equal \(g(r) - g(r+1\,\mathrm m) = 2GM/R^3\), and \(2g/R_\oplus = 2\times9.80665/6.378137\times10^6 = 3.075\times10^{-6}\) — agreeing to the three figures that \(g \ne GM/R^2\) allows ✓.

Rindler: gravity's symptoms without gravity

P3 Rindler, from the hyperbola medium

exercises: a chart built from a congruence, a metric by substitution, the image of a map, a causal horizon

Work in \(1{+}1\) dimensions with coordinates \((ct, x)\) on Minkowski space. Fix \(g \gt 0\) and define, for \(X \gt 0\) and \(T \in \mathbb R\), \[ct = X\sinh\frac{gT}{c},\qquad x = X\cosh\frac{gT}{c}.\]

  1. Show that each curve \(X = \) const is the worldline of an observer with constant proper acceleration \(c^2/X\), and that \(T\) is proper time along the curve \(X = c^2/g\).
  2. Compute \(\mathrm ds^2\) in the coordinates \((T, X)\).
  3. Prove that the map \((T,X)\mapsto(ct,x)\) is a bijection onto \(\{x \gt |ct|\}\), and exhibit the inverse.
  4. Show that a light signal travelling from \(X_1\) to \(X_2\) in the wedge follows \(T(X) = (c/g)\ln(X/X_1) + \) const, and deduce the coordinate flight time.
  5. An event \(E\) has \(ct_E \ge x_E\) — it lies on or beyond the horizon. Prove that no signal from \(E\), moving at or below \(c\), ever reaches the observer at any fixed \(X \gt 0\).
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1–2 are computations, part 3 is a proof of bijectivity (construct the inverse and check both composites), part 4 is a one-line ODE, and part 5 is a proof by inequality about the future light cone of \(E\).

Rung 2

For part 1, differentiate with respect to proper time, not \(T\), and use unit 27: along \(X = \) const the rapidity is \(\theta = gT/c\), and unit 27 showed constant proper acceleration \(\alpha\) means \(\theta = \alpha\tau/c\). For part 2 just differentiate the two defining equations, form \(-\mathrm d(ct)^2 + \mathrm dx^2\) and watch the cross terms.

Rung 3

For part 3 the two invariants to build are \(x^2 - c^2t^2\) and \(ct/x\); notice that \(|ct/x| \lt 1\) exactly on the wedge, which is where \(\operatorname{artanh}\) is defined. For part 5, the observer's worldline has \(x^2 - c^2t^2 = X^2 \gt 0\), so it stays in the region \(x \gt ct\); a causal signal from \(E\) stays in \(\{ct - x \ge ct_E - x_E\}\).

Rung 4

First line of part 2: with \(\theta := gT/c\), \(\mathrm d(ct) = \sinh\theta\,\mathrm dX + (gX/c)\cosh\theta\,\mathrm dT\). First line of part 5: the null coordinate \(u := ct - x\) is non-decreasing along any causal future-directed curve, and the observer has \(u = X(\sinh\theta - \cosh\theta) \lt 0\) for every \(T\), while \(u_E \ge 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. The chart is not guessed: it is unit 27's Born-rigid family of hyperbolae, relabelled. Once the metric is in hand, the horizon is a statement about one null coordinate being monotone, which is three lines and no calculation.

1. On \(X = \) const, \(x^2 - c^2t^2 = X^2(\cosh^2 - \sinh^2) = X^2\), so the worldline is unit 27's hyperbola with \(c^2/\alpha = X\), i.e. proper acceleration \(\alpha = c^2/X\). Its four-velocity is \(u = \mathrm d(ct,x)/\mathrm d\tau\); since unit 27 showed \(\theta = \alpha\tau/c\) for the rapidity, and here \(\theta = gT/c\), we get \(\tau = c\theta/\alpha = (X/c)\,\theta = (X/c^2)\,gT\). For \(X = c^2/g\) that is \(\tau = T\): the reference observer's proper time is the coordinate \(T\).

2. With \(\theta = gT/c\), \[\mathrm d(ct) = \sinh\theta\,\mathrm dX + \frac{gX}{c}\cosh\theta\,\mathrm dT,\qquad \mathrm dx = \cosh\theta\,\mathrm dX + \frac{gX}{c}\sinh\theta\,\mathrm dT .\] Then \[-\mathrm d(ct)^2 + \mathrm dx^2 = (\cosh^2\theta - \sinh^2\theta)\,\mathrm dX^2 + \Big(\frac{gX}{c}\Big)^2(\sinh^2\theta - \cosh^2\theta)\,\mathrm dT^2,\] the cross terms cancelling because they are \(2(gX/c)(-\sinh\theta\cosh\theta + \cosh\theta\sinh\theta)\,\mathrm dX\,\mathrm dT = 0\). Hence \[\mathrm ds^2 = -\Big(\frac{gX}{c}\Big)^2\mathrm dT^2 + \mathrm dX^2 .\]

3. Into the wedge. \(x = X\cosh\theta \gt 0\) since \(X \gt 0\), and \(x^2 - c^2t^2 = X^2 \gt 0\); together these give \(x \gt |ct|\). Onto. Given \(x \gt |ct|\), put \(X := \sqrt{x^2 - c^2t^2} \gt 0\) and \(\theta := \operatorname{artanh}(ct/x)\), legitimate because \(|ct/x| \lt 1\); then \(X\cosh\theta = x\) and \(X\sinh\theta = ct\), using \(\cosh(\operatorname{artanh}s) = 1/\sqrt{1-s^2}\). Injective. \(X\) and \(\theta\) are determined by \((ct,x)\) through those two formulas, and \(T = c\theta/g\). So the map is a bijection with the stated inverse. \(\square\)

4. A null curve has \(\mathrm ds^2 = 0\), so \((gX/c)^2\mathrm dT^2 = \mathrm dX^2\), i.e. \(\mathrm dT/\mathrm dX = \pm c/(gX)\). The outgoing sign integrates to \(T = (c/g)\ln X + \) const, so \(T(X) = T_1 + (c/g)\ln(X/X_1)\) and the coordinate flight time from \(X_1\) to \(X_2\) is \((c/g)\ln(X_2/X_1)\). Note that it depends only on the ratio, and diverges as \(X_1\to0\).

5. Use the null coordinate \(u := ct - x\). Along any future-directed causal curve \(\mathrm d(ct) \ge |\mathrm dx|\), so \(\mathrm du = \mathrm d(ct) - \mathrm dx \ge 0\): \(u\) is non-decreasing. At \(E\), \(u_E = ct_E - x_E \ge 0\). But on the observer's worldline \[u = X(\sinh\theta - \cosh\theta) = -X e^{-\theta} \lt 0\] for every \(\theta\in\mathbb R\), since \(X \gt 0\). A signal from \(E\) has \(u \ge u_E \ge 0\) for ever, so it never meets a point with \(u \lt 0\). Hence it never reaches the observer, at any \(X\), ever. \(\square\)

Check. Dimensions: \(gX/c\) is \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and multiplies \(\mathrm dT\) in seconds, giving metres, matching \(\mathrm dX\) ✓. Limit: at \(X = c^2/g\) and \(|gT/c| \ll 1\), \(x \approx c^2/g\) and \(ct \approx cT\), and the metric is \(-c^2\mathrm dT^2 + \mathrm dX^2\), Minkowski ✓. A number: \(u = -Xe^{-\theta}\) tends to \(0^-\) as \(\theta\to+\infty\), so the observer approaches the horizon asymptotically and never touches it — consistent with part 4's divergent flight time ✓.

Another way

Part 5 also follows from part 4. A signal emitted at \(X_1\) arrives at \(X_2\) after coordinate time \((c/g)\ln(X_2/X_1)\), which diverges as \(X_1\to0^+\). Pushing the emitter down to the horizon pushes the arrival to \(T = +\infty\), and events beyond it are worse than that. The null-coordinate argument is cleaner because it never has to take a limit.

P4 The Rindler tower routine

exercises: reading a static metric, an exact redshift, first-order versus exact, a number

Use the Rindler metric \(\mathrm ds^2 = -(gX/c)^2\mathrm dT^2 + \mathrm dX^2\) of P3.

  1. Give the proper time \(\mathrm d\tau\) of a static observer at \(X\) per unit \(\mathrm dT\), and the proper distance between the observers at \(X_1\) and \(X_2\).
  2. Show that the ratio of clock rates of two static observers is exactly \(X_2/X_1\), and that it is unbounded.
  3. A laboratory floor sits at \(X_1 = c^2/g\) with \(g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), and its ceiling \(h = 22.5\) m higher. Compute \(X_1\) in metres and in light years, and the exact fractional shift of a signal from floor to ceiling. Compare with the deck's \(-gh/c^2\).
  4. Write the same shift in terms of \(g_2 := c^2/X_2\), the ceiling's proper acceleration. Which of the two expressions, \(-g_1h/c^2\) or \(-g_2h/c^2\), is exact?
  5. Is the Rindler "gravitational field" uniform? Quantify: by what fraction does the proper acceleration change over the \(22.5\) m, and how does that compare with the Earth's?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four numbers and one short argument. Everything comes from reading coefficients off the metric: \(\mathrm d\tau\) from the \(\mathrm dT^2\) coefficient with \(\mathrm dX = 0\), distance from the \(\mathrm dX^2\) coefficient with \(\mathrm dT = 0\).

Rung 2

The metric has no \(T\) in it, so the coordinate interval between two signals is preserved — that is the deck's static-redshift theorem, and here \(\sqrt{-g_{TT}} = gX/c\). For part 5, the proper acceleration of the observer at \(X\) is \(c^2/X\) (P3 part 1).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: \(X_1 = c^2/g = 8.987552\times10^{16}/9.80665\). Do not compute \(X_2/X_1 - 1\) by forming \(X_2\) and subtracting: rearrange to \(h/X_1\) first, or double precision will eat every significant figure.

Worked solution

Idea. A static metric hands you clock rates by inspection. The instructive part is that the "approximate" elevator answer turns out to be exact, once you are careful about whose acceleration \(g\) is.

1. Static means \(\mathrm dX = 0\), so \(-c^2\mathrm d\tau^2 = \mathrm ds^2 = -(gX/c)^2\mathrm dT^2\), giving \(\mathrm d\tau = (gX/c^2)\,\mathrm dT = (X/X_0)\,\mathrm dT\) with \(X_0 = c^2/g\). At fixed \(T\), \(\mathrm ds^2 = \mathrm dX^2\), so the proper distance is simply \(X_2 - X_1\): the coordinate \(X\) is proper distance along a line of simultaneity.

2. Since the metric components do not depend on \(T\), two signals emitted \(\Delta T\) apart arrive \(\Delta T\) apart (P3 part 4: the null curves differ by a \(T\)-translation). Converting to proper time at each end, \[\frac{\mathrm d\tau_2}{\mathrm d\tau_1} = \frac{gX_2/c^2}{gX_1/c^2} = \frac{X_2}{X_1},\] exactly, and therefore \(\nu_2/\nu_1 = X_1/X_2\). The ratio is unbounded: as \(X_1\to0^+\) at fixed \(X_2\) it grows without limit, so a Rindler observer near the horizon ages arbitrarily slowly compared with one far from it — in flat spacetime, with no matter anywhere.

3. \(X_1 = 8.987552\times10^{16}/9.80665 = 9.16475\times10^{15}\) m, which is \(9.16475\times10^{15}/9.4607\times10^{15} = 0.96872\) ly. Then \[\frac{\nu_2}{\nu_1} = \frac{X_1}{X_1+h} = \frac{1}{1 + h/X_1},\qquad \frac{h}{X_1} = \frac{gh}{c^2} = \frac{9.80665\times22.5}{8.987552\times10^{16}} = 2.45506\times10^{-15}.\] So \(\Delta\nu/\nu = -2.45506\times10^{-15}/(1 + 2.45506\times10^{-15}) = -2.45506\times10^{-15}\) to fifteen significant figures: the correction is \((gh/c^2)^2 = 6.03\times10^{-30}\), which first shows up in the thirtieth decimal place.

4. \(\nu_2/\nu_1 = X_1/X_2 = (X_2 - h)/X_2 = 1 - h/X_2 = 1 - g_2h/c^2\). So \(\Delta\nu/\nu = -g_2h/c^2\) exactly, with the ceiling's proper acceleration, while \(-g_1h/c^2\) is exact only to first order. The two differ by \((gh/c^2)^2 \approx 6\times10^{-30}\), which is why nobody has ever had to care. The statement that is exact both ways is the ratio \(\nu_1/\nu_2 = X_2/X_1 = 1 + g_1h/c^2\).

5. Rindler: \(a(X) = c^2/X\), so \(|\Delta a|/a = h/X_1 = 2.455\times10^{-15}\) over the tower. Earth: \(a(r) = GM/r^2\), so \(|\Delta a|/a = 2h/R_\oplus = 45/6.378137\times10^6 = 7.06\times10^{-6}\). The Earth's field is nine orders of magnitude less uniform than the Rindler field over the same height. That difference is the tidal tensor, and it is what P2 measured and P5 proves is irremovable.

Check. Dimensions: \(gX/c^2\) is \(\mathrm{(m\,s^{-2})(m)/(m^2s^{-2})}\), dimensionless ✓. Limits: \(h\to0\) gives ratio \(1\) ✓; \(X_1\to\infty\) at fixed \(h\) gives \(g_1\to0\) and no shift ✓. A number: \(0.96872\) ly is unit 27's \(c^2/g\), unchanged, as it must be — this is the same hyperbola ✓.

What cannot be transformed away

P5 Proof: tides are the real thing hard

exercises: Taylor coefficients of a coordinate change, a dimension count, invariance under a freely falling frame

Fix an event \(p\) and choose coordinates with \(p\) at the origin. Consider a smooth change of coordinates written as a Taylor series about \(p\), \[y^\mu = A^\mu{}_\nu x^\nu + \tfrac12 B^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}x^\nu x^\rho + \tfrac16 C^\mu{}_{\nu\rho\sigma}x^\nu x^\rho x^\sigma + \dots,\] with \(B\) symmetric in its two lower indices and \(C\) totally symmetric in its three.

  1. Count the independent components of \(g_{\mu\nu}(p)\), of \(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\nu}(p)\), and of \(\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta g_{\mu\nu}(p)\), in \(n = 4\) dimensions. Say which symmetry each count uses.
  2. Count the free components of \(A\), \(B\) and \(C\).
  3. Deduce: how many parameters remain after setting \(g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}\), and what group are they? How many second-derivative combinations must survive every coordinate change? Prove that direction of the claim rigorously.
  4. Redo the count in \(n\) dimensions and show the surviving number is \(n^2(n^2-1)/12\). Evaluate for \(n = 1, 2, 3, 4\) and say what each answer means.
  5. Now the Newtonian shadow, in full. Let \(\vec x_0(t)\) be any \(C^2\) curve and \(\vec x' = \vec x - \vec x_0(t)\). Show that the equation of motion in the primed frame is that of a particle in the potential \(\Phi' (\vec x',t) = \Phi(\vec x_0 + \vec x') + \ddot{\vec x}_0\cdot\vec x'\), that \(\nabla'\Phi'\) can be made to vanish at \(\vec x' = \vec 0\) for all \(t\), and that \(\partial'_i\partial'_j\Phi' = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi\) regardless. Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1–4 are counting: every answer is a binomial coefficient. Part 3's "prove rigorously" is a one-line rank argument about linear maps, not a construction. Part 5 is a proof in three lines of calculus.

Rung 2

A symmetric pair of indices in \(n\) dimensions has \(\binom{n+1}{2}\) values; a symmetric triple has \(\binom{n+2}{3}\). The metric is symmetric; its second derivatives are symmetric in the derivative pair by Clairaut (unit 03) and in the metric pair, and those two symmetries are independent.

Rung 3

For part 3: the coefficients \(C\) enter the transformed second derivatives linearly, so "how many can be removed" is the rank of a linear map from an \(80\)-dimensional space, and a linear map cannot be onto a space of larger dimension. That argument gives \(\ge 20\) survivors without knowing anything about the map.

Rung 4

First line of part 5: substitute \(\vec x = \vec x_0(t) + \vec x'\) into \(\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla\Phi(\vec x)\) to get \(\ddot{\vec x}' = -\nabla\Phi(\vec x_0 + \vec x') - \ddot{\vec x}_0\); then notice that \(-\ddot{\vec x}_0\) is minus the gradient (in \(\vec x'\)) of \(\ddot{\vec x}_0\cdot\vec x'\).

Worked solution

Idea. Gauge freedom is a vector space and so is the thing being gauged away; comparing dimensions settles what must remain, before anyone knows how to compute it. The Newtonian part is the same statement in a case where the whole calculation fits in three lines, and it names the survivor: the tidal tensor.

1. The targets. \(g_{\mu\nu}(p)\): symmetric in \(\mu\nu\), so \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\). \(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\nu}(p)\): one free index times \(10\), so \(4\times10 = 40\). \(\partial_\alpha\partial_\beta g_{\mu\nu}(p)\): symmetric in \(\alpha\beta\) by Clairaut, so \(\binom{5}{2} = 10\) derivative pairs, times \(10\) metric pairs, \(= 100\).

2. The freedom. \(A^\mu{}_\nu\): \(4\times4 = 16\). \(B^\mu{}_{\nu\rho}\): symmetric lower pair, \(4\times\binom52 = 4\times10 = 40\). \(C^\mu{}_{\nu\rho\sigma}\): totally symmetric lower triple, \(4\times\binom{6}{3} = 4\times20 = 80\).

3. The deduction. Setting \(g_{\mu\nu}(p) = \eta_{\mu\nu}\) imposes \(10\) conditions on \(A\)'s \(16\) parameters, leaving \(6\): precisely \(\dim O(1,3) = \binom42 = 6\), the three boosts and three rotations of unit 26 — the residual freedom to choose which inertial frame the falling observer uses. At first-derivative order, \(40\) parameters face \(40\) targets: the counts match, and unit 32's construction of normal coordinates shows they really can all be removed. At second-derivative order, the transformed \(\partial\partial g\) depends on \(C\) affinely: it is (something independent of \(C\)) plus a linear function \(L(C)\). Removing a given configuration means solving \(L(C) = v\) for \(v\) in a \(100\)-dimensional space, with \(C\) ranging over an \(80\)-dimensional one. The image \(L(\mathbb R^{80})\) has dimension at most \(80\), so the quotient \(\mathbb R^{100}/\operatorname{im}L\) has dimension at least \(20\): at least twenty combinations of second derivatives are unchanged by every coordinate change. That is rigorous, and it needs nothing about what \(L\) is. \(\square\)

(The converse — that exactly \(20\) survive, so that \(L\) has full rank \(80\) — requires exhibiting normal coordinates, which unit 32 does. Unit 33 then identifies the \(20\) as the components of the Riemann tensor.)

4. General \(n\). Second derivatives: \(\binom{n+1}{2}^2 = \frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4}\). Free \(C\): \(n\binom{n+2}{3} = \frac{n^2(n+1)(n+2)}{6}\). The difference is \[\frac{n^2(n+1)^2}{4} - \frac{n^2(n+1)(n+2)}{6} = \frac{n^2(n+1)}{12}\big[3(n+1) - 2(n+2)\big] = \frac{n^2(n+1)(n-1)}{12} = \frac{n^2(n^2-1)}{12}.\] Values: \(n=1\) gives \(0\) — a curve has no intrinsic geometry, which is why a line and a circle are locally indistinguishable to an ant confined to them. \(n=2\) gives \(1\) — one invariant, the Gaussian curvature, and Gauss's Theorema Egregium is the statement that it is intrinsic. \(n=3\) gives \(6\). \(n=4\) gives \(20\), of which unit 35's field equations constrain ten, leaving ten free to propagate as unit 38's gravitational waves.

5. The Newtonian shadow. Substituting \(\vec x = \vec x_0(t) + \vec x'\) into \(\ddot{\vec x} = -\nabla\Phi(\vec x)\): \[\ddot{\vec x}_0 + \ddot{\vec x}' = -\nabla\Phi(\vec x_0 + \vec x') \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot{\vec x}' = -\nabla\Phi(\vec x_0 + \vec x') - \ddot{\vec x}_0 .\] Since \(\nabla'(\ddot{\vec x}_0\cdot\vec x') = \ddot{\vec x}_0\) (the vector \(\ddot{\vec x}_0\) does not depend on \(\vec x'\)), the right-hand side is \(-\nabla'\Phi'\) with \(\Phi'(\vec x',t) := \Phi(\vec x_0+\vec x') + \ddot{\vec x}_0\cdot\vec x'\). Killing the field. \(\nabla'\Phi'(\vec 0,t) = \nabla\Phi(\vec x_0(t)) + \ddot{\vec x}_0(t)\), which vanishes for all \(t\) exactly when \(\ddot{\vec x}_0 = -\nabla\Phi(\vec x_0)\) — that is, when \(\vec x_0\) is itself a free-fall worldline. Such a curve exists by Picard–Lindelöf (unit 01). What survives. \[\partial'_i\partial'_j\Phi' = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi(\vec x_0+\vec x') + \partial'_i\partial'_j\big(\ddot x_{0k}x'_k\big) = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi + 0,\] because the added term is linear in \(\vec x'\) and its second derivatives vanish identically. So the tidal tensor \(T_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi\) is unchanged by every such frame change, whatever \(\vec x_0\) is. \(\square\) It is the surviving object, and unit 05's deviation equation \(\ddot\xi_i = -T_{ij}\xi_j\) is what the falling observer still measures.

Check. Dimensions: \(T_{ij}\) is \(\mathrm s^{-2}\), the same before and after, as an invariant must be ✓. Special case: a genuinely uniform field \(\Phi = gz\) has \(T = 0\), and indeed the choice \(\ddot{\vec x}_0 = -g\hat z\) removes it everywhere at once — the elevator, working exactly ✓. Counting check: at \(n=2\), \(\binom32^2 = 9\) second derivatives against \(2\binom43 = 8\) free coefficients leaves \(1 = 2^2(2^2-1)/12\) ✓.

P6 Proof: no global inertial frame hard

exercises: turning a physical claim into a statement about a PDE, a contrapositive, what "gravity is not a force" means precisely

Let \(U\subset\mathbb R^3\) be open and connected and let \(\Phi\) be \(C^2\) on \(U\times\mathbb R\). Say that a global inertial frame for \(U\) is a \(C^2\) curve \(\vec x_0(t)\) such that, in the coordinates \(\vec x' = \vec x - \vec x_0(t)\), every free-fall worldline that stays in \(U\) is a straight line traversed at constant speed.

  1. Prove that a global inertial frame for \(U\) exists if and only if \(\partial_i\partial_j\Phi \equiv 0\) on \(U\) for each \(t\).
  2. Deduce that a global inertial frame exists on a region containing a point mass's field only if that region is empty of field variation — and, more sharply, that no neighbourhood of any point outside a spherical mass admits one.
  3. Extend the notion slightly: allow \(\vec x'' = R(t)\vec x' \) with \(R(t) \in SO(3)\) as well. Show that the conclusion of part 1 is unchanged, and say which term in unit 03's fictitious-force list the rotation adds and why it does not help.
  4. State the corresponding claim for spacetime, using P5's language, and say exactly which step of your Newtonian proof does not carry over without units 32 and 33.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A proof, with an iff. The easy direction is a construction; the hard direction assumes the frame exists and squeezes a differential equation out of it.

Rung 2

"Every free-fall worldline is straight at constant speed in the primed frame" means \(\ddot{\vec x}' = \vec 0\) for every solution. From P5, \(\ddot{\vec x}' = -\nabla\Phi(\vec x_0 + \vec x') - \ddot{\vec x}_0\). So the hypothesis says that a certain function of \(\vec x'\) vanishes identically on a region — and a function that vanishes identically has vanishing derivatives.

Rung 3

First line of the hard direction: the hypothesis gives \(\nabla\Phi(\vec x) = -\ddot{\vec x}_0(t)\) for every \(\vec x \in U\), a right-hand side with no \(\vec x\) in it. Differentiate with respect to \(x_j\).

Worked solution

Idea. "Gravity is a force on a flat background" is the claim that a single frame can be inertial for a whole region. Written out, that claim says \(\nabla\Phi\) is independent of position, which says the tidal tensor vanishes. So the existence of tides and the existence of a global inertial frame are literally the same question with opposite answers.

1. Proof. (\(\Leftarrow\)) Suppose \(\partial_i\partial_j\Phi \equiv 0\) on \(U\), which is connected, so \(\nabla\Phi\) is a function \(\vec a(t)\) of \(t\) alone there. Pick any solution \(\vec x_0\) of \(\ddot{\vec x}_0 = -\vec a(t)\); it exists by integrating twice. Then for any free-fall worldline in \(U\), by P5, \(\ddot{\vec x}' = -\nabla\Phi(\vec x_0 + \vec x') - \ddot{\vec x}_0 = -\vec a + \vec a = \vec 0\). Every such worldline is straight at constant speed: a global inertial frame.
(\(\Rightarrow\)) Suppose a global inertial frame \(\vec x_0\) exists. Fix \(t\) and fix any \(\vec x\in U\). Picard–Lindelöf (unit 01) supplies a free-fall worldline through \(\vec x\) at time \(t\) with any initial velocity, and it stays in \(U\) for a short interval; by hypothesis it satisfies \(\ddot{\vec x}' = \vec 0\) there. Therefore \(\nabla\Phi(\vec x) + \ddot{\vec x}_0(t) = \vec 0\), i.e. \(\nabla\Phi(\vec x) = -\ddot{\vec x}_0(t)\), and this holds for every \(\vec x\in U\) with the same right-hand side. Differentiating with respect to \(x_j\) and using that the right-hand side has no \(\vec x\)-dependence, \(\partial_j\partial_i\Phi(\vec x) = 0\) for all \(i,j\) and all \(\vec x\in U\). \(\square\)

2. Outside a spherical mass \(M\), \(\Phi = -GM/r\) and \(T_{ij} = (GM/r^3)(\delta_{ij} - 3n_in_j)\) with eigenvalues \((-2,1,1)GM/r^3\), none of which is zero for \(r \lt \infty\). So on any open set \(U\) outside the mass, \(\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\) is nowhere the zero matrix, and part 1 says no global inertial frame exists on \(U\) — however small \(U\) is. Shrinking the region shrinks the size of the tidal effect (P2), never its presence.

3. With \(\vec x'' = R(t)\vec x'\) the transformed equation acquires unit 03's centrifugal, Coriolis and Euler terms: \(\ddot{\vec x}'' = R(-\nabla\Phi - \ddot{\vec x}_0) - 2\vec\omega\times\dot{\vec x}'' - \vec\omega\times(\vec\omega\times\vec x'') - \dot{\vec\omega}\times\vec x''\). Demanding \(\ddot{\vec x}'' = \vec 0\) for every initial velocity at a fixed point forces the Coriolis coefficient to vanish, since it is the only velocity-dependent term and the others are not: hence \(\vec\omega = \vec 0\), and the rest reduces to part 1. The rotation adds a term linear in \(\vec x''\) — centrifugal — which is exactly the kind of term P5 showed cannot touch \(\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\): its own second derivatives vanish. Rotating the frame cannot manufacture a second derivative, so it cannot cancel one.

4. The spacetime claim: there is a chart on a region in which \(g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu}\) throughout if and only if the \(20\) surviving second-derivative combinations of P5 vanish throughout. The Newtonian proof does not carry over as written at two points. (i) "Free-fall worldlines are straight lines" must become "free-fall worldlines are the straight lines of the chart", which needs the notion of a geodesic — unit 32. (ii) The step "a function of \(\vec x\) equals a function of \(t\) alone, so differentiate" becomes an integrability condition on a system of PDEs for the coordinate change, and the object whose vanishing makes the system integrable is the Riemann tensor — unit 33. What does carry over verbatim is the shape of the argument, and the conclusion.

Check. Dimensions: \(\partial_i\partial_j\Phi\) in \(\mathrm s^{-2}\), and the claim is that it vanishes — dimensionally trivial, which it should be, since an "if and only if" between a geometric statement and a vanishing tensor cannot depend on units ✓. A limit: as the source mass \(M\to0\), \(T\to0\) and the global inertial frame reappears, which is unit 26's Minkowski space ✓. A number: at the Earth's surface the largest eigenvalue is \(2GM_\oplus/R_\oplus^3 = 3.07\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{s^{-2}}\), so the obstruction is small but exactly as nonzero as P2 measured ✓.

P7 Proof: why not a field on flat spacetime hard

exercises: field energy from the divergence theorem, \(E = mc^2\) as a source, an iteration that does not stop

Consider the Newtonian theory \(\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho\) as a field theory on flat space, with \(\Phi\to0\) at infinity and \(\rho\) of compact support.

  1. Prove that the assembly energy \(U = \tfrac12\int\rho\Phi\,\mathrm dV\) equals \(-\frac{1}{8\pi G}\int|\nabla\Phi|^2\,\mathrm dV\), stating where each hypothesis is used. Conclude that the field carries energy density \(u = -|\nabla\Phi|^2/8\pi G\), and comment on its sign.
  2. Evaluate \(U\) for a uniform sphere of mass \(M\) and radius \(R\), and compute the fractional self-energy \(|U|/Mc^2\) for the Earth and for the Sun.
  3. Argue from unit 27's \(E = mc^2\) and the weak equivalence principle that this energy must itself gravitate, and write down the corrected field equation. Show that substituting the corrected \(\Phi\) generates a further correction, and estimate the size of the first one for the Sun.
  4. State what the analogous construction for a symmetric tensor field \(h_{\mu\nu}\) on Minkowski space demands, and why the result destroys the premise. (This part is a statement: it is Deser's theorem, and this course proves it in unit 35, not here.)
  5. Where exactly does the weak equivalence principle enter part 3? Which of the three principles of the deck is being used, and would the argument survive if only one of the weaker ones held?
Hints · 4 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an integration by parts; part 2 is two numbers; part 3 is an argument plus an estimate; parts 4 and 5 are prose, and the prose is the point of the problem.

Rung 2

For part 1 substitute \(\rho = \nabla^2\Phi/4\pi G\) and use the divergence theorem of unit 05 on \(\nabla\cdot(\Phi\nabla\Phi) = |\nabla\Phi|^2 + \Phi\nabla^2\Phi\). The compact support and the boundary condition are what make the surface term die; count powers of \(r\) to see it.

Rung 3

For part 3, the correction to the source is \(u/c^2\), so the corrected equation is \(\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G(\rho + u/c^2)\). Estimate its size by comparing \(\int|u|/c^2\) with \(M\) — which is exactly the fractional self-energy of part 2.

Rung 4

First line of part 1: \(U = \tfrac12\int\rho\Phi = \frac1{8\pi G}\int\Phi\nabla^2\Phi\). First line of part 2: for a uniform sphere \(U = -3GM^2/5R\), obtained by assembling it shell by shell.

Worked solution

Idea. Gravity's source is energy; gravity's field has energy; therefore gravity sources itself. That one sentence makes a linear theory impossible, and everything after it is bookkeeping — up to the point where the bookkeeping, done relativistically, eats the flat background it was written on.

1. Substitute \(\rho = \nabla^2\Phi/4\pi G\): \[U = \tfrac12\int\rho\Phi\,\mathrm dV = \frac{1}{8\pi G}\int\Phi\,\nabla^2\Phi\,\mathrm dV .\] Now use the identity \(\nabla\cdot(\Phi\nabla\Phi) = |\nabla\Phi|^2 + \Phi\nabla^2\Phi\) and the divergence theorem (unit 05) over a ball of radius \(r\): \[\int_{B_r}\Phi\nabla^2\Phi\,\mathrm dV = \oint_{S_r}\Phi\,\nabla\Phi\cdot\mathrm d\vec S - \int_{B_r}|\nabla\Phi|^2\,\mathrm dV .\] Outside the support of \(\rho\), \(\Phi \sim -GM/r\) and \(|\nabla\Phi| \sim GM/r^2\), while the sphere's area is \(4\pi r^2\); so the surface term is \(O(1/r)\) and vanishes as \(r\to\infty\). (This is where compact support and \(\Phi\to0\) are used, and both are needed: a mass distribution extending to infinity can make the surface term finite or divergent.) Hence \[U = -\frac{1}{8\pi G}\int|\nabla\Phi|^2\,\mathrm dV ,\] which is manifestly \(\le 0\). Reading the integrand as a local energy density gives \(u = -|\nabla\Phi|^2/8\pi G\), negative everywhere: unlike the electrostatic case, where \(u = \epsilon_0|\vec E|^2/2 \gt 0\), gravitational field energy is negative, because gravity attracts and assembling a body releases energy.

2. Assembling a uniform sphere shell by shell gives \(U = -\int_0^R \frac{G m(r)}{r}\,4\pi r^2\rho\,\mathrm dr\) with \(m(r) = M r^3/R^3\), which integrates to \(U = -\frac{3GM^2}{5R}\). So \[\frac{|U|}{Mc^2} = \frac{3GM}{5Rc^2} .\] Earth: \(3\times3.986004\times10^{14}/(5\times6.378137\times10^6\times8.987552\times10^{16}) = 4.17\times10^{-10}\). Sun: \(3\times1.327124\times10^{20}/(5\times6.957\times10^{8}\times8.987552\times10^{16}) = 1.27\times10^{-6}\). (The Sun is centrally condensed, so the true figure is a few times larger; the uniform-sphere value is the right order.)

3. By \(E = mc^2\) the field energy \(u\) is equivalent to a mass density \(u/c^2\). The weak equivalence principle says gravitational mass equals inertial mass for every form of energy, so \(u/c^2\) must source the field like any other mass density: \[\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\Big(\rho - \frac{|\nabla\Phi|^2}{8\pi Gc^2}\Big) .\] This is nonlinear. Solving it perturbatively, \(\Phi = \Phi_0 + \Phi_1 + \dots\) with \(\nabla^2\Phi_0 = 4\pi G\rho\) and \(\nabla^2\Phi_1 = -|\nabla\Phi_0|^2/2c^2\), the second solution changes \(|\nabla\Phi|^2\), which changes the source again, for ever: an infinite iteration, each term smaller by roughly \(GM/Rc^2\). For the Sun the first correction is of relative size \(\sim1.3\times10^{-6}\) — small, and of exactly the order of the perihelion and light-bending effects, which is not a coincidence.

4. Do the same relativistically: let \(h_{\mu\nu}\) be a massless spin-2 field on Minkowski space whose linear field equation is sourced by the matter stress-energy tensor. Consistency of the linear equation requires the source to be conserved, \(\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{matter}} = 0\); but the field does work on matter, so matter's stress-energy alone is not conserved once the coupling is switched on. The repair is to source the field with the total stress-energy, matter plus field. Deser (1970) showed that this repair, carried out consistently, has for its endpoint the Einstein–Hilbert action of \(g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}\). The premise is then destroyed: in the resulting theory every rod, clock and light ray responds to \(g_{\mu\nu}\), and no experiment measures \(\eta_{\mu\nu}\). A background that no measurement can reach is not a background; the split \(g = \eta + h\) is a change of variables inside general relativity, not a theory of gravity on flat spacetime.

5. The weak equivalence principle enters at exactly one place: the claim that the mass-equivalent of the field energy gravitates with the same constant of proportionality as ordinary matter. That is the statement \(m_{\mathrm g} = m_{\mathrm i}\) applied to a form of energy that is neither a particle nor a material body. Strictly, the principle being used is the strong one: gravitational field energy is precisely the self-energy that WEP and even EEP exclude from their statements, both of which speak only of test bodies and of non-gravitational experiments. If only WEP held, the argument would give no constraint at all on how field energy gravitates, and theories in which it gravitates differently — Brans–Dicke, for instance — are exactly the ones the Nordtvedt effect tests.

Check. Dimensions: \(|\nabla\Phi|^2/G\) is \(\mathrm{(m\,s^{-2})^2/(m^3kg^{-1}s^{-2})} = \mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}s^{-2}} = \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\) ✓, an energy density. Special case: a point mass gives a divergent \(\int|\nabla\Phi|^2\) near the origin, matching \(U = -3GM^2/5R \to -\infty\) as \(R\to0\) — the classical self-energy divergence, and a reminder that "point mass" is an idealisation ✓. A number: for the Sun \(3GM/5Rc^2 = 1.27\times10^{-6}\), and the observed solar gravitational redshift is \(GM/Rc^2 = 2.12\times10^{-6}\) — the same order, as both measure \(GM/Rc^2\) ✓.

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1, 3, 4 are algebra — expand one square, then substitute into one formula. Part 2 is a number, part 5 is a sentence, and part 5 is the one that is actually being examined.

Rung 2

For part 1 only \(\rho^2\mathrm d\phi^2\) changes: expand \((\mathrm d\phi' + \omega\,\mathrm dt)^2\) and collect the \(\mathrm dt^2\) coefficient with the \(-c^2\mathrm dt^2\) already there. Rung 3

Worked solution

Idea. Every step is a substitution, and the surprise is at the end: a family of observers in flat spacetime measures a space in which circles have too much circumference. Nothing has gone wrong; "space" was never defined until the radar construction defined it.

1. \(\rho^2\mathrm d\phi^2 = \rho^2\mathrm d\phi'^2 + 2\omega\rho^2\mathrm d\phi'\mathrm dt + \omega^2\rho^2\mathrm dt^2\), so \[\mathrm ds^2 = -\Big(1-\frac{\omega^2\rho^2}{c^2}\Big)c^2\mathrm dt^2 + 2\omega\rho^2\,\mathrm d\phi'\,\mathrm dt + \mathrm d\rho^2 + \rho^2\mathrm d\phi'^2 + \mathrm dz^2 .\] With \(x^0 = ct\): \(g_{00} = -(1-\omega^2\rho^2/c^2)\), \(g_{0\phi'} = \omega\rho^2/c\), \(g_{\phi'\phi'} = \rho^2\), \(g_{\rho\rho} = g_{zz} = 1\).

2. \(g_{00} = 0\) at \(\rho = c/\omega\), the light cylinder. There the curve "fixed \(\rho,\phi',z\)" is null, and beyond it spacelike: no observer can co-rotate, because doing so would require moving faster than light. For \(716\) Hz, \(\omega = 2\pi\times716 = 4499\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}\) and \(c/\omega = 2.998\times10^{8}/4499 = 6.66\times10^{4}\ \mathrm m = 66.6\) km — a few radii outside a \(12\) km neutron star, which is why the light cylinder bounds a pulsar's co-rotating magnetosphere. (The real star's spacetime is not flat; this is an order-of-magnitude statement about the flat-space idealisation.)

3. Radially \(g_{0\rho} = 0\), so \(\gamma_{\rho\rho} = g_{\rho\rho} = 1\) and the measured radius is \(\int_0^\rho\mathrm d\rho = \rho\). Around the rim, \[\gamma_{\phi'\phi'} = \rho^2 - \frac{(\omega\rho^2/c)^2}{-(1-\omega^2\rho^2/c^2)} = \rho^2\Big[1 + \frac{\omega^2\rho^2/c^2}{1-\omega^2\rho^2/c^2}\Big] = \frac{\rho^2}{1-\omega^2\rho^2/c^2} = \gamma^2\rho^2 .\] So the measured circumference is \(C = \int_0^{2\pi}\gamma\rho\,\mathrm d\phi' = 2\pi\gamma\rho\) and the measured radius is \(R = \rho\).

4. Therefore \(C/2R = \pi\gamma \gt \pi\). Rim speed \(0.1c\): \(\gamma = (1-0.01)^{-1/2} = 1.005038\), a \(0.50\%\) excess. Ultracentrifuge rim at \(10^3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\): \(\beta^2 = 1.113\times10^{-11}\) and \(\gamma - 1 = 5.56\times10^{-12}\) — real, and hopelessly below any mechanical measurement, though the associated time dilation was measured directly with Mössbauer rotors in the 1960s.

5. No: the spacetime is Minkowski space, exactly, and every event in the chart is an ordinary event of it. The non-Euclidean geometry belongs to the family of co-rotating observers — it is the geometry of the distances they measure with radar, not the geometry of a slice of spacetime. (Strictly, their simultaneity does not even close up around the rim; that failure is the Sagnac effect, and it is why "the disc's space" is a quotient rather than a hypersurface.)

Check. Dimensions: \(\omega\rho/c\) dimensionless ✓; \(g_{0\phi'} = \omega\rho^2/c\) has the units of \(\rho^2\) divided by nothing, matching \(g_{\phi'\phi'}\), since \(\phi'\) and \(x^0\)'s metric slots must pair consistently ✓. Limit: \(\omega\to0\) gives \(\gamma\to1\), \(C = 2\pi R\), Euclid back ✓. Second limit: \(\rho\to c/\omega\) sends \(C\to\infty\) at finite \(R\), consistent with the light cylinder being unreachable by a co-rotating ruler ✓.

Another way

Without the \(\gamma_{ij}\) formula: in the lab frame the rim moves at \(v = \omega\rho\), so a co-rotating ruler of proper length \(\ell\) is seen as \(\ell/\gamma\). Laying rulers end to end around a lab circle of circumference \(2\pi\rho\) therefore takes \(2\pi\gamma\rho\) of them, and the riders count \(2\pi\gamma\rho\) of proper length. Radial rulers move perpendicular to their own length and are uncontracted, so \(R = \rho\). Same answer, less machinery — but it does not generalise, and it hides the fact that "the circumference the riders measure" required a definition of simultaneity.

P9 The GPS budget, and where it cancels medium

exercises: combining the two first-order rate corrections, a signed budget, solving for a cancellation, a number

A clock moving with speed \(v\) at Newtonian potential \(\Phi\) runs, relative to coordinate time in the Earth-centred inertial frame, at \(\mathrm d\tau/\mathrm dt = 1 + \Phi/c^2 - v^2/2c^2 + O(c^{-4})\).

  1. Say where each of the two correction terms comes from — which unit, which result.
  2. For a GPS satellite in a circular orbit of radius \(a = 26\,561\) km, compute the two terms separately in fractional rate and in microseconds per day, taking the ground clock to be at rest at \(r = R_\oplus\).
  3. Give the net, and the range error \(c\,\delta t\) that accumulates in one day if it is ignored.
  4. Redo the kinematic term with the ground clock moving at the equator with \(\omega_\oplus R_\oplus\). By how much does the net change, and does this explain why published figures vary between \(38.4\) and \(38.6\ \mu\mathrm{s/day}\)?
  5. At what orbital radius do the two effects cancel exactly, for a ground clock at rest? Give the altitude, and say what would be true of a clock in that orbit.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four numbers and one algebraic solve. Everything is first order and the two effects simply add; the only place to go wrong is a sign.

Rung 2

The satellite is higher (less negative \(\Phi\)), so its potential term makes it run fast; it is also faster, which makes it run slow. Compare rates by differencing: \(\Delta(\mathrm d\tau/\mathrm dt) = (\Phi_s - \Phi_g)/c^2 - (v_s^2 - v_g^2)/2c^2\). For a circular orbit \(v^2 = GM/a\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \((\Phi_s-\Phi_g)/c^2 = (GM_\oplus/c^2)(1/R_\oplus - 1/a)\). For part 5, set \(-GM/r + (-GM/2r) = -GM/R\) — that is, \(3/(2r) = 1/R\).

Worked solution

Idea. Two first-order corrections of opposite sign, differing by a factor of six, in a system that stops working if either is dropped. The arithmetic is short; the lesson is that the two halves of relativity are one subject.

1. \(-v^2/2c^2\) is unit 26's time dilation, \(\mathrm d\tau/\mathrm dt = \sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}\), expanded to first order. \(+\Phi/c^2\) is this unit's gravitational redshift, read as a statement about clock rates — the box on the rocket slide, with the reference clock at \(\Phi = 0\).

2. Potential. \(1/R_\oplus = 1.567855\times10^{-7}\), \(1/a = 3.764919\times10^{-8}\), difference \(1.191363\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}\); times \(GM_\oplus\) gives \(4.74858\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\); divided by \(c^2\), \[\frac{\Phi_s - \Phi_g}{c^2} = +5.2837\times10^{-10} \;\Rightarrow\; +5.2837\times10^{-10}\times86400 = +45.65\ \mu\mathrm{s/day}.\] Speed. \(v_s^2 = GM_\oplus/a = 1.50070\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\), so \(v_s = 3873.9\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and \(-v_s^2/2c^2 = -8.3488\times10^{-11} \Rightarrow -7.21\ \mu\mathrm{s/day}\).

3. Net \(+45.65 - 7.21 = +38.44\ \mu\mathrm{s/day}\). Ignored, the accumulated range error in one day is \(c\times3.844\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm s = 1.152\times10^{4}\ \mathrm m \approx 11.5\) km.

4. \(v_g = \omega_\oplus R_\oplus = 7.292115\times10^{-5}\times 6.378137\times10^{6} = 465.1\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), so \(+v_g^2/2c^2 = +1.203\times10^{-12} = +0.104\ \mu\mathrm{s/day}\): the ground clock is itself slowed, so the satellite gains a little more. The net becomes \(+38.54\ \mu\mathrm{s/day}\). That \(0.1\ \mu\mathrm{s/day}\) is exactly the scale of the spread in published figures, and the rest of the spread comes from whether the ground clock is placed on the equator or on the geoid (whose defining potential already folds in the centrifugal term) and whether the Earth's quadrupole \(J_2\) is included. "About \(38.5\ \mu\mathrm{s/day}\)" is the honest statement.

5. Setting the satellite's total equal to the ground clock's: \[-\frac{GM}{r} - \frac{GM}{2r} = -\frac{GM}{R_\oplus} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{3}{2r} = \frac1{R_\oplus} \;\Longrightarrow\; r = \tfrac32R_\oplus = 9567.2\ \mathrm{km},\] an altitude of \(\tfrac12R_\oplus = 3189.1\) km. A clock there keeps the same rate as one on the ground — not because relativity has switched off, but because two effects of opposite sign happen to be equal. Below that altitude the kinematic term wins and orbiting clocks run slow; the Space Station, at about \(410\) km, is one of them.

Check. Dimensions: \(GM/rc^2\) dimensionless ✓; \(c\,\delta t\) in metres ✓. Limit: \(a\to\infty\) gives \(+GM/R_\oplus c^2 = 6.95\times10^{-10}\) with no kinematic term, the Earth's own surface redshift ✓. A number: the GPS satellites are built with a fractional rate offset of \(-4.4647\times10^{-10}\), which is \(38.57\ \mu\mathrm{s/day}\) — the design figure, agreeing with part 4 ✓.

Another way

Part 5 without arithmetic: for a circular orbit the virial theorem (unit 08) gives \(\langle T\rangle = -\tfrac12\langle V\rangle\) per unit mass, i.e. \(v^2/2 = GM/2r\), so the total rate correction is \(-GM/r - GM/2r = -\tfrac32 GM/r\): the orbital clock behaves as if it sat at rest at \(\tfrac23 r\). Setting \(\tfrac23r = R_\oplus\) gives \(r = \tfrac32R_\oplus\) immediately, and explains the factor cleanly.

P10 Proof: a perpetual motion machine, unless clocks run slow medium

exercises: conservation of energy as a constraint on a new effect, Einstein's 1911 argument, a limit check

Suppose, for contradiction, that light suffers no gravitational frequency shift: a photon emitted at the bottom of a tower of height \(h\) arrives at the top with exactly the energy it left with. Assume only: the weak equivalence principle; conservation of energy; and unit 27's \(E = mc^2\), in the form that a body of rest mass \(m\) may be converted entirely into radiation of energy \(mc^2\) and back.

  1. Describe a cycle that extracts energy from nothing. Be explicit about each of the four steps and the energy bookkeeping.
  2. Compute the energy gained per cycle, to first order in \(gh/c^2\).
  3. Now require that the cycle break even, and deduce the exact ratio \(E_{\text{top}}/E_{\text{bottom}}\) and its first-order form.
  4. Compare with the deck's rocket derivation. What does this argument assume that the rocket argument does not, and what does it not need that the rocket argument does?
  5. Where does the weak equivalence principle enter, and what would go wrong if \(m_{\mathrm g}\ne m_{\mathrm i}\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A proof by contradiction ending in a formula. The cycle has four steps and returns the apparatus to its initial state; the quantity to track is energy, and the contradiction is that it increases.

Rung 2

Start with a mass \(m\) at the top. Drop it. Convert what arrives at the bottom into a photon. Send the photon up. Convert it back into a mass at the top. Compare the mass you end with against the one you started with.

Rung 3

First line: the mass arrives at the bottom with total energy \(mc^2 + mgh\), since it has fallen through \(h\). If the photon carrying that energy arrives at the top unshifted, the new mass is \(m' = m(1 + gh/c^2) \gt m\).

Worked solution

Idea. If a photon's energy were unchanged by climbing, the conversion between mass and radiation would let you ratchet mass upward for free. Requiring the ratchet to fail determines the redshift exactly — no fields, no metrics, no geometry, only bookkeeping. This is Einstein's argument of 1911.

1. The cycle. (i) A body of rest mass \(m\) sits at the top of the tower; its energy is \(mc^2\). (ii) Lower it to the bottom, extracting the work \(mgh\) and storing it. (Here the WEP is used: the work is \(m_{\mathrm g}gh\) and the body's inertia is \(m_{\mathrm i}\), and the argument needs them equal — see part 5.) At the bottom its energy is \(mc^2\), with \(mgh\) now in the store. (iii) At the bottom, convert the body and the stored work entirely into radiation of energy \(mc^2 + mgh\), and send it up the tower. (iv) At the top, by hypothesis the radiation still has energy \(mc^2 + mgh\); convert it back into a body at rest, of rest mass \(m' = m + mgh/c^2\). The apparatus is back where it began, and the mass at the top has grown.

2. The gain per cycle is \(\Delta E = (m'-m)c^2 = mgh\): exactly the work extracted in step (ii), now available again and for ever. Running the cycle \(N\) times yields \(Nmgh\) from nothing, which contradicts conservation of energy.

3. Break-even requires \(m' = m\), i.e. the radiation must arrive at the top with energy \(mc^2\) having left with \(mc^2 + mgh\): \[\frac{E_{\text{top}}}{E_{\text{bottom}}} = \frac{mc^2}{mc^2 + mgh} = \frac{1}{1 + gh/c^2} = 1 - \frac{gh}{c^2} + O\!\Big(\frac{g^2h^2}{c^4}\Big).\] Since \(E = h_{\mathrm P}\nu\) with \(h_{\mathrm P}\) Planck's constant, the same ratio holds for frequencies: \(\Delta\nu/\nu = -gh/c^2\) to first order, which is the deck's boxed result. \(\square\)

4. This argument assumes the mass–energy interconversion of unit 27 and global conservation of energy in a static field — neither of which the rocket derivation needs. It does not need any statement about Doppler shifts, inertial frames or propagation: no kinematics at all appears. The two derivations are genuinely independent, which is why Einstein gave both, and their agreement is the first evidence that the redshift is forced rather than modelled.

5. The WEP enters in step (ii): the work recovered by lowering the body is \(m_{\mathrm g}gh\), while the mass that is later converted to radiation is the rest mass \(m_{\mathrm i}\) (inertia is what \(E = mc^2\) refers to). If \(m_{\mathrm g} = (1+\eta)m_{\mathrm i}\), the cycle would break even only if \(E_{\text{top}}/E_{\text{bottom}} = 1/(1+(1+\eta)gh/c^2)\), so the redshift would be composition-dependent — different for a tower loaded with beryllium and one loaded with titanium. A composition-dependent redshift is exactly what a clock-comparison experiment does not see, and it is why a bound on \(\eta\) and a redshift measurement are testing related but distinct things.

Check. Dimensions: \(gh/c^2\) dimensionless ✓; \(mgh\) an energy ✓. Limit: \(h\to0\) gives ratio \(1\), no shift, and the cycle degenerates ✓. A number: for the Harvard tower the ratio is \(1/(1+2.455\times10^{-15})\), and the energy that could be ratcheted per kilogram per cycle would be \(mgh = 220.6\) J — small, but free, and repeatable, which is what makes the contradiction fatal rather than negligible ✓.

P11 Light bends, and by how much medium

exercises: integrating a transverse acceleration along a ray, a trigonometric substitution, a famous factor of two, two numbers

In the equivalence-principle picture, a light ray crossing a region of gravitational field acquires transverse velocity at the rate \(\mathrm dv_\perp/\mathrm dt = g_\perp\), and the deflection is \(\Delta\theta = \Delta v_\perp/c\) for small angles.

  1. A laboratory of width \(L = 10\) m sits on the Earth. By how much does a horizontal light beam drop in crossing it, and through what angle is it deflected? Express the angle in arcseconds.
  2. For a ray passing a point mass \(M\) at impact parameter \(b\), set up and evaluate \(\Delta\theta = c^{-2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} g_\perp\,\mathrm dx\), with \(g_\perp = GMb/(b^2+x^2)^{3/2}\). Show \(\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{EP}} = 2GM/c^2b\).
  3. Evaluate for a ray grazing the Sun's limb, in radians and in arcseconds.
  4. The measured value is twice yours. Say precisely which ingredient the equivalence principle could not supply, and why the argument of the deck's clock slide could not have supplied it.
  5. Would an equivalence-principle argument get the redshift wrong by a similar factor? Justify in one sentence.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two numbers, one integral and two sentences. The integral is a standard one; the sentences are what is being examined.

Rung 2

Part 1: the beam takes \(L/c\) to cross, so it drops \(\tfrac12g(L/c)^2\) and turns through \(g(L/c)/c = gL/c^2\). Part 2: convert \(\mathrm dt\) to \(\mathrm dx/c\) along the unperturbed straight ray — this is the standard Born approximation, and it is consistent because the deflection is \(10^{-6}\) rad.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\Delta\theta = \frac1{c^2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{GMb}{(b^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\mathrm dx\); substitute \(x = b\tan\psi\), which turns \((b^2+x^2)^{3/2}\) into \(b^3\sec^3\psi\) and \(\mathrm dx\) into \(b\sec^2\psi\,\mathrm d\psi\).

Worked solution

Idea. The elevator argument gives light a transverse acceleration; adding it up along the ray is a one-line integral. The factor of two at the end is the sharpest statement in the course of what the equivalence principle is and is not for.

1. Crossing time \(L/c = 10/2.998\times10^{8} = 3.336\times10^{-8}\) s. Drop \[\tfrac12 g\Big(\frac Lc\Big)^2 = \tfrac12(9.80665)(3.336\times10^{-8})^2 = 5.46\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm m ,\] about five femtometres, the scale of an atomic nucleus. Deflection angle \(gL/c^2 = 1.091\times10^{-15}\) rad \(= 2.25\times10^{-10}\) arcseconds. This is why terrestrial light-bending experiments do not exist.

2. Along the unperturbed ray, \(\mathrm dt = \mathrm dx/c\), so \[\Delta\theta = \frac{1}{c^2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{GMb\,\mathrm dx}{(b^2+x^2)^{3/2}} .\] Put \(x = b\tan\psi\), \(\mathrm dx = b\sec^2\psi\,\mathrm d\psi\), \((b^2+x^2)^{3/2} = b^3\sec^3\psi\). Then \[\Delta\theta = \frac{GM}{c^2}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\frac{b\cdot b\sec^2\psi}{b^3\sec^3\psi}\,\mathrm d\psi = \frac{GM}{bc^2}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos\psi\,\mathrm d\psi = \frac{2GM}{bc^2}.\]

3. At the Sun's limb, \(b = R_\odot = 6.957\times10^{8}\) m: \[\Delta\theta_{\mathrm{EP}} = \frac{2\times1.327124\times10^{20}} {6.957\times10^{8}\times8.987552\times10^{16}} = 4.2450\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{rad},\] which is \(4.2450\times10^{-6}\times206264.8 = 0.8756''\).

4. The missing ingredient is the spatial part of the metric, \(g_{ij}\). The equivalence principle fixes only \(g_{00} = -(1+2\Phi/c^2)\), because it is a statement about what a local freely falling frame sees, and the only thing a static observer can compare with a neighbour without leaving the neighbourhood is a clock rate. The deck's clock slide got \(g_{00}\) from the redshift and explicitly left \(g_{ij}\) undetermined; nothing about clocks can determine it. The full calculation (unit 37) finds that the two halves contribute equally, giving \(4GM/c^2b = 1.7512''\).

5. No: the redshift depends only on \(g_{00}\), which is exactly what the equivalence principle does supply, so the equivalence-principle answer is right — as P4 showed, right even beyond first order for the Rindler case.

Check. Dimensions: \(GM/bc^2\) is \(\mathrm{(m^3s^{-2})/(m\cdot m^2s^{-2})}\), a pure number, correctly read as radians ✓. Limit: \(b\to\infty\) gives \(\Delta\theta\to0\) ✓; \(M\to0\) likewise ✓. A number: the ratio of part 3 to part 1 is \(4.245\times10^{-6}/1.091\times10^{-15} = 3.9\times10^{9}\) — the Sun bends light four billion times harder than a ten-metre laboratory, which is why the measurement had to wait for an eclipse ✓.

P12 Designing an Eötvös experiment medium

exercises: turning a bound into an instrument specification, a torsion pendulum's stiffness, a number

A torsion balance carries two masses \(m = 10\) g of different composition at the ends of a beam, each a distance \(d = 2\) cm from the axis, hung on a fibre. Its free torsional period is \(T = 800\) s. The horizontal component of the effective field at the site, supplied by the Earth's rotation, is \(a_\perp = 1.69\ \mathrm{cm\,s^{-2}}\).

  1. Write the moment of inertia \(I\) of the two masses about the axis, and deduce the fibre's torsional stiffness \(\kappa\) from the period.
  2. If the two materials differ by an Eötvös parameter \(\eta\), give the differential acceleration each feels and the resulting torque on the beam.
  3. Compute the equilibrium twist angle for \(\eta = 10^{-13}\).
  4. Eöt-Wash reached \(\eta \sim 2\times10^{-13}\), and MICROSCOPE \(1.5\times10^{-15}\), a factor of over a hundred better. Name two reasons why going to orbit buys that, and one reason why the ground experiment is not obsolete.
  5. Why is the balance mounted on a slowly rotating turntable rather than held still?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three numbers and two short answers. The mechanics is unit 06's torsional oscillator: \(I\ddot\theta = -\kappa\theta + \tau\), with period \(2\pi\sqrt{I/\kappa}\) and equilibrium \(\theta = \tau/\kappa\).

Rung 2

The differential acceleration is \(\eta a_\perp\), and it acts in opposite senses on the two masses (one is "too heavy" and the other "too light" relative to the mean), so the two torques add: \(\tau = 2m(\eta a_\perp)d\).

Rung 3

First line: \(I = 2md^2 = 2(0.01)(0.02)^2\), and \(\kappa = I(2\pi/T)^2\). Keep everything in SI and expect an answer in nanoradians.

Worked solution

Idea. A bound on a dimensionless number becomes a bound on an angle, and the angle turns out to be measurable but tiny — which explains why the whole art of the experiment is in modulation, not in sensitivity.

1. \(I = 2md^2 = 2(0.01)(0.02)^2 = 8.00\times10^{-6}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2}\). From \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{I/\kappa}\), \[\kappa = I\Big(\frac{2\pi}{T}\Big)^2 = 8.00\times10^{-6}\times \Big(\frac{2\pi}{800}\Big)^2 = 4.935\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{N\,m\,rad^{-1}} .\]

2. Each mass feels \(\eta a_\perp\) relative to the mean, in opposite senses, so each contributes a force \(m\eta a_\perp\) at a lever arm \(d\) and the torques add: \(\tau = 2m\,\eta a_\perp\,d\).

3. For \(\eta = 10^{-13}\): \(\eta a_\perp = 10^{-13}\times1.69\times10^{-2} = 1.69\times10^{-15}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), so \[\tau = 2(0.01)(1.69\times10^{-15})(0.02) = 6.76\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{N\,m},\qquad \theta = \frac{\tau}{\kappa} = 1.37\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{rad}.\] About \(1.4\) nanoradians — a \(1.4\ \mu\mathrm m\) displacement of a spot on a screen a kilometre away, or, with an optical lever folded a few times, a readily measurable fringe shift.

4. Orbit buys, first, a much larger and steadier signal source: in free fall around the Earth the full \(8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) of the Earth's field is the driving acceleration, against \(1.69\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\) on the ground — nearly three orders of magnitude — because on the ground the vertical component is taken up by the suspension. Second, it removes the ground's noise: seismic tilt, thermal gradients, and above all the time-varying gravity gradients of nearby mass (people, groundwater, the experimenter's car). The ground experiment is not obsolete because it can be repeated, rebuilt and reconfigured — different material pairs, different source masses (including laboratory attractors, which test short-range deviations from the inverse-square law) — at a cost that is not a satellite's.

5. Because the signal is otherwise at zero frequency, where every drift lives: fibre creep, thermal expansion, slow tilt. Rotating the apparatus at a known slow rate moves the signal to a known nonzero frequency — twice the turntable rate, since the effect reverses when the beam reverses — where the noise is far quieter and a matched filter can pull the amplitude out of hours of data. It is the mechanical version of a lock-in amplifier, and it is what separates the modern generation of balances from Eötvös's.

Check. Dimensions: \(\kappa\) as \(I\omega^2\) is \(\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}} = \mathrm{N\,m}\) per radian ✓; \(\theta = \tau/\kappa\) is a pure number ✓. Limit: \(\eta\to0\) gives \(\theta\to0\), no signal ✓. Scaling: \(\theta\) is linear in \(\eta\), so MICROSCOPE's \(1.5\times10^{-15}\) on this instrument would be \(2\times10^{-11}\) rad — below what a ground balance can do, which is the quantitative reason the satellite was flown ✓.
