The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: the unit sphere \(S^2\) with two stereographic charts,
\(\mathbf x\) from the north pole and \(\tilde{\mathbf x}\) from the south, with
transition \((\tilde u,\tilde v) = (u,v)/(u^2+v^2)\). Test point
\(P = (\tfrac23,\tfrac13,\tfrac23)\), at \((2,1)\) in the first chart and
\((\tfrac25,\tfrac15)\) in the second. Round metric
\(g = 4(\mathrm{d}u^2+\mathrm{d}v^2)/(1+u^2+v^2)^2\), whose components at \(P\)
are \(\tfrac19\) in one chart and \(\tfrac{25}9\) in the other. The rotation
field \(R_z = -v\,\partial_u + u\,\partial_v\) has \(\lVert R_z\rVert^2 = \tfrac59\)
at \(P\) computed three ways, and satisfies \(\mathcal L_{R_z}g = 0\).

* Coordinates stop being data

- topological manifold :: A Hausdorff, second countable topological space in
  which every point has a neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open subset of
  \(\mathbb R^n\). The \(n\) is the /dimension/, well defined by invariance of
  domain (cited) or, in the smooth case, by linear algebra.
  /Slide: What a manifold is./
- chart :: A pair \((U,\mathbf x)\) with \(U\subseteq M\) open and \(\mathbf x\)
  a homeomorphism onto an open subset of \(\mathbb R^n\). A chart is a page of an
  atlas, never data: nothing physical may depend on which one is open.
- coordinates :: The component functions \(\mathbf x = (x^1,\dots,x^n)\) of a
  chart. Each \(x^i\) is a real-valued function on \(U\), so \(\mathrm{d}x^i\)
  and \(\partial_i\) both make sense later.
- Hausdorff :: Distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoods. Not automatic for a
  locally Euclidean space: the line with two origins fails it, and unit 01's
  global uniqueness of ODE solutions fails with it.
- second countable :: The topology has a countable base. With Hausdorff it buys
  paracompactness, hence partitions of unity glued out of bump functions, hence
  the existence of metrics and connections.
- locally Euclidean :: Every point has a chart. The weakest of the three
  conditions, and the only one that is about shape rather than hygiene.
- transition map :: \(\tilde{\mathbf x}\circ\mathbf x^{-1}\) on
  \(\mathbf x(U\cap V)\): a map between open subsets of \(\mathbb R^n\), which is
  the only kind of map whose smoothness is known before a manifold is defined.
  For the running example it is inversion in the unit circle.
  /Slide: Atlases, and what smooth can mean with no ambient space./
- smoothly compatible :: Of two charts: their transition map and its inverse are
  \(C^\infty\). Exactly the condition making "smooth" chart-independent.
- atlas :: A family of pairwise smoothly compatible charts covering \(M\).
- smooth structure :: A maximal atlas. Every atlas sits in exactly one, so two
  charts are enough to specify a smooth manifold and nobody writes a maximal
  atlas down.
- smooth (of a map of manifolds) :: \(F: M\to N\) whose coordinate
  representations are \(C^\infty\). Well defined because a change of chart
  conjugates the representation by transition maps.
  /Slide: Smooth maps, and the one theorem that makes them well defined./
- coordinate representation :: \(\hat F = \mathbf y\circ F\circ\mathbf x^{-1}\),
  the map between open subsets of Euclidean spaces that a chart pair turns \(F\)
  into.
- diffeomorphism :: A smooth bijection with smooth inverse. "The same manifold"
  always means "diffeomorphic"; in general relativity it also means "the same
  physical situation", which is the content of the hole argument.
- cotangent bundle :: \(T^*Q = \bigsqcup_q T_q^*Q\), a \(2n\)-manifold. Unit 14's
  phase space: momenta are covectors, which is why \(p_i\) carries a lower index
  and transforms oppositely to \(\dot q^i\).
  /Slide: Every configuration space you have used was one./

* Tangent vectors

- tangent vector :: An element of \(T_pM\), defined equivalently as an
  equivalence class of curves through \(p\) (A), a derivation of germs at \(p\)
  (B), or a chart-indexed family of \(n\)-tuples obeying the Jacobian
  transformation law (C). The three are canonically isomorphic.
  /Slide: The three definitions describe one space./
- germ :: An equivalence class of smooth functions defined near \(p\), two being
  identified when they agree on some neighbourhood of \(p\). They form an
  algebra \(C^\infty_p\). /Slide: Definition B: a derivation of germs./
- derivation :: A linear \(D: C^\infty_p\to\mathbb R\) with
  \(D(fg) = f(p)Dg + g(p)Df\). It kills constants, and by Hadamard's lemma every
  one is \(\xi^i\partial_i|_p\): this is why \(\dim T_pM = n\).
  /Slide: Definition B: a derivation of germs./
- Hadamard's lemma :: \(\hat f(s) = \hat f(a) + (s^i-a^i)h_i(s)\) with \(h_i\)
  smooth and \(h_i(a) = \partial_i\hat f(a)\), proved by the fundamental theorem
  of calculus along the segment. The single place the theorem spends
  \(C^\infty\), and the reason the derivation definition fails for \(C^k\).
  /Slide: The three definitions describe one space./
- coordinate basis :: \(\{\partial_i|_p\}\), the derivations
  \(f\mapsto\partial(f\circ\mathbf x^{-1})/\partial s^i\). A basis of \(T_pM\),
  and it depends on all \(n\) coordinates, not only on \(x^i\).
  /Slide: The coordinate basis, and the count that follows./
- tangent bundle :: \(TM = \bigsqcup_pT_pM\), a smooth \(2n\)-manifold with
  charts \((x^i, X^i)\) and transitions \((s,\xi)\mapsto(\tau(s), D\tau(s)\xi)\),
  linear in the fibre — a vector bundle.
- section :: A smooth map \(M\to E\) into a bundle over \(M\) that is the
  identity after projection. A vector field is a section of \(TM\), a tensor
  field a section of \(T^p_qM\).
- differential (pushforward) :: \(\mathrm{d}F_p: T_pM\to T_{F(p)}N\),
  \((\mathrm{d}F_pX)(f) = X(f\circ F)\). Linear; its matrix in charts is the
  Jacobian; \(\mathrm{d}(G\circ F) = \mathrm{d}G\circ\mathrm{d}F\) is the chain
  rule in its final form. /Slide: The differential of a smooth map./

* Covectors and tensor fields

- cotangent space :: \(T^*_pM = (T_pM)^*\), unit 16's dual space built on the
  tangent space. Its elements are covectors.
  /Slide: The cotangent space, and \(\mathrm{d}f\)./
- differential of a function :: \(\mathrm{d}f_p(X) = X(f)\), an element of
  \(T^*_pM\). Defined with no metric and no choice — unlike the gradient, which
  is \(g^{ij}\partial_jf\) and needs one. On a bare manifold there is no
  gradient at all.
- dual basis :: \(\{\mathrm{d}x^i\}\), characterised by
  \(\mathrm{d}x^i(\partial_j) = \delta^i_j\); unit 16's \(\varepsilon^j\) at each
  point. Then \(\mathrm{d}f = \partial_i\hat f\,\mathrm{d}x^i\).
- tensor field :: An assignment \(m\mapsto T_m\) of a \((p,q)\)-tensor on
  \(T_mM\) — a multilinear map, exactly unit 16's definition — with components
  smooth in every chart. Equivalently a smooth section of \(T^p_qM\).
  /Slide: Tensor fields: unit 16, one point at a time./
- transformation law (on a manifold) :: \(\tilde T^{a\dots}{}_{\dots b} =
  A^a{}_i\cdots S^j{}_b\cdots T^{i\dots}{}_{\dots j}\) with
  \(A^a{}_i = \partial\tilde x^a/\partial x^i\) and \(S = A^{-1}\). A theorem,
  proved by multilinearity, exactly as in unit 16 — the one change being that
  \(A\) and \(S\) now vary from point to point.
  /Slide: "Transforms like a tensor" is a theorem, again./
- equivariant :: What a rule assigning arrays to charts must be to come from a
  tensor field: it must commute with every change of chart. The rule
  "\(T_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\) in every chart" is not, and the sphere convicts it at
  \(P\) — the rule says 1, the law says 25.
  /Slide: Components are not the tensor./
- Riemannian metric :: A \((0,2)\)-tensor field, symmetric and positive definite
  at every point. Lengths \(\lVert X\rVert^2 = g_{ij}X^iX^j\), and the
  isomorphism \(X\mapsto g(X,\cdot)\) that raises and lowers indices. Every
  second countable smooth manifold has one.
  /Slide: A metric is a bilinear form on each tangent space./
- Lorentzian metric :: The same with signature \((-,+,+,+)\) at every point.
  Unlike Riemannian metrics these need not exist: \(S^2\) admits none, because a
  line field on a compact surface forces \(\chi = 0\) and \(\chi(S^2) = 2\).
- conformally flat :: Of a metric written as \(\Omega^2\delta_{ij}\) in some
  chart. The round metric is, in both stereographic charts, with
  \(\Omega = 2/(1+r^2)\); every surface metric is locally.
  /Slide: The round metric, in both charts at once./

* Vector fields, flows and the bracket

- vector field :: A smooth section of \(TM\); equivalently a derivation of
  \(C^\infty(M)\), acting as \(f\mapsto X^i\partial_if\).
  /Slide: A vector field is a flow./
- integral curve :: A curve with \(\dot\gamma(t) = X_{\gamma(t)}\). In a chart it
  is the autonomous system \(\dot s^i = X^i(s)\) of unit 02, with unit 01's
  Picard–Lindelöf giving existence and uniqueness.
- flow :: \(\varphi^X_t(m) = \gamma_m(t)\), satisfying
  \(\varphi_{t+s} = \varphi_t\circ\varphi_s\) and \(\varphi_0 = \mathrm{id}\)
  where defined: a local one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms.
- complete :: Of a vector field whose integral curves all run for all \(t\).
  Every field on a compact manifold is complete; \(x^2\partial_x\) on
  \(\mathbb R\) is not, and incompleteness of timelike geodesics is how a
  spacetime singularity is defined.
- Lie bracket :: \([X,Y]f = X(Yf) - Y(Xf)\), a vector field with components
  \([X,Y]^i = X^j\partial_jY^i - Y^j\partial_jX^i\); the second-derivative terms
  cancel by Clairaut's theorem. Not \(C^\infty\)-bilinear:
  \([X,fY] = f[X,Y] + (Xf)Y\), so it is a genuine derivative and not a tensor.
  /Slide: The Lie bracket./
- Lie algebra :: A vector space with a bilinear antisymmetric bracket satisfying
  the Jacobi identity. The vector fields on \(M\) form one, infinite
  dimensional; the rotation fields on \(S^2\) span a three-dimensional
  subalgebra.
- commutator square :: \(\varphi^Y_{-s}\varphi^X_{-t}\varphi^Y_s\varphi^X_t(m)
  = m + ts[X,Y]_m + O(3)\): go along \(X\), then \(Y\), then back along each, and
  the gap is the bracket. Checked on the sphere at \(t=s=0.02\), where the
  measured gap is \((7.8804, 8.1238)\times10^{-4}\) against the predicted
  \((8,8)\times10^{-4}\).
  /Slide: The bracket measures the failure of flows to commute./
- anti-homomorphism (of the rotation generators) :: The vector fields generating
  a left group action satisfy \([R_A,R_B] = -R_{[A,B]}\). So unit 16's matrices
  with \([J_x,J_y]=+J_z\) correspond to fields with \([R_x,R_y]=-R_z\), and
  \(J_k\mapsto -R_k\) is the isomorphism onto \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\).
  /Slide: \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\), realised by flows./

* The Lie derivative and Killing fields

- pullback :: \((F^*T)_m(X_1,\dots) = T_{F(m)}(\mathrm{d}F_mX_1,\dots)\) for a
  \((0,q)\)-tensor and a diffeomorphism \(F\); upper slots use \((F^{-1})^*\).
  The only canonical way to move a tensor between two tangent spaces.
  /Slide: Differentiating a tensor field along a flow./
- Lie derivative :: \(\mathcal L_XT = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big|_0
  (\varphi^X_t)^*T\). Needs no connection and no metric — only a flow. Its value
  at \(m\) depends on \(X\) near \(m\), so there is no \(\mathcal L_vT\) for a
  single vector, which is the gap unit 32's connection fills.
- \(\mathcal L_XY = [X,Y]\) :: The Lie derivative of a vector field is the
  bracket, proved by expanding
  \((\varphi_t^*Y)_m = \mathrm{d}\varphi_{-t}\,Y_{\varphi_t(m)}\) to first order.
  /Slide: The formulas, and the theorem behind the first one./
- Lie derivative in components :: Transport term \(X^k\partial_k\), then one
  correction per slot: \(+T_{k\dots}\partial_{\text{slot}}X^k\) for a lower
  index, \(-T^{k\dots}\partial_kX^{\text{slot}}\) for an upper one. So
  \((\mathcal L_XT)_{ij} = X^k\partial_kT_{ij} + T_{kj}\partial_iX^k +
  T_{ik}\partial_jX^k\).
- why \(\mathcal L_XT\) is tensorial :: The transport term and the corrections
  each transform with second derivatives of the transition map, and those cancel
  between them. \(\partial_kT_{ij}\) alone does not, and no term of the formula
  is a tensor by itself.
  /Slide: Why \(\mathcal L_XT\) is a tensor and \(\partial_kT_{ij}\) is not./
- Killing vector field :: \(X\) with \(\mathcal L_Xg = 0\), that is
  \(X^k\partial_kg_{ij} + g_{kj}\partial_iX^k + g_{ik}\partial_jX^k = 0\).
  Equivalently: every \(\varphi^X_t\) is an isometry. A continuous symmetry of a
  geometry, stated with no coordinates and no connection.
  /Slide: Killing vector fields./
- isometry :: A diffeomorphism with \(F^*g = g\). Killing fields are the
  infinitesimal isometries, and they form a Lie algebra of dimension at most
  \(n(n+1)/2\) — three for \(S^2\), six for a maximally symmetric three-space,
  ten for a maximally symmetric spacetime.
- conformal Killing :: \(\mathcal L_Xg = \lambda g\) for some function
  \(\lambda\): the flow preserves angles but not lengths. The dilation
  \(D = u\partial_u + v\partial_v\) on the round sphere has
  \(\lambda = 2(1-r^2)/(1+r^2)\), which is \(-\tfrac43\) at \(P\) and zero only
  on the equator.
  /Slide: The rotation field is Killing; the dilation is not./

* Submanifolds, and what covariance does not say

- immersion :: A smooth map whose differential is injective at every point.
  /Slide: Submanifolds, immersions, embeddings./
- submersion :: A smooth map whose differential is surjective at every point.
- embedding :: An immersion that is also a homeomorphism onto its image with the
  subspace topology. The figure-eight \(t\mapsto(\sin2t,\sin t)\) is an
  injective immersion and not an embedding.
- embedded submanifold :: The image of an embedding, itself a manifold whose
  inclusion is smooth. \(S^2\) and \(SO(3)\) are both, by the regular value
  theorem.
- regular value :: A \(c\) with \(\mathrm{d}F\) surjective at every point of
  \(F^{-1}(c)\); then \(F^{-1}(c)\) is an embedded submanifold of dimension
  \(\dim N - k\). Proved from unit 04's implicit function theorem. /Cited./
- distribution :: A smooth assignment of a \(k\)-dimensional subspace
  \(\mathcal D_m\subseteq T_mM\) to each point.
  /Slide: Frobenius: when a bracket closes, a foliation exists./
- involutive :: Of a distribution closed under the Lie bracket of its sections.
- integrable :: Of a distribution whose subspaces are the tangent spaces of a
  family of submanifolds — its leaves.
- Frobenius' theorem :: Involutive if and only if integrable, with charts in
  which the leaves are coordinate slices. /Cited/: Lee, /Introduction to Smooth
  Manifolds/, chapter 19. The failure case is
  \(X = \partial_x,\ Y = \partial_y + x\partial_z\) on \(\mathbb R^3\), whose
  bracket \(\partial_z\) escapes the span — the parallel-parking distribution,
  and the meaning of a non-holonomic constraint.
- partition of unity :: Smooth \(\psi_\alpha\in[0,1]\) subordinate to an open
  cover, locally finite, summing to 1. /Cited/; needs second countability and
  the bump function \(\mathrm e^{-1/t}\). Buys the existence of metrics and
  connections. /Slide: Three results used, not proved./
- Whitney embedding theorem :: Every smooth \(n\)-manifold embeds in
  \(\mathbb R^{2n}\). /Cited/: Whitney 1936, 1944. It says nothing exotic is
  being smuggled in by the abstract definition — and that the embedding is never
  canonical, so it must not be used.
- hairy ball theorem :: Every smooth vector field on \(S^{2k}\), \(k\ge1\),
  vanishes somewhere. /Cited/: the Poincaré–Hopf theorem, with indices summing to
  \(\chi(M)\) and \(\chi(S^2) = 2\). \(R_z\)'s two zeros, one at each pole, each
  of index \(+1\), realise the minimum.
- general covariance :: "The laws take the same form in all coordinate systems".
  Kretschmann's objection of 1917: any theory can be rewritten that way —
  Newtonian gravity becomes Newton–Cartan theory — so this alone has no physical
  content.
  /Slide: General covariance says less than it sounds like./
- diffeomorphism invariance :: If \((M,g,\Phi)\) solves the field equations then
  so does \((M,F^*g,F^*\Phi)\), and the two are the /same/ physical situation.
  Points of \(M\) have no identity independent of the fields.
- hole argument :: Einstein's 1913 objection to his own programme: a
  diffeomorphism that is the identity outside a compact region produces a second
  solution agreeing with the first outside it, apparently destroying
  determinism. Resolved by diffeomorphism invariance — the two are one
  spacetime described twice — but it cost two years.
- absolute object :: A field that is the same, up to diffeomorphism, in every
  solution. Minkowski's \(\eta\) is one; general relativity's \(g\) is not, which
  is the honest version of "background independence". Anderson 1967, with known
  difficulties.
