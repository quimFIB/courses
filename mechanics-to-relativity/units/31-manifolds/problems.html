Manifolds: doing all of it without coordinates

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You construct an atlas for a given manifold and verify the transition maps smooth; you define a tensor field in one chart and transform it to another, verifying agreement on the overlap; you compute Lie brackets and Lie derivatives in coordinates; and you determine whether a given vector field is Killing for a given metric.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Thirteen problems, about six hours. Conventions as in the deck: indices \(i,j,k\) label a chart on an \(n\)-manifold, repeated indices are summed, \(\partial_i = \partial/\partial x^i\), and \(A^a{}_i = \partial\tilde x^a/\partial x^i\) with \(S = A^{-1}\), so \(S^i{}_a = \partial x^i/\partial\tilde x^a\).

The sphere throughout is the unit \(S^2\subset\mathbb R^3\) with the two stereographic charts of the deck: \(\mathbf x = \big(\tfrac{x}{1-z},\tfrac{y}{1-z}\big)\) on \(S^2\setminus\{N\}\) and \(\tilde{\mathbf x} = \big(\tfrac{x}{1+z},\tfrac{y}{1+z}\big)\) on \(S^2\setminus\{S\}\), with transition \((\tilde u,\tilde v) = (u,v)/(u^2+v^2)\), inverse \(\mathbf x^{-1}(u,v) = (2u,2v,u^2+v^2-1)/(u^2+v^2+1)\), and round metric \(g = 4(\mathrm{d}u^2+\mathrm{d}v^2)/(1+u^2+v^2)^2\). The test point is \(P = \big(\tfrac23,\tfrac13,\tfrac23\big)\), at \((2,1)\) and \(\big(\tfrac25,\tfrac15\big)\) in the two charts, where \(S = \begin{pmatrix}-3&-4\\-4&3\end{pmatrix}\).

Charts, atlases and smooth structures

P1 Two projections, one sphere routine

exercises: deriving a transition map, its Jacobian, smooth compatibility

  1. Derive \(\mathbf x^{-1}\) from \(\mathbf x\), and verify that the result lies on \(S^2\).
  2. Derive the transition map \(\tilde{\mathbf x}\circ\mathbf x^{-1}\) and show it is its own inverse.
  3. Say precisely on which set it is defined, and why it is smooth there but would not be if that set were enlarged by one point.
  4. Compute the Jacobian \(A^a{}_i = \partial\tilde x^a/\partial x^i\) in general, and evaluate it and its determinant at \((u,v) = (3,4)\). Which point of \(S^2\) is that, and what are its coordinates in the second chart?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 end in formulas; part 3 in a one-sentence topological statement; part 4 in a \(2\times2\) matrix of numbers, a single number, and a point of \(\mathbb R^3\). Nothing needs more than the definition of the two projections and the equation of the sphere.

Rung 2

The whole of part 2 is the single identity \(u^2+v^2 = (1+z)/(1-z)\), which comes from \(x^2+y^2 = 1-z^2\) and the difference of two squares. For part 1, invert that identity to get \(z\) in terms of \(r^2 = u^2+v^2\), then read off \(1-z\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\ \displaystyle u^2+v^2 = \frac{x^2+y^2}{(1-z)^2} = \frac{1-z^2}{(1-z)^2} = \frac{1+z}{1-z}\). For part 4, differentiate \(\tilde u = u/r^2\) using \(\partial_u r^2 = 2u\), and remember that a transition map's Jacobian is never singular.

Worked solution

Idea. One identity does all of it. On the sphere \(x^2+y^2 = 1-z^2 = (1-z)(1+z)\), so dividing by \((1-z)^2\) gives \(r^2 = (1+z)/(1-z)\), which converts every \(z\) into an \(r^2\) and back.

1. The inverse. From \(r^2(1-z) = 1+z\) we get \(z = \dfrac{r^2-1}{r^2+1}\), hence \(1-z = \dfrac{2}{1+r^2}\) and

\[ x = u(1-z) = \frac{2u}{1+r^2},\qquad y = \frac{2v}{1+r^2},\qquad z = \frac{r^2-1}{r^2+1}. \]

On the sphere: \(4u^2+4v^2+(r^2-1)^2 = 4r^2 + r^4 - 2r^2 + 1 = (r^2+1)^2\), so the sum of squares is 1. ✓

2. The transition.

\[ \tilde u = \frac{x}{1+z} = \frac{x}{1-z}\cdot\frac{1-z}{1+z} = \frac{u}{r^2},\qquad \tilde v = \frac{v}{r^2}. \]

Its own inverse: if \(\tilde r^2 = \tilde u^2+\tilde v^2 = r^2/r^4 = 1/r^2\), then \(\tilde u/\tilde r^2 = (u/r^2)\cdot r^2 = u\). ✓

3. Domain. The overlap is \(U_N\cap U_S = S^2\setminus\{N,S\}\), whose image under \(\mathbf x\) is \(\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\) — the south pole is the origin of the first chart and the north pole is missing from it altogether. Inversion is a quotient by \(r^2\), smooth wherever \(r^2\ne0\); adding the origin would put a zero in the denominator, and no continuous extension exists, since \(|\tilde{\mathbf x}|\to\infty\) there.

4. The Jacobian. \(\partial_u(u/r^2) = 1/r^2 - 2u^2/r^4 = (v^2-u^2)/r^4\) and \(\partial_v(u/r^2) = -2uv/r^4\); the second row swaps \(u\) and \(v\):

\[ A = \frac{1}{r^4}\begin{pmatrix} v^2-u^2 & -2uv \\ -2uv & u^2-v^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det A = \frac{-(u^2-v^2)^2 - 4u^2v^2}{r^8} = -\frac{1}{r^4}. \]

At \((3,4)\): \(r^2 = 25\), \(r^4 = 625\), \(v^2-u^2 = 7\), \(-2uv = -24\), so

\[ A\big|_{(3,4)} = \frac{1}{625}\begin{pmatrix}7 & -24\\ -24 & -7\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det = -\frac{1}{625}. \]

The point is \(\mathbf x^{-1}(3,4) = (6,8,24)/26 = \big(\tfrac3{13},\tfrac4{13},\tfrac{12}{13}\big)\), and its second-chart coordinates are \((3,4)/25 = \big(\tfrac3{25},\tfrac4{25}\big)\).

Check. Dimensions: everything here is dimensionless, the sphere having unit radius; if the radius were \(R\) then \(u\) would carry a length and \(\tilde u\) would be \(R^2/u\), so the transition is \(\tilde{\mathbf u} = R^2\mathbf u/r^2\) and \(\det A = -R^4/r^4\), still dimensionless as a Jacobian of coordinates with the same dimension must be. Special case: on the equator \(z=0\), \(r=1\) and the transition is the identity — both projections agree there, as the picture demands. Number: \(\big(\tfrac3{13}\big)^2+\big(\tfrac4{13}\big)^2+\big(\tfrac{12}{13}\big)^2 = (9+16+144)/169 = 1\). ✓

Another way

Complex notation collapses part 2. Write \(w = u+\mathrm iv\); then \(\tilde w = \tilde u + \mathrm i\tilde v = (u+\mathrm iv)/|w|^2 = w/(w\bar w) = 1/\bar w\), and \(1/\overline{(1/\bar w)} = w\) immediately. The map is anti-holomorphic, which is why \(\det A\lt0\); the reflected chart \((x,-y)/(1+z)\) gives \(\tilde w = 1/w\) and the Riemann sphere of unit 20.

P2 The torus is a configuration space medium

exercises: building an atlas, products of manifolds, a kinetic energy read as a metric

The plane double pendulum: a bob of mass \(m_1\) on a rod of length \(l_1\) from a fixed pivot, and a bob of mass \(m_2\) on a rod of length \(l_2\) hinged at the first bob. Angles \(\theta_1,\theta_2\) from the downward vertical.

  1. Give an atlas for \(S^1\) with two charts and smooth transition maps, then an atlas for \(T^2 = S^1\times S^1\) with four charts. Verify one transition map of the torus atlas explicitly.
  2. Show from the definition that a product of smooth manifolds is a smooth manifold of the sum dimension.
  3. Unit 12's kinetic energy is \(T = \tfrac12(m_1+m_2)l_1^2\dot\theta_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2l_2^2\dot\theta_2^2 + m_2l_1l_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\). Exhibit the \((0,2)\)-tensor field \(g\) on \(T^2\) with \(T = \tfrac12 g_{ij}\dot\theta^i\dot\theta^j\), show it is a Riemannian metric, and write its components in a second chart of your atlas.
  4. Evaluate \(g\) and \(\det g\) for \(m_1 = 0.5\) kg, \(m_2 = 0.3\) kg, \(l_1 = 0.4\) m, \(l_2 = 0.3\) m, \(\theta_1-\theta_2 = \pi/3\), and give \(T\) for \(\dot\theta_1 = 2\), \(\dot\theta_2 = -3\) rad s\(^{-1}\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 wants maps, with domains stated; part 2 a short proof; part 3 a symmetric \(2\times2\) array of functions plus a positivity argument; part 4 three numbers with units. The metric's components in a second chart are almost the same array — think about why.

Rung 2

For \(S^1\) do not use "the angle" as a single chart: it is not a homeomorphism onto an open interval, because the circle is compact. Use two overlapping arcs, each missing one point. For part 3, a quadratic form is a metric iff it is symmetric and positive definite, and Sylvester's criterion — \(g_{11}\gt0\) and \(\det g\gt0\) — is the two-dimensional test.

Rung 3

First line: \(U_1 = S^1\setminus\{(1,0)\}\) with \(\vartheta_1: U_1\to(0,2\pi)\) the angle measured from \((1,0)\) anticlockwise, and \(U_2 = S^1\setminus\{(-1,0)\}\) with \(\vartheta_2: U_2\to(-\pi,\pi)\). For part 4, \(\det g = m_2l_1^2l_2^2(m_1+m_2\sin^2\Delta)\) — derive that and then substitute.

Worked solution

Idea. A kinetic energy quadratic in the velocities is a metric on the configuration space, because velocities are tangent vectors and \(T\) is a symmetric bilinear form on them. Once that is seen, the only work is the atlas, and the atlas is the product of two circles'.

1. The atlas. Take \(U_1 = S^1\setminus\{(1,0)\}\) with \(\vartheta_1\in(0,2\pi)\) and \(U_2 = S^1\setminus\{(-1,0)\}\) with \(\vartheta_2\in(-\pi,\pi)\), both measuring the anticlockwise angle from \((1,0)\). Their union is \(S^1\) and their overlap is the two open half-circles. On the upper one \(\vartheta_2 = \vartheta_1\); on the lower one \(\vartheta_2 = \vartheta_1 - 2\pi\). Both are translations, hence \(C^\infty\) with \(C^\infty\) inverses, so this is a smooth atlas. (The transition is not one formula — it is locally constant on a disconnected overlap, which is perfectly allowed.)

For \(T^2\) take the four products \(U_\alpha\times U_\beta\) with charts \((\vartheta_\alpha,\vartheta_\beta)\), \(\alpha,\beta\in\{1,2\}\). A sample transition, from \((U_1\times U_1)\) to \((U_2\times U_1)\) on the part of the overlap where \(\theta_1\) is in the lower half-circle: \((\theta_1,\theta_2)\mapsto(\theta_1-2\pi,\ \theta_2)\), a translation of \(\mathbb R^2\). ✓

2. Products. Let \(M^m, N^n\) have atlases \(\{(U,\mathbf x)\}\), \(\{(V,\mathbf y)\}\). Give \(M\times N\) the product topology, which is Hausdorff and second countable because both factors are. The sets \(U\times V\) are open and cover, and \(\mathbf x\times\mathbf y: U\times V\to\mathbf x(U)\times\mathbf y(V)\subseteq\mathbb R^{m+n}\) is a homeomorphism onto an open set. Two such charts have transition \((\tilde{\mathbf x}\circ\mathbf x^{-1})\times(\tilde{\mathbf y}\circ\mathbf y^{-1})\), smooth because each factor is and smoothness of a map into a product is componentwise. Dimension \(m+n\). \(\square\)

3. The metric. Polarise: \(T = \tfrac12g_{ij}\dot\theta^i\dot\theta^j\) with

\[ g = \begin{pmatrix} (m_1+m_2)l_1^2 & m_2l_1l_2\cos\Delta \\ m_2l_1l_2\cos\Delta & m_2l_2^2\end{pmatrix},\qquad \Delta = \theta_1-\theta_2. \]

Symmetric by construction. Positive definite by Sylvester: \(g_{11} = (m_1+m_2)l_1^2\gt0\), and

\[ \det g = (m_1+m_2)m_2l_1^2l_2^2 - m_2^2l_1^2l_2^2\cos^2\Delta = m_2l_1^2l_2^2\big(m_1+m_2\sin^2\Delta\big) \gt 0 \]

for every \(\Delta\), since \(m_1\gt0\). It is a tensor field rather than an array because the components depend on position only through \(\cos\Delta\), and \(\cos\Delta\) is a well-defined function on \(T^2\) — unchanged by \(\theta_i\mapsto\theta_i+2\pi\), which \(\Delta\) itself is not. In a second chart of the atlas the coordinates differ from the first by a translation, so \(A = \mathbb 1\) and the components are the same functions of \(\Delta\); that is the agreement on the overlap, and it is why this metric is globally defined on the torus.

4. Numbers. \(\cos(\pi/3) = \tfrac12\), \(\sin^2(\pi/3) = \tfrac34\):

\[ g_{11} = 0.8\times0.16 = 0.128,\quad g_{12} = 0.3\times0.4\times0.3\times0.5 = 0.018,\quad g_{22} = 0.3\times0.09 = 0.027 \]

all in kg m\(^2\), and \(\det g = 0.128\times0.027 - 0.018^2 = 0.003456 - 0.000324 = 0.003132\ \mathrm{kg^2m^4}\), which the formula confirms: \(0.3\times0.16\times0.09\times(0.5+0.3\times0.75) = 0.00432\times0.725 = 0.003132\). ✓ Then

\[ T = \tfrac12\big(0.128\times4 + 2\times0.018\times2\times(-3) + 0.027\times9\big) = \tfrac12(0.512-0.216+0.243) = 0.2695\ \mathrm J. \]

Check. Dimensions: \(g_{ij}\) is kg m\(^2\) and \(\dot\theta^i\) is s\(^{-1}\), so \(g_{ij}\dot\theta^i\dot\theta^j\) is kg m\(^2\) s\(^{-2}\) = J. ✓ Limit: as \(m_2\to0\) the metric degenerates to \(\mathrm{diag}(m_1l_1^2,0)\) with \(\det g\to0\) — the second bob is massless, its angle costs no energy, and the configuration space stops being metrically two-dimensional, which is exactly the degenerate Legendre transform unit 14 warned about. Special case \(\Delta = \pi/2\): \(g_{12} = 0\), the two angles decouple instantaneously, and \(\det g\) is largest, \(=m_2l_1^2l_2^2(m_1+m_2)\).

P3 Proof: one chart is never enough hard

exercises: compactness and connectedness arguments, what a chart is, minimal atlases

  1. Prove: a compact \(n\)-manifold with \(n\ge1\) has no chart whose domain is the whole manifold.
  2. Deduce that \(S^1\), \(S^2\), \(T^2\) and \(SO(3)\) each need at least two charts, stating for each one why it is compact.
  3. Give an atlas for \(S^1\) with exactly two charts, and prove that no atlas for \(S^1\) has one. Then show that the non-compact manifold \(\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\) is covered by a single chart, so compactness, not "having a hole", is what forces several.
  4. Where exactly does the proof of part 1 use \(n\ge1\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is a proof, three lines long, using only facts about \(\mathbb R^n\) from metric-space topology: continuous images of compact sets, closed and bounded subsets, and connectedness. No algebraic topology is needed or allowed.

Rung 2

A chart is a homeomorphism onto an open subset of \(\mathbb R^n\). If its domain is all of \(M\), the image is both open and something else — what does compactness of \(M\) give you about the image? Then use that \(\mathbb R^n\) is connected.

Rung 3

First line: suppose \(\mathbf x: M\to W\subseteq\mathbb R^n\) is a homeomorphism with \(W\) open. Then \(W = \mathbf x(M)\) is compact, hence closed in \(\mathbb R^n\). For part 3's last piece, try polar-like coordinates: the map \((r,\theta)\mapsto(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)\) is not injective on all of \(\mathbb R^2\), but \(\log\) of a complex number on a slit plane is — or find a diffeomorphism \(\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\to\) an open subset of \(\mathbb R^2\) directly.

Worked solution

Idea. A global chart would make \(M\) homeomorphic to an open subset of \(\mathbb R^n\). Compactness then forces that subset to be closed as well, and the only non-empty clopen subset of the connected space \(\mathbb R^n\) is \(\mathbb R^n\) itself, which is not compact.

1. The proof. Suppose \((M,\mathbf x)\) is a chart with domain \(M\), so \(\mathbf x: M\to W\) is a homeomorphism onto an open \(W\subseteq\mathbb R^n\). Since \(M\) is compact and \(\mathbf x\) continuous, \(W\) is compact; compact subsets of \(\mathbb R^n\) are closed. So \(W\) is non-empty (as \(M\ne\emptyset\)), open and closed in \(\mathbb R^n\). But \(\mathbb R^n\) is connected, so \(W = \mathbb R^n\), which is not compact — contradiction. \(\square\)

2. The four. Each is a closed bounded subset of a Euclidean space, hence compact by Heine–Borel. \(S^1\subset\mathbb R^2\) and \(S^2\subset\mathbb R^3\): level sets of a continuous function, bounded by \(\|\cdot\| = 1\). \(T^2 = S^1\times S^1\): a product of compact spaces. \(SO(3)\subset\mathbb R^9\): closed because it is defined by the continuous equations \(A^{\mathsf T}A = \mathbb 1\) and \(\det A = 1\), and bounded because each column is a unit vector, so \(\|A\|_F = \sqrt3\). By part 1, none has a global chart, so each needs at least two.

3. \(S^1\), and the moral. The two-arc atlas of P2 works, so two suffice, and by part 1 one does not. Hence the minimum is exactly two.

For \(\mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}\), identify it with \(\mathbb C^*\) and take \(F(w) = w/(1+|w|)\). Since \(|F(w)| = |w|/(1+|w|)\) increases strictly from \(0\) to \(1\) and \(F\) preserves direction, \(F\) is a bijection onto the punctured open unit disc \(\{0\lt|s|\lt1\}\), with inverse \(s\mapsto s/(1-|s|)\); both are smooth, \(|w|\) being smooth away from the origin. The image is open in \(\mathbb R^2\), so \(F\) is a global chart. The space has a hole and is covered by one chart; the sphere has none and is not. Compactness, not connectivity, is the operative property.

4. Where \(n\ge1\) is used. In the last step, "\(\mathbb R^n\) is not compact". For \(n = 0\), \(\mathbb R^0\) is a point, which is compact, and a zero-manifold consisting of a single point does have a global chart. The rest of the argument is unaffected.

Check. Dimensions: none — this is pure topology. Limit: the argument is sharp in the sense that dropping compactness destroys it, and part 3 exhibits the counterexample. Special case: \(M = \mathbb R^n\) itself is a non-compact manifold with a global chart, and the proof correctly produces no contradiction there, since \(W = \mathbb R^n\) is allowed when \(M\) is not compact.

Another way

Part 1 also follows from an extreme-value argument, which never mentions connectedness. Let \(x^1\) be the first coordinate function of the supposed global chart. It is continuous on the compact \(M\), so it attains a maximum, at some \(p\). But \(\mathbf x(M)\) is open, so it contains a ball around \(\mathbf x(p)\), and that ball contains points whose first coordinate exceeds \(x^1(p)\). Those points are \(\mathbf x(q)\) for some \(q\in M\), contradicting maximality.

P4 Two smooth structures on the line routine

exercises: compatibility versus diffeomorphism, what a smooth structure is

On \(M = \mathbb R\) with its usual topology, let \(\mathcal A\) be the atlas whose single chart is the identity \(\mathbf x(t) = t\), and \(\mathcal B\) the atlas whose single chart is \(\mathbf y(t) = t^3\).

  1. Show \(\mathcal B\) is an atlas.
  2. Show \(\mathcal A\) and \(\mathcal B\) are not compatible, so they give different smooth structures.
  3. Show that \(f(t) = t\) is not a smooth function on \((\mathbb R,\mathcal B)\), while \(f(t) = t^3\) is. So the two structures genuinely have different smooth functions.
  4. Nevertheless exhibit a diffeomorphism \((\mathbb R,\mathcal A)\to(\mathbb R,\mathcal B)\). Explain why this does not contradict part 2.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1–3 are checks of definitions; part 4 is an explicit map plus one sentence. The one sentence is the point of the problem: "not compatible" and "not diffeomorphic" are different statements.

Rung 2

Compatibility is about the transition \(\mathbf y\circ\mathbf x^{-1}\) and its inverse. One of the two is fine; look hard at the other at \(t = 0\). For part 4, a diffeomorphism is allowed to move points, and you want its coordinate representation \(\mathbf y\circ F\circ\mathbf x^{-1}\) to be the identity.

Rung 3

First line: \(\mathbf y\circ\mathbf x^{-1}(s) = s^3\), which is smooth; its inverse is \(s\mapsto s^{1/3}\), whose derivative \(\tfrac13s^{-2/3}\) blows up at \(0\). For part 4 take \(F(t) = t^{1/3}\) and compute its coordinate representation.

Worked solution

Idea. A smooth structure is extra data on a topological space, and two different sets of data can still be carried into one another by a map. "Different structures" is a statement about the identity map; "diffeomorphic" allows any map.

1. \(\mathbf y(t) = t^3\) is a continuous bijection \(\mathbb R\to\mathbb R\) with continuous inverse \(s\mapsto s^{1/3}\), and \(\mathbb R\) is open. A single chart covering \(M\) is trivially an atlas — the only compatibility condition is the chart with itself, whose transition is the identity.

2. \(\mathbf y\circ\mathbf x^{-1}(s) = s^3\) is \(C^\infty\). But \(\mathbf x\circ\mathbf y^{-1}(s) = s^{1/3}\) is not differentiable at \(s = 0\): its difference quotient is \(h^{1/3}/h = h^{-2/3}\to\infty\). Compatibility requires both directions, so the charts are incompatible and \(\bar{\mathcal A}\ne\bar{\mathcal B}\).

3. Smoothness of \(f: M\to\mathbb R\) in the structure \(\mathcal B\) means \(f\circ\mathbf y^{-1}\) is \(C^\infty\). For \(f(t) = t\) that is \(s\mapsto s^{1/3}\), not differentiable at \(0\) — so the identity function is not smooth on \((\mathbb R,\mathcal B)\). For \(f(t) = t^3\) it is \(s\mapsto s\), which is smooth. ✓ In \(\mathcal A\) both are smooth, since \(f\circ\mathbf x^{-1}\) is \(s\) and \(s^3\) respectively, so \(C^\infty(\mathbb R,\mathcal A)\ne C^\infty(\mathbb R,\mathcal B)\) as sets of functions: the identity function belongs to the first and not to the second. In fact the containment is one-way: \(f\) is \(\mathcal B\)-smooth exactly when \(f(t) = h(t^3)\) for some smooth \(h\), and every such \(f\) is a composite of smooth maps, so \(C^\infty(\mathbb R,\mathcal B)\subsetneq C^\infty(\mathbb R,\mathcal A)\).

4. Take \(F: (\mathbb R,\mathcal A)\to(\mathbb R,\mathcal B)\), \(F(t) = t^{1/3}\). Its coordinate representation is \(\mathbf y\circ F\circ\mathbf x^{-1}(s) = \big(s^{1/3}\big)^3 = s\), the identity, which is smooth; the same computation for \(F^{-1}(t) = t^3\) gives \(\mathbf x\circ F^{-1}\circ\mathbf y^{-1}(s) = \big(s^{1/3}\big)^3 = s\). So \(F\) is a diffeomorphism.

No contradiction: part 2 says the identity map is not a diffeomorphism between the two structures, which is a statement about one particular map. Part 4 produces a different map that is. "Different smooth structures on the same set" and "non-diffeomorphic manifolds" are distinct notions, and only the second is of any interest.

Check. Dimensions: none. Limit: away from \(t=0\) everything in sight is smooth and the two structures agree locally — the incompatibility is a single point, and one point is enough. Special case worth knowing: \(\mathbb R^n\) carries exactly one smooth structure up to diffeomorphism for every \(n\ne4\); for \(n=4\) there are uncountably many, and there the distinction this problem trivialises becomes deep.

Tangent vectors, covectors, tensor fields

P5 Four candidates, and which are tensors medium

exercises: the transformation law as a test, why components are not the object

Each rule below assigns four smooth functions \(T_{ij}\) to a chart of \(S^2\). For each, decide whether it defines a \((0,2)\)-tensor field, and justify the verdict: prove it, or convict it with numbers at \(P\), where \(S = \begin{pmatrix}-3&-4\\-4&3\end{pmatrix}\).

  1. \(T_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\) in every chart.
  2. \(T_{ij} = \dfrac{4}{(1+r^2)^2}\delta_{ij}\) in the first chart and \(\dfrac{4}{(1+\tilde r^2)^2}\delta_{ij}\) in the second.
  3. \(T_{ij} = \partial_i\partial_j f\) in every chart, where \(f\) is the height function \(z\).
  4. \(T_{ij} = (\partial_if)(\partial_jf)\) in every chart, same \(f\).

Then state, in one sentence, what distinguishes 3 from 4, and say where on \(S^2\) rule 3 does give a well-defined tensor.

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four verdicts, each with a reason. Two of the four can be proved tensorial in one line from things the deck established; the other two must be tested, and the test is arithmetic: compute the array in the first chart, push it through \(\tilde T_{ab} = S^i{}_aS^j{}_b T_{ij}\), then compute the array in the second chart directly and compare.

Rung 2

In the first chart \(f = (r^2-1)/(r^2+1)\); in the second, \(f = (1-\tilde r^2)/(1+\tilde r^2)\) — note the sign, since the second chart projects from the other pole. You will need \(\partial_uf = 4u/(1+r^2)^2\). For rule 4, what object is \(\mathrm{d}f\), and what operation is being applied to it?

Rung 3

First line for rule 1: \(S^{\mathsf T}\delta S = \begin{pmatrix}25&0\\0&25\end{pmatrix}\), since \(9+16 = 25\) and \(12-12 = 0\). For rule 3, compute \(\partial_u\partial_uf = 4/(1+r^2)^2 - 16u^2/(1+r^2)^3\) and evaluate at \(r^2 = 5\).

Worked solution

Idea. A rule assigning arrays to charts is a tensor field exactly when it is equivariant: one chart's array, pushed through the law, must reproduce the other chart's array. Two of these rules are built from tensors by tensorial operations and pass automatically; two are built by differentiating, and differentiation is where the law breaks.

Rule 1: not a tensor. \(S^{\mathsf T}\delta S = S^{\mathsf T}S\), and

\[ S^{\mathsf T}S = \begin{pmatrix}-3&-4\\-4&3\end{pmatrix}^2 = \begin{pmatrix}9+16 & 12-12\\ 12-12 & 16+9\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}25&0\\0&25\end{pmatrix}. \]

The law predicts \(\tilde T_{11} = 25\); the rule says \(\tilde T_{11} = 1\). Convicted. (It would be a tensor only if every transition Jacobian were orthogonal, which inversion is not: it is \(5\) times an orthogonal matrix at \(P\), and its determinant is \(-1/25\) generally.)

Rule 2: a tensor — it is the round metric, and the deck verified \(\tilde g = g\) on the whole overlap. At \(P\): the array is \(\tfrac19\mathbb 1\), the law gives \(\tfrac19S^{\mathsf T}S = \tfrac{25}9\mathbb 1\), and the second chart's array is \(4/(1+\tfrac15)^2 = 4\cdot\tfrac{25}{36} = \tfrac{25}9\) on the diagonal. ✓

Rule 3: not a tensor. With \(\partial_uf = 4u/(1+r^2)^2\),

\[ \partial_u\partial_uf = \frac{4}{(1+r^2)^2} - \frac{16u^2}{(1+r^2)^3},\qquad \partial_u\partial_vf = -\frac{16uv}{(1+r^2)^3}. \]

At \(P\), \(r^2 = 5\), \((1+r^2)^2 = 36\), \((1+r^2)^3 = 216\):

\[ T = \frac1{27}\begin{pmatrix}-5&-4\\-4&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \text{law:}\quad S^{\mathsf T}TS = \frac1{27}\begin{pmatrix}-125&-100\\-100&25\end{pmatrix}. \]

Directly in the second chart, with \(f = (1-\tilde r^2)/(1+\tilde r^2)\), \(\partial_{\tilde u}f = -4\tilde u/(1+\tilde r^2)^2\) and \(\partial_{\tilde u}\partial_{\tilde u}f = -4/(1+\tilde r^2)^2 + 16\tilde u^2/(1+\tilde r^2)^3\); at \(\tilde r^2 = \tfrac15\), where \((1+\tilde r^2)^2 = \tfrac{36}{25}\) and \((1+\tilde r^2)^3 = \tfrac{216}{125}\),

\[ \tilde T = \frac1{27}\begin{pmatrix}-35&20\\20&-65\end{pmatrix} \ \ne\ \frac1{27}\begin{pmatrix}-125&-100\\-100&25\end{pmatrix}. \]

Convicted, decisively — even the signs disagree.

Rule 4: a tensor. It is \(\mathrm{d}f\otimes\mathrm{d}f\), a tensor product of two covector fields, and the tensor product of tensors is a tensor. Arithmetic confirms: at \(P\), \(\mathrm{d}f = \big(\tfrac29,\tfrac19\big)\), so \(T = \tfrac1{81}\begin{pmatrix}4&2\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\) and \(S^{\mathsf T}TS = \tfrac1{81}\begin{pmatrix}100&50\\50&25\end{pmatrix}\); directly, \(\mathrm{d}f = \big(-\tfrac{10}9,-\tfrac59\big)\) in the second chart, whose outer product is \(\tfrac1{81}\begin{pmatrix}100&50\\50&25\end{pmatrix}\). ✓

The distinction. Rule 4 applies a tensorial operation — a tensor product — to the tensor \(\mathrm{d}f\), whereas rule 3 applies \(\partial_i\) to the components of \(\mathrm{d}f\), and the transformation law of a derivative picks up the second derivatives of the transition map:

\[ \tilde\partial_a\tilde\partial_bf = S^i{}_aS^j{}_b\,\partial_i\partial_jf + \frac{\partial^2x^i}{\partial\tilde x^a\partial\tilde x^b}\,\partial_if . \]

The extra term vanishes exactly where \(\partial_if = 0\), that is at a critical point of \(f\). So rule 3 does give a well-defined tensor at the north and south poles — the Hessian at a critical point — and nowhere else. That is the fact Morse theory runs on.

Check. Dimensions: all four rules are dimensionless on the unit sphere. Limit: on the equator, \(r = 1\), the transition is the identity, \(S = \mathbb 1\), and every rule passes the test there — a reminder that equivariance must hold at every point, and that testing at a fixed point of the transition proves nothing. Number: the trace of rule 3's array in the first chart at \(P\) is \((-5+1)/27 = -\tfrac4{27}\); in the second it is \((-35-65)/27 = -\tfrac{100}{27}\). Neither is the other, and neither should be: the trace of a \((0,2)\)-array is not basis-independent, as unit 16 warned.

P7 Proof: every derivation is a directional derivative hard

  2. Deduce that every derivation \(D\) at \(p\in M\) satisfies \(D = \xi^i\,\partial_i|_p\) with \(\xi^i = D(x^i)\), and hence that \(\dim T_pM = n\).

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an integral identity; part 2 is three lines of Leibniz; part 3 needs a bump function; part 4 is one formula and one word. The whole problem is the proof the deck put in its notes, done by you.

Rung 2

For part 1, apply the fundamental theorem of calculus to \(t\mapsto\hat f(a+t(s-a))\) on \([0,1]\) and use the multivariable chain rule of unit 03 on the integrand. For part 2, feed the Hadamard decomposition to \(D\) and use that \(D\) kills constants — which you should prove first from \(D(1\cdot1)\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\displaystyle \hat f(s)-\hat f(a) = \int_0^1\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\hat f\big(a+t(s-a)\big)\,\mathrm dt = (s^i-a^i)\int_0^1\partial_i\hat f\big(a+t(s-a)\big)\,\mathrm dt\), and name the last integral \(h_i(s)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The Leibniz rule sees only first-order information, and Hadamard's lemma is the statement that a smooth function can be split into a constant plus something first order with smooth coefficients. Put the two together and the derivation has nowhere to hide.

1. Hadamard. Fix \(s\in V\). Convexity puts the segment \(a+t(s-a)\), \(t\in[0,1]\), in \(V\). The map \(t\mapsto\hat f(a+t(s-a))\) is smooth on a neighbourhood of \([0,1]\), with derivative \((s^i-a^i)\partial_i\hat f(a+t(s-a))\) by the chain rule. The fundamental theorem of calculus gives

\[ \hat f(s)-\hat f(a) = (s^i-a^i)\underbrace{\int_0^1\partial_i\hat f\big(a+t(s-a)\big)\,\mathrm dt}_{=:\ h_i(s)} . \]

Each \(h_i\) is smooth: the integrand is smooth jointly in \((t,s)\) and \(t\) ranges over a compact interval, so differentiation under the integral sign is legitimate to all orders. And \(h_i(a) = \int_0^1\partial_i\hat f(a)\,\mathrm dt = \partial_i\hat f(a)\). \(\square\)

2. Derivations. First, \(D\) kills constants: \(D(1) = D(1\cdot1) = 1\cdot D1+1\cdot D1 = 2D1\), so \(D1 = 0\), and \(D(c) = cD(1) = 0\) by linearity. Now take a chart with \(a = \mathbf x(p)\) and a convex \(V\); as germs at \(p\),

\[ f = f(p) + (x^i-a^i)\,(h_i\circ\mathbf x). \]

Apply \(D\): the constant dies, and Leibniz on each product gives

\[ Df = \big(a^i-a^i\big)D(h_i\circ\mathbf x) + h_i(a)\,D(x^i) = \partial_i\hat f(a)\,\xi^i,\qquad \xi^i := D(x^i), \]

since \((x^i-a^i)(p) = 0\). So \(D = \xi^i\partial_i|_p\), and \(\{\partial_i|_p\}\) spans \(T_pM\). They are independent: if \(c^i\partial_i|_p = 0\), apply it to \(x^j\) to get \(c^j = 0\). Hence \(\dim T_pM = n\). \(\square\)

3. Locality. By linearity it suffices to show that \(Df = 0\) when \(f\) vanishes on a neighbourhood \(W\) of \(p\). Choose a smooth \(\chi\) with \(\chi(p) = 1\) and \(\operatorname{supp}\chi\subseteq W\) — a bump function, built from \(\mathrm e^{-1/t}\). Then \(\chi f\equiv0\) on all of \(M\), so

\[ 0 = D(\chi f) = \chi(p)\,Df + f(p)\,D\chi = Df . \]

It needs proof because \(D\) is defined by two algebraic axioms — linearity and Leibniz — neither of which mentions neighbourhoods. Nothing in the axioms forbids a functional from consulting the function far away; it is the Leibniz rule together with the existence of bump functions that forbids it. On germs the statement is automatic by construction, which is why the deck used germs.

4. A non-derivation. \(D(f) = f''(0)\) is linear and is not a derivation: \((fg)'' = f''g + 2f'g' + fg''\), so \(D(fg) = f(0)Dg + g(0)Df + 2f'(0)g'(0)\), and the last term is not zero in general — take \(f = g = t\), where \(D(fg) = 2\) while \(f(0)Dg+g(0)Df = 0\). It fails the Leibniz rule; linearity is fine. (Similarly \(D(f) = f(0)\) fails Leibniz, and \(D(f) = f'(0)^2\) fails linearity.)

Check. Dimensions: none; this is pure analysis. Limit: for \(n=1\) and \(\hat f(s) = s^2\) at \(a=0\), Hadamard gives \(h_1(s) = \int_0^12ts\,\mathrm dt = s\), so \(\hat f = 0 + s\cdot s\) ✓, and \(h_1(0) = 0 = \hat f'(0)\) ✓. Special case: the theorem fails for \(C^1\) germs — \(h_i\) is then only \(C^0\) and the space of derivations on \(C^1\) germs is in fact infinite-dimensional, which is exactly why manifolds are defined with \(C^\infty\).

and \(\tilde\partial_aS^j{}_b = \dfrac{\partial^2x^j}{\partial\tilde x^a\partial\tilde x^b}\): second derivatives of the transition map, symmetric in \(a,b\) by Clairaut. Idea. A one-parameter group of diffeomorphisms of the sphere descends to a flow in each chart; differentiating the chart coordinates along it at \(t = 0\) is the vector field, by definition A of a tangent vector.

0. The identity. \(r^2 = (1+z)/(1-z)\) gives \(\dfrac{z}{1-z} = \dfrac{(1+z)-(1-z)}{2(1-z)} = \dfrac{r^2-1}{2}\). ✓

1. The three fields. For \(R_z\): \(\dot x = -y,\ \dot y = x,\ \dot z = 0\), so \(\dot u = -y/(1-z) = -v\) and \(\dot v = u\). For \(R_x\): \(\dot x = 0,\ \dot y = -z,\ \dot z = y\), so

\[ \dot u = \frac{x\dot z}{(1-z)^2} = \frac{xy}{(1-z)^2} = uv,\qquad \dot v = \frac{-z(1-z)+y^2}{(1-z)^2} = -\frac{z}{1-z}+v^2 = \frac{1-r^2}{2}+v^2 . \]

For \(R_y\): \(\dot x = z,\ \dot z = -x\), so \(\dot u = \dfrac{z}{1-z} - u^2 = \dfrac{r^2-1}{2}-u^2\) and \(\dot v = -xy/(1-z)^2 = -uv\). Collecting:

\[ R_z = -v\,\partial_u+u\,\partial_v,\quad R_x = uv\,\partial_u+\tfrac12(1-u^2+v^2)\,\partial_v,\quad R_y = -\tfrac12(1+u^2-v^2)\,\partial_u - uv\,\partial_v . \]

2. The brackets. Using \([X,Y]^i = X^j\partial_jY^i-Y^j\partial_jX^i\). For \([R_z,R_x]\), first component:

\[ (-v)(v) + (u)(u) - \Big[uv\cdot0 + \tfrac12(1-u^2+v^2)(-1)\Big] = u^2-v^2+\tfrac12(1-u^2+v^2) = \tfrac12(1+u^2-v^2), \]

second component: \((-v)(-u)+u\,v - \big[uv\cdot1 + \tfrac12(1-u^2+v^2)\cdot0\big] = uv\). Together that is \(-R_y\). The same computation gives

\[ [R_x,R_y] = -R_z,\qquad [R_y,R_z] = -R_x,\qquad [R_z,R_x] = -R_y . \]

3. The sign. The generators of a left action form an anti-homomorphic image of the Lie algebra: \([R_A,R_B] = -R_{[A,B]}\), because the flow of \(R_A\) is left multiplication by \(\exp(tA)\) and composing left multiplications reverses the order of the corresponding operators on functions. The map \(\varphi(J_k) = -R_k\) fixes it:

\[ [\varphi(J_x),\varphi(J_y)] = [-R_x,-R_y] = [R_x,R_y] = -R_z = \varphi(J_z), \]

and cyclically. \(\varphi\) is linear, bijective onto the span, and bracket-preserving: a Lie algebra isomorphism \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\to\mathrm{span}\{R_x,R_y,R_z\}\).

4. The zeros. \(R_z = (-v,u)\) vanishes only at \(u=v=0\), the south pole; and in the second chart \(\tilde R_z = (-\tilde v,\tilde u)\) vanishes only at the north pole. Two zeros. The hairy ball theorem says no smooth vector field on \(S^2\) is nowhere-zero, so at least one was compulsory; Poincaré–Hopf sharpens this: the indices must sum to \(\chi(S^2) = 2\), and each of these zeros is a rotation, of index \(+1\), so two is the minimum and \(R_z\) attains it.

Check. Dimensions: none. Limit: at the origin of the first chart (the south pole) \(R_x = (0,\tfrac12)\) and \(R_y = (-\tfrac12,0)\) — non-zero and orthogonal, as rotations about two horizontal axes should be at the bottom of the sphere, and the factor \(\tfrac12\) is the stereographic scale there, where \(g_{ij} = 4\delta_{ij}\) and so \(\|R_x\|^2 = 4\cdot\tfrac14 = 1\): unit speed at unit distance from the axis. ✓ Special case: on the equator \(r = 1\), \(R_x = (uv,\ v^2)= v(u,v)\) — radial, and vanishing at \((\pm1,0)\), which are the two ends of the \(x\)-axis, where rotation about \(x\) indeed moves nothing.

Worked solution

Idea. \(\mathcal L_Xg = 0\) says the flow of \(X\) is a family of isometries. For a conformally flat metric \(\Omega^2\delta\) the equation splits into "how much the flow stretches" plus "how much \(\Omega\) changes along it", and the two must cancel.

1. \(R_x\) is Killing. Write \(X = R_x\), \(X^1 = uv\), \(X^2 = \tfrac12(1-u^2+v^2)\). Then

\[ \partial_1X^1 = v,\quad \partial_2X^1 = u,\quad \partial_1X^2 = -u,\quad \partial_2X^2 = v. \]

Transport: \(X^k\partial_kr^2 = 2u^2v + v(1-u^2+v^2) = v(1+u^2+v^2) = v(1+r^2)\), so

\[ X^k\partial_k\Omega^2 = \frac{-8}{(1+r^2)^3}\cdot v(1+r^2) = \frac{-8v}{(1+r^2)^2} = -2v\,\Omega^2 . \]

Now assemble each component.

\[ (\mathcal L_Xg)_{11} = -2v\Omega^2 + 2\Omega^2\partial_1X^1 = \Omega^2(-2v+2v) = 0, \] \[ (\mathcal L_Xg)_{22} = -2v\Omega^2 + 2\Omega^2\partial_2X^2 = \Omega^2(-2v+2v) = 0, \] \[ (\mathcal L_Xg)_{12} = 0 + \Omega^2\big(\partial_1X^2+\partial_2X^1\big) = \Omega^2(-u+u) = 0 . \]

All three vanish. ✓ The off-diagonal cancellation is the statement that the flow is conformal, and the diagonal one that the conformal factor exactly compensates the change in \(\Omega\).

2. The dilation. \(\partial_iD^j = \delta_i^j\) and \(D^k\partial_kr^2 = 2r^2\), so

\[ (\mathcal L_Dg)_{ij} = \frac{-8}{(1+r^2)^3}2r^2\delta_{ij} + 2\Omega^2\delta_{ij} = \Big[\frac{-16r^2}{(1+r^2)^3}+\frac{8}{(1+r^2)^2}\Big]\delta_{ij} = \frac{8(1-r^2)}{(1+r^2)^3}\delta_{ij}, \] \[ \text{so}\qquad \mathcal L_Dg = \lambda g,\qquad \lambda = \frac{2(1-r^2)}{1+r^2} . \]

Zero only on \(r = 1\), the equator. At \(P\), \(r^2 = 5\): \(\lambda = 2(-4)/6 = -\tfrac43\). So \(D\) is conformal Killing and not Killing: its flow slides points along meridians, preserving angles and shrinking lengths near \(P\).

3. Surfaces of revolution. General fact: if \(g_{ij}\) is independent of the coordinate \(x^m\), then \(X = \partial_m\) has \(X^k = \delta^k_m\) constant, so both correction terms vanish and \(\mathcal L_Xg_{ij} = \partial_mg_{ij} = 0\). Here \(g = \mathrm{diag}(1,F(\rho)^2)\) has no \(\phi\)-dependence, so \(\partial_\phi\) is Killing for every \(F\) — the surface is rotationally symmetric by construction. For \(\partial_\rho\), the same criterion demands \(\partial_\rho g_{ij} = 0\), that is \(2FF' = 0\), so \(F\) must be a non-zero constant: the cylinder, and no other surface of revolution. (Indeed a cylinder is flat and translation-invariant; a sphere, where \(F = \sin\rho\), is not.)

4. Rindler. The shift is \(X_{\text{old}} = c^2/a + X\), which leaves \(\mathrm{d}X^2\) alone and turns the lapse into \((a/c)^2\big(c^2/a+X\big)^2 = c^2\big(1+aX/c^2\big)^2\) — the same metric, with the reference observer now at the origin. Its components \(-(1+aX/c^2)^2c^2\) and \(1\) depend on \(X\) only, so by the same criterion \(\partial_T\) is Killing. Physically: the Rindler metric is static, an accelerating observer's description of flat spacetime does not change with their own time coordinate, and the conserved quantity \(g(\partial_T,\dot\gamma)\) is what unit 30's redshift argument was implicitly using — the ratio of clock rates between two heights is constant in time, which is why the Pound–Rebka experiment has a steady answer.

Check. Dimensions: in part 4, \(aX/c^2\) is \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}\cdot m/(m^2s^{-2})} = 1\), dimensionless ✓, and both metric terms carry m\(^2\). Limit: in part 2, \(\lambda\to-2\) as \(r\to\infty\), the north pole, where the dilation's flow is fastest in the stereographic picture and slowest on the sphere; and \(\lambda\to+2\) at the south pole. Number: the three rotations give \(3 = n(n+1)/2\) Killing fields for \(n = 2\), so \(S^2\) is maximally symmetric and no fourth Killing field exists — \(D\) could not have been one.

Let \(g = \delta_{ij}\) on \(\mathbb R^2\).

  2. Identify the resulting fields geometrically, and compute all their brackets. Which Lie algebra is it?
  3. Show that the bound \(\dim\le n(n+1)/2\) is attained here, and explain in words why the bound exists at all: what data at a single point determines a Killing field?

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a general solution with constants; part 2 a table of brackets and a name; part 3 a count and a sentence; part 4 the same computation with one sign changed. The system is three equations for two functions.

Rung 2

With constant \(g\), the Killing equation is just \(\partial_iX_j+\partial_jX_i = 0\). The diagonal entries say each \(X^i\) is independent of its own variable; differentiate the off-diagonal equation once more and use those.

Rung 3

First line: \(\partial_xX^1 = 0\) and \(\partial_yX^2 = 0\), so \(X^1 = X^1(y)\) and \(X^2 = X^2(x)\). Then \(\partial_xX^2 = -\partial_yX^1\) has a function of \(x\) on the left and of \(y\) on the right, so both are a constant.

Worked solution

Idea. With constant metric components the transport term dies and the Killing equation becomes a statement that the derivative matrix of \(X\) is antisymmetric. Antisymmetric matrices plus constants is exactly the Euclidean group's Lie algebra, and the counting comes out on its own.

1. Solving. With \(g_{ij} = \delta_{ij}\) constant,

\[ (\mathcal L_Xg)_{ij} = \delta_{kj}\partial_iX^k + \delta_{ik}\partial_jX^k = \partial_iX^j+\partial_jX^i = 0 . \]

Taking \(ij = 11\) and \(22\): \(\partial_xX^1 = 0\) and \(\partial_yX^2 = 0\), so \(X^1\) depends only on \(y\) and \(X^2\) only on \(x\). Taking \(ij = 12\): \(\partial_xX^2 = -\partial_yX^1\). The left side is a function of \(x\) alone, the right of \(y\) alone, so both equal a constant \(\omega\). Integrating, \(X^2 = \omega x + b\) and \(X^1 = -\omega y + a\):

\[ X = (a-\omega y)\,\partial_x + (b+\omega x)\,\partial_y, \]

three free constants \(a, b, \omega\).

2. The algebra. \(T_1 = \partial_x\) and \(T_2 = \partial_y\) are translations; \(R = -y\,\partial_x+x\,\partial_y\) is rotation about the origin. Brackets:

\[ [T_1,T_2] = 0,\qquad [R,T_1] = -T_2,\qquad [R,T_2] = T_1 . \]

(For instance \([R,T_1]^i = R^j\partial_jT_1^i - T_1^j\partial_jR^i = 0 - \partial_x(-y,x) = -(0,1)\).) This is \(\mathfrak{e}(2)\), the Lie algebra of the Euclidean group: an abelian ideal of translations with the rotation acting on it by a \(90^\circ\) turn.

3. The bound. \(n(n+1)/2 = 3\) for \(n = 2\), and we found three: attained, so the plane is maximally symmetric. The bound exists because a Killing field is determined by \(X_m\) and the antisymmetric matrix \(\partial_{[i}X_{j]}\) at any single point \(m\) — here \((a,b)\) and \(\omega\) — which is \(n + n(n-1)/2 = n(n+1)/2\) numbers. The symmetric part of \(\partial X\) is forced to vanish by the Killing equation itself, and the second derivatives are then determined (on a general manifold, by the curvature), so no further freedom survives.

4. Minkowski. With \(g = \mathrm{diag}(-1,1)\) and coordinates \((t,x)\), the Killing equations are \(-\partial_tX^0 \cdot 2 = 0\) in the \(00\) slot, \(2\partial_xX^1 = 0\) in the \(11\) slot, and \(-\partial_xX^0+\partial_tX^1 = 0\) in the \(01\) slot. So \(X^0 = X^0(x)\), \(X^1 = X^1(t)\) and \(\partial_xX^0 = \partial_tX^1 = \kappa\), constant, giving

\[ X = (a+\kappa x)\,\partial_t + (b+\kappa t)\,\partial_x . \]

Again three constants. The rotation is replaced by the boost \(B = x\,\partial_t+t\,\partial_x\) — note the relative plus sign, the hyperbolic counterpart of the rotation's minus — whose flow is the Lorentz boost of unit 26, and whose integral curves are the hyperbolae of unit 27's uniformly accelerated worldline.

Check. Dimensions: none in part 1; in part 4, \(\kappa\) is dimensionless with \(x^0 = ct\). Limit: setting \(\omega = 0\) leaves only translations, the symmetries of a plane with a marked direction but no marked point; setting \(a = b = 0\) leaves rotations about the origin. Number: the brackets close, so the three-dimensional space really is a subalgebra — computing \([R,[T_1,T_2]] = 0\) and checking the Jacobi identity on \((R,T_1,T_2)\) gives \([R,[T_1,T_2]]+[T_1,[T_2,R]]+[T_2,[R,T_1]] = 0 + [T_1,-T_1]+[T_2,-T_2] = 0\). ✓

P13 Frobenius, and parallel parking hard

  1. Show \(\mathcal D\) is a genuine rank-2 distribution and compute \([X,Y]\). Is \(\mathcal D\) involutive?
  2. Prove directly, without quoting Frobenius, that no surface \(z = h(x,y)\) has \(\mathcal D\) as its tangent planes — and identify which theorem of unit 03 delivers the contradiction.
  3. Show that from any point you can reach any other by concatenating integral curves of \(X\) and \(Y\) run forwards and backwards. Deduce that the leaves, if they existed, would have to be three-dimensional, contradicting rank 2.
  4. A car's configuration is \((x,y,\theta)\), and its two controls are "drive", \(\cos\theta\,\partial_x+\sin\theta\,\partial_y\), and "steer", \(\partial_\theta\). Compute the bracket of the two controls and say, in one sentence, what manoeuvre it is.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 ends in a vector field and a yes/no; part 2 is a proof by contradiction; part 3 a constructive argument; part 4 a vector field and its name. The example is the standard counterexample and also the standard engineering fact.

Rung 2

For part 2: if the surface exists, both \(X\) and \(Y\) are tangent to it, which turns into two equations for \(\partial_xh\) and \(\partial_yh\). Solve them and then differentiate again. For part 3, notice that the commutator square of \(X\) and \(Y\) moves you in \(z\).

Rung 3

First line: \(X\) tangent to \(z = h(x,y)\) requires \(\partial_xh = 0\); \(Y\) tangent requires \(\partial_yh = x\). Now compute \(\partial_y\partial_xh\) and \(\partial_x\partial_yh\).

Worked solution

Idea. Two directions you can move in do not make a surface you can stay on. What decides is whether the bracket escapes the span: if it does, the two directions generate a third, and the "surface" would have to be three-dimensional.

1. \(X = (1,0,0)\) and \(Y = (0,1,x)\) are linearly independent at every point — their first two components already are — so \(\mathcal D\) is a smooth rank-2 distribution. The bracket:

\[ [X,Y]^i = X^j\partial_jY^i - Y^j\partial_jX^i = \partial_x(0,1,x) - 0 = (0,0,1) = \partial_z . \]

Is \(\partial_z\in\mathrm{span}\{X,Y\}\)? A combination \(\alpha X+\beta Y = (\alpha,\beta,\beta x)\) equals \((0,0,1)\) only if \(\alpha = \beta = 0\) and \(\beta x = 1\) — impossible. So \(\mathcal D\) is not involutive.

2. No surface. Suppose \(z = h(x,y)\) had \(\mathcal D\) as its tangent planes on some open set. A vector \((a,b,c)\) is tangent iff \(c = a\,\partial_xh+b\,\partial_yh\). Applying this to \(X = (1,0,0)\) gives \(\partial_xh = 0\); applying it to \(Y = (0,1,x)\) gives \(\partial_yh = x\). Then

\[ \partial_y\partial_xh = \partial_y(0) = 0,\qquad \partial_x\partial_yh = \partial_x(x) = 1 . \]

Clairaut's theorem (unit 03) says the mixed partials of a \(C^2\) function are equal. They are not. Contradiction. \(\square\) The same argument works for a surface given implicitly, by the implicit function theorem.

3. Reachability. Flowing along \(X\) for time \(a\) changes \(x\) by \(a\) and nothing else. Flowing along \(Y\) for time \(b\) changes \(y\) by \(b\) and \(z\) by \(bx\), with \(x\) held fixed. So: move to the target \(y\) with \(Y\), move to the target \(x\) with \(X\), and then adjust \(z\) with the commutator square — go \(X\) by \(a\), \(Y\) by \(b\), back \(X\) by \(a\), back \(Y\) by \(b\), which returns \(x\) and \(y\) to their values and changes \(z\) by \(ab\) exactly (not merely to leading order: \(z\) gains \(b(x+a)\) then loses \(bx\)). Choosing \(ab\) freely reaches any \(z\). Hence the set reachable from a point is all of \(\mathbb R^3\). If the leaves existed they would contain every reachable point, hence be three-dimensional, contradicting rank 2. This is Chow's theorem in its simplest instance, and it is the exact opposite of Frobenius: brackets that escape make the distribution more useful, not less.

4. The car. With \(A = \cos\theta\,\partial_x+\sin\theta\,\partial_y\) and \(B = \partial_\theta\),

\[ [B,A] = \partial_\theta(\cos\theta,\sin\theta,0) = (-\sin\theta,\cos\theta,0), \]

which is the direction perpendicular to the car's heading — exactly the sideways motion the car cannot perform directly. Drive, steer, reverse, straighten: that is parallel parking, and it works because the bracket of the two controls points sideways.

Check. Dimensions: in part 4, \(\theta\) is dimensionless and \(A\) carries inverse length, so \([B,A]\) does too and is comparable with \(A\). ✓ Limit: replacing \(Y\) by \(\partial_y\) (drop the \(x\partial_z\)) gives \([X,Y] = 0\), involutive, and the leaves are the planes \(z = \mathrm{const}\) — the integrable case, recovered by deleting exactly the term that caused the trouble. Number: the commutator square with \(a = b = 0.1\) changes \(z\) by \(ab = 0.01\), exactly, with no higher-order error, because these flows are affine in the coordinates.
