Curvature: tidal forces, with the mask off

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You compute the Riemann tensor of a 2-surface from its metric and verify it has exactly one independent component, the Gaussian curvature; you prove the contracted Bianchi identity \(\nabla^\mu G_{\mu\nu} = 0\) from the second Bianchi identity with every contraction justified; you reduce the geodesic deviation equation to unit 05's tidal equation with \(R^i{}_{0j0} = \partial_i\partial_j\Phi/c^2\) and the \(c^2\) in its place; and you count the components of \(R\) in \(n\) dimensions and say what \(20 = 10 + 10\) means in four.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about seven hours. Conventions are the deck's: \(R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}\), \(R_{\sigma\nu} = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\rho\nu}\), \(R = g^{\sigma\nu}R_{\sigma\nu}\), signature \((-,+,+,+)\), \(x^0 = ct\). The four symmetries are S1 \(R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = -R_{\rho\sigma\nu\mu}\), S2 \(R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = -R_{\sigma\rho\mu\nu}\), S3 \(R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} + R_{\rho\mu\nu\sigma} + R_{\rho\nu\sigma\mu} = 0\), S4 \(R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}\). With these a sphere has positive curvature — check every answer against that.

Constants: \(c = 299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(GM_{\text{Moon}} = 4.9028695\times10^{12}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\), \(GM_\odot = 1.32712440018\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\), Earth–Moon distance \(d = 3.844\times10^{8}\) m, \(1\ \mathrm{AU} = 1.495978707\times10^{11}\) m, Earth treated as a sphere of radius \(R_\oplus = 6.371\times10^{6}\) m, sidereal day \(86\,164.09\) s, Paris (the Panthéon) at latitude \(\lambda = 48.8462^\circ\) N.

Curvature of surfaces

P1 The cone: flat, and still not trivial routine

exercises: Christoffel symbols to Riemann, parallel transport round a loop, what \(R = 0\) does not say

The cone of half-angle \(\alpha \in (0,\tfrac\pi2]\) has metric \(g = \mathrm dr^2 + r^2\sin^2\alpha\,\mathrm d\phi^2\) for \(r > 0\), \(\phi\) an angle of period \(2\pi\).

  1. Find the Christoffel symbols and show \(R^r{}_{\phi r\phi} = 0\). Why does that settle every component?
  2. Parallel-transport a vector once round the circle \(r = r_0\), in the direction of increasing \(\phi\). Working in the orthonormal frame \(\hat e_r = \partial_r\), \(\hat e_\phi = (r_0\sin\alpha)^{-1}\partial_\phi\), find the angle through which it returns rotated relative to that frame.
  3. Give the holonomy angle for \(\alpha = 30^\circ\) and for \(\alpha = 60^\circ\), in degrees. Then explain how a surface with \(R = 0\) at every one of its points can have holonomy at all.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a single component and a counting remark; part 2 is a pair of linear ODEs with constant coefficients whose solution is a rotation; part 3 is two numbers and one sentence about topology.

Rung 2

Only two Christoffel symbols survive, one \(\Gamma^r\) and one \(\Gamma^\phi\) (counted with its symmetric partner). Along the circle, \(\dot x^\mu = (0, 1)\) with \(\phi\) as parameter, so the transport equation \(\dot V^\rho = -\Gamma^\rho_{\phi\sigma}V^\sigma\) couples \(V^r\) and \(V^\phi\) and nothing else.

Rung 3

\(\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -r\sin^2\alpha\), \(\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = 1/r\). Put \(A = V^r\) and \(B = r_0\sin\alpha\,V^\phi\); the first line is \(\mathrm dA/\mathrm d\phi = r_0\sin^2\alpha\,V^\phi = \sin\alpha\,B\).

Worked solution

Idea. Locally the cone is a piece of flat paper rolled up, so its curvature is zero; but the paper is missing a wedge, and a loop round the apex notices the missing wedge. Curvature is local; holonomy round a loop that cannot be shrunk is not.

1. With \(g_{rr} = 1\), \(g_{\phi\phi} = r^2\sin^2\alpha\), the geodesic Lagrangian \(\tfrac12(\dot r^2 + r^2\sin^2\alpha\,\dot\phi^2)\) gives \(\ddot r - r\sin^2\alpha\,\dot\phi^2 = 0\) and \(\ddot\phi + (2/r)\dot r\dot\phi = 0\), so \[ \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -r\sin^2\alpha, \qquad \Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = \frac1r , \] and all others vanish. Then \[ R^r{}_{\phi r\phi} = \partial_r\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} - \partial_\phi\Gamma^r_{r\phi} + \Gamma^r_{r\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\phi\phi} - \Gamma^r_{\phi\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{r\phi} = -\sin^2\alpha - 0 + 0 - (-r\sin^2\alpha)\Big(\frac1r\Big) = 0 . \] In two dimensions the whole tensor is \(K(g_{\rho\mu}g_{\sigma\nu} - g_{\rho\nu}g_{\sigma\mu})\) with \(K = R_{r\phi r\phi}/\det g = g_{rr}R^r{}_{\phi r\phi}/\det g = 0\), so every component vanishes.

2. Along \(r = r_0\), with \(\phi\) as parameter, \(\mathrm dV^r/\mathrm d\phi = -\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}V^\phi = r_0\sin^2\alpha\,V^\phi\) and \(\mathrm dV^\phi/\mathrm d\phi = -\Gamma^\phi_{\phi r}V^r = -V^r/r_0\). In orthonormal components \(A = V^r\), \(B = r_0\sin\alpha\,V^\phi\): \[ \frac{\mathrm dA}{\mathrm d\phi} = \sin\alpha\,B, \qquad \frac{\mathrm dB}{\mathrm d\phi} = -\sin\alpha\,A . \] This is unit 06's oscillator: \((A,B)\) turns clockwise in the \((\hat e_r,\hat e_\phi)\) plane at the constant rate \(\sin\alpha\), and after \(\phi = 2\pi\) it has turned through \(-2\pi\sin\alpha\). The frame itself is back where it began, so the holonomy is a rotation by \[ -2\pi\sin\alpha \;\equiv\; 2\pi(1-\sin\alpha) \pmod{2\pi} . \]

3. \(\alpha = 30^\circ\): \(2\pi(1-\tfrac12) = \pi\), a half-turn, \(180^\circ\). \(\alpha = 60^\circ\): \(2\pi(1 - 0.8660) = 0.8418\) rad \(= 48.23^\circ\). A surface with \(R = 0\) everywhere is locally flat: cut along \(\phi = 0\) and the chart \(\tilde\phi = \phi\sin\alpha\) unrolls it onto a plane with a wedge of angle \(2\pi(1-\sin\alpha)\) removed. A loop that does not enclose the apex bounds a flat disc and has trivial holonomy; the circle encloses the apex, which is not part of the surface, so it bounds nothing on the surface and the holonomy theorem (angle \(=\int K\,\mathrm dA\) over the enclosed region) does not apply to it. This is why the flatness theorem requires simple connectivity. If the apex is put back as a point carrying curvature, the theorem says that point carries \(\int K\,\mathrm dA = 2\pi(1-\sin\alpha)\): a delta function.

Check. Limit \(\alpha = 90^\circ\): the "cone" is the plane in polar coordinates, and the holonomy is \(0\) ✓. A second check: the cone tangent to a sphere along the circle of colatitude \(\theta_0\) has \(\sin\alpha = \cos\theta_0\), and unit 32 found transport round that latitude on the sphere turns a vector through \(2\pi\cos\theta_0\) in magnitude — the same number, because transport along a curve depends only on the metric along an infinitesimal strip round the curve, and the cone and the sphere agree there to first order. Dimensions: angles, nothing to check; \(r_0\) has dropped out, as it must for a cone, which looks the same at every scale.

P2 One component in two dimensions medium

exercises: using the symmetries as constraints, a proof by dimension, Riemann from Christoffel symbols

  1. Prove that on a 2-manifold every component of \(R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}\) is \(0\) or \(\pm R_{1212}\), and that S3 and S4 then impose nothing further. Deduce \(R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = K(g_{\rho\mu}g_{\sigma\nu} - g_{\rho\nu}g_{\sigma\mu})\) with \(K = R_{1212}/\det g\) a function on the surface, independent of the chart.
  2. The hyperbolic plane of radius \(a\) is the upper half-plane \(y > 0\) with \(g = a^2(\mathrm dx^2 + \mathrm dy^2)/y^2\). Compute its Christoffel symbols and \(R^x{}_{yxy}\) directly from the component formula, and find \(K\).
  3. Check your \(K\) with the conformal formula \(K = -e^{-2\varphi}\Delta\varphi\), and say why the blow-up of \(g\) as \(y \to 0\) is not a curvature singularity.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a statement about the dimension of a vector space of index arrays; the answer is "one", and the rest follows by comparing \(R\) with a tensor you already know has the same symmetries. Part 2 ends in a negative constant.

Rung 2

S1 says the last pair must be \(12\) or \(21\); S2 says the same of the first pair. So there are four nonzero slots, related by signs. For part 2, write \(g = e^{2\varphi}(\mathrm dx^2+\mathrm dy^2)\) with \(\varphi = \ln a - \ln y\); the Christoffel symbols of a conformally flat metric are all \(\pm\partial\varphi\).

Rung 3

\(\Gamma^x_{xy} = \Gamma^y_{yy} = -1/y\), \(\Gamma^y_{xx} = +1/y\), and the other three are zero. First line: \(R^x{}_{yxy} = \partial_x\Gamma^x_{yy} - \partial_y\Gamma^x_{xy} + \Gamma^x_{x\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{yy} - \Gamma^x_{y\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{xy}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Antisymmetry in two slots of a two-dimensional index leaves a one-dimensional space; so the Riemann tensor is a multiple of any nonzero tensor with the same symmetries, and \(g\) supplies one.

1. By S1, \(R_{\rho\sigma\mu\mu} = 0\), so a nonzero component has \(\mu\nu \in \{12, 21\}\); by S2 likewise \(\rho\sigma \in \{12, 21\}\). The four candidates are \(R_{1212}\), \(R_{1221} = -R_{1212}\), \(R_{2112} = -R_{1212}\), \(R_{2121} = R_{1212}\). So the space of arrays obeying S1 and S2 is one-dimensional. S4 holds automatically: exchanging the pairs of \(R_{1212}\) gives \(R_{1212}\), of \(R_{1221}\) gives \(R_{2112}\), equal to it. For S3, with only two index values two of \(\sigma,\mu,\nu\) coincide, and then S1 alone kills the cyclic sum: if \(\sigma = \mu\) it is \((R_{\rho\sigma\sigma\nu} + R_{\rho\sigma\nu\sigma}) + R_{\rho\nu\sigma\sigma} = 0 + 0\); if \(\mu = \nu\) it is \(R_{\rho\sigma\mu\mu} + (R_{\rho\mu\mu\sigma} + R_{\rho\mu\sigma\mu}) = 0\); if \(\nu = \sigma\) it is \((R_{\rho\sigma\mu\sigma} + R_{\rho\sigma\sigma\mu}) + R_{\rho\mu\sigma\sigma} = 0\). So nothing further is imposed.

Now \(T_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = g_{\rho\mu}g_{\sigma\nu} - g_{\rho\nu}g_{\sigma\mu}\) obeys S1 and S2 by inspection, and \(T_{1212} = g_{11}g_{22} - g_{12}^2 = \det g > 0\). Both \(R\) and \(T\) lie in the one-dimensional space, \(T \ne 0\), so \(R = fT\) with \(f = R_{1212}/T_{1212} = R_{1212}/\det g\). Since \(R\) and \(T\) are tensors, \(f\) at a point does not depend on the chart; call it \(K\). \(\square\)

2. With \(\varphi = \ln a - \ln y\): \(\varphi_x = 0\), \(\varphi_y = -1/y\). For \(g = e^{2\varphi}\delta\) the Christoffel formula gives \(\Gamma^x_{xx} = \varphi_x\), \(\Gamma^x_{xy} = \varphi_y\), \(\Gamma^x_{yy} = -\varphi_x\), \(\Gamma^y_{yy} = \varphi_y\), \(\Gamma^y_{xy} = \varphi_x\), \(\Gamma^y_{xx} = -\varphi_y\). So \[ \Gamma^x_{xy} = -\frac1y,\quad \Gamma^y_{yy} = -\frac1y,\quad \Gamma^y_{xx} = \frac1y,\quad \Gamma^x_{xx} = \Gamma^x_{yy} = \Gamma^y_{xy} = 0 . \] Term by term: \(\partial_x\Gamma^x_{yy} = 0\); \(\ -\partial_y\Gamma^x_{xy} = -\partial_y(-1/y) = -1/y^2\); \(\ \Gamma^x_{x\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{yy} = \Gamma^x_{xx}\Gamma^x_{yy} + \Gamma^x_{xy}\Gamma^y_{yy} = 0 + 1/y^2\); \(\ -\Gamma^x_{y\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{xy} = -(\Gamma^x_{yx}\Gamma^x_{xy} + \Gamma^x_{yy}\Gamma^y_{xy}) = -1/y^2\). \[ R^x{}_{yxy} = 0 - \frac1{y^2} + \frac1{y^2} - \frac1{y^2} = -\frac1{y^2}, \qquad R_{xyxy} = g_{xx}R^x{}_{yxy} = -\frac{a^2}{y^4}, \qquad K = \frac{R_{xyxy}}{\det g} = \frac{-a^2/y^4}{a^4/y^4} = -\frac1{a^2} . \]

3. \(\Delta\varphi = \partial_y^2(-\ln y) = 1/y^2\) and \(e^{-2\varphi} = y^2/a^2\), so \(K = -(y^2/a^2)(1/y^2) = -1/a^2\) ✓. The metric components blow up as \(y \to 0\), but \(K\) — a scalar — is the same everywhere, and \(y = 0\) is at infinite distance: \(\int_{y}^{1} a\,\mathrm dy'/y' = a\ln(1/y) \to \infty\). The line \(y = 0\) is not part of the surface; it is where the chart sends infinity.

Check. Dimensions: \(K\) is \(\mathrm L^{-2}\) ✓. Limit \(a \to \infty\): \(K \to 0\) ✓. And \(K\) is constant, as it must be: \((x,y) \mapsto (x+b, y)\) and \((x,y)\mapsto(\lambda x,\lambda y)\) are isometries (they leave \(g\) unchanged), they can carry any point to any other, and an isometry preserves \(K\). The sphere is the same surface with the opposite sign.

P3 Surfaces in geodesic polar form medium

exercises: Riemann of a family of metrics at once, geodesic deviation read backwards, uniqueness for ODEs

Let \(g = \mathrm dr^2 + f(r)^2\,\mathrm d\phi^2\) with \(f > 0\) for \(r > 0\).

  1. Show \(K = -f''(r)/f(r)\).
  2. Read off \(K\) for \(f = r\), \(f = a\sin(r/a)\), \(f = a\sinh(r/a)\), \(f = r\sin\alpha\), and name the surfaces.
  3. The curves \(\phi = \text{const}\) are unit-speed geodesics (check this). Show that the separation of two of them at angle \(\Delta\phi\) is \(\xi = f(r)\Delta\phi\) and that the geodesic deviation equation for it is the statement \(f'' = -Kf\) — the formula of part 1, read the other way.
  4. Suppose \(K\) is a constant \(K_0 > 0\) and the metric is smooth at \(r = 0\), which forces \(f(0) = 0\), \(f'(0) = 1\). Prove \(f\) is determined, and find it.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is P1's computation with \(r\sin\alpha\) replaced by a general \(f\); part 4 is an initial-value problem for a linear second-order ODE.

Rung 2

The geodesic Lagrangian gives \(\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -ff'\) and \(\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = f'/f\). For part 3, the curve \(\phi = \phi_0\), \(r = s\) has \(\ddot r = 0\) and \(\dot\phi = 0\); and \(\xi = \partial_\phi\) has length \(f\).

Rung 3

\(R^r{}_{\phi r\phi} = \partial_r(-ff') - \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = -(f'^2 + ff'') + f'^2\). For part 4, unit 01's uniqueness theorem applies to \(f'' + K_0 f = 0\) with two initial conditions.

Worked solution

Idea. In these coordinates the \(r\)-lines are a family of geodesics fanning out from a point, and \(f\) is literally the rate at which they separate. Curvature is the second derivative of that separation — which is the deviation equation, and which is why \(K = -f''/f\).

1. From \(L = \tfrac12(\dot r^2 + f^2\dot\phi^2)\): \(\ddot r - ff'\dot\phi^2 = 0\) and \(\ddot\phi + 2(f'/f)\dot r\dot\phi = 0\), so \(\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -ff'\), \(\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \Gamma^\phi_{\phi r} = f'/f\). Then \[ R^r{}_{\phi r\phi} = \partial_r\Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} - \partial_\phi\Gamma^r_{r\phi} + \Gamma^r_{r\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\phi\phi} - \Gamma^r_{\phi\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{r\phi} = -(f'^2 + ff'') - 0 + 0 - (-ff')\frac{f'}{f} = -ff'' . \] \(R_{r\phi r\phi} = g_{rr}R^r{}_{\phi r\phi} = -ff''\), \(\det g = f^2\), so \(K = -f''/f\). \(\square\)

2. \(f = r\): \(K = 0\), the plane. \(f = a\sin(r/a)\): \(f'' = -f/a^2\), \(K = 1/a^2\), the sphere with \(r\) the distance from the north pole. \(f = a\sinh(r/a)\): \(f'' = f/a^2\), \(K = -1/a^2\), the hyperbolic plane of P2 in polar form. \(f = r\sin\alpha\): \(K = 0\), the cone of P1.

3. For \(\gamma(s) = (r,\phi) = (s, \phi_0)\): \(\ddot r + \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi}\dot\phi^2 = 0\) ✓ and \(\ddot\phi + 2\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi}\dot r\dot\phi = 0\) ✓ since \(\dot\phi = 0\); and \(g(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma) = 1\). The family parameter is \(\phi_0\), so \(\xi = \partial_\phi\), orthogonal to \(u = \partial_r\), with length \(|\xi| = f\); for two members \(\Delta\phi\) apart, separation \(f(r)\Delta\phi\). By the deck's two-dimensional reduction, \(\mathrm D^2\xi/\mathrm ds^2 = -K\xi\) for \(\xi \perp u\) with \(|u| = 1\). In the orthonormal frame \((\partial_r, f^{-1}\partial_\phi)\), which is parallel along the \(r\)-lines — \(\nabla_r\partial_r = 0\) and \(\nabla_r(f^{-1}\partial_\phi) = -f'f^{-2}\partial_\phi + f^{-1}(f'/f)\partial_\phi = 0\) — \(\xi\) has the single component \(f\), so the equation reads \(f'' = -Kf\). The deviation equation and part 1 are one statement.

4. \(f'' + K_0f = 0\), \(f(0) = 0\), \(f'(0) = 1\) is a linear ODE with constant coefficients and two initial conditions; by unit 01's existence and uniqueness theorem it has exactly one solution, and \(f = \sin(\sqrt{K_0}\,r)/\sqrt{K_0}\) is one. So the metric is \(\mathrm dr^2 + K_0^{-1}\sin^2(\sqrt{K_0}\,r)\,\mathrm d\phi^2\): the sphere of radius \(1/\sqrt{K_0}\). Constant positive curvature determines the metric near a point.

Check. Dimensions: \(f\) is a length, \(f''/f\) is \(\mathrm L^{-2}\) ✓. Near \(r = 0\) every smooth case has \(f = r + O(r^3)\), which is P5's starting point, and the first three examples satisfy it ✓. The cone fails \(f'(0) = 1\) unless \(\alpha = 90^\circ\) — the smoothness condition is exactly what the apex violates.

P4 Foucault's pendulum, from curvature medium

exercises: holonomy equals enclosed curvature, orientation, a number unit 03 got another way

Model the Earth as a sphere turning once per sidereal day. Seen from an inertial frame, the pendulum's point of support is carried once round the circle of latitude \(\lambda\), and the horizontal direction of swing is parallel-transported along that circle. That last clause is the physical input, taken as given here; unit 03 reached the same motion through the Coriolis force.

  1. Using the theorem that transport round a positively oriented boundary rotates vectors by \(\int_S K\,\mathrm dA\), find the holonomy angle for the latitude circle traversed eastward, taking \(S\) to be the northern cap.
  2. The local frame (east, north) is itself carried round the circle. Find the angle, relative to that frame, through which the swing plane turns in one sidereal day, and its sense seen from above.
  3. Evaluate for the Panthéon: degrees per sidereal day, and the time for the swing plane to turn once relative to the building.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a cap area times \(1/R_\oplus^2\). Part 2 needs the relation between rotation relative to the transported vector and rotation relative to a frame that goes once round with the circle. Part 3 is two numbers.

Rung 2

The cap above colatitude \(\theta_0 = 90^\circ - \lambda\) has area \(2\pi R^2(1-\cos\theta_0)\). Eastward is anticlockwise seen from above the north pole, so the cap is on the left: positively oriented. For part 2, do the transport in the orthonormal frame as in P1 — the equations are the same with \(\sin\alpha\) replaced by \(\cos\theta_0\).

Rung 3

Holonomy \(= 2\pi(1-\cos\theta_0) = 2\pi(1 - \sin\lambda)\). In the frame, with \(A, B\) the south and east components, \(\mathrm dA/\mathrm d\phi = \cos\theta_0\,B\), \(\mathrm dB/\mathrm d\phi = -\cos\theta_0\,A\).

Worked solution

Idea. A rotation angle is only defined modulo \(2\pi\), and "relative to what" is the whole question. The curvature theorem gives the rotation relative to the starting vector; the Earth-bound observer measures it relative to a frame that has itself gone once round.

1. \(\int_{\text{cap}}K\,\mathrm dA = \frac{1}{R_\oplus^2}\cdot 2\pi R_\oplus^2(1-\cos\theta_0) = 2\pi(1-\sin\lambda)\). For Paris, \(\sin\lambda = 0.75295\), so this is \(1.5523\) rad \(= 88.94^\circ\), anticlockwise seen from above.

2. With unit 32's Christoffel symbols on the latitude circle (parameter \(\phi\)), \(\dot V^\theta = \sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0\,V^\phi\), \(\dot V^\phi = -\cot\theta_0\,V^\theta\). In orthonormal components \(A = R_\oplus V^\theta\) (south), \(B = R_\oplus\sin\theta_0\,V^\phi\) (east): \(\dot A = \cos\theta_0\,B\), \(\dot B = -\cos\theta_0\,A\). So relative to the (south, east) frame — equivalently the (east, north) frame, which differs by a fixed rotation — the vector turns at rate \(\cos\theta_0 = \sin\lambda\), clockwise seen from above, through \[ -2\pi\sin\lambda \text{ per sidereal day}. \] Consistency with part 1: \(-2\pi\sin\lambda = 2\pi(1-\sin\lambda) - 2\pi\), the same rotation modulo a full turn ✓. The extra \(-2\pi\) is the frame's own turn: on a tiny circle round the pole the cap, and so the holonomy, shrink to nothing, yet the east direction still swings once round as it circles the pole.

3. \(360^\circ\times0.75295 = 271.06^\circ\) per sidereal day, clockwise seen from above. A full turn relative to the building takes \(86\,164.09\ \mathrm s/0.75295 = 1.1443\times10^5\) s \(= 31.79\) h — unit 03's Coriolis answer, obtained with no force at all.

Check. At the pole, \(\sin\lambda = 1\): one full turn per sidereal day, the Earth simply turning under an inertial swing plane ✓. At the equator: no turning, and the cap is a hemisphere with \(\int K\,\mathrm dA = 2\pi\), a full turn, invisible ✓. In the southern hemisphere \(\sin\lambda < 0\) and the sense reverses ✓. Dimensions: angles and times only.

P5 Circles fall short medium

exercises: Taylor expansion of a Jacobi field, curvature as a measurable deficit, a number on the Earth

  1. In the geodesic polar form of P3, the geodesic circle of radius \(r\) about the origin has circumference \(C(r) = 2\pi f(r)\). Show that \[ C(r) = 2\pi r\Big(1 - \frac{K(0)}{6}r^2 + o(r^2)\Big), \] where \(K(0)\) is the curvature at the centre, and hence that \(K(0) = \frac{3}{\pi}\lim_{r\to0}\frac{2\pi r - C(r)}{r^3}\).
  2. A surveyor on a spherical Earth walks out \(1000\) km in every direction from a point. What is the exact circumference of the circle, how far short of \(2\pi r\) does it fall, and what does the leading term predict?
  3. If the surveyor used the measured shortfall and the limit formula at \(r = 1000\) km to estimate \(K\), how far off would the implied radius of the Earth be?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

You need \(f\) to third order at \(r = 0\). Everything you need is in P3: the initial conditions and the equation \(f'' = -Kf\).

Rung 2

\(f(0) = 0\), \(f'(0) = 1\), \(f''(0) = -K(0)f(0) = 0\). Differentiate \(f'' = -Kf\) once more and evaluate at \(0\). For part 2 the sphere has \(f = R\sin(r/R)\) exactly.

Rung 3

\(f''' = -K'f - Kf'\), so \(f'''(0) = -K(0)\), and Taylor's theorem gives \(f(r) = r - K(0)r^3/6 + o(r^3)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Radial geodesics from a point separate like \(f\), and positive curvature makes them reconverge (the meridians of the deck), so circles are shorter than Euclid says. The deficit is cubic in \(r\) with coefficient \(K/6\): curvature can be measured with a tape measure, from inside, which is Gauss's point.

1. In this form \(K = -f''/f\) is a function of \(r\), and \(K(0)\) is its value at the centre. From \(f'' = -Kf\): \(f''(0) = 0\); \(f''' = -K'f - Kf'\), so \(f'''(0) = -K(0)\). Taylor to third order: \[ f(r) = r - \frac{K(0)}{6}r^3 + o(r^3), \qquad C(r) = 2\pi r\Big(1 - \frac{K(0)}{6}r^2 + o(r^2)\Big). \] Rearranging, \((2\pi r - C)/r^3 = \pi K(0)/3 + o(1)\), which is the limit formula. \(\square\)

2. \(C = 2\pi R_\oplus\sin(r/R_\oplus)\) with \(r/R_\oplus = 0.15696\): \(C = 6257.42\) km against \(2\pi r = 6283.19\) km, a shortfall of \(25.77\) km, or \(0.41\%\). The Christoffel symbols are P3's with \(f = r\): \(\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r\), \(\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = 1/r\). So \(\nabla_r\partial_r = 0\), \(\nabla_\theta\partial_r = \nabla_r\partial_\theta = r^{-1}\partial_\theta\).

Rung 3

\(\nabla_YZ = r^{-1}\nabla_\theta\partial_r = r^{-2}\partial_\theta\); now apply \(\nabla_X = \nabla_r\) with the Leibniz rule, since the coefficient \(r^{-2}\) is not constant.

Worked solution

Idea. The bare commutator of \(\nabla_X\) and \(\nabla_Y\) detects curvature and also the failure of the fields \(X, Y\) themselves to commute. The bracket term removes the second.

1. \(\nabla_YZ = r^{-2}\partial_\theta\). Then \(\nabla_X(r^{-2}\partial_\theta) = (\partial_r r^{-2})\partial_\theta + r^{-2}\nabla_r\partial_\theta = -2r^{-3}\partial_\theta + r^{-3}\partial_\theta = -r^{-3}\partial_\theta\). And \(\nabla_XZ = \nabla_r\partial_r = 0\), so \(\nabla_Y\nabla_XZ = 0\). The bare commutator is \(-r^{-3}\partial_\theta \ne 0\).

2. \([X,Y]f = \partial_r(r^{-1}\partial_\theta f) - r^{-1}\partial_\theta\partial_r f = -r^{-2}\partial_\theta f\), so \([X,Y] = -r^{-2}\partial_\theta\). Then \(\nabla_{[X,Y]}Z = -r^{-2}\nabla_\theta\partial_r = -r^{-3}\partial_\theta\), and \[ R(X,Y)Z = -r^{-3}\partial_\theta - (-r^{-3}\partial_\theta) = 0 . \]

3. \(X\) and \(Y\) are not coordinate fields — their flows do not commute, because the arc a unit step in \(\theta\) covers depends on \(r\) — and the bare commutator measures that failure as well as any curvature.

Check. Size: \(|r^{-3}\partial_\theta| = r^{-3}\cdot r = r^{-2}\), dimension \(\mathrm L^{-2}\) — that of curvature, which is why the impostor is convincing ✓. With the coordinate fields \(X = \partial_r\), \(Y = \partial_\theta\) instead, the bracket vanishes and the bare commutator is \(\nabla_r(r^{-1}\partial_\theta) - 0 = -r^{-2}\partial_\theta + r^{-2}\partial_\theta = 0\) directly ✓.

P12 Holonomy of a small loop, every index hard

exercises: iterating an integral equation to second order, signed area, the sign conventions checked on the sphere

  1. Parallel-transport \(V\) from \(p\) round the coordinate parallelogram \(p \to p+\delta a \to p+\delta a+\delta b \to p+\delta b \to p\). Prove that it returns as \(V + \Delta V\) with \(\Delta V^\rho = -R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}V^\sigma\delta a^\mu\delta b^\nu + O(\delta^3)\).
  2. On \(S^2_a\), take \(\delta a = \delta\theta\,\partial_\theta\), \(\delta b = \delta\phi\,\partial_\phi\) at colatitude \(\theta\). Compute \(\Delta V\) in the orthonormal frame \((\hat e_\theta, \hat e_\phi)\) and show it is a rotation by \(K\) times the area of the parallelogram. Is the loop positively oriented?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a Taylor expansion of the transport equation round a closed curve, kept to second order in the edge lengths; the answer's shape is fixed, what matters is the sign. Part 2 is two components and an area.

Rung 2

\(\Delta V^\rho = -\oint\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}(x)V^\sigma(x)\,\mathrm dx^\mu\). Expand \(\Gamma\) to first order about \(p\), and \(V(x)\) to first order using the transport equation itself. The zeroth-order term integrates to zero round a closed loop.

Rung 3

With \(\delta x = x - p\): \(\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}(x)V^\sigma(x) = \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}V^\sigma + \big(\partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma}\big)V^\sigma\delta x^\nu + O(\delta^2)\), and \(\oint\delta x^\nu\,\mathrm dx^\mu = \delta a^\nu\delta b^\mu - \delta a^\mu\delta b^\nu\).

Worked solution

Idea. Going round a loop, the zeroth-order effect of \(\Gamma\) cancels (out and back), and what survives is the first-order variation of \(\Gamma V\) integrated against the enclosed signed area — antisymmetric, so only the antisymmetrised derivative survives, which is \(R\).

1. Transport is \(\mathrm dV^\rho = -\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}V^\sigma\,\mathrm dx^\mu\); integrating round the loop, \(\Delta V^\rho = -\oint\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}(x)V^\sigma(x)\,\mathrm dx^\mu\). To first order in \(\delta x = x - p\), \(\Gamma(x) = \Gamma + (\partial_\nu\Gamma)\delta x^\nu\) and \(V^\sigma(x) = V^\sigma - \Gamma^\sigma_{\nu\lambda}V^\lambda\delta x^\nu\), everything on the right at \(p\). Multiplying, \[ \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}(x)V^\sigma(x) = \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}V^\sigma + X^\rho{}_{\nu\mu}\,\delta x^\nu + O(\delta^2), \qquad X^\rho{}_{\nu\mu} = \big(\partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma}\big)V^\sigma . \] The constant term gives \(\oint\mathrm dx^\mu = 0\). The \(O(\delta^2)\) terms integrate to \(O(\delta^3)\) since the loop has length \(O(\delta)\). So \(\Delta V^\rho = -X^\rho{}_{\nu\mu}\oint\delta x^\nu\,\mathrm dx^\mu + O(\delta^3)\).

The loop integral, leg by leg (\(\delta x\) is \(t\,\delta a\), then \(\delta a + t\,\delta b\), then \(\delta b + (1-t)\delta a\), then \((1-t)\delta b\), with \(\mathrm dx\) equal to \(\delta a\,\mathrm dt\), \(\delta b\,\mathrm dt\), \(-\delta a\,\mathrm dt\), \(-\delta b\,\mathrm dt\)): \[ \oint\delta x^\nu\mathrm dx^\mu = \tfrac12\delta a^\nu\delta a^\mu + \big(\delta a^\nu + \tfrac12\delta b^\nu\big)\delta b^\mu - \big(\delta b^\nu + \tfrac12\delta a^\nu\big)\delta a^\mu - \tfrac12\delta b^\nu\delta b^\mu = \delta a^\nu\delta b^\mu - \delta b^\nu\delta a^\mu . \] Call it \(S^{\nu\mu}\); it is antisymmetric, so only the antisymmetric part of \(X\) in \(\nu\mu\) survives: \(X^\rho{}_{\nu\mu}S^{\nu\mu} = \tfrac12(X^\rho{}_{\nu\mu} - X^\rho{}_{\mu\nu})S^{\nu\mu}\), and \(X^\rho{}_{\nu\mu} - X^\rho{}_{\mu\nu} = \big(\partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} - \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma}\big)V^\sigma = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\nu\mu}V^\sigma\). Hence \[ \Delta V^\rho = -\tfrac12R^\rho{}_{\sigma\nu\mu}V^\sigma\big(\delta a^\nu\delta b^\mu - \delta a^\mu\delta b^\nu\big) = -R^\rho{}_{\sigma\nu\mu}V^\sigma\delta a^\nu\delta b^\mu = -R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}V^\sigma\delta a^\mu\delta b^\nu, \] using S1 on the second half and renaming dummies. \(\square\)

2. Only \(\mu\nu = \theta\phi\) contributes. With the deck's \(R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi} = \sin^2\theta\) and \(R^\phi{}_{\theta\theta\phi} = -R^\phi{}_{\theta\phi\theta} = -1\): \(\Delta V^\theta = -\sin^2\theta\,V^\phi\,\delta\theta\,\delta\phi\), \(\Delta V^\phi = +V^\theta\,\delta\theta\,\delta\phi\). In orthonormal components \(A = aV^\theta\), \(B = a\sin\theta\,V^\phi\): \[ \Delta A = -\sin\theta\,\delta\theta\,\delta\phi\;B, \qquad \Delta B = +\sin\theta\,\delta\theta\,\delta\phi\;A , \] an anticlockwise rotation in the \((\hat e_\theta,\hat e_\phi)\) plane by \(\epsilon = \sin\theta\,\delta\theta\,\delta\phi\). The area is \(a^2\sin\theta\,\delta\theta\,\delta\phi\) and \(K = 1/a^2\), so \(\epsilon = K\times\text{area}\) ✓. And \(\hat e_\theta\times\hat e_\phi = \hat e_r\), the outward normal, so going first along \(\hat e_\theta\) then \(\hat e_\phi\) is positively oriented, and the rotation is positive — as the deck's octant example found on the large scale.

Check. Dimensions: \(R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}\delta a^\mu\delta b^\nu\) is dimensionless in any coordinates, since the two \(\delta\)'s carry the units the two lower indices remove ✓. Flat space: \(R = 0\), no holonomy at second order, and in fact none at all ✓. Reversing the loop swaps \(\delta a\) and \(\delta b\) and flips the sign ✓.
