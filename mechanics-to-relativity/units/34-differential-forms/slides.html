34 · overview

One algebra, one derivative, one theorem

1 · the algebra

A k-form is an alternating multilinear map on tangent vectors. The wedge product makes them an algebra; antisymmetry is what makes a determinant.

2 · the derivative

One operator $\mathrm d$, fixed by three axioms, with $\mathrm d^2 = 0$. Grad, curl and divergence are $\mathrm d$ in degrees $0, 1, 2$.

3 · the theorem

$\int_M \mathrm d\omega = \int_{\partial M}\omega$. Unit 04's gradient theorem, Green, unit 23's curl theorem and unit 05's divergence theorem are its cases $n = 1, 2, 2, 3$.

4 · the holes

$\mathrm d\omega = 0$ does not give $\omega = \mathrm d\alpha$. The failure is de Rham cohomology, and it is a number: unit 04's $2\pi$.

5 · the metric

The Hodge star is the only place a metric is needed. With it, $F$ obeys $\mathrm dF = 0$ and $\mathrm d\star F = \mu_0\star J$.

6 · the payoff

Cartan's structure equations: unit 33's curvature, from a coframe, in a fraction of the arithmetic.

Antisymmetry, and why the object that measures oriented volume is the only one worth integrating.

34 · algebra

Why antisymmetric, and not something else

Unit 16 defined a $(0,k)$-tensor as a multilinear map $V^k\to\mathbb R$ and showed that symmetry and antisymmetry are the two pieces that survive a change of basis. Here is the physical reason to keep the antisymmetric piece.

A thing to be integrated over a $k$-dimensional patch must eat $k$ edge vectors and return a number that scales like the oriented volume they span. Two demands follow at once:

  • multilinear — doubling an edge doubles the patch;
  • zero when two edges coincide — a degenerate patch has no volume.

And "zero on repeats" is antisymmetry: expand $T(u+v,u+v) = 0$ to get $T(u,v) = -T(v,u)$.

the precedent

$\det$ is the unique alternating $n$-linear function of $n$ column vectors with $\det\mathbb 1 = 1$. Unit 05's change-of-variables Jacobian was already this object; so was unit 08's cross product, and unit 16's angular velocity, which is an antisymmetric matrix wearing a vector's clothes.

The expansion, in full. Suppose $T$ is bilinear and $T(w,w) = 0$ for every $w$. Then $0 = T(u+v,u+v) = T(u,u) + T(u,v) + T(v,u) + T(v,v) = T(u,v) + T(v,u)$. Conversely if $T(u,v) = -T(v,u)$ then $T(w,w) = -T(w,w)$, so $2T(w,w) = 0$ and $T(w,w) = 0$ — over $\mathbb R$, where $2 \ne 0$. Over a field of characteristic 2 the two conditions part company and "alternating" means the stronger one; this course never leaves $\mathbb R$.

Where the symmetric piece went. It is not lost, it is elsewhere. The metric of unit 32 is a symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor, the inertia tensor of unit 16 and the Maxwell stress tensor of unit 24 are symmetric, and none of them is integrated over a surface as such — what gets integrated is a contraction of them with a normal, which is a one-form, which is antisymmetric trivially. The division of labour is clean: symmetric tensors measure states, antisymmetric ones measure fluxes through patches.

History. Grassmann published the Ausdehnungslehre in 1844 with essentially this algebra and almost no readers; the 1862 rewrite fared little better. Poincaré (1899) and then Cartan (1899, 1901) made the exterior derivative the centre of the subject, and de Rham's 1931 thesis proved the theorem connecting it to topology. Physics adopted it late: Misner, Thorne and Wheeler (1973) is where most physicists first met it.

34 · algebra

$\Lambda^k V^*$, defined

definition

Let $V$ be a real vector space of dimension $n$. A k-covector on $V$ is a map $\alpha : V^k \to \mathbb R$ that is linear in each argument and satisfies $\alpha(v_{\sigma(1)},\dots,v_{\sigma(k)}) = \operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\,\alpha(v_1,\dots,v_k)$ for every permutation $\sigma$. They form a vector space $\Lambda^kV^*$, the k-th exterior power. By convention $\Lambda^0V^* = \mathbb R$ and $\Lambda^1V^* = V^*$.

basis and dimension

If $e^1,\dots,e^n$ is a basis of $V^*$ then $\{\,e^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k} : i_1 < \cdots < i_k\,\}$ is a basis of $\Lambda^kV^*$, so $$\dim\Lambda^kV^* = \binom nk ,$$ zero for $k > n$.

check it

$n = 4$: dimensions $1,4,6,4,1$, total $16 = 2^4$. The six is the number of independent components of an antisymmetric $4\times4$ array — three of $\vec E$ and three of $\vec B$, which example 1 assembles into one object.

Full proof of the basis claim (the wedge is defined on the next slide; take its properties as given for a moment). Spanning. A $k$-covector is determined by its values on basis $k$-tuples $(e_{i_1},\dots,e_{i_k})$, by multilinearity. Antisymmetry makes the value vanish when two indices repeat and fixes it up to a sign under reordering, so the values on increasing tuples determine $\alpha$. If $\alpha_{i_1\cdots i_k} := \alpha(e_{i_1},\dots,e_{i_k})$ then $\alpha = \sum_{i_1<\cdots<i_k}\alpha_{i_1\cdots i_k}\, e^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k}$, because both sides agree on every increasing tuple: $(e^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k})(e_{j_1},\dots,e_{j_k}) = \delta^{i_1\cdots i_k}_{j_1\cdots j_k}$, which is $1$ if the increasing tuples match and $0$ otherwise. Independence. Pair a vanishing combination with the tuple $(e_{j_1},\dots,e_{j_k})$ and read off that the coefficient of $e^{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{j_k}$ is zero.

Why $k > n$ gives zero. Any $k$ vectors with $k > n$ are linearly dependent, so one is a combination of the others; expand by multilinearity and every term repeats an argument. This is the statement that an $n$-dimensional space has no oriented $(n+1)$-volume, and on spacetime it is why there are no five-forms and why $\mathrm d$ of a four-form is automatically zero.

Dimension check. $\sum_k\binom nk = 2^n$: the exterior algebra $\Lambda V^* = \bigoplus_k\Lambda^kV^*$ has dimension $2^n$, which for $n = 4$ is 16 — the same as the $4\times4$ array of a general $(0,2)$-tensor, a coincidence of small numbers and nothing more.

The wedge product

definition · determinant convention

For $\alpha\in\Lambda^kV^*$ and $\beta\in\Lambda^lV^*$, $$(\alpha\wedge\beta)(v_1,\dots,v_{k+l}) = \sum_{\sigma\in\mathrm{Sh}(k,l)}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\, \alpha(v_{\sigma(1)},\dots,v_{\sigma(k)})\,\beta(v_{\sigma(k+1)},\dots,v_{\sigma(k+l)}),$$ the sum running over $(k,l)$-shuffles: permutations increasing on $1..k$ and on $k{+}1..k{+}l$.

bilinear, associative

$(\alpha\wedge\beta)\wedge\gamma = \alpha\wedge(\beta\wedge\gamma)$, so brackets may be dropped.

graded commutative

$\alpha\wedge\beta = (-1)^{kl}\beta\wedge\alpha$. One-forms anticommute; a two-form commutes with everything of even degree.

the normalisation

$(e^1\wedge\cdots\wedge e^n)(v_1,\dots,v_n) = \det[\,v^i_j\,]$, with no factorial. That is what the shuffle sum buys.

two conventions exist

Some texts define $\alpha\wedge\beta = \frac{(k+l)!}{k!\,l!}\operatorname{Alt}(\alpha\otimes\beta)$ — the same thing — and others drop the factorial, which puts a $1/k!$ into every determinant. This course uses the determinant convention above, always. A formula copied from elsewhere may differ by $k!\,l!/(k+l)!$.

Full proof of graded commutativity. It suffices to check it on basis elements, by bilinearity. Moving $e^{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{j_l}$ past $e^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge e^{i_k}$ means moving each of the $l$ factors past each of the $k$ factors, one transposition at a time, which is $kl$ transpositions and hence a sign $(-1)^{kl}$. For $k = l = 1$ this is $e^i\wedge e^j = -e^j\wedge e^i$, and setting $i = j$ gives $e^i\wedge e^i = 0$.

An immediate corollary. If $k$ is odd, $\alpha\wedge\alpha = -\alpha\wedge\alpha = 0$. If $k$ is even this is false: on $\mathbb R^4$, $(e^1\wedge e^2 + e^3\wedge e^4)^{\wedge 2} = 2\,e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3\wedge e^4 \ne 0$. Section 5's invariant $F\wedge F$ is exactly a case where this matters, and section 6's symplectic form is another: $\omega^{\wedge n}$ being nonzero is the definition of nondegeneracy.

Associativity is the one tedious check. With the shuffle convention, both $(\alpha\wedge\beta)\wedge\gamma$ and $\alpha\wedge(\beta\wedge\gamma)$ equal the sum over $(k,l,m)$-shuffles of $\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\alpha(\cdot)\beta(\cdot)\gamma(\cdot)$; the combinatorial identity needed is that every $(k+l,m)$-shuffle composed with a $(k,l)$-shuffle of the first block arises exactly once as a $(k,l,m)$-shuffle, and signs multiply. Cited: Lee, Introduction to Smooth Manifolds, 2nd ed., ch. 14, the proposition listing the properties of the wedge product.

The normalisation, checked. For $k = l = 1$ the shuffles of $(1,1)$ are the identity and the transposition, so $(e^1\wedge e^2)(v_1,v_2) = v^1_1v^2_2 - v^1_2v^2_1 = \det$ of the $2\times 2$ matrix of components. The general case is the Leibniz expansion of a determinant, term by term.

Forms on a manifold

definition

A differential k-form on a smooth manifold $M$ is a smooth section of $\Lambda^kT^*M$: an assignment $p\mapsto\omega_p\in\Lambda^kT_p^*M$ with smooth components in every chart. Write $\Omega^k(M)$ for the space of them. In a chart, $$\omega = \tfrac1{k!}\,\omega_{i_1\cdots i_k}\,\mathrm dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^{i_k} = \!\!\sum_{i_1<\cdots<i_k}\!\!\omega_{i_1\cdots i_k}\,\mathrm dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^{i_k}.$$

Unit 31 built $T^*_pM$, the dual basis $\mathrm dx^i$, and what "smooth section" means. A $k$-form is a $(0,k)$-tensor field that happens to be antisymmetric — nothing new is being assumed, only a subspace being named.

degrees you have already met

$\Omega^0$: functions.
$\Omega^1$: $\mathrm df$ from unit 31, and $\sum p_i\,\mathrm dq_i$ from unit 14.
$\Omega^2$: unit 14's $\omega = \sum\mathrm dp_i\wedge\mathrm dq_i$ — written with a wedge, thirty units before the wedge was defined.
$\Omega^n$: the thing you integrate.

The two displayed sums are the same form. In the first, the indices run independently and the $1/k!$ compensates for counting each increasing tuple $k!$ times; in the second they increase and there is no factor. Physics papers use the first, mathematics books the second. Mixing them silently is the commonest source of stray factorials in this subject.

What is not new. Unit 31 defined tensor fields as sections of bundles of multilinear maps and proved that "transforms like a tensor" is a theorem about change of basis. Everything here inherits that. In particular a $k$-form's components transform with $k$ copies of the inverse Jacobian, and the antisymmetry is basis-independent because permuting arguments commutes with changing basis.

Unit 14, in hindsight. That unit wrote the symplectic form as $\omega = \sum_i\mathrm dp_i\wedge\mathrm dq_i$ and defined it by the bilinear formula $\omega(u,v) = \sum_i(u_{p_i}v_{q_i} - u_{q_i}v_{p_i})$, which is exactly the shuffle definition for $k = l = 1$ summed over $i$. Nothing said there needs revising; section 6 only replaces a two-page computation by one line.

Worked example 1 — the field as a two-form

example 1 · building $F$

Unit 24's potentials are $\varphi$ and $\vec A$, with $\vec E = -\nabla\varphi - \partial_t\vec A$ and $\vec B = \nabla\times\vec A$. Assemble the potential one-form $A = A_i\,\mathrm dx^i - \varphi\,\mathrm dt$ and set $F = \mathrm dA$. Then

$$\mathrm dA = \underbrace{\partial_jA_i\,\mathrm dx^j\wedge\mathrm dx^i}_{\text{space--space}} \;+\;\underbrace{\partial_tA_i\,\mathrm dt\wedge\mathrm dx^i - \partial_i\varphi\,\mathrm dx^i\wedge\mathrm dt}_{\text{space--time}} .$$

Space–space. $\partial_jA_i\,\mathrm dx^j\wedge\mathrm dx^i = \tfrac12(\partial_jA_i-\partial_iA_j)\mathrm dx^j\wedge\mathrm dx^i$, whose $\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$ coefficient is $\partial_xA_y-\partial_yA_x = B_z$; likewise for the other two.

Space–time. $\partial_tA_i\,\mathrm dt\wedge\mathrm dx^i - \partial_i\varphi\,\mathrm dx^i\wedge\mathrm dt = -(\partial_tA_i+\partial_i\varphi)\,\mathrm dx^i\wedge\mathrm dt = E_i\,\mathrm dx^i\wedge\mathrm dt .$

the field two-form $$F = B_x\,\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz + B_y\,\mathrm dz\wedge\mathrm dx + B_z\,\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy + (E_x\,\mathrm dx+E_y\,\mathrm dy+E_z\,\mathrm dz)\wedge\mathrm dt$$

Dimensions. $[B\,\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz] = \mathrm{T\,m^2}$ and $[E\,\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dt] = (\mathrm{V/m})\,\mathrm{m\,s} = \mathrm{V\,s} = \mathrm{Wb} = \mathrm{T\,m^2}$: the two halves agree, as they must to be added. Special case. Electrostatics, $\vec B = 0$ and $\vec E$ static: $F = E_i\,\mathrm dx^i\wedge\mathrm dt$, six components down to three.

The step that looks like a trick. $\partial_jA_i\,\mathrm dx^j\wedge\mathrm dx^i$ — the indices are summed independently, and $\mathrm dx^j\wedge\mathrm dx^i$ is antisymmetric in $(j,i)$, so only the antisymmetric part of $\partial_jA_i$ survives. Replacing a coefficient by its antisymmetric part when it is contracted with something antisymmetric is the single manipulation this whole unit runs on; it is unit 16's symmetric/antisymmetric decomposition, used as a computational rule.

Checking $B_z$. The $\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$ terms of $\partial_jA_i\,\mathrm dx^j\wedge\mathrm dx^i$ are $\partial_xA_y\,\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$ and $\partial_yA_x\,\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dx = -\partial_yA_x\,\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$, so the coefficient is $\partial_xA_y - \partial_yA_x = (\nabla\times\vec A)_z = B_z$. The cyclic pattern $\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz,\ \mathrm dz\wedge\mathrm dx,\ \mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$ carries $B_x, B_y, B_z$ — the same cyclic pattern as the flux integrals of unit 05.

In index form, for readers who will meet it elsewhere: $F = \tfrac12F_{\mu\nu}\mathrm dx^\mu\wedge\mathrm dx^\nu$ with $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$, $A_0 = -\varphi/c$, $A_i$ the vector potential, and $x^0 = ct$. This gives $F_{0i} = -E_i/c$ and $F_{ij} = \epsilon_{ijk}B_k$. Everything on this slide can be read off that, and the slide's version is what a computation actually uses.

A remark worth keeping. $F$ is defined here without any metric, without any orientation, and without any choice of frame. Nothing so far distinguishes the electric from the magnetic part — that split is a choice of time coordinate, and section 5 will show it is exactly the choice that $\star$ depends on. Unit 28, which this unit does not need, reaches the same object as the tensor $F_{\mu\nu}$ from the Lorentz transformation of fields; here it is assembled from potentials and a wedge, and its six components are the ones listed above.

The exterior derivative, axiomatically

theorem · existence and uniqueness

There is exactly one family of $\mathbb R$-linear maps $\mathrm d:\Omega^k(M)\to\Omega^{k+1}(M)$, the exterior derivative, with

  1. on functions, $\mathrm df$ is the differential of unit 31;
  2. $\mathrm d(\alpha\wedge\beta) = \mathrm d\alpha\wedge\beta + (-1)^k\alpha\wedge\mathrm d\beta$ for $\alpha\in\Omega^k$;
  3. $\mathrm d\circ\mathrm d = 0$.

In any chart it is $\;\mathrm d\big(f\,\mathrm dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^{i_k}\big) = \mathrm df\wedge\mathrm dx^{i_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^{i_k}$.

uniqueness, on the slide

$\mathrm d$ is local (notes). In a chart write $\omega = \sum f_I\,\mathrm dx^I$. Axiom 2 gives $\mathrm d(f_I\,\mathrm dx^I) = \mathrm df_I\wedge\mathrm dx^I + f_I\,\mathrm d(\mathrm dx^I)$, and $\mathrm d(\mathrm dx^I) = 0$ by axioms 2 and 3 applied factor by factor. So the chart formula is forced.

$\mathrm d$ is dimensionless

Count each $\mathrm dx$ as a length: $\mathrm d = \partial_\mu\,\mathrm dx^\mu$ divides by a length and multiplies by one. So $[\mathrm d\omega] = [\omega]$ — and $[F] = [\mathrm dA] = [A] = \mathrm{Wb}$, which is the first check on every equation in this unit.

Full proof of existence. Define $\mathrm d$ in a chart by the displayed formula. It is $\mathbb R$-linear. Axiom 1 holds by inspection ($k = 0$). Axiom 3: for $\omega = f\,\mathrm dx^I$, $\mathrm d\mathrm d\omega = \mathrm d(\partial_jf\,\mathrm dx^j\wedge\mathrm dx^I) = \partial_i\partial_jf\,\mathrm dx^i\wedge\mathrm dx^j\wedge\mathrm dx^I$, and $\partial_i\partial_jf$ is symmetric in $(i,j)$ by Clairaut (unit 03) while $\mathrm dx^i\wedge\mathrm dx^j$ is antisymmetric, so the contraction vanishes term by term. Axiom 2: for $\alpha = f\,\mathrm dx^I$, $\beta = g\,\mathrm dx^J$, $\mathrm d(\alpha\wedge\beta) = \mathrm d(fg)\wedge\mathrm dx^I\wedge\mathrm dx^J = (g\,\mathrm df + f\,\mathrm dg)\wedge\mathrm dx^I\wedge\mathrm dx^J$, and moving $\mathrm dg$ past $\mathrm dx^I$ costs $(-1)^k$. Finally, two charts give the same answer because both satisfy the axioms on the overlap and uniqueness applies there.

Why $\mathrm d$ is local. If $\omega$ vanishes on an open $W$ and $p\in W$, pick a bump function $\chi$ supported in $W$ with $\chi(p) = 1$ (unit 31, partitions of unity). Then $\chi\omega = 0$, so $0 = \mathrm d(\chi\omega) = \mathrm d\chi\wedge\omega + \chi\,\mathrm d\omega$; evaluate at $p$, where $\omega_p = 0$ and $\chi(p) = 1$, to get $(\mathrm d\omega)_p = 0$. Hence $\mathrm d\omega$ on $W$ depends only on $\omega$ on $W$, which is what "compute it in a chart" requires.

Axiom 3 is not an extra assumption in disguise. It is equivalent, given the others, to $\mathrm d\mathrm df = 0$ for functions, which is Clairaut. That is the whole content: the single most consequential identity in this unit is the symmetry of second partial derivatives. Magnetic flux conservation, charge conservation and the existence of gauge freedom all come from it, as the rest of this deck shows.

Grad, curl and divergence are one operator

On $\mathbb R^3$ with the Euclidean metric and the standard orientation, write $\vec v^\flat = v_i\,\mathrm dx^i$ for the one-form of a vector field (unit 32's lowering) and $\star$ for section 5's star. Then the whole of unit 04's and unit 05's differential calculus is:

degree$\mathrm d$ doesclassical name
$\Omega^0\to\Omega^1$$f\mapsto\mathrm df$gradient, $(\mathrm df)^\sharp = \nabla f$
$\Omega^1\to\Omega^2$$\vec v^\flat\mapsto\mathrm d\vec v^\flat$curl, $\ \nabla\times\vec v = (\star\,\mathrm d\,\vec v^\flat)^\sharp$
$\Omega^2\to\Omega^3$$\star\vec v^\flat\mapsto\mathrm d{\star}\vec v^\flat$divergence, $\ (\nabla\cdot\vec v)\,\mathrm{vol} = \mathrm d{\star}\vec v^\flat$
two identities, one line

$\nabla\times\nabla f = 0$ is $\mathrm d\mathrm df = 0$.
$\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec v) = 0$ is $\mathrm d(\star\star\mathrm d\vec v^\flat) = \mathrm d\mathrm d\vec v^\flat = 0$, using $\star\star = 1$ in Euclidean $\mathbb R^3$.

the accident of three dimensions

Curl exists because $\binom32 = 3$: a two-form on $\mathbb R^3$ has as many components as a vector. In four dimensions $\binom42 = 6$ and there is no curl of a vector field — only $\mathrm d$, which is why $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ had to merge.

Verifying the curl entry. $\vec v^\flat = v_1\mathrm dx + v_2\mathrm dy + v_3\mathrm dz$, so $\mathrm d\vec v^\flat = (\partial_1v_2-\partial_2v_1)\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy + (\partial_2v_3-\partial_3v_2)\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz + (\partial_3v_1-\partial_1v_3)\mathrm dz\wedge\mathrm dx$. The Euclidean star sends $\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz\mapsto\mathrm dx$ and cyclically, so $\star\mathrm d\vec v^\flat = (\nabla\times\vec v)_i\,\mathrm dx^i$, and raising gives the vector.

Verifying the divergence entry. $\star\vec v^\flat = v_1\,\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz + v_2\,\mathrm dz\wedge\mathrm dx + v_3\,\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$ — the flux two-form of unit 05. Its exterior derivative is $(\partial_1v_1+\partial_2v_2+\partial_3v_3)\,\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz$. Unit 05 wrote the same object as $\vec v\cdot\mathrm d\vec S$ and called the integral a flux; the two-form is what $\mathrm d\vec S$ always meant.

Why the identities were ever separate. Unit 04 proved $\nabla\times\nabla f = 0$ by Clairaut and unit 23 proved $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec v) = 0$ by Clairaut, twice, with different index bookkeeping. They are the same theorem in degrees 0 and 1. A useful rule of thumb: any vector identity whose proof is "the mixed partials cancel" is $\mathrm d^2 = 0$ in disguise, and the problem sheet's d squared in disguise makes that precise for Maxwell.

The curl is a two-form, and that settles unit 04's anomaly. Unit 04 noticed that the curl does not transform like a vector under a reflection. Here is why: the object $\mathrm d\vec v^\flat$ is a two-form and transforms as one under every smooth map, reflections included. Turning it into a vector takes $\star$, and $\star$ depends on the orientation (section 5): reverse the orientation and $\star$ changes sign. So $\nabla\times\vec v$ picks up an extra minus under a reflection, which is all "pseudovector" ever meant. The cross product and angular momentum are the same story.

Laplacian. On functions, $\star\,\mathrm d\star\mathrm df = \nabla^2f$, unit 05's operator. The generalisation to forms is the Hodge Laplacian $\Delta = \mathrm d\delta + \delta\mathrm d$ with $\delta = \pm\star\mathrm d\star$; it is not needed here and is named only so that the notation is not a surprise later.

Pullback, and why forms are worth the trouble

definition

For a smooth $\phi:N\to M$ and $\omega\in\Omega^k(M)$, the pullback $\phi^*\omega\in\Omega^k(N)$ is $$(\phi^*\omega)_p(v_1,\dots,v_k) = \omega_{\phi(p)}\big(\mathrm d\phi_p v_1,\dots,\mathrm d\phi_p v_k\big).$$ Unit 31 defined it for tensors; nothing is new except the name for the antisymmetric case.

naturality

$\phi^*(\alpha\wedge\beta) = \phi^*\alpha\wedge\phi^*\beta$ and $$\boxed{\ \phi^*(\mathrm d\omega) = \mathrm d(\phi^*\omega)\ }$$ for every smooth $\phi$ — no assumption that $\phi$ is a diffeomorphism, and no metric anywhere.

proof

On functions, $\phi^*(\mathrm df) = \mathrm d(f\circ\phi)$ is the chain rule. Both $\omega\mapsto\phi^*\mathrm d\omega$ and $\omega\mapsto\mathrm d\phi^*\omega$ are linear and satisfy the same Leibniz rule, and every form is locally a sum of $f\,\mathrm dg^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dg^k$; on such a form both sides give $\mathrm d(f\circ\phi)\wedge\mathrm d(g^1\circ\phi)\wedge\cdots$. $\square$

what it buys

Change of variables costs nothing: pull back and differentiate in either order. Compare unit 05, where every change of coordinates produced a Jacobian to be tracked by hand, and unit 31, where the same content needed the chain rule written out per index.

Why this is the load-bearing property. A vector field cannot be pulled back — you can push it forward only along a diffeomorphism — and a $(1,1)$-tensor cannot be pulled back at all. Forms can, always, along any smooth map: restriction to a submanifold, projection, a flow, a coordinate change, a degenerate map that collapses dimensions. That, plus naturality of $\mathrm d$, is the technical reason Stokes' theorem can be proved once on a cube and exported everywhere: the cube maps into the manifold, and both $\mathrm d$ and $\int$ commute with the map.

Full proof that pullback respects the wedge. Both sides are evaluated on $k+l$ vectors; the right side's shuffle sum has $\alpha(\mathrm d\phi\,v_{\sigma(1)},\dots)$ where the left has $\alpha$ applied to the same pushed-forward vectors. The two expressions are term-by-term identical because $\mathrm d\phi_p$ is a single linear map applied to each argument. No smoothness beyond $C^1$ is used pointwise.

A caution about "locally a sum of $f\,\mathrm dg^1\wedge\cdots$". In a chart take $g^i = x^i$; that is the whole content. The proof above is a genuine proof, not a sketch, provided one also knows $\mathrm d$ is local, which the previous slide's notes established.

$\mathrm d$ without coordinates, and where structure equations come from

theorem · the invariant formula

For $\alpha\in\Omega^1(M)$ and vector fields $X, Y$, $$\mathrm d\alpha(X,Y) = X\big(\alpha(Y)\big) - Y\big(\alpha(X)\big) - \alpha\big([X,Y]\big),$$ with $[X,Y]$ unit 31's Lie bracket.

proof

Both sides are $\mathbb R$-bilinear and antisymmetric, and both are local, so check on $\alpha = f\,\mathrm dg$. Left: $(\mathrm df\wedge\mathrm dg)(X,Y) = (Xf)(Yg) - (Yf)(Xg)$. Right: $X(f\,Yg) - Y(f\,Xg) - f[X,Y]g = (Xf)(Yg) + fXYg - (Yf)(Xg) - fYXg - f(XY-YX)g$, and the four $f$-terms cancel. $\square$

read it twice

Left to right: $\mathrm d$ needs no coordinates.
Right to left: the bracket term is the price of using a non-coordinate frame — and it is precisely the term that becomes Cartan's connection one-form in section 6.

The general formula, for a $k$-form, is $\mathrm d\omega(X_0,\dots,X_k) = \sum_i(-1)^iX_i\big(\omega(\dots\widehat{X_i}\dots)\big) + \sum_{i<j}(-1)^{i+j}\omega([X_i,X_j],\dots\widehat{X_i}\dots\widehat{X_j}\dots)$. Cited: Lee, Introduction to Smooth Manifolds, 2nd ed., ch. 14, the invariant formula for the exterior derivative; the proof is the same check on $f\,\mathrm dg^1\wedge\cdots$, longer. Only the $k = 1$ case is used in this course.

The test that makes it believable. Take a coordinate frame, $X = \partial_i$, $Y = \partial_j$. Then $[X,Y] = 0$ by Clairaut (unit 31 proved coordinate fields commute) and the formula reduces to $\mathrm d\alpha(\partial_i,\partial_j) = \partial_i\alpha_j - \partial_j\alpha_i$ — the chart formula. In an orthonormal frame, which is what section 6 uses, the brackets are not zero, and the extra term is exactly the structure functions $c^a{}_{bc}$ that appear when $\mathrm de^a$ is computed.

Why this matters for the physics. The formula says $\mathrm d\alpha$ measures the failure of a circulation to be recoverable from directional derivatives alone — the leftover is the bracket, the failure of the flows to commute. Unit 31 interpreted $[X,Y]$ as the gap in a commutator square; this says the curl is that gap, paired with $\alpha$.

Gauge freedom is the freedom to add an exact form

Unit 24's gauge transformation is $\vec A\mapsto\vec A + \nabla\lambda$, $\varphi\mapsto\varphi - \partial_t\lambda$. In one line:

the converse

Stating it as $\mathrm d\star A = 0$ makes obvious what unit 24 had to argue: the condition is Lorentz invariant, because $\star$ and $\mathrm d$ are built from the metric and the orientation, both of which a Lorentz transformation preserves.

It is not exact: $\oint\vec A\cdot\mathrm d\vec\ell = \Phi_B \ne 0$. So there is no gauge function $\lambda$ with $\vec A = \nabla\lambda$ outside, even though $\vec B$ vanishes there. 34 · integration

Orientation and the volume form

definition

An orientation of an $n$-manifold is a choice of nowhere-vanishing $\mu\in\Omega^n(M)$, two such being equivalent if $\mu' = f\mu$ with $f > 0$. A manifold admitting one is orientable; $M$ connected and orientable has exactly two orientations.

the metric volume form

Given a metric $g$ and an orientation, there is a unique $n$-form $\mathrm{vol}_g$, the volume form, equal to $+1$ on every positively oriented orthonormal frame, and in a chart $$\mathrm{vol}_g = \sqrt{|\det g_{ij}|}\;\mathrm dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^n .$$

the Möbius band

Not orientable: carry a frame once around and it comes back reversed. There is no nowhere-zero top form, so no consistent notion of "flux through it" — which is why unit 05 quietly required every surface to have a chosen normal.

dimension check

On $\mathbb R^3$ in spherical coordinates $g = \operatorname{diag}(1, r^2, r^2\sin^2\theta)$, so $\sqrt{\det g} = r^2\sin\theta$ and $\mathrm{vol} = r^2\sin\theta\,\mathrm dr\wedge\mathrm d\theta\wedge\mathrm d\phi$ — unit 05's Jacobian, derived rather than remembered.

Full proof of the chart formula. Let $(e_a)$ be a positively oriented orthonormal frame and $\partial_i = \Lambda^a{}_i e_a$. Then $g_{ij} = \Lambda^a{}_i\Lambda^b{}_j\,\eta_{ab}$, so $\det g = \det(\Lambda)^2\det\eta$ and $|\det\Lambda| = \sqrt{|\det g|}$. Evaluating the top form on $(\partial_1,\dots,\partial_n)$ gives $\det\Lambda$, positive for a positively oriented chart. Hence $\mathrm{vol}_g = \det\Lambda\;\mathrm dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^n$, which is the displayed formula.

Why $|\det g|$ and not $\det g$. In Lorentzian signature $\det g < 0$: for $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$, $\det\eta = -1$ and $\sqrt{|\det\eta|} = 1$, so in inertial coordinates $\mathrm{vol} = \mathrm dx^0\wedge\mathrm dx^1\wedge\mathrm dx^2\wedge\mathrm dx^3$, as one would want. In Schwarzschild coordinates (unit 36) the same formula gives $r^2\sin\theta\,\mathrm dt\wedge\mathrm dr\wedge\mathrm d\theta\wedge\mathrm d\phi$, the metric functions cancelling — a fact worth remembering before that unit.

Orientability is a real restriction, but not in relativity. A spacetime is normally required to be orientable and time-orientable, because without the first there is no consistent volume element to integrate an action over and without the second no consistent distinction between future and past. Both are assumptions, stated as such; nothing in the field equations forces them.

Integrating a form, and where the Jacobian went

definition

For $\omega = f\,\mathrm dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^n$ compactly supported in a positively oriented chart, $\displaystyle\int_M\omega := \int_{\mathbf x(U)}f\circ\mathbf x^{-1}\,\mathrm d^n x$, an ordinary multiple integral (unit 05). For general $\omega$, chop it up with a partition of unity and add. Cited: independence of the partition, Lee, ch. 16.

theorem · no Jacobian appears

If $\phi: U\to V$ is an orientation-preserving diffeomorphism, then $\int_V\omega = \int_U\phi^*\omega$.

proof

$\phi^*(\mathrm dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dy^n) = \phi^*\mathrm dy^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\phi^*\mathrm dy^n = \frac{\partial\phi^1}{\partial x^{j_1}}\cdots\frac{\partial\phi^n}{\partial x^{j_n}}\, \mathrm dx^{j_1}\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^{j_n} = \det(D\phi)\,\mathrm dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^n$, the last step being the Leibniz formula for a determinant. So $\int_U\phi^*\omega = \int(f\circ\phi)\det(D\phi)\,\mathrm d^nx$, and unit 05's change-of-variables theorem says that is $\int f\,\mathrm d^ny$ provided $\det D\phi > 0$, which orientation-preserving means. $\square$

The Jacobian did not vanish: it is inside $\phi^*$, put there by antisymmetry. That is the whole reason $n$-forms, and not functions, are the things one integrates.

Read the proof backwards. Unit 05 had to carry $|\det D\phi|$ explicitly and to argue about the absolute value. Here the sign is carried by the orientation and the magnitude by the form, and the two bookkeeping problems become one. If $\phi$ reverses orientation both sides change sign, and the identity still holds with a minus — which is the correct statement of what unit 05 handled by insisting on $|\cdot|$.

The determinant step in detail. In the wedge of $n$ one-forms $\sum_j(\partial\phi^a/\partial x^j)\mathrm dx^j$, the only surviving terms have $j_1,\dots,j_n$ a permutation of $1,\dots,n$, and reordering to increasing order contributes $\operatorname{sgn}$ of that permutation. The sum $\sum_\sigma\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_a \partial\phi^a/\partial x^{\sigma(a)}$ is $\det D\phi$ by definition.

Unit 32's volume element, as promised. For a function $f$ on an oriented Riemannian or Lorentzian manifold, $\int_Mf\,\mathrm{vol}_g = \int f\sqrt{|g|}\,\mathrm d^nx$ in any positively oriented chart — the definition above applied to the previous slide's chart formula. That is unit 32's integral of a scalar against its volume element. The two agree on an oriented manifold; the form carries a sign that the element's absolute value threw away, and the orientation is what fixes it.

Compactly supported is not a technicality to skip. Without it the integral need not converge, and Stokes' theorem fails: $\omega = \mathrm dx$ on $\mathbb R$ has $\int_{\mathbb R}\mathrm d(x) $ divergent, and $\partial\mathbb R = \emptyset$. Every statement of Stokes in this unit carries the hypothesis.

The generalised Stokes theorem

theorem · Stokes, in Cartan's form

Let $M$ be an oriented smooth $n$-manifold with boundary, $\partial M$ carrying the induced orientation, and let $\omega\in\Omega^{n-1}(M)$ be compactly supported. Then $$\int_M\mathrm d\omega \;=\; \int_{\partial M}\omega .$$

degree bookkeeping

$\mathrm d$ raises degree by one; $\partial$ lowers dimension by one. The theorem says these two are adjoint — and $\partial\partial = \emptyset$ mirrors $\mathrm d\mathrm d = 0$, which is not a coincidence but de Rham's theorem in embryo.

the induced orientation

"Outward normal first": a frame $(v_1,\dots,v_{n-1})$ of $T_p\partial M$ is positive when $(\nu, v_1,\dots,v_{n-1})$ is positive in $T_pM$, $\nu$ outward. Every sign in unit 05's and unit 23's theorems is this convention and nothing else.

The proof of Stokes' theorem has two halves: the cube, which is a computation, and the passage to a general manifold, which is a partition of unity and the naturality of $\mathrm d$. The next slide does the first.

The second half, in outline. Cover $M$ by charts, each mapping to either an open subset of $\mathbb R^n$ or a half-space; take a partition of unity $\{\chi_a\}$ subordinate to the cover, with finitely many terms on the compact support of $\omega$. Then $\omega = \sum_a\chi_a\omega$ and both sides are linear, so it suffices to treat each $\chi_a\omega$, which lives in one chart. Pull it back to the model cube by the chart map; naturality of $\mathrm d$ and the previous slide's change-of-variables theorem say both integrals are unchanged. What is left is the cube computation. A loose end: $\sum_a\mathrm d(\chi_a\omega) = \mathrm d\omega$ because $\sum_a\mathrm d\chi_a = \mathrm d(1) = 0$. Cited for the partition of unity itself: unit 31, which cited it in turn to Lee, ch. 2.

Who proved what. "Stokes' theorem" is a historical accident: the curl theorem appeared as a problem set by Stokes in the 1854 Smith's Prize examination, having been told to him by Kelvin in an 1850 letter. The general statement is Poincaré's and Cartan's, and its modern form and name settled after Cartan's 1945 Les systèmes différentiels extérieurs. Élie Cartan is also the Cartan of section 6; his son Henri is the Cartan of several other theorems.

Manifolds with boundary were not defined in unit 31. The definition needed here is the obvious one: charts map to open subsets of the half-space $\{x^n\ge0\}$, and $\partial M$ is the set of points mapping to $x^n = 0$, which is an $(n-1)$-manifold. Smoothness of transition maps is required up to the boundary. Nothing else in this deck depends on the details.

The proof, on the square

Let $M = [0,1]^2$ with $\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$ positive, and $\omega = P\,\mathrm dx + Q\,\mathrm dy$. Then $\mathrm d\omega = (\partial_xQ - \partial_yP)\,\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$ and, by Fubini (unit 05),

$$\begin{aligned}\int_M\!\partial_xQ &= \int_0^1\!\!\big[Q(1,y)-Q(0,y)\big]\mathrm dy,\\ \int_M\!\partial_yP &= \int_0^1\!\!\big[P(x,1)-P(x,0)\big]\mathrm dx .\end{aligned}$$

Run the boundary anticlockwise, as "outward normal first" requires. On the horizontal edges $\mathrm dy$ pulls back to zero, on the vertical ones $\mathrm dx$ does, so

$$\begin{aligned}\oint_{\partial M}\!\!\omega = {}&\int_0^1\!\!P(x,0)\,\mathrm dx + \int_0^1\!\!Q(1,y)\,\mathrm dy\\ &- \int_0^1\!\!P(x,1)\,\mathrm dx - \int_0^1\!\!Q(0,y)\,\mathrm dy ,\end{aligned}$$

which is the previous line. $\square$  This is Green's theorem, and it is also the whole of Stokes: the general case is the same computation in $n$ slots.

the sign, checked on the vortex

$\oint_{S^1}\theta$ round the unit circle anticlockwise is $2\pi$; clockwise it is $-2\pi$. The orientation convention is the difference between the two, and it is fixed once by "outward normal first".

Full proof on the $n$-cube. Write $\omega = \sum_i f_i\,\mathrm dx^1\wedge\cdots\widehat{\mathrm dx^i}\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^n$ (hat = omitted). Then $\mathrm d\omega = \sum_i\partial_if_i\,\mathrm dx^i\wedge\mathrm dx^1\wedge \cdots\widehat{\mathrm dx^i}\cdots = \sum_i(-1)^{i-1}\partial_if_i\,\mathrm dx^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dx^n$, the sign coming from moving $\mathrm dx^i$ past $i-1$ factors. Integrating over $[0,1]^n$ and doing the $x^i$ integral first, $\int_{[0,1]^n}\mathrm d\omega = \sum_i(-1)^{i-1}\int\big[f_i|_{x^i=1} - f_i|_{x^i=0}\big]\,\mathrm d^{n-1}x$. On the face $x^i = c$ the pullback of $\omega$ keeps only the $i$-th term, since every other term contains $\mathrm dx^i$, which pulls back to zero. The outward normal on $x^i=1$ is $+\partial_i$ and on $x^i=0$ is $-\partial_i$, and moving $\partial_i$ to the front of $(\partial_1,\dots,\widehat{\partial_i},\dots,\partial_n)$ costs $(-1)^{i-1}$; so the induced orientation on the two faces contributes exactly $(-1)^{i-1}$ and $-(-1)^{i-1}$. Summing gives $\int_{\partial[0,1]^n}\omega$. The corners and edges are a measure-zero set and contribute nothing. $\square$

Where compact support is used. On the cube it is not: the cube is compact. It is used when the chart is a half-space, where the missing faces are at infinity and must carry no integral.

The four edges. Anticlockwise means bottom ($y=0$, $x$ increasing), right ($x=1$, $y$ increasing), top ($y=1$, $x$ decreasing), left ($x=0$, $y$ decreasing). The top and left edges run backwards, which is where the two minus signs in the boundary sum come from.

The anticlockwise claim. On the bottom edge the outward normal is $-\partial_y$, so a positive frame $(v)$ of the edge satisfies $(-\partial_y, v)$ positive, i.e. $-\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy(\partial_y, v) > 0$, giving $v = +\partial_x$: $x$ increasing. Run the same argument on the other three edges.

Closed does not mean exact

definition

$\omega\in\Omega^k$ is closed if $\mathrm d\omega = 0$ and exact if $\omega = \mathrm d\alpha$ for some $\alpha\in\Omega^{k-1}$. Exact $\Rightarrow$ closed, because $\mathrm d^2 = 0$. The converse is the question.

Poincaré lemma

If $U\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ is star-shaped about $0$, every closed $k$-form on $U$ with $k\ge1$ is exact.

proof for $k = 1$, in full

Let $\alpha = a_i\,\mathrm dx^i$ be closed, so $\partial_ja_i = \partial_ia_j$. Put $f(x) = \int_0^1 a_i(tx)\,x^i\,\mathrm dt$. Then $$\begin{aligned}\partial_jf &= \int_0^1\!\big[t\,x^i\partial_ja_i(tx) + a_j(tx)\big]\mathrm dt\\ &= \int_0^1\!\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\big[t\,a_j(tx)\big]\mathrm dt = a_j(x),\end{aligned}$$ using closedness in the middle step. So $\alpha = \mathrm df$. $\square$

The proof integrates along rays from the origin — which is exactly the "build the potential by integrating along a path" of unit 04, with star-shapedness as the hypothesis that the path exists and is canonical.

The middle step, spelled out. $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}[t\,a_j(tx)] = a_j(tx) + t\,x^i\,\partial_ia_j(tx)$. Closedness replaces $\partial_ia_j$ by $\partial_ja_i$, which is the integrand's first term. Evaluating $[t\,a_j(tx)]_0^1 = a_j(x) - 0$ finishes it. Differentiating under the integral sign is legitimate: the integrand is smooth in $(t,x)$ and $[0,1]$ is compact.

General $k$. Define $(K\omega)(x)(v_1,\dots,v_{k-1}) = \int_0^1t^{k-1}\, \omega(tx)(x, v_1,\dots,v_{k-1})\,\mathrm dt$. Then $\mathrm dK + K\mathrm d = \mathrm{id}$ on $\Omega^k(U)$ for $k\ge1$, so $\mathrm d\omega = 0$ gives $\omega = \mathrm d(K\omega)$. The verification is the same computation with more indices. Cited: Lee, Introduction to Smooth Manifolds, 2nd ed., ch. 17, the Poincaré lemma for star-shaped sets; Bott and Tu, §4. The statement generalises: on any contractible manifold — star-shaped is a special case — closed implies exact, because homotopic maps induce the same map on cohomology (cited, Lee ch. 17; the degree-one case is proved on the homotopy-invariance slide, which comes next).

The hypothesis is not decorative. On $U = \mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}$, which is not star-shaped about any point, $\theta$ is closed and not exact. That single example is the reason the lemma is stated with a hypothesis rather than as "closed equals exact", and it is what unit 04 ran into.

Homotopy invariance: unit 04's promise kept

theorem

Let $\alpha$ be a closed one-form on $U$, and $\gamma_0,\gamma_1:[0,1]\to U$ loops joined by a smooth homotopy of loops $H:[0,1]^2\to U$, $H(\cdot,0) = \gamma_0$, $H(\cdot,1) = \gamma_1$, $H(0,t) = H(1,t)$. Then $\oint_{\gamma_0}\alpha = \oint_{\gamma_1}\alpha$.

proof · pull back, then Stokes on the square

$H^*\alpha$ is a one-form on the square, and it is closed: $\mathrm d(H^*\alpha) = H^*(\mathrm d\alpha) = 0$ by naturality. Stokes on $[0,1]^2$ gives $0 = \oint_{\partial[0,1]^2}H^*\alpha$. Round the boundary anticlockwise: the bottom edge is $\oint_{\gamma_0}\alpha$, the top edge run backwards is $-\oint_{\gamma_1}\alpha$, and the two side edges are the same path $t\mapsto H(0,t) = H(1,t)$ run in opposite directions, so they cancel. $\square$

corollary · unit 04's cited theorem

On a simply connected open $U$, every closed one-form is exact.

proof

Every loop is homotopic to a constant loop, whose integral is $0$. So every circulation vanishes, integrals are path-independent, and unit 04's $f(x) = \int_{x_0}^x\alpha$ has $\mathrm df = \alpha$. $\square$

only one thing cited

A continuous homotopy can be replaced by a smooth one with the same ends.

Why this needed forms. Unit 04 proved the rectangle case and sketched a grid argument for a general homotopy, noting that making "the image of each small square lies in a ball" precise for a merely continuous $H$ takes care. Here all the care goes into one operation, pullback, which works along any smooth map: $H$ need not be injective, and its image can fold over itself or collapse onto a point. The square does not care what $H$ does to it. This is the concrete meaning of "forms can always be pulled back", and it is worth comparing with how awkward the same argument is for a vector field, which cannot be pulled back along a map that is not a diffeomorphism.

The side edges, carefully. The anticlockwise boundary of $[0,1]^2$ runs up the right side ($s = 1$, $t$ increasing) and down the left side ($s = 0$, $t$ decreasing). Both sides are mapped by $H$ to the same curve $t\mapsto H(0,t) = H(1,t)$, so their pulled-back integrals are $+\int$ and $-\int$ of the same thing. For loops with a fixed base point the sides are constant and each contributes zero; the theorem as stated covers the free case too.

What is cited. The smoothing step: if two smooth loops are homotopic through continuous loops, they are homotopic through smooth ones. Cited: Lee, ch. 6, the Whitney approximation theorem for homotopies. Everything else on the slide is proved.

The corollary's last step is unit 04's own construction: fix $x_0$, define $f(x)$ as the integral of $\alpha$ along any path from $x_0$ to $x$ (well defined because two paths differ by a loop, and loops give zero), and differentiate along a short straight segment at $x$ to get $\partial_if = \alpha_i$.

34 · cohomology

de Rham cohomology

definition

$$H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M) \;=\; \frac{\ker\big(\mathrm d:\Omega^k\to\Omega^{k+1}\big)}{\operatorname{im}\big(\mathrm d:\Omega^{k-1}\to\Omega^k\big)} \;=\; \frac{\text{closed }k\text{-forms}}{\text{exact }k\text{-forms}} .$$ The quotient makes sense precisely because $\mathrm d^2 = 0$: the image sits inside the kernel. $\dim H^k$ is the $k$-th Betti number $b_k$.

$H^0$ counts pieces

A closed $0$-form is a locally constant function, and there are no exact ones. So $b_0$ is the number of connected components.

Poincaré restated

$M$ contractible $\Rightarrow$ $H^k(M) = 0$ for $k\ge1$. Cohomology vanishes where there is nothing to detect.

de Rham's theorem · cited

$H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}(M)\cong H^k_{\text{sing}}(M;\mathbb R)$: a purely analytic object computes a purely topological one. de Rham 1931.

an invariant of the space, not of the field

$H^k$ depends on $M$ alone. Changing $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ moves $F$ around inside a class; changing the region the fields live on can change which classes exist at all. Unit 04 hit this exactly: the same formula, two different domains, two different verdicts.

Why the quotient is the right object. Two closed forms differing by an exact form have the same integral over every closed submanifold, by Stokes: $\int_Z\mathrm d\alpha = \int_{\partial Z}\alpha = 0$ when $\partial Z = \emptyset$. So the class, not the form, is what an integral can see. Conversely, de Rham's theorem says the integrals over cycles see the class completely. The next slide is that statement with numbers.

What "cited" covers here. de Rham's theorem needs singular homology, which this course does not build. Everything else in this section — the computation of $H^1$ of the punctured plane, of the torus, and the monopole obstruction — is proved from the Poincaré lemma and Stokes, with no algebraic topology at all.

Finite dimensionality is a theorem for compact manifolds (cited: Bott and Tu, §5, by the Mayer–Vietoris argument on a finite good cover) and false in general for the naive reason: $H^0$ of an infinite discrete set is infinite dimensional. For the spaces in this unit — punctured plane, punctured space, torus — the Betti numbers are small and computed explicitly.

Worked example 3 — $H^1$ of the punctured plane

example 3 · unit 04's question, closed

On $U = \mathbb R^2\setminus\{0\}$, the vortex $\theta = (x\,\mathrm dy - y\,\mathrm dx)/(x^2+y^2)$ has $\mathrm d\theta = 0$ (compute: both terms give $\pm(y^2-x^2)/r^4\,\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$ and cancel) and $\oint_{S^1}\theta = 2\pi$.

Claim. $H^1(U) = \mathbb R$, generated by $[\theta]$.

Step 1 — $[\theta]\ne0$. If $\theta = \mathrm df$ then $\oint_{S^1}\theta = 0$ by Stokes, since $S^1$ has no boundary. But it is $2\pi$.

Step 2 — the circle integral does not depend on the circle. For radii $r_1<r_2$ the annulus $\mathcal A$ between them lies in $U$, and $\int_{\mathcal A}\mathrm d\alpha = 0$ for closed $\alpha$, so $\oint_{r_2}\alpha - \oint_{r_1}\alpha = 0$.

Step 3 — every class is a multiple. Let $\alpha$ be closed on $U$, and put $c = \frac1{2\pi}\oint_{S^1}\alpha$. Pull back by $\Phi(r,\phi) = (r\cos\phi, r\sin\phi)$ to the convex half-plane $H = (0,\infty)\times\mathbb R$. There $\Phi^*\alpha = \mathrm dg$ by the Poincaré lemma, and $\Phi^*\theta = \mathrm d\phi$. By step 2, $g(r,\phi+2\pi) - g(r,\phi) = \oint_{r}\alpha = 2\pi c$ for every $r$. So $g - c\phi$ is $2\pi$-periodic in $\phi$, hence equals $f\circ\Phi$ for a smooth $f$ on $U$, and $\alpha - c\,\theta = \mathrm df$. $\square$

$\mathrm d\theta = 0$, in full. $\mathrm d\!\left(\frac{x}{r^2}\mathrm dy\right) = \partial_x\!\left(\frac{x}{r^2}\right)\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy = \frac{r^2-2x^2}{r^4}\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy = \frac{y^2-x^2}{r^4}\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$, and $\mathrm d\!\left(-\frac{y}{r^2}\mathrm dx\right) = -\partial_y\!\left(\frac{y}{r^2}\right)\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dx = \frac{r^2-2y^2}{r^4}\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy = \frac{x^2-y^2}{r^4}\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy$. They cancel. Unit 04 computed the same thing as "the curl of the vortex vanishes".

Why $\Phi^*\theta = \mathrm d\phi$. $\Phi^*\mathrm dx = \cos\phi\,\mathrm dr - r\sin\phi\,\mathrm d\phi$ and $\Phi^*\mathrm dy = \sin\phi\,\mathrm dr + r\cos\phi\,\mathrm d\phi$, so $\Phi^*(x\,\mathrm dy - y\,\mathrm dx) = r\cos\phi(\sin\phi\,\mathrm dr + r\cos\phi\,\mathrm d\phi) - r\sin\phi(\cos\phi\,\mathrm dr - r\sin\phi\,\mathrm d\phi) = r^2\,\mathrm d\phi$, and dividing by $\Phi^*r^2$ leaves $\mathrm d\phi$. The point of the whole argument is that $\phi$ exists upstairs and not downstairs.

The descent step. $g - c\phi$ being $2\pi$-periodic means it is constant on the fibres of $\Phi$, so it defines a function $f$ on $U$; smoothness is inherited because $\Phi$ is a local diffeomorphism. Then $\mathrm d(f\circ\Phi) = \Phi^*\alpha - c\,\mathrm d\phi = \Phi^*(\alpha - c\theta)$, and since $\Phi^*$ is injective on forms (it is a submersion onto $U$), $\alpha - c\theta = \mathrm df$.

What unit 04 could and could not do. It proved the vortex was not conservative by exhibiting a loop, and it proved that on a simply connected domain vanishing curl implies a potential. What it could not say was how much obstruction there is. The answer is: one real number per hole, and the number attached to a given field is its circulation.

The pairing: a class against a cycle

periods

If $Z\subseteq M$ is a compact oriented $k$-manifold without boundary, then $$\big([\omega], Z\big)\longmapsto\int_Z\omega$$ is well defined on classes: changing $\omega$ by $\mathrm d\alpha$ changes nothing, by Stokes. The numbers so obtained are the periods of the class.

On the punctured plane there is one independent cycle, the circle, and the period of the generator is

$$\oint_{S^1}\theta = \int_0^{2\pi}\mathrm d\phi = 2\pi = 6.283185307\ldots$$

Every closed one-form on $U$ has period $2\pi c$ for exactly one $c\in\mathbb R$, and $c$ is its class. Unit 04's $2\pi$ was a measurement of the hole all along.

the dictionary

$b_1 = 1$: one hole.
generator: $[\theta]$.
period of generator: $2\pi$.
physical content: an irrotational field can have circulation, and no local measurement can predict it.

Why $2\pi$ and not $1$. Nothing forces the normalisation. $[\theta/2\pi]$ generates just as well and has period 1; that is the convention under which the monopole charge later comes out an integer. The number $2\pi$ is an artefact of writing $\theta$ in the form that makes $\mathrm d\theta = 0$ obvious. What is not an artefact is that the set of periods of all closed one-forms is $\mathbb R$, one dimension, not two and not zero.

Independence of the cycle's shape. Any loop in $U$ winding once about the origin gives $2\pi$ — deform it to a circle through loops in $U$ and apply the homotopy-invariance slide. Unit 04 found $2\pi$ on circles of every radius and called $\frac1{2\pi}$ of the circulation the winding number, unchanged by continuous deformation; the reason it is unchanged is here.

Looking ahead. de Rham's theorem says the period map is a perfect pairing between $H^k_{\mathrm{dR}}$ and $H_k$ with real coefficients. That is why a statement about integrals of fields — a physicist's statement — and a statement about holes — a topologist's — are the same statement. The monopole slide is the case $k = 2$.

34 · the star

The Hodge star

definition

A metric $g$ induces an inner product on $\Lambda^kT_p^*M$ by $\langle\alpha^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\alpha^k,\ \beta^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\beta^k\rangle = \det\big[\langle\alpha^i,\beta^j\rangle\big]$. With an orientation, the Hodge star $\star:\Lambda^k\to\Lambda^{n-k}$ is the unique linear map with $$\alpha\wedge\star\beta = \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\,\mathrm{vol}_g\qquad\text{for all }\alpha,\beta\in\Lambda^k .$$

the double star

$\star\star = (-1)^{k(n-k)}s$ on $\Lambda^k$, where $s = \operatorname{sign}\det g$. Euclidean $\mathbb R^3$: $s=1$ and $\star\star = 1$ in every degree. Minkowski, $n=4$, $k=2$: $\star\star = (-1)^{4}\cdot(-1) = -1$.

worked in an orthonormal coframe

$\star(e^0\wedge e^i) = -\tfrac12\epsilon_{ijk}\,e^j\wedge e^k$,   $\star(e^i\wedge e^j) = \epsilon_{ijk}\,e^0\wedge e^k$,   $\star e^0 = -e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3$,   $\star e^i = -e^0\wedge\star_3 e^i$, with $\mathrm{vol} = e^0\wedge e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3$.

this is where the metric lives

$\wedge$, $\mathrm d$, pullback, closed, exact, cohomology, Stokes: no metric. $\star$: metric. So every metric-dependence of Maxwell's equations sits in one symbol — and in unit 32's curved spacetime only that symbol changes.

Deriving one of the frame formulas. Take $\beta = e^0\wedge e^1$ in $\eta = \operatorname{diag}(-1,1,1,1)$. Then $\langle\beta,\beta\rangle = \eta^{00}\eta^{11} = -1$. We need $\beta\wedge\star\beta = -\mathrm{vol}$. Writing $\star\beta = c\,e^2\wedge e^3$ gives $\beta\wedge\star\beta = c\,e^0\wedge e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3 = c\,\mathrm{vol}$, so $c = -1$ and $\star(e^0\wedge e^1) = -e^2\wedge e^3$, which is the stated formula at $i = 1$. The others go the same way; the only care needed is reordering the wedge to $e^0e^1e^2e^3$ and counting transpositions.

Full proof of the double-star formula. By linearity check on a basis element $e^I$ with $|I| = k$. Then $\star e^I = \pm e^{I^c}$ with the sign determined by $e^I\wedge e^{I^c} = \varepsilon(I)\,\mathrm{vol}$ and by the product of the $\eta^{ii}$ for $i\in I$. Applying $\star$ again reverses the roles of $I$ and $I^c$, and $e^{I^c}\wedge e^{I} = (-1)^{k(n-k)}e^I\wedge e^{I^c}$; the metric factors from $I$ and $I^c$ multiply to $\det\eta^{-1}$, whose sign is $s$. Collecting, $\star\star e^I = (-1)^{k(n-k)}s\,e^I$.

Why it matters that $\star\star = -1$ here. In Euclidean signature, $\star$ on two-forms in four dimensions squares to $+1$ and splits $\Lambda^2$ into self-dual and anti-self-dual pieces. In Lorentzian signature it squares to $-1$: no real eigenvectors, no such split, and every attempt to decompose the electromagnetic field into two independent halves over the reals fails. Complexifying is the standard way round it: $F\mp\mathrm i\star F$ are eigenforms of $\star$ with eigenvalues $\pm\mathrm i$ (apply $\star$ and use $\star\star = -1$), and each is determined by the complex vector $\vec E + \mathrm ic\vec B$ or its conjugate.

Full proof that $\star$ exists and is unique. The inner product on $\Lambda^k$ is well defined because the determinant is multilinear and alternating in the $\alpha^i$ and in the $\beta^j$, so it extends bilinearly from decomposable forms; in an orthonormal coframe the $e^I$ are orthogonal with $\langle e^I,e^I\rangle = \pm1$. The pairing $\Lambda^k\times\Lambda^{n-k}\to\Lambda^n$, $(\alpha,\gamma)\mapsto\alpha\wedge\gamma$, is nondegenerate, since $e^I\wedge e^J$ is nonzero only for $J = I^c$. So for each $\beta$ the linear functional $\alpha\mapsto\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle$ is $\alpha\mapsto\alpha\wedge\gamma/\mathrm{vol}$ for exactly one $\gamma$, and $\star\beta := \gamma$; linearity in $\beta$ follows from the uniqueness.

The conserved charge, properly. This is the sharpest version of unit 24's discovery: Ampère's law as Ampère left it corresponds to a $\star J$ that is not closed for time-dependent sources, so no $F$ solves it, and the repair is unique.

The two invariants, and a number

$\wedge$ and $\star$ use only the metric and the orientation, both preserved by unit 26's proper Lorentz transformations. So any scalar built from them is frame-independent, and there are exactly two of degree two in $F$:

$$\begin{aligned}F\wedge F &= -\frac2c\,(\vec E\cdot\vec B)\,\mathrm{vol},\\ F\wedge\star F &= \Big(|\vec B|^2 - \frac{|\vec E|^2}{c^2}\Big)\mathrm{vol} .\end{aligned}$$

Obtained without a single boost: the invariance is a property of $\wedge$ and $\star$, not a computation.

a number

$|\vec E| = 2.50\times10^5\ \mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}$ perpendicular to $|\vec B| = 1.00\times10^{-3}$ T. Then $\vec E\cdot\vec B = 0$, and $$\begin{aligned}|\vec B|^2 - |\vec E|^2/c^2 &= 1.000\times10^{-6} - 6.954\times10^{-7}\\ &= 3.046\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T^2},\end{aligned}$$ positive: magnetically dominated, in every frame.

limit check

A plane wave has $|\vec E| = c|\vec B|$ and $\vec E\perp\vec B$, so both invariants vanish — a null field, and no frame makes it purely electric or purely magnetic.

Computing $F\wedge F$. With $F = \beta + \epsilon\wedge\mathrm dt$, where $\beta$ is the $\vec B$ flux two-form and $\epsilon = E_i\mathrm dx^i$: $\beta\wedge\beta = 0$ (every term repeats a factor) and $(\epsilon\wedge\mathrm dt)\wedge(\epsilon\wedge\mathrm dt) = 0$ (two $\mathrm dt$s), so $F\wedge F = 2\beta\wedge\epsilon\wedge\mathrm dt = 2(\vec E\cdot\vec B)\, \mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz\wedge\mathrm dt$. Since $\mathrm{vol} = e^0\wedge e^1\wedge e^2\wedge e^3 = c\,\mathrm dt\wedge\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz$ and moving $\mathrm dt$ to the back costs three transpositions, $\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz\wedge\mathrm dt = -\mathrm{vol}/c$, giving the stated $-\frac2c(\vec E\cdot\vec B)\mathrm{vol}$.

Computing $F\wedge\star F$. Same decomposition: the flux-times-flux terms vanish, leaving $-c\,\beta\wedge b\wedge\mathrm dt + \frac1c(\epsilon\wedge\mathrm dt)\wedge\eta$ with $b = B_i\mathrm dx^i$ and $\eta$ the $\vec E$ flux two-form. The first is $-c|\vec B|^2\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz\wedge\mathrm dt = |\vec B|^2\mathrm{vol}$ and the second is $\frac1c|\vec E|^2\mathrm dx\wedge\mathrm dy\wedge\mathrm dz\wedge\mathrm dt = -\frac{|\vec E|^2}{c^2}\mathrm{vol}$.

The numbers. $c = 2.99792458\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ exactly, so $|\vec E|/c = 2.50\times10^5/2.99792458\times10^8 = 8.3391\times10^{-4}$ T and $(|\vec E|/c)^2 = 6.9541\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T^2}$. Subtracting from $1.00\times10^{-6}$ gives $3.046\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T^2}$.

Full proof of the invariance. A proper Lorentz transformation $L$ is a linear map with $L^*\eta = \eta$ and $\det L = +1$, so $L^*\mathrm{vol} = \mathrm{vol}$ (the pullback of a top form multiplies it by $\det L$, as the integration slide showed). The inner product on two-forms is built from $\eta$ alone, so $\langle L^*\alpha, L^*\beta\rangle = \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\circ L$, and then the defining identity $\alpha\wedge\star\beta = \langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\mathrm{vol}$ forces $L^*(\star\beta) = \star(L^*\beta)$. Hence $L^*(F\wedge\star F) = L^*F\wedge\star L^*F$: the new observer's field gives the same coefficient of $\mathrm{vol}$ at the same event. The same holds for $F\wedge F$ without using $\star$ at all.

Parity. A reflection has $\det L = -1$ and reverses the orientation. $F\wedge\star F$ survives (both $\star$ and $\mathrm{vol}$ flip, and the signs cancel) but the coefficient of $F\wedge F$ changes sign: $\vec E\cdot\vec B$ is a pseudoscalar, for the reason the notes on grad, curl and divergence gave for the curl.

Why only two. $F\wedge F$ and $F\wedge\star F$ are the only scalars quadratic in $F$ that can be built from $\wedge$, $\star$ and nothing else: the other candidates reduce to them, $\star F\wedge F = F\wedge\star F$ (two-forms commute) and $\star F\wedge\star F = -F\wedge F$ (put $\alpha = \star F$, $\beta = F$ in the defining identity, then use $\star\star = -1$). Higher wedge powers vanish in four dimensions because $F\wedge F$ is already a top form. If you have taken unit 28, these are its $F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}$ and $F_{\mu\nu}\widetilde F^{\mu\nu}$ up to constant factors — the same objects in index notation.

Coframes and the first structure equation

Take an orthonormal coframe $\{e^a\}$: one-forms with $g = \eta_{ab}\,e^a\otimes e^b$, $\eta$ the flat metric of the right signature. Unit 32's connection appears as one-forms $\omega^a{}_b = \Gamma^a{}_{cb}\,e^c$.

Cartan's first structure equation

A torsion-free metric connection has connection one-forms $\omega^a{}_b$ determined uniquely by $$\mathrm de^a = -\,\omega^a{}_b\wedge e^b,\qquad \omega_{ab} = -\,\omega_{ba},$$ where $\omega_{ab} = \eta_{ac}\omega^c{}_b$.

uniqueness · proof

If $\omega$ and $\omega'$ both work, $\lambda^a{}_b = \omega^a{}_b-\omega'^a{}_b$ has $\lambda^a{}_b\wedge e^b = 0$ and $\lambda_{ab} = -\lambda_{ba}$. Writing $\lambda_{ab} = \lambda_{abc}e^c$, the first gives $\lambda_{abc} = \lambda_{acb}$: symmetric in the last two, antisymmetric in the first two. Then $\lambda_{abc} = \lambda_{acb} = -\lambda_{cab} = -\lambda_{cba} = \lambda_{bca} = \lambda_{bac} = -\lambda_{abc}$, so $\lambda = 0$. $\square$

the arithmetic

In four dimensions: $\Gamma^\lambda{}_{\mu\nu}$ has $4\times10 = 40$ independent components. $\omega_{ab}$, antisymmetric, has $\binom42 = 6$ one-forms. And they are usually read off from $\mathrm de^a$ by inspection, not solved for.

Where the two conditions come from. Antisymmetry of $\omega_{ab}$ is metric compatibility: $\nabla g = 0$ with $g = \eta_{ab}e^a\otimes e^b$ and $\eta$ constant forces $\eta_{cb}\omega^c{}_a + \eta_{ac}\omega^c{}_b = 0$. The first structure equation is vanishing torsion: unit 32 defined $T(X,Y) = \nabla_XY - \nabla_YX - [X,Y]$, and expanding it in the frame with the invariant formula for $\mathrm d$ from section 2 gives $T^a = \mathrm de^a + \omega^a{}_b\wedge e^b$. Setting $T^a = 0$ is the equation on the slide.

The uniqueness chain, read slowly. Each step uses one of the two symmetries alternately: symmetric in $(bc)$, antisymmetric in $(ab)$, symmetric, antisymmetric, symmetric, antisymmetric. Six steps return to $\lambda_{abc}$ with a minus sign. It is the same argument that gives uniqueness of the Christoffel symbols in unit 32 — that proof cycled $\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\nu}$ through its index symmetries in exactly this way.

Existence follows by solving: the same index cycling, run forwards rather than towards a contradiction, gives $\omega_{abc} = \frac12(c_{cab} - c_{abc} - c_{bca})$, where $\mathrm de^a = -\frac12c^a{}_{bc}\,e^b\wedge e^c$, $c_{abc} = \eta_{ad}c^d{}_{bc}$ and $\omega_{ab} = \omega_{abc}e^c$. Check it on example 5 below: there $c^2{}_{12} = -\cot\vartheta/a$, and the formula gives $\omega_{212} = \cot\vartheta/a$, i.e. $\omega^2{}_1 = \cot\vartheta\,e^2/a = \cos\vartheta\,\mathrm d\phi$, which is what inspection finds. In practice nobody uses the formula: one computes $\mathrm de^a$, guesses $\omega$, and checks. The guess is forced because uniqueness has been proved.

The second structure equation, and Bianchi for free

Cartan's second structure equation

The curvature two-forms $\Omega^a{}_b := \mathrm d\omega^a{}_b + \omega^a{}_c\wedge\omega^c{}_b$ carry unit 33's Riemann tensor in the frame: $$\Omega^a{}_b = \tfrac12R^a{}_{bcd}\,e^c\wedge e^d .$$

both Bianchi identities, on the slide

Take $\mathrm d$ of the first structure equation: $0 = -\mathrm d\omega^a{}_b\wedge e^b + \omega^a{}_b\wedge\mathrm de^b$, and substituting both structure equations leaves $\ \Omega^a{}_b\wedge e^b = 0$ — the first Bianchi identity.
Take $\mathrm d$ of the second: the cubic terms cancel in pairs and $\ \mathrm d\Omega^a{}_b = \Omega^a{}_c\wedge\omega^c{}_b - \omega^a{}_c\wedge\Omega^c{}_b$ — the second, whose contraction is what makes unit 33's Einstein tensor divergence-free.

the counting

Coordinates: $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}$ from $\partial\Gamma-\partial\Gamma+\Gamma\Gamma-\Gamma\Gamma$, with 40 Christoffels to produce first.
Cartan: $\binom n2$ curvature forms, each one $\mathrm d$ and one wedge. In four dimensions, 6 against 40.

Full proof of the second structure equation. The curvature of unit 33 is $R(X,Y)Z = \nabla_X\nabla_YZ - \nabla_Y\nabla_XZ - \nabla_{[X,Y]}Z$. Write $\nabla e_b = \omega^a{}_b\otimes e_a$ for the frame fields. Then for the frame vectors, $R(X,Y)e_b = \big(\mathrm d\omega^a{}_b(X,Y) + (\omega^a{}_c\wedge\omega^c{}_b)(X,Y)\big)e_a$ — the $\mathrm d\omega$ term coming from the invariant formula of section 2 (this is exactly where the $-\alpha([X,Y])$ term earns its keep) and the wedge term from the two nested connections. The cubic cancellation, in full. $\mathrm d\Omega^a{}_b = \mathrm d\omega^a{}_c\wedge\omega^c{}_b - \omega^a{}_c\wedge\mathrm d\omega^c{}_b$ (the sign from moving $\mathrm d$ past a one-form). Gauss–Bonnet, from Stokes and one connection form

theorem · local Gauss–Bonnet

Let $D$ be a closed disc in one chart of an oriented surface, bounded by a smooth simple loop run anticlockwise at unit speed, with geodesic curvature $\kappa_g$. Then $$\int_DK\,\mathrm dA + \oint_{\partial D}\kappa_g\,\mathrm ds = 2\pi .$$

proof

Gram–Schmidt on $\partial_1,\partial_2$ gives an orthonormal frame on $D$. In two dimensions $\omega\wedge\omega = 0$, so $\mathrm d\omega^1{}_2 = \Omega^1{}_2 = K\,\mathrm dA$. With $T = \cos\psi\,e_1 + \sin\psi\,e_2$, $\kappa_g = \psi' - \omega^1{}_2(T)$ (notes). Integrate and use Stokes: $\oint\kappa_g\,\mathrm ds = \Delta\psi - \int_DK\,\mathrm dA$, and $\Delta\psi = 2\pi$. $\square$

check · a polar cap

Cap $\vartheta\le\vartheta_0$ of a sphere: $\int K\,\mathrm dA = 2\pi(1-\cos\vartheta_0)$; rim $\kappa_g = \cot\vartheta_0/a$ over length $2\pi a\sin\vartheta_0$ gives $2\pi\cos\vartheta_0$. Sum $2\pi$. Limit $\vartheta_0\to\pi/2$: the equator is a geodesic. Number: the Arctic Circle, $\vartheta_0 = 23.44^\circ$, has $\int K\,\mathrm dA = 0.5185$ rad $= 29.71^\circ$ — the turn of a vector parallel-transported once round it.

the vortex again

The flat plane's polar frame has $\omega^2{}_1 = \mathrm d\phi = \theta$. Closed because $K = 0$; period $2\pi$ because the frame turns once round the origin.

The formula for $\kappa_g$. The geodesic curvature of a unit-speed curve is $\kappa_g = \langle\nabla_TT, N\rangle$ with $N = -\sin\psi\,e_1 + \cos\psi\,e_2$ the inward normal for an anticlockwise loop. Using $\nabla e_b = \omega^a{}_b\otimes e_a$, $\nabla_TT = \psi'N + \cos\psi\,\omega^2{}_1(T)\,e_2 + \sin\psi\,\omega^1{}_2(T)\,e_1 = \big(\psi' + \omega^2{}_1(T)\big)N$, since $\omega^1{}_2 = -\omega^2{}_1$. So $\kappa_g = \psi' - \omega^1{}_2(T)$. A geodesic has $\kappa_g = 0$ — unit 32's auto-parallel condition — and then $\psi' = \omega^1{}_2(T)$ says the tangent turns relative to the frame exactly as fast as the frame turns relative to parallel transport.

What is cited. $\Delta\psi = 2\pi$: the tangent of a simple closed loop turns once, measured against a frame defined on the whole disc. This is Hopf's theorem of turning tangents (cited: do Carmo, Differential Geometry of Curves and Surfaces, §5-7); in the flat plane with the Cartesian frame it is the familiar fact that walking round a simple loop turns you through one full turn. Liouville's theorem, in one line

two operations

The interior product $i_X:\Omega^k\to\Omega^{k-1}$ is $(i_X\omega)(v_1,\dots,v_{k-1}) = \omega(X, v_1,\dots,v_{k-1})$. Cartan's magic formula relates it to unit 31's Lie derivative: $$\mathcal L_X = \mathrm d\circ i_X + i_X\circ\mathrm d .$$

Liouville

Unit 14's $\omega = \sum_i\mathrm dp_i\wedge\mathrm dq_i = \mathrm d\big(\sum_ip_i\,\mathrm dq_i\big)$ is closed, and the Hamiltonian field satisfies $i_{X_H}\omega = -\mathrm dH$. So $$\mathcal L_{X_H}\omega = \mathrm d(i_{X_H}\omega) + i_{X_H}(\mathrm d\omega) = -\mathrm d\mathrm dH + 0 = 0 ,$$ and hence $\mathcal L_{X_H}\,\omega^{\wedge n} = 0$. Since $\omega^{\wedge n}/n!$ is the phase-space volume form, the flow preserves volume. $\square$

against unit 14

There the proof was: compute $\nabla\cdot(\dot q,\dot p) = \partial_q\partial_pH - \partial_p\partial_qH = 0$. Same Clairaut, half a page, and no route to the stronger statement that every $\omega^{\wedge k}$ is preserved — Poincaré's integral invariants, which the divergence argument cannot see.

Full proof of the magic formula. Both sides are $\mathbb R$-linear, degree-preserving, and derivations of the wedge algebra: $\mathcal L_X$ because the flow pullback is an algebra map, and $\mathrm di_X + i_X\mathrm d$ because $i_X$ is an antiderivation of degree $-1$ and $\mathrm d$ one of degree $+1$, so their anticommutator is a derivation of degree 0. Both commute with $\mathrm d$: $\mathcal L_X\mathrm d = \mathrm d\mathcal L_X$ since pullback does, and $(\mathrm di_X + i_X\mathrm d)\mathrm d = \mathrm di_X\mathrm d = \mathrm d(\mathrm di_X + i_X\mathrm d)$. They agree on functions: $\mathcal L_Xf = Xf = i_X\mathrm df$, and $\mathrm di_Xf = 0$ as $i_X$ kills $0$-forms. Two derivations that commute with $\mathrm d$ and agree on functions agree on every $f\,\mathrm dg^1\wedge\cdots\wedge\mathrm dg^k$, hence everywhere, since forms are locally sums of these. $\square$

The sign in $i_{X_H}\omega = -\mathrm dH$. With unit 14's convention $\omega = \sum\mathrm dp_i\wedge\mathrm dq_i$ and Hamilton's equations $\dot q = \partial H/\partial p$, $\dot p = -\partial H/\partial q$, one computes $\omega(X_H, v) = (X_H)_pv_q - (X_H)_qv_p = -H_qv_q - H_pv_p = -\mathrm dH(v)$. Texts that write $\omega = \sum\mathrm dq_i\wedge\mathrm dp_i$ get $+\mathrm dH$. Either way $\mathcal L_{X_H}\omega = 0$, because $\mathrm d$ of a sign is still zero.

Poincaré's integral invariants. $\mathcal L_{X_H}\omega^{\wedge k} = 0$ for every $k$, so the integral of $\omega^{\wedge k}$ over any $2k$-dimensional surface carried along by the flow is constant. $k = n$ is Liouville; $k = 1$ says the sum of the projected areas $\oint\sum p_i\,\mathrm dq_i$ over a loop is conserved (by Stokes, the loop integral is the integral of $\omega$ over a surface it bounds). The area-preserving return maps of unit 17, for those who take that branch, rest on exactly this invariant.

Unit 14's generating functions are $\mathrm d^2 = 0$. In one line that is $\sum p_i\,\mathrm dq_i - \sum P_i\,\mathrm dQ_i = \mathrm dF_1$. Apply $\mathrm d$: $\sum\mathrm dp_i\wedge\mathrm dq_i - \sum\mathrm dP_i\wedge\mathrm dQ_i = \mathrm d\mathrm dF_1 = 0$, so the map preserves $\omega$ — it is symplectic. Conversely, if a map preserves $\omega$ then $\sum p\,\mathrm dq - \sum P\,\mathrm dQ$ is closed, and on a star-shaped region the Poincaré lemma makes it $\mathrm d$ of something: a generating function exists locally, of type $F_1(q,Q)$ wherever $(q,Q)$ can serve as coordinates (for the identity map they cannot, and one of unit 14's other types is used). The first row is unit 32's remark, explained. That $\mathrm dF = 0$ needs no metric is why minimal coupling has nothing to do for the homogeneous pair: there is no comma to turn into a semicolon, because there was never a partial derivative in the statement. The inhomogeneous pair does change, and it changes only through $\star$ and $\mathrm{vol}_g$ — a factor of $\sqrt{-g}$, which in components is the curved-space Maxwell equation $\partial_\nu(\sqrt{-g}F^{\mu\nu}) = \mu_0\sqrt{-g}J^\mu$ — with this unit's $F_{0i} = -E_i/c$ the derivative goes on the second index, as the flat case $\partial_iF^{0i} = \nabla\cdot\vec E/c = \mu_0c\rho$ confirms (cited: MTW ch. 22, where it is derived by minimal coupling; the form version makes it one symbol).

The third row is the reason Hamiltonian mechanics never needed a metric. Unit 14's phase space has no natural inner product — $q$ and $p$ have different dimensions, so there is nothing to compare them with — and everything it proved used only $\omega$. Its canonical transformations and Liouville's theorem are corollaries of that one fact.

Where this unit stops. Not covered: the global Gauss–Bonnet theorem with its Euler characteristic, the Hodge decomposition theorem, the Hodge Laplacian's kernel computing $H^k$ on a compact Riemannian manifold, characteristic classes, and the Chern–Weil theory that reads curvature invariants as cohomology classes. Nothing later in this course depends on them.
