Schwarzschild: the first exact solution, and what its two singularities are

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You derive the Schwarzschild metric from the ansatz, with the Christoffel symbols and Ricci components produced and the constant fixed by the Newtonian limit; you obtain the geodesic constants of motion from its Killing vectors and name the unit-13 theorem they are an instance of; and you demonstrate, by an explicit coordinate transformation plus an invariant scalar, that exactly one of its two apparent singularities is real.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Thirteen problems, about seven hours. Conventions are the deck's: signature \((-,+,+,+)\), the time coordinate \(t\) in seconds so that \(g_{tt}\) carries a \(c^2\), \(f = 1 - r_s/r\), \(\mathrm d\Omega^2 = \mathrm d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\varphi^2\), and unit 33's curvature sign \(R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}\), \(R_{\sigma\nu} = R^\mu{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}\).

Constants: \(c = 299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(G = 6.67430\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\), \(GM_\odot = 1.32712440018\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\) (so \(M_\odot = 1.98841\times10^{30}\) kg), \(GM_\oplus = 3.986004418\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\), \(R_\odot = 6.957\times10^{8}\) m, \(R_\oplus = 6.378137\times10^{6}\) m, \(g = 9.80665\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). The running example is a hole of \(10\,M_\odot\), \(r_s = 29\,532.5\) m; the Milky Way's central hole is taken as \(4.3\times10^{6}M_\odot\).

The solution, derived and checked

P1 Four radii and a density routine

exercises: the Schwarzschild radius, compactness, what \(M\) buys you in a number

  1. Compute \(r_s\) and the compactness \(r_s/R\) for: the Sun; the Earth; a neutron star of \(1.4\,M_\odot\) and radius 12 km; a white dwarf of \(0.6\,M_\odot\) and radius 9000 km. Which of them, if any, has an event horizon?
  2. Define the bookkeeping density \(\bar\rho := M\big/\big(\tfrac43\pi r_s^3\big)\) and show that \(\bar\rho = 3c^6/(32\pi G^3M^2)\). Evaluate it for the \(10\,M_\odot\) hole and for \(4.3\times10^{6}M_\odot\).
  3. For what mass, in solar masses, is \(\bar\rho\) equal to the density of water?
  4. Say in one sentence why \(\bar\rho\) is not the density of anything.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four lengths and four dimensionless ratios, then an algebraic identity, then two numbers, then one number, then a sentence. Nothing here needs more than \(r_s = 2GM/c^2\) — but keep \(GM\) together wherever you can, since it is known far better than \(G\) and \(M\) separately.

Rung 2

For part 2, substitute \(r_s = 2GM/c^2\) into \(\tfrac43\pi r_s^3\) and watch \(M\) end up in the denominator: the bigger the hole, the thinner it is. For part 3 solve \(\bar\rho = 1000\) for \(M\) and divide by \(M_\odot\).

Rung 3

\(r_{s,\odot} = 2\times1.32712440018\times10^{20}/(2.99792458\times10^{8})^2\), and every other \(r_s\) is this times \(M/M_\odot\). For part 2, \(\bar\rho = \frac{M}{\frac43\pi}\Big(\frac{c^2}{2GM}\Big)^3 = \frac{3c^6}{32\pi G^3M^2}\).

Worked solution

Idea. \(r_s\) is linear in \(M\), so one number generates the table; the density is the same number cubed, and its inverse-square dependence on \(M\) is the surprise worth remembering.

1. The radii. \(r_{s,\odot} = 2GM_\odot/c^2 = 2\times1.32712440018\times10^{20}/ 8.98755179\times10^{16} = 2953.25\) m.

  • Sun: \(r_s = 2.953\) km, \(r_s/R_\odot = 2953.25/6.957\times10^{8} = 4.245\times10^{-6}\).
  • Earth: \(r_s = 2GM_\oplus/c^2 = 8.870\) mm, \(r_s/R_\oplus = 1.391\times10^{-9}\).
  • Neutron star: \(r_s = 1.4\times2953.25 = 4134.6\) m, \(r_s/R = 4134.6/12\,000 = 0.345\).
  • White dwarf: \(r_s = 0.6\times2953.25 = 1772.0\) m, \(r_s/R = 1.969\times10^{-4}\).

None has a horizon: in every case \(r_s \lt R\), so the Schwarzschild radius lies inside the matter, where \(T_{\mu\nu}\ne0\) and the vacuum solution does not apply. The neutron star is the interesting entry — a third of the way there, which is why it is the laboratory for strong-field gravity.

2. The density. \[\bar\rho = \frac{M}{\frac43\pi r_s^3} = \frac{3M}{4\pi}\Big(\frac{c^2}{2GM}\Big)^3 = \frac{3c^6}{32\pi G^3M^2}.\] For \(M = 10M_\odot = 1.98841\times10^{31}\) kg: \(\bar\rho = 1.84\times10^{17}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), comparable with nuclear matter, \(2.3\times10^{17}\). For \(4.3\times10^{6}M_\odot\): \(\bar\rho = 9.97\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), about a thousand times water.

3. Water. Setting \(\bar\rho = 1000\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), \[M = \sqrt{\frac{3c^6}{32\pi G^3\times1000}} = 2.70\times10^{38}\ \text{kg} = 1.36\times10^{8}M_\odot .\] A hole of a hundred million solar masses — an ordinary quasar engine — is, by this bookkeeping, as thin as water.

4. It is a mass divided by a Euclidean volume computed from a radius that is not a radius: \(r_s\) labels a sphere by its area, the region inside it is not a ball of anything, and the matter that made the hole is not spread through it.

Check. Dimensions: \(c^6/(G^3M^2)\) is \(\mathrm{(m/s)^6}/(\mathrm{m^3kg^{-1}s^{-2}})^3\mathrm{kg^2} = \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) ✓. Limit: as \(M\to\infty\), \(\bar\rho\to0\), consistent with \(r_s\propto M\) growing faster than \(M^{1/3}\) ✓. Cross-check on part 1: \(r_s/R = 2GM/(Rc^2) = 2|\Phi|/c^2\), and unit 35 quoted \(|\Phi|/c^2 = 2.12\times10^{-6}\) at the Sun's surface — twice that is \(4.24\times10^{-6}\) ✓.

P2 The symbols, checked twice routine

exercises: the Koszul formula, metric compatibility as an independent test

For the ansatz \(\mathrm ds^2 = -A(r)c^2\mathrm dt^2 + B(r)\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2\):

  1. Obtain \(\Gamma^t_{tr}\), \(\Gamma^r_{rr}\), \(\Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi}\) and \(\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi}\) from the coordinate formula \(\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12g^{\mu\nu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\nu\beta} + \partial_\beta g_{\nu\alpha} - \partial_\nu g_{\alpha\beta})\), and compare with the deck's list.
  2. Prove that \(\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = 0\) whenever \(\mu, \alpha, \beta\) are three distinct indices and \(g\) is diagonal with each component depending only on the coordinates it is "diagonal in" — state the condition you actually need and give the proof.
  3. Run unit 32's metric-compatibility check \(\partial_kg_{ij} = \Gamma^l{}_{ki}g_{lj} + \Gamma^l{}_{kj}g_{il}\) on \((k,i,j) = (r,\theta,\theta)\) and on \((r,t,t)\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four expressions, then a small proof, then two identities that either hold or do not. The inverse metric is diagonal with entries \(-1/(Ac^2), 1/B, 1/r^2, 1/(r^2\sin^2\theta)\); every sum over \(\nu\) collapses to one term.

Rung 2

For part 2, write out the three terms of the Koszul formula with \(g\) diagonal: the sum over \(\nu\) forces \(\nu = \mu\), and then each of the three terms is a derivative of a component \(g_{\mu\beta}\), \(g_{\mu\alpha}\) or \(g_{\alpha\beta}\) that is off-diagonal, hence zero, unless an index coincides.

Rung 3

First line: \(\Gamma^t_{tr} = \tfrac12g^{tt}(\partial_tg_{tr} + \partial_rg_{tt} - \partial_tg_{tr}) = \tfrac12\big(\tfrac{-1}{Ac^2}\big)\partial_r(-Ac^2)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The Koszul formula is mechanical; the point of the exercise is the second, independent check, which catches the errors the first one hides.

1. Four symbols. With \(g^{tt} = -1/(Ac^2)\), \(g^{rr} = 1/B\), \(g^{\varphi\varphi} = 1/(r^2\sin^2\theta)\):

  • \(\Gamma^t_{tr} = \tfrac12g^{tt}\big(\partial_tg_{tr} + \partial_rg_{tt} - \partial_tg_{tr}\big) = \tfrac12\cdot\frac{-1}{Ac^2}\cdot(-A'c^2) = \frac{A'}{2A}\) ✓
  • \(\Gamma^r_{rr} = \tfrac12g^{rr}\partial_rg_{rr} = \frac{B'}{2B}\) ✓
  • \(\Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi} = \tfrac12g^{rr}\big(2\partial_\varphi g_{r\varphi} - \partial_rg_{\varphi\varphi}\big) = -\frac{1}{2B}\partial_r(r^2\sin^2\theta) = -\frac{r\sin^2\theta}{B}\) ✓
  • \(\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi} = \tfrac12g^{\varphi\varphi}\partial_\theta g_{\varphi\varphi} = \frac{1}{2r^2\sin^2\theta}\cdot2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \cot\theta\) ✓

2. Three distinct indices. Let \(g\) be diagonal. Then \(g^{\mu\nu}\) is diagonal too, so the sum over \(\nu\) gives \(\nu = \mu\) only: \[\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12g^{\mu\mu}\big(\partial_\alpha g_{\mu\beta} + \partial_\beta g_{\mu\alpha} - \partial_\mu g_{\alpha\beta}\big)\quad(\text{no sum}).\] If \(\mu, \alpha, \beta\) are distinct, all three components \(g_{\mu\beta}\), \(g_{\mu\alpha}\), \(g_{\alpha\beta}\) are off-diagonal, hence identically zero, so all three derivatives vanish. The condition needed is only that \(g\) be diagonal in a neighbourhood — so that the derivatives of the vanishing components also vanish — and nothing about which coordinates each component depends on. \(\square\)

3. Compatibility. \((r,\theta,\theta)\): the left side is \(\partial_rg_{\theta\theta} = 2r\). The right side is \(2\Gamma^l{}_{r\theta}g_{l\theta} = 2\Gamma^\theta{}_{r\theta}g_{\theta\theta} = 2\cdot\frac1r\cdot r^2 = 2r\) ✓.

\((r,t,t)\): left side \(\partial_rg_{tt} = -A'c^2\). Right side \(2\Gamma^l{}_{rt}g_{lt} = 2\Gamma^t{}_{rt}g_{tt} = 2\cdot\frac{A'}{2A}\cdot(-Ac^2) = -A'c^2\) ✓.

Check. Dimensions: \(\Gamma^t_{tr}\) is \(1/\)length, and it multiplies \(\dot r\dot t\) to give \(\ddot t\) ✓. Special case \(A = B = 1\): \(\Gamma^t_{tr} = 0\), \(\Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi} = -r\sin^2\theta\), which are flat space's spherical-coordinate symbols ✓. Second special case: \(A = f\), \(B = 1/f\) reproduces unit 32's worked example 5 ✓.

Another way

Part 3 can be run as a test of the whole list at once: metric compatibility with the Levi-Civita connection is equivalent to \(\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}\) being given by the Koszul formula, so if all sixteen independent \((k,i,j)\) identities hold, every symbol is right. Two of them are enough paranoia in practice, and they should be chosen to involve different symbols.

P3 The other two Ricci components medium

exercises: the Ricci contraction term by term, bookkeeping under pressure

Using the deck's nine Christoffel symbols and \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r} = \frac{A'}{2A} + \frac{B'}{2B} + \frac2r\), compute, term by term as worked example 2 did for \(R_{\theta\theta}\):

  1. \(R_{tt}\), and check it equals \(c^2\Big[\frac{A''}{2B} - \frac{A'}{4B}\big(\frac{A'}{A} + \frac{B'}{B}\big) + \frac{A'}{rB}\Big]\);
  2. \(R_{rr}\), and check it equals \(-\frac{A''}{2A} + \frac{A'}{4A}\big(\frac{A'}{A} + \frac{B'}{B}\big) + \frac{B'}{rB}\);
  3. then verify the combination \(\frac{B}{c^2A}R_{tt} + R_{rr} = \frac{(AB)'}{rAB}\), and say in one sentence why a combination in which \(A''\) cancels had to exist.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two expressions in \(A\), \(B\) and their first two derivatives, then one identity. Each is four terms of \(R_{\sigma\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}\).

Rung 2

For \(R_{tt}\), the second term vanishes because \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu t} = \partial_t\ln\sqrt{-g} = 0\); in the fourth, the only pairs with a nonzero product are \((\mu,\lambda) = (t, r)\) and \((r, t)\), which are equal, giving \(-2\Gamma^t_{tr}\Gamma^r_{tt}\). For \(R_{rr}\), the second term is \(-\partial_r\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r}\), which is where \(A''\) enters with the opposite sign.

Rung 3

First line of \(R_{tt}\): \(\partial_r\Gamma^r_{tt} = \partial_r\big(\frac{c^2A'}{2B}\big) = \frac{c^2A''}{2B} - \frac{c^2A'B'}{2B^2}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Both components are four terms; the discipline is to list, for each term, exactly which index values survive before writing anything down.

1. \(R_{tt}\).

  • Term 1: \(\partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{tt} = \partial_r\Gamma^r_{tt} = \frac{c^2A''}{2B} - \frac{c^2A'B'}{2B^2}\).
  • Term 2: \(-\partial_t\Gamma^\mu_{\mu t} = 0\), since \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu t} = \partial_t\ln\sqrt{-g} = 0\).
  • Term 3: \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{tt}\) has only \(\lambda = r\): \(\big(\frac{A'}{2A} + \frac{B'}{2B} + \frac2r\big)\frac{c^2A'}{2B}\).
  • Term 4: \(-\Gamma^\mu_{t\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu t}\) has \((\mu,\lambda) = (t,r)\) and \((r,t)\), each giving \(\Gamma^t_{tr}\Gamma^r_{tt}\): \(-2\cdot\frac{A'}{2A}\cdot\frac{c^2A'}{2B} = -\frac{c^2A'^2}{2AB}\).

Adding, and dividing out \(c^2\): \[\frac{A''}{2B} - \frac{A'B'}{2B^2} + \frac{A'^2}{4AB} + \frac{A'B'}{4B^2} + \frac{A'}{rB} - \frac{A'^2}{2AB} = \frac{A''}{2B} - \frac{A'^2}{4AB} - \frac{A'B'}{4B^2} + \frac{A'}{rB},\] which is the quoted expression, since \(\frac{A'}{4B}\big(\frac{A'}{A} + \frac{B'}{B}\big) = \frac{A'^2}{4AB} + \frac{A'B'}{4B^2}\) ✓.

2. \(R_{rr}\).

  • Term 1: \(\partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{rr} = \partial_r\Gamma^r_{rr} = \partial_r\frac{B'}{2B} = \frac{B''}{2B} - \frac{B'^2}{2B^2}\).
  • Term 2: \(-\partial_r\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r} = -\partial_r\big(\frac{A'}{2A} + \frac{B'}{2B} + \frac2r\big) = -\frac{A''}{2A} + \frac{A'^2}{2A^2} - \frac{B''}{2B} + \frac{B'^2}{2B^2} + \frac{2}{r^2}\).
  • Term 3: \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r}\Gamma^r_{rr} = \big(\frac{A'}{2A} + \frac{B'}{2B} + \frac2r\big) \frac{B'}{2B}\).
  • Term 4: \(-\Gamma^\mu_{r\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu r}\). The pairs are \((t,t)\), \((r,r)\), \((\theta,\theta)\), \((\varphi,\varphi)\): \(-\big[\big(\frac{A'}{2A}\big)^2 + \big(\frac{B'}{2B}\big)^2 + \frac{1}{r^2} + \frac1{r^2}\big]\).

Terms 1 and 2 cancel the \(B''\) and the \(B'^2\) pieces; the \(2/r^2\) of term 2 cancels the \(-2/r^2\) of term 4; what is left is \[-\frac{A''}{2A} + \frac{A'^2}{2A^2} + \frac{A'B'}{4AB} + \frac{B'^2}{4B^2} + \frac{B'}{rB} - \frac{A'^2}{4A^2} - \frac{B'^2}{4B^2} = -\frac{A''}{2A} + \frac{A'^2}{4A^2} + \frac{A'B'}{4AB} + \frac{B'}{rB},\] which is the quoted expression ✓.

3. The combination. Multiplying \(R_{tt}\) by \(B/(c^2A)\) gives \(\frac{A''}{2A} - \frac{A'}{4A}\big(\frac{A'}{A} + \frac{B'}{B}\big) + \frac{A'}{rA}\). The first two terms are exactly minus the first two of \(R_{rr}\), so the sum is \(\frac{A'}{rA} + \frac{B'}{rB} = \frac{(AB)'}{rAB}\) ✓. Such a combination had to exist because \(R_{tt}\) and \(R_{rr}\) are both second order in \(A\) and the \(A''\) enters each of them through the same object, \(\Gamma^r_{tt}\) in one case and \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r}\) in the other, with opposite signs; more structurally, the contracted Bianchi identity guarantees one relation among the three equations, and this is its first-order shadow.

Check. Dimensions: \(R_{tt}\) carries \(c^2\) because \(g_{tt}\) does, and \(R_{rr}\) does not ✓. Flat: \(A = B = 1\) kills both ✓. Schwarzschild, \(A = f\), \(B = 1/f\): the brackets vanish, leaving \(R_{tt} = c^2f(\frac{f''}2 + \frac{f'}r) = 0\) and \(R_{rr} = -\frac{1}{f}(\frac{f''}{2} + \frac{f'}{r}) = 0\) ✓ — both by the same cancellation \(f'' /2 = -r_s/r^3 = -f'/r\).

P4 Proof: Birkhoff medium

exercises: a time-dependent ansatz, \(R_{tr}\), and what "forced to be static" buys

Take the spherically symmetric but possibly time-dependent metric \(\mathrm ds^2 = -A(t,r)c^2\mathrm dt^2 + B(t,r)\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2\). Write \(\dot{\ } = \partial_t\), \(' = \partial_r\).

  1. Show that the Christoffel list gains exactly three symbols, \(\Gamma^t_{tt} = \dot A/2A\), \(\Gamma^t_{rr} = \dot B/(2Ac^2)\), \(\Gamma^r_{tr} = \dot B/2B\), and that \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu t} = \dot A/2A + \dot B/2B\).
  2. Prove \(R_{tr} = \dot B/(rB)\), keeping every term.
  3. Complete the proof of Birkhoff's theorem from there.
  4. State the two physical consequences the deck drew, and say for each one which step of the proof it uses.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 2 is an identity between functions, not a number; parts 3 and 4 are prose with equations in them. The hard part is part 2, and it is pure bookkeeping: four terms, each a short list of surviving index values.

Rung 2

In \(R_{tr} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{rt} - \partial_r\Gamma^\mu_{\mu t} + \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{rt} - \Gamma^\mu_{r\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu t}\), the first two terms involve only \(\partial_t\partial_r\ln A\) and \(\partial_r(\dot B/2B)\) and cancel by equality of mixed partials. In the last two, list the surviving \((\mu,\lambda)\) pairs and cross them off in pairs; the survivor is the one carrying the \(2/r\) from \(\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} + \Gamma^\varphi_{\varphi r}\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\Gamma^\mu_{rt}\) is nonzero for \(\mu = t\) (value \(A'/2A\)) and \(\mu = r\) (value \(\dot B/2B\)), so term 1 is \(\partial_t(A'/2A) + \partial_r(\dot B/2B)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Time dependence enters the Ricci tensor first through the one component that was previously forbidden by symmetry, \(R_{tr}\); that component is proportional to \(\dot B\), so vacuum kills the time dependence of \(B\) immediately, and \(A\)'s survives only as a factor that a change of clock removes.

1. The new symbols. From the Lagrangian \(\mathcal L = -Ac^2\dot t^2 + B\dot r^2 + \ldots\), the \(t\) equation is now \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}(-2Ac^2\dot t) = \partial_t\mathcal L = -\dot Ac^2\dot t^2 + \dot B\dot r^2\), i.e. \[\ddot t + \frac{\dot A}{2A}\dot t^2 + \frac{A'}{A}\dot t\dot r + \frac{\dot B}{2Ac^2}\dot r^2 = 0,\] giving \(\Gamma^t_{tt} = \dot A/2A\), \(\Gamma^t_{tr} = A'/2A\) (unchanged) and \(\Gamma^t_{rr} = \dot B/(2Ac^2)\). The \(r\) equation gains \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}(2B\dot r) \supset 2\dot B\dot t\dot r\), giving \(\Gamma^r_{tr} = \dot B/2B\); all the old symbols keep their form. And \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu t} = \partial_t\ln\sqrt{-g} = \partial_t\ln(c\sqrt{AB}r^2\sin\theta) = \frac{\dot A}{2A} + \frac{\dot B}{2B}\).

2. \(R_{tr}\).

  • Term 1: \(\partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{rt} = \partial_t\Gamma^t_{rt} + \partial_r\Gamma^r_{rt} = \partial_t\frac{A'}{2A} + \partial_r\frac{\dot B}{2B}\).
  • Term 2: \(-\partial_r\Gamma^\mu_{\mu t} = -\partial_r\frac{\dot A}{2A} - \partial_r\frac{\dot B}{2B}\). Since \(\partial_t\frac{A'}{2A} = \partial_t\partial_r\tfrac12\ln A = \partial_r\frac{\dot A}{2A}\), terms 1 and 2 cancel entirely.
  • Term 3: \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{rt}\) with \(\lambda = t\) and \(\lambda = r\): \(\big(\frac{\dot A}{2A} + \frac{\dot B}{2B}\big)\frac{A'}{2A} + \big(\frac{A'}{2A} + \frac{B'}{2B} + \frac2r\big)\frac{\dot B}{2B}\).
  • Term 4: \(-\Gamma^\mu_{r\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu t}\). The nonzero products are \((\mu,\lambda) = (t,t)\): \(\Gamma^t_{rt}\Gamma^t_{tt} = \frac{A'}{2A}\frac{\dot A}{2A}\); \((r,t)\): \(\Gamma^r_{rt}\Gamma^t_{rt} = \frac{\dot B}{2B}\frac{A'}{2A}\); \((t,r)\): \(\Gamma^t_{rr}\Gamma^r_{tt} = \frac{\dot B}{2Ac^2}\cdot\frac{c^2A'}{2B} = \frac{A'\dot B}{4AB}\); \((r,r)\): \(\Gamma^r_{rr}\Gamma^r_{rt} = \frac{B'}{2B}\frac{\dot B}{2B}\). All four enter with a minus sign.

Term 3 expands to \(\frac{\dot AA'}{4A^2} + \frac{\dot BA'}{4AB} + \frac{A'\dot B}{4AB} + \frac{B'\dot B}{4B^2} + \frac{\dot B}{rB}\), and term 4 subtracts \(\frac{\dot AA'}{4A^2} + \frac{\dot BA'}{4AB} + \frac{A'\dot B}{4AB} + \frac{B'\dot B}{4B^2}\). Everything cancels except \[R_{tr} = \frac{\dot B}{rB}. \qquad\square\]

3. The rest of the proof. Vacuum gives \(\dot B = 0\), so \(B = B(r)\). The component \(R_{\theta\theta}\) involves only \(\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}\), \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r}\) and the angular symbols, none of them differentiated in \(t\), so it keeps its static form \(1 - \frac1B + \frac{r}{2B}\big(\frac{B'}{B} - \frac{A'}{A}\big)\). Setting it to zero, \[\frac{A'}{A} = \frac{B'}{B} + \frac{2(B-1)}{r},\] whose right-hand side is a function of \(r\) alone; integrating in \(r\) at fixed \(t\), \(\ln A = F(r) + h(t)\), i.e. \(A = e^{h(t)}a(r)\) with \(a = e^F\). Define a new time by \(\mathrm d\tilde t = e^{h(t)/2}\mathrm dt\); then \(A c^2\mathrm dt^2 = a(r)c^2\mathrm d\tilde t^2\) and the metric is the static ansatz, whose unique vacuum solution is Schwarzschild (deck, section 2). \(\square\)

4. Consequences. (i) No monopole radiation. A round star that pulsates or collapses has an exterior that is Schwarzschild with a constant \(r_s\) at every instant, so nothing propagates outwards. This uses the whole theorem, and in particular step 1: it is \(\dot B = 0\) that forbids a time-dependent exterior. (ii) A flat cavity. Inside an empty round cavity, the theorem gives Schwarzschild with some \(r_s\), and regularity at the centre — which would otherwise carry a curvature singularity, since \(K = 12r_s^2/r^6\) — forces \(r_s = 0\). This uses the theorem's conclusion plus the invariant of P9.

Check. Dimensions: \(\dot B/(rB)\) is \(\mathrm{s^{-1}m^{-1}}\), and \(R_{tr}\) should be \([g_{tr}]/\text{length}^2 = \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}/\mathrm{m^2}\) ✓. Limit: \(B\) static gives \(R_{tr} = 0\), recovering the symmetry argument of the deck, which said \(R_{tr} = 0\) for a static metric ✓. Special case \(A = B = 1\): flat, all components zero ✓.

4. The reconciliation. So the crossing has no \(t\)-value at all, and "\(t\to\infty\)" is the statement that a coordinate runs out, not that a process slows down. The ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinate \(v = ct + r_*\) assigns the crossing a perfectly finite value, and in that chart the body's worldline passes through \(r = r_s\) with \(\mathrm dv/\mathrm d\tau\) finite and positive.

Check. Dimensions: the cycloid prefactor is \((\text{m}/(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}))\times \text{dimensionless} = \)s ✓. Newtonian limit: for \(r_0\ll\) nothing in particular — set \(c^2r_s = 2GM\) and the equation \(\dot r^2 = 2GM(1/r - 1/r_0)\) is Newton's exactly, whose solution is the classical cycloid with period \(2\pi\sqrt{r_0^3/(8GM)}\); our \(\tau(\pi) = \frac{\pi r_0}{2c}\sqrt{r_0/r_s} = \frac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{r_0^3/(2GM)}\), which is half that period ✓, as it must be for a fall rather than a round trip. Number check on part 2: the last stretch, \(67.76\ \mu\)s, is longer than the \(65.67\ \mu\)s the deck computed for a body that arrives from rest at infinity, which is right — arriving slower means taking longer ✓.

Each nonzero component has the form \(R_{abab}\) with \(a\ne b\); the orderings \(abab, baba, abba, baab\) give \(x, x, -x, -x\), all squaring to \(x^2\). Count the six unordered pairs. For part 3, solve \(12/r_s^4 = K_\oplus\) for \(r_s\) and convert.

Rung 3

\(K = 4\big[(r_s/r^3)^2 + (r_s/r^3)^2 + 4(r_s/2r^3)^2\big]\): two "big" pairs (\(\hat t\hat r\) and \(\hat\theta\hat\varphi\)) and four "small" ones.

Worked solution

Idea. The contraction is a sum of squares because each nonzero component carries the time index twice or not at all; the arithmetic is then a count, and the physics is in how violently the answer falls with \(M\).

1. The contraction. In an orthonormal frame, \(R^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta} = \eta^{\alpha\alpha'}\eta^{\beta\beta'}\eta^{\gamma\gamma'} \eta^{\delta\delta'}R_{\alpha'\beta'\gamma'\delta'}\), and each raised \(\hat t\) contributes \(-1\). Every nonzero component of Schwarzschild has either no \(\hat t\) index or exactly two, so each product \(R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}R^{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\) (no sum) equals \(+(R_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta})^2\). The nonzero components are the six "sectional" ones \(R_{abab}\), \(a\ne b\), and for each unordered pair the symmetries \(R_{abab} = R_{baba} = -R_{abba} = -R_{baab}\) give four index orderings with the same square. Hence \[K = 4\sum_{\{a,b\}}(R_{abab})^2 = 4\Big[2\Big(\frac{r_s}{r^3}\Big)^2 + 4\Big(\frac{r_s}{2r^3}\Big)^2\Big] = 4\cdot3\,\frac{r_s^2}{r^6} = \frac{12r_s^2}{r^6} = \frac{48G^2M^2}{c^4r^6}.\]

2. Two numbers. At a horizon, \(K = 12/r_s^4\). For \(r_s = 29\,532.5\) m: \(K = 12/(29\,532.5)^4 = 1.58\times10^{-17}\ \mathrm{m^{-4}}\). At the Earth's surface, \(r_s = 8.870\times10^{-3}\) m and \(r = R_\oplus = 6.378137\times10^{6}\) m: \(K = 12\times(8.870\times10^{-3})^2/(6.378137\times10^{6})^6 = 1.40\times10^{-44}\ \mathrm{m^{-4}}\).

3. The crossover. \(12/r_s^4 = 1.402\times10^{-44}\) gives \(r_s = (12/1.402\times10^{-44})^{1/4} = 1.710\times10^{11}\) m, and \(M = r_sc^2/2G = 1.15\times10^{38}\) kg \(= 5.8\times10^{7}M_\odot\). A hole of some fifty-eight million solar masses has a horizon at which spacetime is less curved than it is in this room.

4. The logic. The colleague's argument is invalid: finiteness of one invariant, or even of all polynomial curvature invariants, does not imply that the geometry extends. Exact plane gravitational waves have every such invariant zero and are not flat; Minkowski space with a point deleted has all invariants zero and is singular by any reasonable definition. The correct argument for the horizon is constructive: exhibit a chart — ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein — in which the metric components are smooth and \(\det g = -r^4\sin^2\theta \ne 0\) at \(r = r_s\). That proves the geometry extends, because it displays the extension.

At \(r = 0\) the argument runs the other way and is immune to the objection because it is a proof by contradiction about every chart at once: \(K\) is a scalar, so its value along a curve does not depend on coordinates; a timelike geodesic reaches \(r\to0\) in finite proper time, with \(K = 12r_s^2/r^6\to\infty\) along it; if the metric extended as a \(C^2\) tensor field to a manifold containing that curve's endpoint, \(K\) would be continuous, hence bounded, near the endpoint. Contradiction. No chart can exist, so none need be searched for.

Check. Dimensions: \([K] = \mathrm{m^{-4}}\), the square of a curvature ✓. Limits: \(M\to0\) gives \(K\to0\); \(r\to\infty\) gives \(K\to0\) ✓. Scaling check on part 3: \(K\) at a horizon scales as \(M^{-4}\), so going from \(10\,M_\odot\) (\(1.58\times10^{-17}\)) down to \(1.40\times10^{-44}\) needs a factor \((1.13\times10^{27})^{1/4} = 5.8\times10^{6}\) in mass, i.e. \(5.8\times10^{7}M_\odot\) ✓.

Rung 2

\(\mathrm dv = \ell\sec^2p\,\mathrm dp\) and \(\mathrm du = \ell\sec^2q\,\mathrm dq\); and \(2\cos p\cos q = \cos(p - q) + \cos(p + q)\) is the identity that turns \(r\) into the quoted expression.

Idea. Squash the two null coordinates separately with a bounded increasing function. Light cones do not notice, because a conformal factor cannot change the sign of \(g(X,X)\); so the picture is exact about causality and wrong about lengths, which is the trade one wants.

1. The metric. First, \(-c^2\mathrm dt^2 + \mathrm dr^2 = -\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv\) since \(u = ct - r\), \(v = ct + r\) give \(\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv = c^2\mathrm dt^2 - \mathrm dr^2\). The two formulas. \(r = \frac{v - u}{2} = \frac{\ell}{2}(\tan p - \tan q) = \frac{\ell}{2}\cdot\frac{\sin(p - q)}{\cos p\cos q} = \frac{\ell\sin R}{2\cos p\cos q}\), and \(2\cos p\cos q = \cos(p-q) + \cos(p+q) = \cos R + \cos T\), giving \(r/\ell = \frac{\sin R}{\cos R + \cos T}\) ✓. Identically, \(ct = \frac{u + v}{2} = \frac{\ell}{2}(\tan p + \tan q) = \frac{\ell\sin(p+q)}{2\cos p\cos q} = \frac{\ell\sin T}{\cos R + \cos T}\) ✓.

4. Three curves. \(r = \) const is a timelike curve running from \(i^-\) to \(i^+\), bulging to the right (larger \(R\)) as \(r\) grows, since by part 2 a constant \(r\) requires \(\sin R \propto \cos R + \cos T\), which is largest at \(T = 0\). \(t = \) const runs from the left edge \(R = 0\) (which is \(r = 0\), the centre) to \(i^0\), passing through \(T = 0\) when \(t = 0\). Rung 1

Worked solution

Idea. Inside the horizon \(r\) is a time, and every future-directed curve must spend it. The proper time elapsed is largest when nothing else is happening — no motion in \(t\), no motion in the angles — and that is a specific geodesic, not a hovering observer, because hovering is not available.

1. The inequality. For \(r \lt r_s\), write \(F := r_s/r - 1 \gt 0\). Then \(f = -F\) and \[\mathrm ds^2 = Fc^2\mathrm dt^2 - \frac{\mathrm dr^2}{F} + r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2 .\] The coefficient of \(\mathrm dr^2\) is negative and that of \(\mathrm dt^2\) positive: \(r\) is the timelike coordinate. Along a timelike curve, \(\mathrm ds^2 = -c^2\mathrm d\tau^2\), so \[c^2\mathrm d\tau^2 = \frac{\mathrm dr^2}{F} - Fc^2\mathrm dt^2 - r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2 \le \frac{\mathrm dr^2}{F},\] since the two subtracted terms are non-negative. 2. The integral. Substitute \(r = r_s\sin^2\psi\), \(\psi: 0\to\pi/2\). 3. The extremal curve. Equality needs \(\mathrm d\Omega = 0\) and \(\mathrm dt = 0\) — no angular motion, and \(t\) constant. It is the limit of falls released from rest at \(r_0\to r_s^+\): a body that is dropped from just outside the horizon, with no outward or sideways motion at all, lives longest.

4. The numbers, and the advice. \(10\,M_\odot\): \(\tau_{\max} = \pi\times29\,532.5/(2\times2.99792458\times10^{8}) = 1.547\times10^{-4}\) s \(= 154.7\ \mu\)s. \(4.3\times10^{6}M_\odot\): \(r_s = 1.2699\times10^{10}\) m and \(\tau_{\max} = 66.54\) s. The advice: do not fire the engines.
