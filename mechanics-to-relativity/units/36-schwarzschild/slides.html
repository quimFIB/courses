Schwarzschild: the first exact solution, and what its two singularities are

Solve the vacuum field equations for a round, static star, once and slowly. The answer breaks down in two places: one is a badly chosen clock, and the other is the end of time.

needs 35, 13~3½ h slides + 7 h problemsproblems: ./mr problems 36

Unit 35 produced an equation. This unit produces the first thing that equation says, and it is worth pausing on how unusual that is: Einstein published the field equations in November 1915 believing an exact solution would be out of reach, and Karl Schwarzschild, serving on the Russian front, sent him one within weeks. Everything in unit 37 — Mercury's 43 arcseconds, the bending of starlight, the radar echo — is read off the metric derived here.

The unit has two halves of different character. The first half is a computation: impose symmetry, solve $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$, fix one constant. It is done in full, every Christoffel symbol and every Ricci component, because this is the one place in the course where the machinery of units 31–33 runs from start to finish on a problem whose answer matters, and because doing it once by hand is what makes every later citation of "the Schwarzschild metric" feel earned. The second half is interpretation: the metric has two places where its components misbehave, and deciding what each one is requires two different tools — a change of chart for one, an invariant for the other.

section 1

The ansatz

What "static" and "spherically symmetric" mean as statements about a metric, and why together they leave two functions of one variable.

36 · ansatz

Static and spherically symmetric, defined

definition · round

$(M, g)$ is spherically symmetric if $SO(3)$ acts on $M$ by isometries and every orbit is a round 2-sphere (or a point). The areal radius of a point is $$r(p) := \sqrt{\operatorname{Area}(\text{orbit of } p)/4\pi}.$$

definition · unchanging

$(M,g)$ is stationary if it has a Killing field $\xi$ (unit 31) that is timelike, and static if moreover $\xi$ is orthogonal to a family of hypersurfaces.

the physics

Outside a non-rotating star nothing depends on direction or on time. "Round" is the first statement, "static" the second. A rotating star is stationary but not static: the orthogonality fails, and that failure is frame dragging (unit 40).

what $r$ is not

$r$ is not a distance from a centre. It is defined by the area of a sphere, and the "centre" need not even be part of the spacetime. Radial distances will turn out longer than differences of $r$.

Why define $r$ by area. In flat space the sphere of radius $r$ has area $4\pi r^2$, and there are three candidate definitions of "radius" that agree: area, circumference over $2\pi$, and distance from the centre. In curved space they disagree, and only the first two are available without first knowing where the centre is. Areal radius is also a scalar function on spacetime, fixed by the geometry with no choice: that is what makes it a legitimate coordinate to solve for things in.

Static, unpacked. By Frobenius (unit 31), $\xi$ is orthogonal to a family of hypersurfaces exactly when the distribution $\xi^\perp$ is involutive. Take one such hypersurface $\Sigma$, put coordinates $x^i$ on it, and carry them along the flow of $\xi$; call the flow parameter $t$. Then $\xi = \partial_t$, and because the flow is by isometries, $\partial_tg_{\mu\nu} = 0$ (unit 31's Killing condition in adapted coordinates). Because $\partial_t$ is orthogonal to the slices, $g_{ti} = 0$. So a static metric has the form $-N(x)^2c^2\mathrm dt^2 + h_{ij}(x)\,\mathrm dx^i\mathrm dx^j$, with nothing depending on $t$ and no cross terms. Unit 30's Rindler metric is of exactly this form — the reminder that "static" describes a symmetry, not the presence of gravity.

Why the distinction matters physically. Time reversal $t\mapsto-t$ is an isometry of a static metric: a film of the geometry run backwards is the same film. A rotating star run backwards rotates the other way, which is a different geometry. That is the whole content of "stationary but not static".

36 · ansatz

Why the ansatz has four terms

theorem · the Schwarzschild ansatz

A static, spherically symmetric metric, in a region where $\xi$ is timelike and $\mathrm dr \ne 0$, has coordinates in which

$$\mathrm ds^2 = -A(r)\,c^2\mathrm dt^2 + B(r)\,\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\,\mathrm d\Omega^2$$

with $A, B \gt 0$: the ansatz.

lemma · rotation fixes nothing

Rotation by $90^\circ$ fixes no nonzero vector of $\mathbb R^2$, and the symmetric bilinear forms it preserves are exactly the multiples of the dot product.

Proof. $\rho(x,y) = (-y, x)$. If $b$ is $\rho$-invariant, $b(e_1,e_1) = b(e_2,e_2)$ and $b(e_1,e_2) = b(e_2,-e_1) = -b(e_1,e_2)$, so $b(e_1,e_2) = 0$. $\square$

proof, in three moves

1. Static: $g_{ti} = 0$, no $t$-dependence.
2. At a point $p$, the rotations fixing $p$ form an $SO(2)$ acting on the sphere's tangent plane and fixing the $(t, r)$ directions. By the lemma, cross terms $\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\theta$, $\mathrm dr\,\mathrm d\varphi$ vanish and the angular part is $\lambda\,\mathrm d\Omega^2$; the definition of $r$ makes $\lambda = r^2$.
3. What remains, $-Ac^2\mathrm dt^2 + B\,\mathrm dr^2$, cannot depend on $t$ (step 1) or on the angles (rotations), so $A, B$ are functions of $r$. $\square$

Ten functions of four variables have become two functions of one — and for the hole those two will turn out to be fixed by a single length, $29.53$ km.

Full proof. Step 2 needs care. Fix $p$ and let $H \cong SO(2)$ be its isotropy group, the rotations about the axis through $p$'s orbit point. Each $h\in H$ is an isometry fixing $p$, so its differential $\mathrm dh_p$ is a linear isometry of $T_pM$. $H$ maps each orbit to itself, so $\mathrm dh_p$ preserves the 2-plane $S_p$ tangent to the orbit and acts on it as a rotation, and preserves the orthogonal 2-plane $N_p = S_p^\perp$. On $N_p$ it acts trivially: $N_p$ is spanned by $\xi$ (which commutes with the rotations — built into "static and spherically symmetric", the two symmetries being taken together) and by $\nabla r$ ($r$ is rotation-invariant, so $\mathrm dh_p$ fixes its gradient). Now let $n \in N_p$. The linear form $s \mapsto g(n, s)$ on $S_p$ is $H$-invariant, so the vector representing it in $S_p$ is fixed by a $90^\circ$ rotation, and by the lemma it is zero: there are no cross terms between $N_p$ and $S_p$. The restriction of $g$ to $S_p$ is an $H$-invariant symmetric form, so by the lemma a multiple of the round metric, and the multiple is $r^2$ by the definition of areal radius. With $t$ the Killing parameter and $r$ the areal radius as coordinates on $N$ (legitimate where $\mathrm dr \ne 0$), $g_{tr} = 0$ because $\partial_t$ is orthogonal to the slices and $\partial_r$ is tangent to them. $\square$

Cited: that the orthogonal planes $N_p$ fit together into surfaces, so that $(t, r)$ are coordinates on them — Frobenius again, applied to the orthogonal distribution. A careful account is in R. M. Wald, General Relativity (1984), §6.1, and in Hawking and Ellis, The Large Scale Structure of Space-Time (1973), Appendix B.

The residual freedom. The theorem leaves exactly one: $t \mapsto kt$ for a constant $k \gt 0$, which multiplies $A$ by $k^{-2}$. Keep it in mind; it is spent in section 2.

Why no $\mathrm dt\,\mathrm d\varphi$? That is the term a rotating star has, and the lemma is exactly what forbids it here: it would be a nonzero invariant vector on the sphere.

Worked example 1 — the nine Christoffel symbols

unit 32's shortcut: Euler–Lagrange for $\mathcal L = g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu$

$\mathcal L = -Ac^2\dot t^2 + B\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2$, $\ ' = \mathrm d/\mathrm dr$.

$t$: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}(-2Ac^2\dot t) = 0$, i.e. $A\ddot t + A'\dot r\dot t = 0$: $$\ddot t + \frac{A'}{A}\dot r\dot t = 0 .$$

$r$: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}(2B\dot r) = 2B\ddot r + 2B'\dot r^2$ equals $\partial_r\mathcal L = -A'c^2\dot t^2 + B'\dot r^2 + 2r\dot\theta^2 + 2r\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi^2$. Divide by $2B$: $$\begin{aligned}\ddot r &+ \frac{c^2A'}{2B}\dot t^2 + \frac{B'}{2B}\dot r^2\\ &- \frac rB\dot\theta^2 - \frac{r\sin^2\theta}{B}\dot\varphi^2 = 0 .\end{aligned}$$

$\theta$, $\varphi$: the sphere's, with $r$ variable (unit 32).

Read off each $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$ as the coefficient of $\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta$ (halved when $\alpha\ne\beta$). Nine of forty survive:

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma^t_{tr} &= \frac{A'}{2A}, & \Gamma^r_{tt} &= \frac{c^2A'}{2B},\\ \Gamma^r_{rr} &= \frac{B'}{2B}, & \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} &= -\frac rB,\\ \Gamma^r_{\varphi\varphi} &= -\frac{r\sin^2\theta}{B}, & \Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} &= \Gamma^\varphi_{r\varphi} = \frac1r,\\ \Gamma^\theta_{\varphi\varphi} &= -\sin\theta\cos\theta, & \Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi} &= \cot\theta . \end{aligned}$$
checks

$B = 1/A$ gives unit 32's worked example 5 ✓. $A = B = 1$: flat space in spherical coordinates ✓. $[\Gamma^r_{tt}\dot t^2] = [c^2/\text{length}]\,\mathrm s^2/[\lambda]^2$, a length per $[\lambda]^2$, like $\ddot r$ ✓.

Why the shortcut is legitimate. Unit 32 proved that the Euler–Lagrange equations of the energy functional $\int g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu\,\mathrm d\lambda$ are the geodesic equations $\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta = 0$, with the Levi-Civita $\Gamma$. Since $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$ is symmetric in its lower indices, the coefficient of $\dot x^\alpha\dot x^\beta$ with $\alpha \ne \beta$ in the $\mu$-th equation is $2\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$ — hence the halving, which is where $\Gamma^t_{tr} = A'/2A$ comes from.

The angular equations, written out. $\theta$: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}(2r^2\dot\theta) = 2r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2$, so $\ddot\theta + \frac2r\dot r\dot\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2 = 0$. $\varphi$: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}(2r^2\sin^2\theta\, \dot\varphi) = 0$, so $\ddot\varphi + \frac2r\dot r\dot\varphi + 2\cot\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\varphi = 0$. Neither contains $A$ or $B$, because $g_{\theta\theta}$ and $g_{\varphi\varphi}$ do not.

Two symbols checked against the Koszul formula (unit 32), $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta} = \tfrac12g^{\mu\nu}(\partial_\alpha g_{\nu\beta} + \partial_\beta g_{\nu\alpha} - \partial_\nu g_{\alpha\beta})$: $\Gamma^r_{tt} = \tfrac12 g^{rr}(-\partial_rg_{tt}) = \tfrac1{2B}\,\partial_r(Ac^2) = c^2A'/2B$ ✓; $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac12g^{rr}(-\partial_r r^2) = -r/B$ ✓. Problem P2 checks the rest and runs unit 32's metric-compatibility test.

Why thirty-one vanish. Every metric component is independent of $t$ and $\varphi$, and the metric is diagonal. For a diagonal metric $\Gamma^\mu_{\alpha\beta}$ with three distinct indices is zero, and every surviving symbol involves a derivative of some $g_{\mu\mu}$ by $r$ or $\theta$ — which is exactly the list.

The Ricci tensor, and the two tools that shorten it

unit 33's contraction, written out $$\begin{aligned}R_{\sigma\nu} = {}&\partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\sigma}\\ &+ \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}\end{aligned}$$
tool 1 · the trace of $\Gamma$ $$\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda} = \partial_\lambda\ln\sqrt{-g},$$ $$\sqrt{-g} = c\sqrt{AB}\,r^2\sin\theta .$$

So $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r} = \frac{A'}{2A} + \frac{B'}{2B} + \frac2r$ and $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\theta} = \cot\theta$.

tool 2 · symmetry kills the off-diagonal

$R_{\mu\nu}$ is built from $g$ alone, so every isometry preserves it. Under $t\mapsto -t$, $R_{tr}$, $R_{t\theta}$, $R_{t\varphi}$ change sign: they vanish. $R_{r\theta}$, $R_{r\varphi}$ would define an invariant vector on the sphere: zero by the lemma. $R_{\theta\varphi} = 0$ and $R_{\varphi\varphi} = \sin^2\theta\,R_{\theta\theta}$ by the lemma's second half.

Four components remain: $R_{tt}$, $R_{rr}$, $R_{\theta\theta}$, and $R_{\varphi\varphi}$, which comes free.

Full proof of tool 1. $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda} = \tfrac12g^{\mu\nu}(\partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\lambda g_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\nu g_{\mu\lambda})$. The first and third terms cancel after swapping the dummy indices $\mu\leftrightarrow\nu$ (using $g^{\mu\nu}$ symmetric). What remains is $\tfrac12g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(g^{-1}\partial_\lambda g) = \tfrac12\partial_\lambda\ln|\det g|$ by Jacobi's formula for the derivative of a determinant, which unit 35 used in varying $\sqrt{-g}$. Here $\det g = -Ac^2\cdot B\cdot r^2\cdot r^2\sin^2\theta$. $\square$

Full proof of tool 2. An isometry $\phi$ satisfies $\phi^*g = g$; the Levi-Civita connection is determined by $g$ (unit 32), so $\phi$ also preserves it, and therefore preserves every tensor built from it: $\phi^*\mathrm{Ric} = \mathrm{Ric}$. The map $t\mapsto -t$ is an isometry of the ansatz (it appears only as $\mathrm dt^2$), and it pulls $R_{tr}\,\mathrm dt\,\mathrm dr$ back to $-R_{tr}\,\mathrm dt\,\mathrm dr$, evaluated at the same point since nothing depends on $t$. So $R_{tr} = -R_{tr} = 0$; likewise $R_{t\theta}, R_{t\varphi}$. For $R_{r\theta}$, $R_{r\varphi}$: the rotations fixing a point act trivially on $\partial_r$, so $s\mapsto\mathrm{Ric}(\partial_r, s)$ is an invariant linear form on the sphere's tangent plane, zero by the lemma. The angular block of $\mathrm{Ric}$ is an invariant symmetric form on that plane, so a multiple of the round metric $\mathrm d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\varphi^2$, which says both $R_{\theta\varphi} = 0$ and $R_{\varphi\varphi} = \sin^2\theta\,R_{\theta\theta}$. $\square$

Why bother. The Ricci tensor has ten components, each a sum of up to $4 + 4 + 16 + 16$ terms. The two tools cut the work to three components, and the symmetry argument doubles as a check: if a direct computation of $R_{tr}$ gives something nonzero, there is an error upstream.

Worked example 2 — $R_{\theta\theta}$, every term

$R_{\theta\theta} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{\theta\theta} - \partial_\theta\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\theta} + \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\theta\theta} - \Gamma^\mu_{\theta\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\theta}$

Term 1. Only $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r/B$ is nonzero: $$\partial_r\Big(-\frac rB\Big) = -\frac1B + \frac{rB'}{B^2}.$$

Term 2. $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\theta} = \cot\theta$ (tool 1): $$-\partial_\theta\cot\theta = \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}.$$

Term 3. Only $\lambda = r$ survives: $$\Big(\frac{A'}{2A} + \frac{B'}{2B} + \frac2r\Big)\Big(-\frac rB\Big).$$

Term 4. Three pairs $(\mu,\lambda)$ survive: $(r,\theta)$, $(\theta,r)$, $(\varphi,\varphi)$: $$-\Big[2\cdot\Big(-\frac rB\Big)\frac1r + \cot^2\theta\Big] = \frac2B - \cot^2\theta .$$

Sum. The $\theta$-dependence cancels, $\csc^2\theta - \cot^2\theta = 1$, and the $2/B$ terms cancel: $$R_{\theta\theta} = 1 - \frac1B + \frac{r}{2B}\Big(\frac{B'}{B} - \frac{A'}{A}\Big).$$

checks

$A = B = 1$: $R_{\theta\theta} = 0$ ✓. Dimensionless, as $R_{\theta\theta}$ must be since $\theta$ is ✓. The angles dropped out, as tool 2 promised ✓.

Term 4 in detail. $\Gamma^\mu_{\theta\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\theta}$ summed over $\mu,\lambda$. For the product to be nonzero, $\Gamma^\mu_{\theta\lambda}$ must be on the list with one lower index $\theta$: the candidates are $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}$ ($\mu = r, \lambda = \theta$), $\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}$ ($\mu = \theta,\lambda = r$), and $\Gamma^\varphi_{\theta\varphi}$ ($\mu = \lambda = \varphi$). The partners are $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = 1/r$, $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r/B$ and $\Gamma^\varphi_{\varphi\theta} = \cot\theta$. So the sum is $(-r/B)(1/r) + (1/r)(-r/B) + \cot^2\theta = -2/B + \cot^2\theta$, and with the leading minus sign, $2/B - \cot^2\theta$.

The sum in detail. $-\frac1B + \frac{rB'}{B^2} + \csc^2\theta - \frac{rA'}{2AB} - \frac{rB'}{2B^2} - \frac2B + \frac2B - \cot^2\theta = 1 - \frac1B + \frac{rB'}{2B^2} - \frac{rA'}{2AB}$, which is the boxed form.

The same computation for the other two is problem P3, and its answers are on the next slide. Doing one component in full on the slide and the other two on paper is the right division of labour: the first time, every term needs to be seen; the second and third time, the skill is in not losing a term, and that is learnt only by doing it.

All four Ricci components

the Ricci tensor of the ansatz $$\begin{aligned} R_{tt} &= c^2\Big[\frac{A''}{2B} - \frac{A'}{4B}\Big(\frac{A'}{A} + \frac{B'}{B}\Big) + \frac{A'}{rB}\Big],\\ R_{rr} &= -\frac{A''}{2A} + \frac{A'}{4A}\Big(\frac{A'}{A} + \frac{B'}{B}\Big) + \frac{B'}{rB},\\ R_{\theta\theta} &= 1 - \frac1B + \frac{r}{2B}\Big(\frac{B'}{B} - \frac{A'}{A}\Big),\qquad R_{\varphi\varphi} = \sin^2\theta\,R_{\theta\theta}. \end{aligned}$$

All other components vanish.

checks

$A = B = 1$: all zero ✓. Dimensions: $R_{tt}$ is $c^2/\text{length}^2$ since $g_{tt}$ carries $c^2$; $R_{rr}$ is $1/\text{length}^2$; $R_{\theta\theta}$ is a pure number ✓.

the combination to look for

$R_{tt}$ and $R_{rr}$ share the bracket with $A''$. Multiply $R_{tt}$ by $B/(c^2A)$ and add $R_{rr}$: the $A''$ terms and the brackets cancel.

Where these come from. $R_{tt}$: term 1 is $\partial_r\Gamma^r_{tt} = \partial_r(c^2A'/2B)$; term 2 vanishes ($\Gamma^\mu_{\mu t} = 0$); term 3 is $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r}\Gamma^r_{tt}$; term 4 is $-2\Gamma^t_{tr}\Gamma^r_{tt}$. Adding: $c^2\big[\frac{A''}{2B} - \frac{A'B'}{2B^2} + \big(\frac{A'}{2A} + \frac{B'}{2B} + \frac2r\big)\frac{A'}{2B} - \frac{A'^2}{2AB}\big]$, which collects to the boxed form. $R_{rr}$ is problem P3, where every term is laid out.

An independent check of the whole table. Flat space, $A = B = 1$, is a weak test: it only checks that no term survives when everything is constant. A sharper check is a line times a round 3-sphere, $A = 1$, $B = (1 - r^2/a^2)^{-1}$, a static spacetime whose spatial slices are 3-spheres of radius $a$ (unit 39's closed universe, frozen). Then $R_{tt} = 0$, and unit 33's formula for a maximally symmetric 3-space, $R_{ij} = (2/a^2)h_{ij}$, predicts $R_{rr} = 2B/a^2$ and $R_{\theta\theta} = 2r^2/a^2$. From the table: $B'/B = 2r/(a^2 - r^2)$, so $R_{rr} = \frac{B'}{rB} = \frac{2}{a^2 - r^2} = \frac{2B}{a^2}$ ✓, and $R_{\theta\theta} = \frac{r^2}{a^2} + \frac r2\big(1 - \frac{r^2}{a^2}\big)\frac{2r}{a^2 - r^2} = \frac{r^2}{a^2} + \frac{r^2}{a^2} = \frac{2r^2}{a^2}$ ✓. A formula that passes a non-trivial curved case as well as the flat one is very probably right.

Solving: two lines and one constant

Line 1. The combination: $$\frac{B}{c^2A}R_{tt} + R_{rr} = \frac1r\Big(\frac{A'}{A} + \frac{B'}{B}\Big) = \frac{(AB)'}{rAB}.$$ Vacuum makes it zero, so $AB$ is constant. Asymptotic flatness — $A, B \to 1$ as $r\to\infty$ — makes the constant $1$: $$B = 1/A .$$

Line 2. Put $B = 1/A$, so $B'/B = -A'/A$, into $R_{\theta\theta}$: $$R_{\theta\theta} = 1 - A - rA' = 1 - (rA)' = 0 .$$ So $rA = r - r_s$ for a constant $r_s$: $$A = 1 - \frac{r_s}{r},\qquad B = \Big(1 - \frac{r_s}{r}\Big)^{-1}.$$

the third equation holds by itself

With $f = 1 - r_s/r$: $f' = r_s/r^2$, $f'' = -2r_s/r^3$, and $B = 1/A$ kills the bracket in $R_{tt}$: $$R_{tt} = c^2f\Big(\frac{f''}2 + \frac{f'}r\Big) = c^2f\Big(-\frac{r_s}{r^3} + \frac{r_s}{r^3}\Big) = 0\ ✓$$

why three equations had a solution

Three equations, two unknowns — overdetermined, yet consistent. That is unit 33's contracted Bianchi identity at work: $\nabla^\mu G_{\mu\nu} = 0$ holds for every metric and ties the components together.

Line 1, the algebra. $\frac{B}{c^2A}R_{tt} = \frac{A''}{2A} - \frac{A'}{4A}\big(\frac{A'}{A} + \frac{B'}{B}\big) + \frac{A'}{rA}$. Adding $R_{rr}$, the first two terms cancel against the first two of $R_{rr}$ exactly, leaving $\frac{A'}{rA} + \frac{B'}{rB}$. $\square$

Spending the residual freedom. Line 1 gives $AB = k$ for a constant $k \gt 0$. Section 1 left the freedom to rescale $t$ by a constant: replacing $t$ by $\tilde t = \sqrt{k}\,t$ turns $-Ac^2\mathrm dt^2$ into $-(A/k)c^2\mathrm d\tilde t^2$, so $\tilde A\tilde B = (A/k)B = 1$. Choosing $t$ to be the time of a clock at rest far away — which is what asymptotic flatness means — is exactly this choice. So "$AB = 1$" is not an extra assumption; it is a choice of units for $t$.

Line 2, the integration. $(rA)' = 1$ integrates to $rA = r + C$; writing $C = -r_s$ is only naming the constant, whose sign is not yet known. It is fixed on the next slide, and turns out positive for positive mass.

Why $R_{tt} = 0$ comes free, precisely. For any metric, $\nabla^\mu G_{\mu r} = 0$. For the ansatz, written out with the Christoffel symbols above, this identity expresses $G_{\theta\theta}$ in terms of $G_{tt}$, $G_{rr}$ and $\partial_rG_{rr}$. So once $G_{tt}$ and $G_{rr}$ vanish identically in $r$, the angular equation is automatic. The direct check on the slide is independent of this argument, which is why both are given.

Birkhoff's theorem

Birkhoff's theorem

A spherically symmetric solution of $R_{\mu\nu} = 0$, in a region where $\nabla r$ is spacelike, is isometric to part of the Schwarzschild metric for some $r_s$. In particular it is static: it has a fourth Killing field it was never asked to have.

Drop "static" from the ansatz. Spherical symmetry alone allows (cited: section 1's argument without step 1)

$$\mathrm ds^2 = -A(t,r)c^2\mathrm dt^2 + B(t,r)\,\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\,\mathrm d\Omega^2 .$$
proof

1. The one new component is $$R_{tr} = \frac{\partial_tB}{rB}.$$ Vacuum: $\partial_t B = 0$, so $B = B(r)$.
2. $R_{\theta\theta}$ contains no $t$-derivatives, so $R_{\theta\theta} = 0$ reads as before: $\frac{\partial_rA}{A} = \frac{B'}{B} + \frac{2(B - 1)}{r}$, a function of $r$ alone. So $A = e^{h(t)}a(r)$.
3. New time $\tilde t = \int e^{h(t)/2}\,\mathrm dt$: then $-Ac^2\mathrm dt^2 = -a(r)c^2\mathrm d\tilde t^2$. The metric is now the static ansatz, and section 2 finishes it. $\square$

Why the time-dependent ansatz is general. Section 1's argument without step 1 still gives a metric of the form $h_{ab}\,\mathrm dx^a\mathrm dx^b + r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2$, with $h$ a Lorentzian metric on the two-dimensional orbit space. Where $\nabla r$ is spacelike, $r$ can be one coordinate. For the other, take a function $t$ whose level curves are the integral curves of $\nabla r$ — in two dimensions any nonvanishing vector field has such a function locally, since Frobenius is automatic for a line field. Then $\nabla t \perp \nabla r$, so $h_{tr} = 0$. Cited: the full construction is in Hawking and Ellis, Appendix B, and in Wald, §6.1.

Full proof of step 1. With $A$, $B$ depending on $t$ the Christoffel list gains $\Gamma^t_{tt} = \dot A/2A$, $\Gamma^t_{rr} = \dot B/(2Ac^2)$, $\Gamma^r_{tr} = \dot B/2B$ (dots are $\partial_t$ here). In $R_{tr} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{rt} - \partial_r\Gamma^\mu_{\mu t} + \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{rt} - \Gamma^\mu_{r\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu t}$, the first two terms are $\partial_t(A'/2A) + \partial_r(\dot B/2B) - \partial_r(\dot A/2A + \dot B/2B)$, which is zero by equality of mixed partials of $\ln A$. In the last two, every product cancels against a partner except the one in which $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r}$ contributes its $2/r$ (from $\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r} + \Gamma^\varphi_{\varphi r}$), times $\Gamma^r_{rt} = \dot B/2B$. The net is $\dot B/(rB)$. Problem P4 lays out every term. $\square$

Step 2 in detail. With $B = B(r)$, $R_{\theta\theta} = 1 - \frac1B + \frac r{2B}\big(\frac{B'}B - \frac{\partial_rA}{A}\big)$ exactly as before — the symbols entering $R_{\theta\theta}$ are $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}$, $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r}$ and the angular ones, and none is differentiated in $t$. Setting it to zero and solving for $\partial_rA/A$ gives a function of $r$; integrating in $r$ at fixed $t$ gives $\ln A = F(r) + h(t)$.

After step 3, $A$ and $B$ are functions of $r$ alone, so every $t$-derivative in every component is zero and section 2's computation applies verbatim: $A = 1 - r_s/r$, $B = 1/A$.

History and the name. Jørg Jebsen proved it in 1921 and George Birkhoff independently in 1923; the theorem bears the second name. In the region where $\nabla r$ is timelike — inside the horizon — the same argument with the roles of $t$ and $r$ exchanged gives the Schwarzschild interior, which is not static there; "static" in the theorem is a statement about the exterior.

Two consequences: silence, and a flat cavity

no monopole radiation

A star that pulsates, collapses or explodes, keeping round, has exactly the Schwarzschild metric outside it at every moment, with the same $M$. Nothing propagates outwards: a spherically symmetric gravitational wave does not exist.

The same is true of charge: Gauss's law (units 23, 24) fixes the field outside a round charge distribution, so a pulsating charged sphere does not radiate either. Gravitational waves start at the quadrupole (unit 38).

The hole was once a star that collapsed inside $29.53$ km. At every moment of that collapse the geometry outside the star's surface was Schwarzschild with the same $r_s$: a distant planet's orbit never flinched.

the relativistic shell theorem

Inside an empty spherical cavity — a hollow shell, round, of any mass — spacetime is flat.

Proof. Birkhoff gives Schwarzschild with some $r_s$ for $0 \lt r \lt r_{\text{shell}}$. The cavity's centre is a regular point of spacetime, and section 5's invariant $12r_s^2/r^6$ would diverge there unless $r_s = 0$. $\square$

flat is not the same as unaffected

A clock in the cavity runs slow relative to one at infinity by a constant factor. Newton's shell theorem (unit 05) predicted no force; general relativity adds a redshift.

The pulsating star, precisely. Birkhoff applies in the vacuum region $r \gt R(t)$ outside the star, however $R(t)$ moves. It says the metric there is Schwarzschild for some $r_s$; could $r_s$ change with time? No: the constant $r_s$ is a single number for the whole connected vacuum region, and far away it is fixed by Kepler's law for a distant satellite, which cannot know the star is pulsating, because no signal carrying that news exists. Energy conservation is the physical reason: radiation would carry away mass, and there is no mass change available to carry.

The analogy with electromagnetism, and where it stops. For charge, the absence of monopole radiation follows from charge conservation plus Gauss's law, and the first radiating multipole is the dipole. For gravity the dipole is also silent: the time derivative of the mass dipole moment is the total momentum, which is conserved. So gravitational radiation starts at the quadrupole, which is why it is so weak and why it took until 2015 to see it. Unit 38 derives this.

The cavity, precisely. Inside the shell, Birkhoff gives $A = k(1 - r_s/r)$, $B = (1 - r_s/r)^{-1}$, where now the constant $k$ cannot be set to $1$: $t$ was already fixed by the clocks at infinity, and matching across the shell determines $k$ (cited: continuity of the metric across a thin shell, Israel's junction conditions, 1966). Regularity at $r = 0$ forces $r_s = 0$: with $r_s \ne 0$ the curvature invariant diverges at $r\to 0$, but $r = 0$ is the centre of an empty cavity, which a timelike geodesic passes through in finite proper time. So inside, $\mathrm ds^2 = -k\,c^2\mathrm dt^2 + \mathrm dr^2 + r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2$ — Minkowski, with clocks running at the rate $\sqrt{k}$ relative to infinity. For a thin shell of mass $M$ and radius $R$, continuity of $g_{tt}$ across it gives $k = 1 - r_s/R$ with $r_s = 2GM/c^2$: exactly the rate of a static clock just outside the shell.

Newton's version. Unit 05 proved by Gauss's theorem that a uniform spherical shell exerts no force inside. The relativistic statement is stronger — flatness, meaning no tidal force either, and no need for uniformity beyond spherical symmetry — and says nothing about shells that are not exactly round, where Newton's theorem also fails.

theorem

proof — one line

$$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\big(K_\mu\dot x^\mu\big) = \dot x^\nu\nabla_\nu\big(K_\mu\dot x^\mu\big) = \underbrace{(\nabla_\nu K_\mu)\,\dot x^\nu\dot x^\mu}_{\text{antisymmetric}\ \times\ \text{symmetric}\ =\ 0} + K_\mu\underbrace{\dot x^\nu\nabla_\nu\dot x^\mu}_{\text{geodesic}\ =\ 0} = 0 .\ \square$$

the ingredient from unit 35

$\mathcal L_Kg = 0$ is equivalent to Killing's equation $\nabla_\mu K_\nu + \nabla_\nu K_\mu = 0$: replace the partials by covariant derivatives (the $\Gamma$ terms cancel, $\Gamma$ being symmetric) and use $\nabla g = 0$.

checks

Full proof, with every step justified. Along the curve, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}$ of a scalar is $\dot x^\nu\partial_\nu$ of it, which equals $\dot x^\nu\nabla_\nu$ of it since the covariant derivative of a scalar is its partial derivative. The Leibniz rule for $\nabla$ (unit 32) splits $\nabla_\nu(K_\mu\dot x^\mu)$ into $(\nabla_\nu K_\mu)\dot x^\mu + K_\mu\nabla_\nu\dot x^\mu$; strictly $\dot x$ is only defined along the curve, but $\dot x^\nu\nabla_\nu\dot x^\mu$ is the covariant derivative along the curve, which is all that appears. The geodesic equation says it is zero. For the first term: by Killing's equation $\nabla_\nu K_\mu = -\nabla_\mu K_\nu$, so $T_{\nu\mu} := \nabla_\nu K_\mu$ is antisymmetric, and $T_{\nu\mu}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\mu = T_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = -T_{\nu\mu}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\mu$ (rename, then use antisymmetry), so it is zero. $\square$

Killing's equation from $\mathcal L_Kg = 0$, as unit 35 showed: in $K^\lambda\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} + g_{\lambda\nu}\partial_\mu K^\lambda + g_{\mu\lambda}\partial_\nu K^\lambda$, replace every $\partial$ by $\nabla$. Then $\nabla g = 0$ removes the first term and the remaining two are $\nabla_\mu K_\nu + \nabla_\nu K_\mu$.

Two singularities, one of them real

A chart to dismiss one, an invariant to convict the other. Neither tool can do the other's job.

36 · singularities

Where the components misbehave

In the metric, two places:

  • $r = r_s$: $g_{tt}\to0$ and $g_{rr}\to\infty$.
  • $r = 0$: $g_{tt}\to\infty$ and $g_{rr}\to0$.

Neither fact, by itself, says anything about the geometry. Components are a chart's description, and charts fail for their own reasons.

two failures seen before

The plane in polar coordinates, $\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\mathrm d\phi^2$: $\det g = r^2 \to 0$ at the origin (unit 05). Rindler's metric on flat spacetime: $g_{tt}\to0$ at the Rindler horizon (unit 30). Both are flat.

two kinds

A coordinate singularity is a place where a chart fails but the geometry extends smoothly: some other chart covers it.

A curvature singularity is an end of spacetime reached by a geodesic in finite affine parameter, along which some curvature scalar is unbounded.

the strategy, and its asymmetry

To dismiss a singularity, exhibit a chart in which the metric is smooth and non-degenerate there. To convict one, exhibit an invariant that diverges. A finite invariant proves nothing; a failed search for a chart proves nothing.

Why a finite invariant proves nothing. A spacetime can have every polynomial curvature scalar finite, even zero, and still be singular: exact plane gravitational waves have all such scalars zero everywhere, and there are geodesically incomplete flat spacetimes (Minkowski with a point removed, or a cone-shaped identification). So "$K$ is finite at $r_s$" is suggestive, not a proof. The proof that $r_s$ is harmless has to be the chart.

Why a divergent invariant does prove something. A curvature scalar is a function on spacetime whose value at a point does not depend on any chart. If the metric extended as a $C^2$ tensor to a manifold containing the endpoint of a curve along which the scalar is unbounded, then the scalar, built from $g$ and its first two derivatives, would be continuous at that endpoint and hence bounded near it — contradiction. So no extension, in any coordinates whatever, makes it regular.

The definition of a singularity is subtle, and the one on the slide is the working definition physicists use. The mathematically careful notion is geodesic incompleteness: a geodesic that cannot be extended to all values of its affine parameter, in a spacetime that cannot be extended. Unit 40 states the Penrose singularity theorem in those terms. For Schwarzschild the two notions agree at $r = 0$.

The polar example, precisely. In $\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\mathrm d\phi^2$ the metric is degenerate at $r = 0$, yet the plane is perfectly smooth there, as $x = r\cos\phi$, $y = r\sin\phi$ shows. The failure is that $\phi$ is not a coordinate at the origin. At $r = r_s$ the failure will turn out to be that $t$ is not a coordinate there.

Worked example 4 — ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates

Step 1 — follow light. Radial null curves have $\mathrm ds^2 = 0$, $\mathrm d\Omega = 0$: $c\,\mathrm dt = \pm\mathrm dr/f$. Define the tortoise coordinate by $\mathrm dr_*/\mathrm dr = 1/f$: $$r_* = r + r_s\ln\Big|\frac r{r_s} - 1\Big| .$$ Check: $\frac{\mathrm dr_*}{\mathrm dr} = 1 + \frac{r_s}{r - r_s} = \frac{r}{r - r_s} = \frac1f$ ✓. Step 2 — label by incoming light. $v := ct + r_*$. Then $c\,\mathrm dt = \mathrm dv - \mathrm dr/f$.

Step 3 — substitute. $$-f(\mathrm dv - \tfrac{\mathrm dr}f)^2 + \tfrac{\mathrm dr^2}f = -f\,\mathrm dv^2 + 2\,\mathrm dv\,\mathrm dr .$$ So, in ingoing Eddington–Finkelstein coordinates $(v, r, \theta, \varphi)$: $$\mathrm ds^2 = -\Big(1 - \frac{r_s}r\Big)\mathrm dv^2 + 2\,\mathrm dv\,\mathrm dr + r^2\,\mathrm d\Omega^2 .$$

Step 4 — inspect. Every component is smooth for $r \gt 0$. And $\det g = -r^4\sin^2\theta \ne 0$, including at $r = r_s$. The chart runs straight through; the region $0 \lt r \lt r_s$ is glued on smoothly.

checks

$r_s\to0$: $v = ct + r$ and $-\mathrm dv^2 + 2\mathrm dv\,\mathrm dr = -c^2\mathrm dt^2 + \mathrm dr^2$ ✓. $v$ is a length ✓.

Step 3 in full. $-fc^2\mathrm dt^2 = -f(\mathrm dv - \mathrm dr/f)^2 = -f\,\mathrm dv^2 + 2\,\mathrm dv\,\mathrm dr - \mathrm dr^2/f$. Adding $+\mathrm dr^2/f$ cancels the last term. The angular part is untouched.

Step 4 in full. The $(v, r)$ block is $\begin{pmatrix}-f & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, with determinant $-1$ for every $r$, including where $f = 0$. Times $r^2\cdot r^2\sin^2\theta$ from the angles. A metric with smooth components and nonzero determinant is a smooth Lorentzian metric; there is nothing else to check.

What exactly has been shown. The Schwarzschild chart covers $r \gt r_s$. The map $(t, r) \mapsto (v, r)$ is a diffeomorphism from that region onto $\{r \gt r_s\}$ in the new chart, and it is an isometry by construction. But the new metric also makes sense for $0 \lt r \le r_s$. So the exterior is isometric to part of a larger spacetime, in which $r = r_s$ is an ordinary null hypersurface. That is what "the singularity at $r_s$ is a coordinate artefact" means, stated as a theorem. The inside part, $0 \lt r \lt r_s$, is isometric to Schwarzschild's chart for $r \lt r_s$, by the same map with $r_* = r + r_s\ln(1 - r/r_s)$ there.

Where it came from. Eddington wrote this form in 1924 without drawing the conclusion; Finkelstein rediscovered it in 1958 and did. Lemaître had shown in 1933 that $r_s$ was not a real singularity, using freely falling coordinates. For a quarter of a century the result was available and not believed.

Why "tortoise". $r_*\to-\infty$ as $r\to r_s^+$: in terms of $r_*$ the horizon is infinitely far away, like the finish line in Zeno's race between Achilles and the tortoise.

The horizon is a one-way null surface

theorem

(i) $r = r_s$ is a null hypersurface.
(ii) For $r \lt r_s$, $r$ strictly decreases along every future-directed causal curve.

proof

(i) Normal $\mathrm dr$: in EF, $g^{rr} = f = 0$ there, so the normal is null.

(ii) Time-orient by $-\partial_r$, which is null ($g_{rr} = 0$) and nowhere zero. Future causal $X$ then has $X^v \ge 0$. From $g(X,X)\le0$: $$2X^vX^r \le fX^{v\,2} \lt 0\quad(f \lt 0,\ X^v \gt 0),$$ so $X^r \lt 0$; if $X^v = 0$, $X \propto -\partial_r$ and again $X^r \lt 0$. $\square$

Light cones in the $(r, v - r)$ plane, where ingoing light runs at $45^\circ$. Outgoing light tips over: outward outside $r_s$, vertical on it, inward inside. Every future inside points toward $r = 0$.

(i) in full. A hypersurface $\{\phi = \text{const}\}$ is null when its normal covector $\mathrm d\phi$ is null, $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial_\nu\phi = 0$. The inverse of the $(v,r)$ block $\begin{pmatrix}-f & 1\\1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ is $\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & f\end{pmatrix}$, so $g^{rr} = f$, which vanishes at $r_s$. A null hypersurface contains its own normal direction: the vector $g^{\mu\nu}\partial_\nu r = (1, f, 0, 0)$ is, at $r_s$, $\partial_v$, which is tangent to the surface. So the horizon is generated by light rays that neither fall in nor escape: outgoing light exactly at $r_s$ stays at $r_s$.

(ii), the time orientation. Outside, is $-\partial_r$ (at fixed $v$) future-directed in the usual sense? $\partial_t$ at fixed $r$ is $c\,\partial_v$, and $g(c\partial_v, -\partial_r) = -c\,g_{vr} = -c \lt 0$; two causal vectors with negative inner product lie in the same half of the cone. So yes: the orientation by $-\partial_r$ agrees with the one given by $\partial_t$ outside, and extends it through the horizon. For causal $X$ in the same half-cone, $g(X, -\partial_r) = -X^v \le 0$, i.e. $X^v \ge 0$.

(ii), the inequality. $g(X, X) = -fX^{v\,2} + 2X^vX^r + r^2|X_\Omega|^2 \le 0$, where $|X_\Omega|^2 \ge 0$ is the angular part. So $2X^vX^r \le fX^{v\,2} - r^2|X_\Omega|^2 \le fX^{v\,2}$, and inside, $f \lt 0$. If $X^v \gt 0$ then $X^r \le fX^v/2 \lt 0$. If $X^v = 0$ then $g(X,X) = r^2|X_\Omega|^2 \le 0$ forces $X_\Omega = 0$, so $X = X^r\partial_r$, and future-directedness makes it a positive multiple of $-\partial_r$. In both cases $X^r \lt 0$.

The figure. In EF coordinates, with $\tilde v := v - r$ as vertical axis, ingoing light ($v$ const) has $\mathrm d\tilde v/\mathrm dr = -1$, and outgoing light ($\mathrm dr/\mathrm dv = f/2$) has direction $(\mathrm dr, \mathrm d\tilde v) \propto (1 - r_s/r,\ 1 + r_s/r)$. That vector points right for $r \gt r_s$, straight up at $r_s$, and left for $r \lt r_s$ — the figure is drawn from exactly this formula.

So what "black" means. Nothing inside can reach $r \gt r_s$: a causal curve starting at $r \lt r_s$ has $r$ decreasing for ever. This is a theorem about the future light cones, not about forces; no rocket helps, because a rocket's worldline is a causal curve.

A worldline of fixed $r, \theta, \varphi$ — tangent to $\partial_t$, so it exists only where $\partial_t$ is timelike: $r \gt r_s$.

for the hole

Along the same fall, $\dot t = E/(fc^2) = 1/f$, so $$\frac{\mathrm dt}{\mathrm dr} = -\frac{1}{c\,f\sqrt{r_s/r}} \approx -\frac{r_s}{c}\,\frac{1}{r - r_s}\quad(r\to r_s),$$ and $t \approx -\frac{r_s}{c}\ln\!\big(\frac r{r_s} - 1\big) \to +\infty$.

the reconciliation

$t$ is the reading of static observers' synchronised clocks. The crossing event lies on $r = r_s$, where the Schwarzschild chart does not reach: it has no $t$-value. "$t\to\infty$" says the chart runs out, not that the body stops.

In EF the crossing has a finite $v$. What the distant observer sees is decided by the outgoing light rays, and the last ray to escape leaves just before the crossing.

the frozen-star picture

"The distant observer sees the body hover at the horizon for ever" is wrong twice: she sees nothing after a few e-foldings, and what she would see is a statement about light, not about the body.

The logarithm. Near $r_s$, $f = (r - r_s)/r \approx (r - r_s)/r_s$ and $\sqrt{r_s/r}\approx1$, so $\mathrm dt/\mathrm dr \approx -\frac{r_s}{c(r - r_s)}$, which integrates to $t = -\frac{r_s}c\ln(r - r_s) +$ const. Every factor of $e$ closer to the horizon costs another $r_s/c = 98.5\ \mu$s of $t$.

The redshift, in full. An outgoing radial ray has $u := ct - r_* =$ const and reaches a distant observer at $r_{\text{obs}}$ at time $ct_{\text{obs}} = u + r_{*\text{obs}}$. So the rate at which the observer receives the infaller's signals is governed by $\mathrm du/\mathrm d\tau$ along the infaller. Near the horizon the infaller has nearly constant $v = v_H$, and $u = v - 2r_*$, with $r_* \approx r_s + r_s\ln(r/r_s - 1)$. So $r - r_s \approx r_s\exp\big((v_H - 2r_s - u)/2r_s\big)$, i.e. $r - r_s \propto e^{-u/2r_s}$. Near the horizon $\dot r \approx -c$, so $\mathrm d\tau \approx -\mathrm dr/c$, and $\frac{\mathrm d\tau}{\mathrm du} = \frac{r - r_s}{2r_sc}\propto e^{-u/2r_s}$. A signal emitted at fixed proper frequency therefore arrives with period stretched by $\mathrm du/\mathrm d\tau \propto e^{u/2r_s} \propto e^{ct_{\text{obs}}/2r_s}$. That is $1 + z$. The quantity $c^2/(2r_s) = c^4/4GM$ is the surface gravity of the horizon, unit 40's $\kappa$; the e-folding time is $c/\kappa$.

The number. $2r_s/c = 2\times29\,532.5/c = 1.970\times10^{-4}$ s. A millisecond is five e-foldings, so the redshift factor grows by about $e^5 \approx 150$ in that time, and the received power, which also suffers from the photons arriving less often, by roughly the square of that.

No hand-waving about "infinite time dilation". The two clocks are never compared at one event, so there is no paradox to resolve by appeal to relativity of simultaneity. What is being compared is an event (the crossing) with a coordinate function ($t$) that is undefined there. Asked properly — at what $t_{\text{obs}}$ does the distant observer receive the signal the body emits at proper time $\tau$? — the answer is a finite, increasing function of $\tau$ for every $\tau$ before the crossing, tending to infinity as $\tau$ approaches the crossing. Both statements on the slide follow.

Conformal compactification, on Minkowski first

conformal compactification

An embedding of $(M, g)$ into a manifold with boundary $(\tilde M, \tilde g)$, with $\tilde g = \omega^2g$ on $M$, the factor $\omega \gt 0$ on $M$ and $\omega = 0$ on the boundary. Infinity becomes a boundary at finite distance. (Most texts call the factor $\Omega$; here that letter is taken by the sphere's $\mathrm d\Omega^2$.)

lemma · cones survive

$\tilde g$ and $g$ have the same null, timelike and spacelike vectors. Proof: $\tilde g(X,X) = \omega^2g(X,X)$ and $\omega^2 \gt 0$. $\square$

Radial Minkowski: $\mathrm ds^2 = -\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv + r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2$, $u = ct - r$, $v = ct + r$ (unit 26). Squash each null coordinate into a finite interval, with $\ell$ any length: $$p = \arctan\frac v\ell,\quad q = \arctan\frac u\ell,\quad T = p + q,\ R = p - q.$$ Then

$$\begin{aligned}\mathrm ds^2 = \frac{\ell^2}{4\cos^2\!p\,\cos^2\!q}\Big(&-\mathrm dT^2 + \mathrm dR^2\\ &+ \sin^2\!R\,\mathrm d\Omega^2\Big)\end{aligned}$$

on $0 \le R \lt \pi$, $|T| + R \lt \pi$. The bracket is smooth up to the boundary; $\omega = 2\cos p\cos q/\ell$.

Full derivation. $\mathrm dv = \ell\sec^2p\,\mathrm dp$, $\mathrm du = \ell\sec^2q\,\mathrm dq$, so $-\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv = -\frac{\ell^2\,\mathrm dp\,\mathrm dq}{\cos^2p\cos^2q}$, and $-4\,\mathrm dp\, \mathrm dq = -(\mathrm dT^2 - \mathrm dR^2)$ since $\mathrm dT^2 - \mathrm dR^2 = (\mathrm dp + \mathrm dq)^2 - (\mathrm dp - \mathrm dq)^2 = 4\,\mathrm dp\,\mathrm dq$. For the angular part, $r = (v - u)/2 = \tfrac\ell2(\tan p - \tan q) = \frac{\ell\sin(p - q)}{2\cos p\cos q}$, so $r^2 = \frac{\ell^2\sin^2R}{4\cos^2p \cos^2q}$. Together this is the slide. The ranges: $u, v\in\mathbb R$ with $v \ge u$ ($r \ge 0$) become $p, q\in(-\pi/2, \pi/2)$ with $p \ge q$, i.e. $R \ge 0$ and $|T| + R \lt \pi$.

What the bracket is. $-\mathrm dT^2 + \mathrm dR^2 + \sin^2R\,\mathrm d\Omega^2$ is the Einstein static universe, $\mathbb R\times S^3$ with the round unit 3-sphere. So all of Minkowski space is conformal to a bounded, lens-shaped piece of it. Penrose introduced the construction in 1963–64; it is the standard way to talk about "infinity" precisely.

Why a conformal factor is the right thing to throw away. It changes lengths and times but, by the lemma, not which curves are causal. Causal structure — who can signal whom — is exactly what one wants to see at a glance, and it survives. Lengths, which do not survive, are what the original metric is still there for.

$\ell$ is arbitrary: changing it changes the picture's interior by a diffeomorphism but not its boundary structure. The arctangent is chosen because it maps $\mathbb R$ onto a bounded interval monotonically; any such function would do.

All of Minkowski space, radial directions only. Every timelike geodesic runs from $i^-$ to $i^+$.

Locating the boundary. $i^+$ is $p = q = \pi/2$, i.e. $T = \pi$, $R = 0$: reached as $u, v\to+\infty$ together, which is what a timelike geodesic does as $t\to\infty$. $i^0$ is $p = \pi/2$, $q = -\pi/2$: $T = 0$, $R = \pi$, reached as $r\to\infty$ at fixed $t$. $\mathscr I^+$ is $p = \pi/2$ with $q$ finite: $v\to\infty$ at fixed $u$, which is where an outgoing light ray goes. The light ray. Ingoing light has $v$ constant, so $p$ constant, which is a line of slope $-1$ in the $(R, T)$ plane; it reaches $R = 0$ ($r = 0$), where it becomes outgoing, $u$ constant, slope $+1$. The drawn ray has $p = -0.37$ coming in and $q = -0.37$ going out: at the centre $u = v$.

Why $\mathscr I$ is pronounced "scri". Script I; the pronunciation is Penrose's. The notion matters because gravitational radiation is defined there: the energy a system radiates is the energy that crosses $\mathscr I^+$.

How to read any Penrose diagram. Light moves at $45^\circ$, so the causal future of a point is the wedge above it bounded by $45^\circ$ lines. Each interior point is a whole round sphere; the left edge, where the spheres have shrunk to points, is the centre $r = 0$.

Worked example 5 — Kruskal–Szekeres, step by step

regularise both horizons at once

Step 1 — tortoise (worked example 4): $r_* = r + r_s\ln(r/r_s - 1)$ on $r \gt r_s$.

Step 2 — both null coordinates. $u = ct - r_*$, $v = ct + r_*$. Since $c\,\mathrm dt = \tfrac12(\mathrm du + \mathrm dv)$ and $\mathrm dr_* = \tfrac12(\mathrm dv - \mathrm du) = \mathrm dr/f$: $$\mathrm ds^2 = -f\,\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv + r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2 .$$ Still singular: $f\to0$ as $u\to\infty$ or $v\to-\infty$.

Step 3 — exponentiate. $U = -e^{-u/2r_s}$, $V = e^{v/2r_s}$. Then $\mathrm dU\,\mathrm dV = -\frac{UV}{4r_s^2}\,\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv$ and $$UV = -e^{r_*/r_s} = \Big(1 - \frac r{r_s}\Big)e^{r/r_s}.$$

Step 4 — substitute. $f\,\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv = \frac{r - r_s}{r}\cdot\frac{4r_s^2\,\mathrm dU\,\mathrm dV}{(r/r_s - 1)e^{r/r_s}}$, so

$$\mathrm ds^2 = -\frac{4r_s^3}{r}e^{-r/r_s}\,\mathrm dU\,\mathrm dV + r^2\,\mathrm d\Omega^2 .$$

The factor $r - r_s$ has cancelled. These are Kruskal–Szekeres coordinates.

Step 5 — extend. $x\mapsto(1 - x)e^x$ has derivative $-xe^x \lt 0$ on $x \gt 0$: a bijection $(0,\infty)\to(-\infty, 1)$. So $r$ is a smooth positive function of $UV$ on all of $\{UV \lt 1\}$, and the metric is smooth and non-degenerate there. That is the maximal extension.

checks

$U, V$ dimensionless, $[r_s^3/r] = $ length$^2$ ✓. $U = 0$ or $V = 0$ $\Leftrightarrow$ $r = r_s$; $UV\to1$ $\Leftrightarrow$ $r\to0$ ✓.

Step 3 in full. $\mathrm dU = \frac1{2r_s}e^{-u/2r_s}\mathrm du = -\frac{U}{2r_s}\mathrm du$ and $\mathrm dV = \frac{V}{2r_s}\mathrm dv$, so $\mathrm dU\,\mathrm dV = -\frac{UV}{4r_s^2}\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv$. And $UV = -e^{(v - u)/2r_s} = -e^{r_*/r_s} = -e^{r/r_s}(r/r_s - 1)$, using $e^{\ln(r/r_s - 1)} = r/r_s - 1$.

Step 4 in full. From step 3, $\mathrm du\,\mathrm dv = -\frac{4r_s^2}{UV}\mathrm dU\,\mathrm dV = \frac{4r_s^2}{(r/r_s - 1)e^{r/r_s}}\mathrm dU\,\mathrm dV$. Multiply by $f = (r - r_s)/r = \frac{r_s}{r}(r/r_s - 1)$: the factor $(r/r_s - 1)$ cancels and leaves $\frac{4r_s^3}{r}e^{-r/r_s}\,\mathrm dU\,\mathrm dV$.

Step 5 in full. $h(x) = (1 - x)e^x$ is smooth with $h'(x) = -xe^x$, negative for $x \gt 0$, and $h(0^+) = 1$, $h(x)\to-\infty$ as $x\to\infty$. So $h$ is a smooth bijection of $(0,\infty)$ onto $(-\infty,1)$ with nonvanishing derivative, and by the inverse function theorem (unit 04) its inverse is smooth. Then $r = r_sh^{-1}(UV)$ is smooth on $\{(U,V): UV \lt 1\}$, positive, and the coefficient $\frac{4r_s^3}{r}e^{-r/r_s}$ is smooth and positive. The $(U,V)$ block has zero diagonal and off-diagonal entries $-\frac{2r_s^3}{r}e^{-r/r_s} \ne 0$, so nonzero determinant. $\square$ The original exterior is the quadrant $U \lt 0 \lt V$; the rest is new.

Why "maximal". Every geodesic in the Kruskal spacetime either runs to infinite affine parameter or ends at $UV = 1$, where $K\to\infty$. So no further extension is possible. Cited: the geodesic completeness analysis is in Hawking and Ellis, §5.5.

The time-and-space version. $T = (V + U)/2$, $X = (V - U)/2$ give $-\mathrm dU\,\mathrm dV = -\mathrm dT^2 + \mathrm dX^2$: light cones at $45^\circ$ in the $(X, T)$ plane, which is what the next slide draws. Kruskal and Szekeres found the chart independently in 1960.

Four regions, and which of them are real

  • I ($U \lt 0 \lt V$): the exterior, our universe, $r \gt r_s$.
  • II ($U, V \gt 0$): the black hole region. Every future causal curve reaches $r = 0$, within proper time $\pi r_s/2c$ at most.
  • III ($V \lt 0 \lt U$): a second exterior, isometric to I, asymptotically flat.
  • IV ($U, V \lt 0$): the white hole region, II run backwards: everything in it must leave.
I and III never meet

No causal curve joins them: from I, $V \gt 0$ and $V$ never decreases along a future curve; III has $V \lt 0$. The slice $T = 0$ joins them through a throat of radius $r_s$ — $29.53$ km for the hole — the Einstein–Rosen bridge, which no observer can cross.

an astrophysical hole has only I and II

A hole formed by collapse is vacuum only outside the star. Inside the star's surface, Birkhoff does not apply and the metric is something else. The surface, a timelike curve from region I into region II, cuts the diagram: everything to its left — III, IV and part of I and II — is replaced by the star's interior.

what survives the collapse

The exterior, the horizon, the black-hole region and its singularity. That is the part the checkpoint is about, and the part unit 37 observes.

"$V$ never decreases". In Kruskal coordinates future-directed causal vectors have $\dot U \ge 0$ and $\dot V \ge 0$: the metric is $-F\,\mathrm dU\,\mathrm dV$ in that block with $F \gt 0$, so a causal vector has $\dot U\dot V \ge 0$, and the future orientation — agreeing with $\partial_t$ in region I, where $\partial_t \propto V\partial_V - U\partial_U$ has both components positive — selects $\dot U, \dot V \ge 0$. So a causal curve starting in I, with $V \gt 0$, keeps $V \gt 0$ and can never reach III, where $V \lt 0$. Symmetrically $U$ never decreases, so nothing in I or II reaches IV, and nothing in II reaches I (II has $U \gt 0$, I has $U \lt 0$).

The throat. On $T = 0$, $U = -V$ and $UV = -X^2 \le 0$, so $r \ge r_s$ with equality at $X = 0$. The slice is two copies of the exterior $\{r \gt r_s\}$ glued along the sphere $r = r_s$: a wormhole. Einstein and Rosen described it in 1935. It pinches off: the slices $T = $ const $\gt 0$ have throat radius shrinking to $0$ at $T = 1$, faster than light could cross. Fuller and Wheeler made the non-traversability explicit in 1962; the argument on the slide is the short form of it.

Region II's time limit. Inside, $r$ is a time coordinate, and the maximum proper time from horizon to singularity along any timelike curve is $\pi r_s/2c$ — for the hole, $154.7\ \mu$s. That is problem P13, with a proof that fits in four lines once one notices $c\,\mathrm d\tau \le |\mathrm dr|/\sqrt{r_s/r - 1}$.

Why the white hole is not believed in. A white hole's horizon is the past boundary of region IV; it would have to exist from the beginning, and it is unstable to small amounts of infalling matter. Nothing in the collapse picture produces one.

event horizon, precisely

The boundary of the causal past of region I's $\mathscr I^+$: the line $U = 0$.

The maximally extended Schwarzschild spacetime. Each exterior has its own $i^0$ and $\mathscr I^\pm$; the singularities are spacelike lines.

The singularity as a line. $\tan\tilde U\tan\tilde V = 1$ means $\cos\tilde U\cos\tilde V - \sin\tilde U \sin\tilde V = 0$, i.e. $\cos(\tilde U + \tilde V) = 0$. With $\tilde U, \tilde V \in(-\pi/2,\pi/2)$ and both of the same sign, $\tilde U + \tilde V = \pm\pi/2$. The event horizon from the definition.
