The classical tests: three numbers, and one of them is 43

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. From scratch, with no formula looked up, you compute Mercury's perihelion advance to within \(1''\) per century of \(43\) and the solar light deflection as \(1.75''\), show the latter is exactly twice the Newtonian value and name the metric component responsible — and you can say why unit 21 forbade you from removing the secular term.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Eleven problems, about seven hours. P2 is the checkpoint's number and P5 its factor of two; P6 is its clause about the secular term. Signature \((-,+,+,+)\), \(c\) explicit, a dot is \(\mathrm d/\mathrm d\tau\) (or an affine parameter for light), a prime is \(\mathrm d/\mathrm d\varphi\). \(E = -g(\partial_t, \dot x)\) and \(L = g(\partial_\varphi, \dot x)\) are unit 36's two Killing constants: per unit rest mass for a massive particle, and for light merely proportional to the affine parameter's scale, so that only \(b = cL/E\) is physical. \(u = 1/r\), and \(r_g = GM/c^2\) is the gravitational radius — half the \(r_s = 2GM/c^2\) unit 36 wrote its formulas with. The Schwarzschild metric is

\[ \mathrm ds^2 = -\Big(1 - \frac{2r_g}{r}\Big)c^2\mathrm dt^2 + \Big(1 - \frac{2r_g}{r}\Big)^{-1}\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\big(\mathrm d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\varphi^2\big). \]

Constants: \(GM_\odot = 1.32712440018\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\), \(c = 299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), so \(r_{g\odot} = 1476.625\) m and \(GM_\odot/c^3 = 4.92549\ \mu\)s; \(R_\odot = 6.957\times10^{8}\) m; 1 au \(= 1.495978707\times10^{11}\) m; 1 pc \(= 3.0857\times10^{16}\) m; 1 rad \(= 206\,264.806''\); a Julian century is \(36\,525\) days.

Geodesics and the orbit equation

P1 The orbit equation from the geodesic Lagrangian routine

exercises: the geodesic-Lagrangian trick, cyclic coordinates as Killing vectors, the Binet substitution, a Newtonian limit

Take the geodesic Lagrangian \(\mathcal L = g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu\) of the Schwarzschild metric, restricted to the equatorial plane \(\theta = \pi/2\).

  1. Write \(\mathcal L\) out. Name its cyclic coordinates, and from the corresponding Euler–Lagrange equations obtain the conserved quantities \(E = (1 - 2r_g/r)c^2\dot t\) and \(L = r^2\dot\varphi\). Say which unit-13 theorem this is and which Killing vectors (unit 36) are behind it.
  2. Using the normalisation \(g(\dot x, \dot x) = -c^2\), show that \(\dot r^2 = E^2/c^2 - (1 - 2r_g/r)(c^2 + L^2/r^2)\).
  3. Substitute \(u = 1/r\) with \(\varphi\) as parameter and derive \(u'' + u = GM/L^2 + 3r_gu^2\). Where did you divide by something that can vanish, and why is the result still valid there?
  4. For a slow particle far away, show \(E = c^2 + \tfrac12 v^2 - GM/r + \dots\), using \(\mathrm dt/\mathrm d\tau \approx 1 + v^2/2c^2 + r_g/r\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two constants, one first-order ODE for \(r(\tau)\), one second-order ODE for \(u(\varphi)\), and an expansion. Each step is a substitution; nothing needs solving.

Rung 2

A coordinate that does not appear in \(\mathcal L\) has a conserved conjugate momentum \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot q\). For part 3, the chain rule gives \(\dot r\) in terms of \(u'\) and \(\dot\varphi\), and \(\dot\varphi = Lu^2\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: \(\dot r = \dfrac{\mathrm dr}{\mathrm d\varphi}\dot\varphi = -\dfrac{u'}{u^2}\,Lu^2 = -Lu'\), so the radial equation becomes \(L^2u'^2 = E^2/c^2 - (1 - 2r_gu)(c^2 + L^2u^2)\). Now differentiate in \(\varphi\).

Worked solution

Idea. Symmetries give two first integrals, the normalisation gives a third, and the Binet substitution trades time for angle. This is unit 08's reduction with a metric in place of a force.

1. With \(f = 1 - 2r_g/r\), \[ \mathcal L = -f c^2\dot t^2 + f^{-1}\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2 . \] \(t\) and \(\varphi\) do not appear, so \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\tau}(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot t) = 0\) and likewise for \(\varphi\): \(-2fc^2\dot t\) and \(2r^2\dot\varphi\) are constant. Halving and fixing signs so that \(E \gt 0\) gives \(E = fc^2\dot t\), \(L = r^2\dot\varphi\). This is Noether's theorem for a cyclic coordinate (unit 13); the coordinate-free statement is that \(\partial_t\) and \(\partial_\varphi\) are Killing vectors, and \(E = -g(\partial_t, \dot x)\), \(L = g(\partial_\varphi, \dot x)\).

2. The normalisation is \(-fc^2\dot t^2 + f^{-1}\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2 = -c^2\). Substitute \(\dot t = E/(fc^2)\), \(\dot\varphi = L/r^2\): \(-E^2/(fc^2) + \dot r^2/f + L^2/r^2 = -c^2\). Multiply by \(f\): \(\dot r^2 = E^2/c^2 - f\,(c^2 + L^2/r^2)\).

3. With \(\dot r = -Lu'\), expand the product: \[ L^2u'^2 = \frac{E^2}{c^2} - c^2 - L^2u^2 + 2r_gc^2u + 2r_gL^2u^3 . \] Differentiate in \(\varphi\): \(2L^2u'u'' = -2L^2uu' + 2r_gc^2u' + 6r_gL^2u^2u'\). Divide by \(2L^2u'\) and use \(r_gc^2 = GM\): \[ u'' + u = \frac{GM}{L^2} + 3r_gu^2 . \] The division fails where \(u' = 0\), at the turning points. But both sides of the final equation are continuous in \(\varphi\), and they agree wherever \(u' \ne 0\); on a non-circular orbit the turning points are isolated, so the two sides agree on a dense set and hence everywhere. Circular orbits (\(u' \equiv 0\)) are exactly the constant solutions of the second-order equation, which is why it, not the first-order one, is the equation to keep.

4. \(E = (1 - 2r_g/r)c^2(1 + v^2/2c^2 + r_g/r) = c^2 + \tfrac12v^2 + c^2r_g/r - 2c^2r_g/r + O(c^{-2}) = c^2 + \tfrac12v^2 - GM/r + O(c^{-2})\).

Check. Dimensions: \([GM/L^2] = \mathsf{L^3T^{-2}}/\mathsf{L^4T^{-2}} = \mathsf{L^{-1}}\) and \([r_gu^2] = \mathsf{L^{-1}}\), like \(u\) ✓. Limit \(r_g \to 0\) (that is, \(c \to \infty\) at fixed \(GM\)): unit 08's \(u'' + u = GM/L^2\) ✓, whose solution is the conic \(u = (1 + e\cos\varphi)GM/L^2\).

P2 The 43, from scratch routine

exercises: the perihelion formula, careful arithmetic, a comparison with data

Mercury has \(a = 5.7909\times10^{10}\) m, \(e = 0.20563\) and sidereal period \(87.9691\) days.

  1. Starting from \(\Delta\varpi = 6\pi GM/(c^2a(1 - e^2))\) per orbit, compute Mercury's perihelion advance in arcseconds per orbit and per Julian century. Compare with the observed anomalous advance \(42.98 \pm 0.04''\) per century.
  2. A common slip is to use \(a\) where \(p = a(1 - e^2)\) belongs. What does that give, and would the checkpoint's "within \(1''\)" catch it?
  3. Repeat for Venus (\(a = 0.723332\) au, \(e = 0.006773\), \(224.701\) d) and the Earth (\(a = 1.000001\) au, \(e = 0.016709\), \(365.256\) d). Show that for near-circular orbits the advance per century scales as \(a^{-5/2}\), and check your three numbers roughly obey it.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Three numbers per planet: a dimensionless angle per orbit, a count of orbits per century, their product. Keep five significant figures until the end.

Rung 2

Compute \(r_g = GM_\odot/c^2\) first; then the per-orbit angle is \(2\pi\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon = 3r_g/p\). For the scaling, the number of orbits per century goes as \(1/T \propto a^{-3/2}\) by Kepler's third law.

Rung 3

First line: \(r_g = 1.32712\times10^{20}/(2.99792\times10^8)^2 = 1476.6\) m, and \(1 - e^2 = 1 - 0.042284 = 0.95772\).

Worked solution

Idea. Everything is fixed: one constant in the field equations, one in the solution, and the orbit's measured elements. There is nothing to adjust.

1. \(p = 5.7909\times10^{10}\times0.95772 = 5.5460\times10^{10}\) m. \(\varepsilon = 3r_g/p = 4429.9/5.5460\times10^{10} = 7.9875\times10^{-8}\). Per orbit \(2\pi\varepsilon = 5.0187\times10^{-7}\) rad \(= 0.10352''\). Orbits per century \(36\,525/87.9691 = 415.20\). So \[ \Delta\varpi = 415.20\times0.10352'' = 42.98''\ \text{per century}, \] in agreement with \(42.98 \pm 0.04''\) — well within the error bar.

2. With \(a\) in place of \(p\), everything is multiplied by \(1 - e^2 = 0.95772\): \(41.16''\). That is \(1.8''\) low, so the checkpoint's \(1''\) tolerance catches it — which is why the tolerance is \(1''\) and not \(2''\).

3.

  • Venus: \(p = 1.08204\times10^{11}\) m, \(\varepsilon = 4.094\times10^{-8}\), \(0.05306''\) per orbit, \(162.55\) orbits per century, \(8.62''\) per century.
  • Earth: \(p = 1.49556\times10^{11}\) m, \(\varepsilon = 2.962\times10^{-8}\), \(0.03839''\) per orbit, \(100.00\) orbits, \(3.84''\) per century.

Scaling: per orbit \(\propto 1/a\), orbits per century \(\propto a^{-3/2}\), product \(\propto a^{-5/2}\). From Mercury: \(42.98\times(0.387098/0.723332)^{5/2} = 9.0''\) for Venus and \(42.98\times0.387098^{5/2} = 4.0''\) for the Earth. The actual \(8.62\) and \(3.84\) are 4% lower, because Mercury's \(1/(1 - e^2) = 1.044\) boosts it and the others' barely.

Check. \(\varepsilon\) is dimensionless ✓; \(c \to \infty\) gives zero ✓; the rounded inputs \(a = 5.79\times10^{10}\), \(e = 0.2056\) give \(42.99''\), so the answer is not fragile ✓.

P3 The ISCO, and what an accretion disc can radiate medium

exercises: circular orbits from the effective potential, stability, a binding energy, comparison with observed efficiencies

Use \(\tfrac12\dot r^2 + V_{\rm eff}(r) = \tfrac12(E^2/c^2 - c^2)\) with \(V_{\rm eff} = -GM/r + L^2/2r^2 - GML^2/c^2r^3\), and write \(x = r_g/r\).

  1. Show that a circular orbit at radius \(r\) has \[ \frac{L^2}{c^2} = \frac{r_g\,r}{1 - 3x},\qquad \frac{E}{c^2} = \frac{1 - 2x}{\sqrt{1 - 3x}} . \] Where do these stop making sense, and what is there?
  2. Show that circular orbits are stable exactly when \(r \gt 6r_g\), so the innermost stable circular orbit is at \(r = 6r_g\).
  3. Gas in a thin accretion disc spirals slowly inwards through near-circular orbits, radiating as it goes, and plunges without further radiation once it passes the ISCO. Show that the fraction of rest energy radiated is \(1 - \sqrt{8/9}\), and evaluate it.
  4. Compare with hydrogen fusion (four protons of \(1.007825\) u each to one helium-4 atom of \(4.002603\) u), and with the mean radiative efficiency of about \(0.1\) inferred for accreting black holes by comparing the quasar light in the universe with the mass now in black holes (Soltan's argument; Yu and Tremaine 2002). What does the comparison suggest?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Circular means \(\dot r = 0\) and \(V_{\rm eff}' = 0\) at once; stable means \(V_{\rm eff}'' \gt 0\) there. The efficiency is \((c^2 - E)/c^2\), because the gas started at rest far away with \(E = c^2\).

Rung 2

\(V_{\rm eff}' = 0\) is linear in \(L^2\), so solve it for \(L^2\). Then \(\dot r = 0\) in the exact radial equation \(\dot r^2 = E^2/c^2 - (1 - 2x)(c^2 + L^2/r^2)\) gives \(E^2\). For stability, it is easiest to ask where \(L^2(r)\) has its minimum: that is where the two roots of \(V_{\rm eff}' = 0\) merge.

Rung 3

First line: \(r^4V_{\rm eff}'/c^2 = r_gr^2 - \ell^2r + 3r_g\ell^2\) with \(\ell = L/c\), and setting it to zero gives \(\ell^2(r - 3r_g) = r_gr^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. A circular orbit is an equilibrium of the one-dimensional problem; stability is the sign of the second derivative; and the energy a disc can release is the depth of the last stable well.

1. \(V_{\rm eff}'(r) = (c^2/r^4)(r_gr^2 - \ell^2r + 3r_g\ell^2)\). Setting it to zero, \(\ell^2 = r_gr^2/(r - 3r_g) = r_gr/(1 - 3x)\). Then \(\dot r = 0\) gives \[ \frac{E^2}{c^4} = (1 - 2x)\Big(1 + \frac{\ell^2}{r^2}\Big) = (1 - 2x)\Big(1 + \frac{x}{1 - 3x}\Big) = \frac{(1 - 2x)^2}{1 - 3x}. \] Both blow up as \(r \to 3r_g\): circular orbits need infinite \(L\) and \(E\) there. That limit is the photon sphere, where only light can orbit: a photon's own \(E\) and \(L\) are finite, but they are per unit affine parameter rather than per unit rest mass, and the finite quantity that survives the limit is the ratio \(b = cL/E \to 3\sqrt3\,r_g\). Between \(3r_g\) and \(2r_g\) there are no circular orbits at all.

2. \(\ell^2(r) = r_gr^2/(r - 3r_g)\) has derivative \(r_g r(r - 6r_g)/(r - 3r_g)^2\): it decreases on \((3r_g, 6r_g)\) and increases for \(r \gt 6r_g\), with minimum \(\ell^2 = 12r_g^2\) at \(6r_g\). For a given \(\ell^2 \gt 12r_g^2\) the bracket \(q(r) = r_gr^2 - \ell^2r + 3r_g\ell^2\) has two roots, one on each side of \(6r_g\). \(q\) is an upward parabola, so \(V_{\rm eff}' = c^2q/r^4\) is positive outside the roots and negative between: the outer root is a minimum of \(V_{\rm eff}\) (stable) and the inner one a maximum (unstable). So circular orbits at \(r \gt 6r_g\) are stable and those in \((3r_g, 6r_g)\) are not; at \(6r_g\) the roots merge into an inflection, marginally stable.

3. At \(x = 1/6\): \(E/c^2 = (2/3)/\sqrt{1/2} = \sqrt{8/9} = 0.942809\). Gas that started at rest far away had \(E = c^2\). The disc radiated the difference: \[ \eta = 1 - \sqrt{8/9} = 0.0572 = 5.72\%. \]

4. Fusion: \(4\times1.007825 - 4.002603 = 0.028697\) u out of \(4.031300\), that is \(0.71\%\). Accretion onto a non-rotating hole is eight times better than burning hydrogen completely — which is why quasars, not galaxies of stars, are the most luminous steady objects. But Soltan's average of about \(0.1\) is nearly twice \(5.7\%\). The natural reading is that typical accreting holes rotate: for a rotating (Kerr) hole with prograde orbits the ISCO moves inwards and the efficiency rises, to \(42\%\) in the extreme case. That is unit 40's business, cited here, not derived.

Check. \(L^2/c^2\) has dimensions \(\mathsf{L\cdot L} = \mathsf{L^2}\) ✓. Large \(r\) (\(x \to 0\)): \(\ell^2 \to r_gr\), i.e. \(L^2 = GMr\), Newton's circular orbit ✓; and \(E/c^2 \approx 1 - 2x + \tfrac32x = 1 - \tfrac12x\), i.e. \(E - c^2 \approx -GM/2r\), Newton's binding energy per unit mass ✓.

Light and clocks

P4 Shapiro from Cassini medium

exercises: integrating a null line element, the Shapiro formula, a measurement with an error bar

Use the weak-field metric \(\mathrm ds^2 = -(1 - 2r_g/r)c^2\mathrm dt^2 + (1 + 2\gamma r_g/r)|\mathrm d\vec x|^2\), with \(\gamma = 1\) in general relativity.

  1. Show that a radar signal from a point at distance \(r_1\) from the Sun to one at \(r_2\) and back, passing the Sun at closest distance \(b \ll r_1, r_2\), is delayed relative to flat space by \[ \Delta t = (1 + \gamma)\frac{2GM}{c^3}\ln\frac{4r_1r_2}{b^2}. \] Say why integrating along the undeflected straight line is legitimate.
  2. In June 2002 the Earth was \(1.0162\) au from the Sun, the Cassini spacecraft \(7.4216\) au on the far side, and the beam passed at \(b = 1.6R_\odot\). Evaluate \(\Delta t\) for \(\gamma = 1\), and the part of it due to \(g_{ij}\).
  3. Bertotti, Iess and Tortora (2003) found \(\gamma - 1 = (2.1 \pm 2.3)\times10^{-5}\). Convert this into a statement about the delay at closest approach, in nanoseconds. Is general relativity consistent with it?
  4. What was actually measured was the fractional frequency shift \(y = \mathrm d\Delta t/\mathrm dt\). Model the line of sight as sweeping past the Sun with \(b(t) = \sqrt{b_0^2 + v^2t^2}\), where \(v\) is the Earth's orbital speed \(29.78\) km/s scaled by \(r_2/(r_1 + r_2)\). Find the largest \(|y|\), and evaluate it.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a one-dimensional integral along a line; part 2 a number of order hundreds of microseconds; part 3 a scaling; part 4 a maximum of a function of \(t\).

Rung 2

Set \(\mathrm ds = 0\) and solve for \(c\,\mathrm dt\) to first order in \(r_g\). Put the line at \(y = b\), parametrised by \(x \in [-x_1, x_2]\), so \(r = \sqrt{x^2 + b^2}\). For part 3, note which factor in the formula carries \(\gamma\).

Rung 3

First line: \(c\,\mathrm dt = \big(1 + (1 + \gamma)r_g/r\big)\,\mathrm dx\), so the one-way excess is \((1 + \gamma)\dfrac{r_g}{c}\displaystyle\int_{-x_1}^{x_2}\frac{\mathrm dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + b^2}}\), and the integrand's antiderivative is \(\operatorname{arsinh}(x/b) = \ln\big((x + \sqrt{x^2 + b^2})/b\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Light moves at \(c\) locally, but in these coordinates both the clocks (\(g_{00}\)) and the rulers (\(g_{ij}\)) are off by order \(r_g/r\), so the coordinate speed is less than \(c\) near the Sun. The delay is the integral of the excess.

1. \(\mathrm ds = 0\) gives \(c^2\mathrm dt^2 = \dfrac{1 + 2\gamma r_g/r}{1 - 2r_g/r}|\mathrm d\vec x|^2\), and the square root is \(1 + (1 + \gamma)r_g/r + O(r_g^2)\). Along the line: \[ c\,\Delta t_{\rm one} = (1 + \gamma)r_g\Big[\operatorname{arsinh}\frac xb\Big]_{-x_1}^{x_2} = (1 + \gamma)r_g\ln\frac{(x_2 + r_2)(x_1 + r_1)}{b^2}, \] using \(\operatorname{arsinh}(-s) = -\operatorname{arsinh}s\) and \(\ln\big((x_1 + r_1)/b\big) = -\ln\big((r_1 - x_1)/b\big)\) — or simply \((\sqrt{x_1^2 + b^2} - x_1)(\sqrt{x_1^2 + b^2} + x_1) = b^2\). For \(b \ll r_i\), \(x_i \approx r_i\), so the argument is \(4r_1r_2/b^2\). Doubling for the round trip and writing \(r_g/c = GM/c^3\) gives the formula. The straight line is legitimate by Fermat's principle: the true ray makes the coordinate travel time stationary among nearby paths — unit 11's principle, proved for this metric in the notes of the deck's slide "The Shapiro delay" — so a path that differs from it at order \(r_g\) changes the time only at order \(r_g^2\).

2. \(r_1 = 1.5202\times10^{11}\) m, \(r_2 = 1.1103\times10^{12}\) m, \(b = 1.1131\times10^{9}\) m. \[ \frac{4r_1r_2}{b^2} = 5.449\times10^{5},\qquad \ln = 13.208, \] \[ \Delta t = 4\times4.92549\ \mu\text{s}\times13.208 = 260.2\ \mu\text{s}. \] Half of it, \(130.1\ \mu\)s, is the \(\gamma\) part: the spatial metric.

3. The delay is proportional to \((1 + \gamma)/2\), so a fractional change \((\gamma - 1)/2 = (1.05 \pm 1.15)\times10^{-5}\). At closest approach: \(260.2\ \mu\text{s}\times(1.05 \pm 1.15)\times10^{-5} = 2.7 \pm 3.0\) ns. The experiment says the delay is general relativity's \(260.2\ \mu\)s to within about 3 ns, and \(\gamma = 1\) sits \(0.9\sigma\) from the central value: consistent. The \(g_{ij}\) half, \(130.1\ \mu\)s, is present in full to about 2 parts in \(10^5\).

4. Only the logarithm depends on \(t\): \(y = \dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\Big(-\dfrac{8GM}{c^3}\ln b\Big) = -\dfrac{8GM}{c^3}\dfrac{\dot b}{b}\), and \(\dot b/b = v^2t/(b_0^2 + v^2t^2)\), which is largest at \(vt = b_0\) with value \(v/2b_0\). So \[ |y|_{\max} = \frac{4GM}{c^3}\,\frac{v}{b_0}. \] \(v = 29.78\times7.4216/8.4378 = 26.19\) km/s (about \(3.3R_\odot\) a day), and \(|y|_{\max} = 19.702\times10^{-6}\times26\,190/1.1131\times10^9 = 4.6\times10^{-10}\).

Check. \([GM/c^3] = \mathsf T\) ✓, and \(y\) is dimensionless ✓. \(M \to 0\): no delay ✓. The exact argument \((x_2 + r_2)(x_1 + r_1)/b^2 = 5.4488\times10^5\) differs from \(4r_1r_2/b^2\) in the fifth figure, so the approximation \(b \ll r_i\) costs nothing ✓.

P5 Proof: the factor of two is \(g_{rr}\) medium

exercises: null geodesics of a general static metric, first-order perturbation, the discriminating prediction, a number

Let \(\mathrm ds^2 = -A(r)c^2\mathrm dt^2 + B(r)\mathrm dr^2 + r^2\mathrm d\Omega^2\) with, to first order, \(A = 1 - 2r_g/r\) and \(B = 1 + 2\gamma r_g/r\).

  1. Prove that null geodesics in the equatorial plane obey, to first order in \(r_g\), \[ u'' + u = \frac{(1 - \gamma)\,r_g}{b^2} + 3\gamma\,r_gu^2, \qquad b = cL/E. \]
  2. Prove that the total deflection of a ray with impact parameter \(b\) is \(\Delta\theta = 2(1 + \gamma)r_g/b\).
  3. Show that \(\gamma = 0\) reproduces, identically, the equation of a Newtonian particle moving at \(c\) far away, so that general relativity's deflection is exactly twice the corpuscle's. Which metric component supplies the difference?
  4. Evaluate both deflections for a ray grazing the Sun, and set them against Eddington's 1919 results \(1.98 \pm 0.12''\) (Sobral) and \(1.61 \pm 0.30''\) (Príncipe), and VLBI's \(1.7510 \pm 0.0004''\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Same three constants as always — \(E\), \(L\), and a normalisation, now null. The answer to part 2 is a sum of two pieces, one from each metric function.

Rung 2

The null normalisation with \(\dot r = -Lu'\) gives \(Bu'^2 = 1/(Ab^2) - u^2\). Expand \(1/A\) and \(1/B\) to first order and differentiate. For part 2, solve with \(u_0 = \sin\varphi/b\) and find where the first-order solution returns to \(u = 0\).

Rung 3

First line: \(u'^2 = (1 - 2\gamma r_gu)\big(\tfrac1{b^2}(1 + 2r_gu) - u^2\big) = \tfrac1{b^2} - u^2 + \tfrac{2(1 - \gamma)r_gu}{b^2} + 2\gamma r_gu^3 + O(r_g^2)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Newton, dressed as a metric, is \(g_{tt}\) with flat space. Put a parameter on the spatial part and watch the deflection split into a \(g_{tt}\) piece and a \(g_{rr}\) piece of equal size.

1. \(E = Ac^2\dot t\) and \(L = r^2\dot\varphi\) are conserved as before (the metric is still independent of \(t\) and \(\varphi\)). The null condition \(-Ac^2\dot t^2 + B\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\varphi^2 = 0\) with \(\dot r = -Lu'\) gives \(BL^2u'^2 = E^2/(Ac^2) - L^2u^2\), i.e. \(Bu'^2 = 1/(Ab^2) - u^2\). With \(1/A = 1 + 2r_gu\), \(1/B = 1 - 2\gamma r_gu\), the expansion in Rung 3 follows. Differentiate in \(\varphi\) and divide by \(2u'\) (valid off turning points, and then everywhere by continuity as in P1): \(u'' = -u + (1 - \gamma)r_g/b^2 + 3\gamma r_gu^2\). \(\square\)

2. Zeroth order: \(u_0 = \sin\varphi/b\). First order: \(u_1'' + u_1 = (1 - \gamma)r_g/b^2 + \tfrac{3\gamma r_g}{2b^2}(1 - \cos2\varphi)\). The constant part is solved by a constant; \(-\tfrac{3\gamma r_g}{2b^2}\cos2\varphi\) by \(C\cos2\varphi\) with \(-3C = -\tfrac{3\gamma r_g}{2b^2}\). At the incoming end, put \(\varphi = -\delta\): \(0 = -\delta/b + \big[(1 - \gamma) + 2\gamma\big]r_g/b^2\), so \(\delta = (1 + \gamma)r_g/b\). The outgoing end gives the same by symmetry about \(\varphi = \pi/2\). Total \(\Delta\theta = 2\delta = 2(1 + \gamma)r_g/b\). \(\square\)

Unit 08's Binet equation for \(-GM/r\) is \(u'' + u = GM/L_N^2\) with \(L_N = bc\) for a particle of speed \(c\) far away — the same equation, not merely the same answer. P6 Proof: the secular term is the answer hard

  5. Someone deletes the secular term "because secular terms are unphysical". What do they predict, and what is the precise error?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a forced linear oscillator with a resonant forcing term. Part 2 is an integral identity. Part 3 is a trigonometric Taylor expansion. Part 4 is where \(\varepsilon\varphi\) stops being small.

Rung 2

Expand \(w_0^2\) into constant, \(\cos\varphi\) and \(\cos2\varphi\) parts, and solve each separately; the \(\cos\varphi\) part resonates with the free oscillation. For part 2 multiply the equation by \(\cos\varphi\) and integrate over a period.

Rung 3

First line: \(w_1'' + w_1 = 1 + \tfrac12e^2 + 2e\cos\varphi + \tfrac12e^2\cos2\varphi\), and \((\varphi\sin\varphi)'' + \varphi\sin\varphi = 2\cos\varphi\).

Worked solution

Idea. The forcing at the free frequency cannot be absorbed by any periodic function; it produces a term growing like \(\varphi\), and that term is the first Taylor coefficient of a shift in frequency. Removing it removes the physics.

1. Order \(\varepsilon\): \(w_1'' + w_1 = w_0^2 = 1 + \tfrac12e^2 + 2e\cos\varphi + \tfrac12e^2\cos2\varphi\). Term by term: the constant gives \(1 + \tfrac12e^2\); \(\tfrac12e^2\cos2\varphi\) gives \(C\cos2\varphi\) with \(-3C = \tfrac12e^2\), so \(-\tfrac16e^2\cos2\varphi\); and \(2e\cos\varphi\), resonant, gives \(e\varphi\sin\varphi\) by the identity in Rung 3 (check: \((\varphi\sin\varphi)' = \sin\varphi + \varphi\cos\varphi\), \((\varphi\sin\varphi)'' = 2\cos\varphi - \varphi\sin\varphi\)). So \[ w_1 = 1 + \tfrac12e^2 - \tfrac16e^2\cos2\varphi + e\,\varphi\sin\varphi . \]

2. Suppose \(w_1\) is \(2\pi\)-periodic and \(C^2\). Integrating by parts twice, \(\int_0^{2\pi}(w_1'' + w_1)\cos\varphi\,\mathrm d\varphi = \int_0^{2\pi}w_1(-\cos\varphi + \cos\varphi)\,\mathrm d\varphi = 0\), the boundary terms cancelling by periodicity. But the right side gives \(\int_0^{2\pi}w_0^2\cos\varphi\,\mathrm d\varphi = 2e\pi \ne 0\). Contradiction. So no periodic \(w_1\) exists: this is unit 21's solvability condition. An orbit that closed after one turn would be a \(2\pi\)-periodic \(w\), and its order-\(\varepsilon\) coefficient would be a periodic \(w_1\); so near the ellipse, for \(e \ne 0\), none closes after one turn. (An orbit may still close after many turns, if its radial frequency \(\nu\) happens to be rational.) \(\square\)

3. \(\cos\big((1 - \varepsilon)\varphi\big) = \cos\varphi\cos\varepsilon\varphi + \sin\varphi\sin\varepsilon\varphi = \cos\varphi + \varepsilon\varphi\sin\varphi + O(\varepsilon^2\varphi^2)\). Hence \(1 + e\cos\varphi + \varepsilon e\varphi\sin\varphi = 1 + e\cos\big((1 - \varepsilon)\varphi\big) + O(\varepsilon^2\varphi^2)\): an ellipse whose argument runs at rate \(1 - \varepsilon\). Perihelia sit at \((1 - \varepsilon)\varphi = 2\pi k\), spaced \(2\pi/(1 - \varepsilon) = 2\pi + 2\pi\varepsilon + O(\varepsilon^2)\) apart. Poincaré–Lindstedt gets this directly, by allowing \(\nu \ne 1\) and choosing \(\nu_1 = -1\) to satisfy the solvability condition.

4. The naive form fails when \(\varepsilon\varphi \sim 1\): \(\varphi \sim 1.25\times10^7\) rad, \(1.99\times10^6\) orbits, \(4.8\times10^5\) years. A century is \(415\) orbits, \(\varepsilon\varphi = 2.1\times10^{-4}\), and the neglected term is \(10^{-4}\) of the secular one: the naive series is perfectly good over the observational record. Over \(10^6\) years it would predict a perihelion "oscillation" of amplitude growing without bound in \(e\varphi\sin\varphi\) — nonsense — while the uniform form just keeps turning.

5. Deleting \(\varepsilon e\varphi\sin\varphi\) leaves a closed, fattened ellipse and predicts zero precession. The error is not an approximation but a contradiction: by part 2, the truncated series with a periodic \(w_1\) does not satisfy the equation at order \(\varepsilon\). Unit 21's method removes a non-uniformity by re-expressing it as a frequency shift; it never removes the effect. Here the accumulated phase is the observable — the \(43''\).

Check. \(e \to 0\): the secular term vanishes, and the circular orbit simply has a slightly different radius, \(w = 1 + \varepsilon\) ✓. Units: \(w\), \(\varphi\), \(\varepsilon\) are all dimensionless ✓. Number: \(2\pi\varepsilon = 5.02\times10^{-7}\) rad per orbit, P2's \(0.1035''\) ✓.

P11 S2: precession and redshift at a black hole medium

exercises: the perihelion formula far from the Sun, the exact redshift to first order, adding two clock effects, comparing with data

The star S2 orbits Sagittarius A* (\(M = 4.26\times10^6M_\odot\)) with \(a = 1031\) au and \(e = 0.8847\).

  1. Check the period against \(16.05\) years using Kepler's third law.
  2. Compute \(\varepsilon = 3r_g/p\) and the pericentre advance per orbit, in arcminutes. Is first order in \(\varepsilon\) enough? The GRAVITY collaboration (2020) measured the advance as \(1.10 \pm 0.19\) times this prediction.
  3. At pericentre, compute the gravitational redshift \(r_g/r_p\) and the transverse-Doppler redshift \(v_p^2/2c^2\), each as an equivalent velocity \(cz\) in km/s. GRAVITY (2018) measured the sum as \(f = 0.90 \pm 0.09\,(\text{stat}) \pm 0.15\,(\text{sys})\) times the prediction. What does \(f = 0\) mean, physically?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four numbers: a period, an angle, two velocities. Each is a formula from the deck with new inputs.

Rung 2

\(r_g\) scales with \(M\): \(4.26\times10^6\times1476.6\) m. \(r_p = a(1 - e)\) and \(v_p^2 = GM(2/r_p - 1/a)\) (vis-viva, unit 08). The two redshifts add at first order because the clock rate factor is \(\sqrt{1 - 2x}\,\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2} \approx 1 - x - v^2/2c^2\).

Rung 3

First line: \(r_g = 6.290\times10^9\) m, \(p = 1031\times1.49598\times10^{11}\times(1 - 0.8847^2)\) m.

Worked solution

Idea. The same formulas as for Mercury and the Sun, with a mass a million times larger and a pericentre only \(1400\) Schwarzschild radii out. Every effect is \(10^4\) times bigger, which makes one orbit enough.

1. \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/GM}\) with \(a = 1.5424\times10^{14}\) m and \(GM = 5.654\times10^{26}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}\): \(T = 16.04\) yr ✓.

2. \(1 - e^2 = 0.21731\), \(p = 3.352\times10^{13}\) m, \(\varepsilon = 3\times6.290\times10^9/3.352\times10^{13} = 5.63\times10^{-4}\). \(2\pi\varepsilon = 3.538\times10^{-3}\) rad \(= 12.2'\) per orbit. The neglected terms are of relative order \(\varepsilon\), a few parts in \(10^4\), far below the \(17\%\) measurement error: first order is plenty. The measured \(1.10 \pm 0.19\) is half a standard error from 1.

3. \(r_p = 1031\times0.1153\) au \(= 118.9\) au \(= 1.778\times10^{13}\) m, i.e. \(1414\) Schwarzschild radii. Gravitational: \(r_g/r_p = 3.537\times10^{-4}\), \(cz = 106.0\) km/s. Speed: \(v_p^2 = 5.654\times10^{26}(2/1.778\times10^{13} - 1/1.5424\times10^{14})\), \(v_p = 7741\) km/s, and \(v_p^2/2c^2 = 3.333\times10^{-4}\), \(cz = 99.9\) km/s. Total about \(206\) km/s, on top of the ordinary Doppler shift of the orbit (thousands of km/s along the line of sight, which the fit models). \(f = 0\) would be pure Newton with Galilean clocks; \(f = 1\) is relativity. The measurement excludes \(0\) at about five combined standard errors and is consistent with \(1\). Like the Sun's redshift, this tests the equivalence principle and special relativity, not the field equations.

Check. Both redshifts dimensionless ✓. Virial-like: for a circular orbit \(v^2/2c^2 = x/2\), half the gravitational term; at pericentre of an eccentric orbit \(v^2\) is larger, \(v_p^2/c^2 = x(1 + e)\), so the two terms are nearly equal: \((1 + e)/2 = 0.94\), and \(99.9/106.0 = 0.94\) ✓.
