The classical tests: three numbers, and one of them is 43

Unit 08's orbit equation acquires exactly one term. From it come the 43 arcseconds of Mercury, the 1.75 arcseconds of starlight at the Sun's edge, a radar echo that returns late, and an orbit that no longer has to be stable.

needs 36, 21, 08, 10~3½ h slides + 7 h problemsproblems: ./mr problems 37

Unit 36 found the metric. This unit asks the only question that makes a metric physics: what do things do in it? The answer is the geodesics, and the geodesics of Schwarzschild reduce — exactly, with no approximation anywhere — to unit 08's Binet equation with one extra term. Everything in the unit is that term, read in different regimes.

Three of the four prerequisites are old friends, and each is used for the thing it was built for. Unit 08 supplies the Binet substitution and the effective potential. Unit 10 supplies secular perturbation, which is how the 531 arcseconds of Newtonian planetary perturbation on Mercury are computed. Unit 21 supplies Poincaré–Lindstedt and, more importantly, the argument that the secular term in the orbit is the answer rather than an error. Unit 36 supplies the metric, its Killing fields — four of them, $\partial_t$ and the three rotations, of which this unit uses $\partial_t$ and $\partial_\varphi$ — and the theorem that each one conserves $g(K, \dot x)$ along a geodesic.

How to read this deck

One example runs through everything: Mercury, and alongside it a ray of light grazing the Sun. Section 1 derives the orbit equation both are governed by. Section 2 reads it through the effective potential, which is where the effects with no Newtonian analogue live. Section 3 computes Mercury's 43 arcseconds per century, end to end. Section 4 bends the ray, twice — once as a Newtonian corpuscle, once as general relativity requires — and names the metric component responsible for the difference. Section 5 delays and reddens it. Section 6 places the classical tests among the modern ones and closes the example.

Conventions

Signature $(-,+,+,+)$, $c$ kept explicit, as in unit 32. $r_g = GM/c^2$ is the gravitational radius, half the Schwarzschild radius $r_s = 2r_g$. Unit 36 wrote every formula with $r_s$, as $f = 1 - r_s/r$; here the same function is $1 - 2r_g/r$, because every expansion in this unit is in $r_g/r$ and the factors of 2 land better this way. A dot is $\mathrm d/\mathrm d\tau$ along a timelike geodesic (proper time) or $\mathrm d/\mathrm d\lambda$ along a null one (affine parameter); a prime is $\mathrm d/\mathrm d\varphi$.

$E := -g(\partial_t, \dot x)$ and $L := g(\partial_\varphi, \dot x)$ are unit 36's two Killing constants, unchanged in definition and in sign. For a massive particle, with $\dot x$ normalised by $g(\dot x, \dot x) = -c^2$, they are unit 36's specific energy and angular momentum: per unit rest mass, so that no mass appears anywhere — the equivalence principle of unit 30, in bookkeeping form. Unit 21 called the same $L$ by the name $h$. For light there is no rest mass to divide by and no proper time: $E$ and $L$ are the same two Killing constants, but they now scale with the arbitrary choice of affine parameter, so neither alone means anything. Only their ratio does, and it is the impact parameter $b = cL/E$ — which is why every light formula in this unit is written in $b$ and never in $E$ or $L$ separately.

Constants used everywhere: $GM_\odot = 1.32712440018\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$, $c = 299\,792\,458$ m/s, $R_\odot = 6.957\times10^{8}$ m, 1 au $= 1.495978707\times10^{11}$ m, one radian $= 206\,264.806''$. Every number in the deck was computed from these.

37 · overview

One orbit, one ray, one extra term

the running example

Mercury: $a = 5.79\times10^{10}$ m, $e = 0.2056$, 415.2 orbits a century. Its perihelion advances by about $5600''$ per century, and Newton accounts for all but $43''$ of it.

A ray grazing the Sun: impact parameter $b = R_\odot$. Newton, treating light as a fast particle, bends it by $0.88''$. The sky says $1.75''$.

Both are geodesics of the Schwarzschild metric, and both obey one equation, in $u = 1/r$ as a function of the angle $\varphi$:

$$u'' + u = \underbrace{\frac{GM}{L^2}}_{\text{unit 08}} + \underbrace{3r_g\,u^2}_{\text{new}}$$

$r_g = GM/c^2$, $L$ the angular momentum per unit mass. The first term comes from the particle's rest mass, so for light it is absent and the ray obeys $u'' + u = 3r_gu^2$ alone.

how the equation is reached · section 1

1. The metric does not involve $t$ or $\varphi$, so each geodesic conserves an energy $E$ and an angular momentum $L$.

2. Symmetry makes the orbit planar, and the tangent's fixed length, $g(\dot x, \dot x) = -c^2$ (zero for light), gives $\dot r^2$ as a function of $r$: an energy equation.

3. Unit 08's trick: $u = 1/r$, $\varphi$ as parameter, $\dot r = -Lu'$.

4. Differentiate in $\varphi$. One cross term, $2r_gL^2u^3$ — the $g_{tt}$ correction times the angular motion — becomes the new $3r_gu^2$.

The plan. 1 the equation · 2 near a black hole · 3 Mercury's $43''$ · 4 $0.88''$ against $1.75''$ · 5 delay and redshift · 6 the tests now.

Dotted underline, like $r_g$: a glossary term. Solid, like Mercury: the running example, on hover. N: notes and long proofs.

The title's three numbers are the perihelion advance ($43''$ per century), the light deflection ($1.75''$) and the Shapiro delay (about $233\ \mu$s for a radar echo from Venus grazing the Sun). The fourth classical test, the gravitational redshift, was closed in unit 30 from the equivalence principle alone, and section 5 only makes it exact. Einstein's own list of 1916 had three tests: perihelion, deflection, redshift. Shapiro added the delay in 1964, and it is now the most precisely measured of them.

The sketch, step by step. Each step is a slide in section 1. Step 1 is Two Killing vectors, two constants: $\partial_t$ and $\partial_\varphi$ are Killing vectors, and each conserves $g(K, \dot x)$. Step 2 is The third constant, and the plane, whose boxed $\dot r^2 = E^2/c^2 - (1 - 2r_g/r)(\sigma + L^2/r^2)$ is the energy equation, with $\sigma = c^2$ for a massive particle and $0$ for light. Steps 3 and 4 are Worked example 1 — the orbit equation, exactly. The new term's origin is visible already in step 2: expanding the product gives $2r_gL^2/r^3 = 2r_gL^2u^3$, a coupling of the $g_{tt}$ correction $2r_g/r$ to the angular kinetic term $L^2/r^2$, and $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varphi}(2r_gL^2u^3) = 6r_gL^2u^2u'$, which after dividing by $2L^2u'$ is $3r_gu^2$. The $GM/L^2$ term comes from $2r_g\sigma u$, the product of the same correction with the rest-mass term $\sigma$, which is why light, with $\sigma = 0$, does without it.

Why these numbers are small. Every effect in this unit is proportional to $r_g/r$ at the place the effect happens. At Mercury's orbit that is $2.5\times10^{-8}$; at the Sun's surface $2.1\times10^{-6}$. That is why first-order perturbation theory is enough in sections 3 to 5, and why section 2, which is about $r$ of a few $r_g$, has nothing to do with the solar system and everything to do with black holes.

37 · geodesics

Two Killing vectors, two constants

The metric's components do not depend on $t$ or $\varphi$, so $\partial_t$ and $\partial_\varphi$ are Killing vectors — two of the four unit 36 counted, the other two rotations being what makes the orbit planar on the next slide. Along any geodesic with tangent $\dot x$, timelike or null, $g(K, \dot x)$ is constant for a Killing $K$ (unit 36). For a massive particle, per unit rest mass:

the two constants $$E := -g(\partial_t, \dot x) = \Big(1 - \frac{2r_g}{r}\Big)c^2\,\dot t,$$ $$L := g(\partial_\varphi, \dot x) = r^2\sin^2\theta\;\dot\varphi .$$

The specific energy $E$ and the specific angular momentum $L$.

they are what the names say

For a slow particle far away, $E = c^2 + \tfrac12v^2 - GM/r + \dots$: rest energy plus Newton's energy, per unit mass. And $L$ is unit 08's $r^2\dot\varphi$, with proper time for time.

this is Noether, again

$t$ and $\varphi$ are absent from the geodesic Lagrangian $\mathcal L = g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu$ (unit 32), so their momenta are conserved: unit 13's theorem for a cyclic coordinate. The Killing vector is the coordinate-free statement of the same symmetry.

Full proof that $g(K,\dot x)$ is constant. Cited from unit 36, where it is proved from Killing's equation $\nabla_\mu K_\nu + \nabla_\nu K_\mu = 0$: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\tau}(K_\mu\dot x^\mu) = \dot x^\nu\nabla_\nu(K_\mu\dot x^\mu) = (\nabla_\nu K_\mu)\dot x^\nu\dot x^\mu + K_\mu\dot x^\nu\nabla_\nu\dot x^\mu$. The second term is zero by the geodesic equation; the first is a symmetric tensor $\dot x^\nu\dot x^\mu$ contracted with the antisymmetric (by Killing's equation) $\nabla_\nu K_\mu$, so zero. For this metric there is a second, entirely elementary proof, and it is the one worth using in practice: the $t$ and $\varphi$ Euler–Lagrange equations of $\mathcal L$ read $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\tau}\big(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot t\big) = \partial_t\mathcal L = 0$ and likewise for $\varphi$, and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot t = -2(1-2r_g/r)c^2\dot t$, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\varphi = 2r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\varphi$.

The Newtonian reading of $E$. For a slow particle $\dot t = \mathrm dt/\mathrm d\tau \approx 1 + v^2/2c^2 + r_g/r$ (unit 27's time dilation plus unit 30's gravitational one). Then $E = (1 - 2r_g/r)c^2(1 + v^2/2c^2 + r_g/r) = c^2 + \tfrac12v^2 - c^2r_g/r + O(c^{-2})$, and $c^2r_g = GM$. Dimensions: $E$ is an energy per unit mass, $\mathsf{L^2T^{-2}}$ ✓; $L$ is $\mathsf{L^2T^{-1}}$ ✓.

And for light. Unit 36's proof of the conservation theorem never used $g(\dot x, \dot x) = -c^2$, and unit 36 says so explicitly, so the same two constants exist for a null geodesic parametrised by an affine $\lambda$. What does not carry over is the word "specific": a photon has no rest mass to divide by, and rescaling $\lambda$ rescales both $E$ and $L$. Sections 4 and 5 therefore use only the scale-free combination $b = cL/E$, and the deflection and the delay are written in $b$ from the start.

Sign convention. The minus in $E = -g(\partial_t, \dot x)$ is there because $\partial_t$ is timelike and future-pointing outside the horizon, so $g(\partial_t, \dot x) \lt 0$ for any future-pointing $\dot x$. With it, $E \gt 0$. Unit 36's warning applies here too: $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) = -(1 - 2r_g/r)c^2$ is negative only for $r \gt 2r_g$, null at $2r_g$ and positive inside, so "$E$ is an energy" is a statement about the exterior. Everything in this unit lives at $r \gt 2r_g$ — section 2 goes down to the photon sphere at $3r_g$ and no further, and follows a plunging particle only up to the horizon.

The third constant, and the plane

the orbit is planar

Every geodesic lies in a plane through the centre; choose coordinates so it is $\theta = \pi/2$.

Proof. The rotations are isometries of the metric (unit 36), and an isometry carries geodesics to geodesics (unit 32), so it is enough to prove it for a rotated copy: rotate until $x(0)$ and $\dot x(0)$ have $\theta = \pi/2$, $\dot\theta = 0$. The $\theta$-equation, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\tau}(r^2\dot\theta) = r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi^2$, is solved by $\theta \equiv \pi/2$, and by uniqueness (unit 01) that is the solution. $\square$

the normalisation

$g(\dot x, \dot x) = -\sigma$, with $\sigma = c^2$ for a massive particle and proper time as parameter (unit 27), and $\sigma = 0$ for light. In the plane, with $f = 1 - 2r_g/r$:

$$-f\,c^2\dot t^2 + \frac{\dot r^2}{f} + r^2\dot\varphi^2 = -\sigma .$$

Substitute $\dot t = E/(fc^2)$ and $\dot\varphi = L/r^2$, multiply by $f$:

$$\boxed{\ \dot r^2 = \frac{E^2}{c^2} - \big(1 - 2r_g/r\big)\big(\sigma + L^2/r^2\big)\ }$$

The substitution, line by line. Write $f = 1 - 2r_g/r$. Then $-fc^2\dot t^2 = -fc^2E^2/(f^2c^4) = -E^2/(fc^2)$ and $r^2\dot\varphi^2 = L^2/r^2$. So the normalisation is $-E^2/(fc^2) + \dot r^2/f + L^2/r^2 = -\sigma$. Multiply by $f$: $-E^2/c^2 + \dot r^2 + fL^2/r^2 = -f\sigma$, which rearranges to the box.

What has happened to the eight equations. Four second-order geodesic equations are eight first-order ones. The plane uses two ($\theta$ and $\dot\theta$), the two Killing constants use two more ($\dot t$ and $\dot\varphi$ are now known functions of $r$), and the normalisation turns the $r$-equation into a first-order one. What remains is one first-order ODE for $r(\tau)$ plus quadratures for $t$ and $\varphi$ — exactly the structure of unit 08's reduction from six to two, and for exactly the same reason: symmetries.

Why the normalisation is a constant of motion. Along a geodesic $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\tau}g(\dot x, \dot x) = 2g(\nabla_{\dot x}\dot x, \dot x) = 0$ by metric compatibility and the geodesic equation (unit 32). So a geodesic that starts timelike stays timelike, and the parameter can be chosen so the constant is $-c^2$.

Why $\sigma$. Many texts call this constant $\kappa$ or $\epsilon$. Neither is free in this course: $\kappa$ is Einstein's $8\pi G/c^4$ in unit 35 and a horizon's surface gravity in unit 40, and $\varepsilon$ is section 3's small parameter $3r_g/p$. So $\sigma$, with this one meaning throughout the unit: $\sigma = c^2$ for a massive particle, $\sigma = 0$ for light.

Planarity. Unit 08 needed the cross product to see that central-force motion is planar; here it is a symmetry plus Picard–Lindelöf.

Checks. Dimensions: every term is $\mathsf{L^2T^{-2}}$ when $\sigma = c^2$ ✓. The limit $r_g \to 0$ gives $\dot r^2 = E^2/c^2 - c^2 - L^2/r^2$: free motion in Minkowski space in polar coordinates, with $E^2/c^2 - c^2 = \gamma^2v^2$ ✓.

The effective potential

For a massive particle ($\sigma = c^2$), halve the box and expand the product:

$$\tfrac12\dot r^2 + V_{\rm eff}(r) = \tfrac12\Big(\frac{E^2}{c^2} - c^2\Big),$$
the relativistic effective potential $$V_{\rm eff}(r) = -\frac{GM}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2r^2} - \frac{GM L^2}{c^2r^3}$$

Unit 08's $V_{\rm eff}$ — gravity plus the centrifugal barrier — and one new term, attractive, $\propto r^{-3}$. Exact.

two things that look Newtonian and are not

The time is proper time $\tau$, not $t$. And the "energy" on the right is $\tfrac12(E^2/c^2 - c^2)$, which is Newton's $E_N$ only when $E \approx c^2 + E_N$.

checks

Dimensions: $GML^2/c^2r^3$ is $\mathsf{L^3T^{-2}\cdot L^4T^{-2}/(L^2T^{-2}\cdot L^3)} = \mathsf{L^2T^{-2}}$, like $GM/r$ ✓.
Limit $c\to\infty$: the new term vanishes and unit 08 returns ✓.
Size at Mercury: new term over centrifugal term is $2r_g/r = 5.1\times10^{-8}$ at mean distance.

The expansion. $(1 - 2r_g/r)(c^2 + L^2/r^2) = c^2 + L^2/r^2 - 2r_gc^2/r - 2r_gL^2/r^3$. With $r_gc^2 = GM$, the box on the previous slide is $\dot r^2 = E^2/c^2 - c^2 - L^2/r^2 + 2GM/r + 2GML^2/(c^2r^3)$. Halving and moving the $r$-dependent terms to the left gives the slide.

Where the new term comes from. It is the product of $g_{tt}$'s correction $-2r_g/r$ with the $L^2/r^2$ that came from $g_{\varphi\varphi}$: a coupling of the gravitational potential to the kinetic energy of angular motion. In Newton, kinetic energy does not gravitate; here it does, as $E = mc^2$ says it must. The "$c^2$ in the denominator" is the cost of converting a kinetic energy per unit mass into a mass.

The ratio quoted. $\frac{GML^2/c^2r^3}{L^2/2r^2} = 2GM/(c^2r) = 2r_g/r$. With $r_g = 1476.6$ m and Mercury's $a = 5.7909\times10^{10}$ m: $5.10\times10^{-8}$.

Why the proper-time caveat does not bite the orbit. The next slide eliminates the parameter entirely in favour of $\varphi$, so the shape of the orbit never knew which clock was used. Only the period does, and no test in this unit uses the period to the precision at which the difference matters.

Worked example 1 — the orbit equation, exactly

from the radial equation to the orbit equation

Step 1 — change variable. $u = 1/r$ and $\varphi$ as parameter. Since $\dot\varphi = Lu^2$, $$\dot r = \frac{\mathrm dr}{\mathrm d\varphi}\dot\varphi = -\frac{u'}{u^2}\,Lu^2 = -Lu'.$$ Unit 08's Binet trick.

Step 2 — substitute into $\dot r^2 = E^2/c^2 - (1 - 2r_gu)(\sigma + L^2u^2)$: $$L^2u'^2 = \frac{E^2}{c^2} - \sigma - L^2u^2 + 2r_g\sigma u + 2r_gL^2u^3 .$$

Step 3 — differentiate in $\varphi$ and divide by $2L^2u'$ (away from turning points, $u' \ne 0$): $$u'' = -u + \frac{r_g\sigma}{L^2} + 3r_gu^2 .$$

Step 4. Massive ($\sigma = c^2$) and massless ($\sigma = 0$):

$$\boxed{\ u'' + u = \frac{GM}{L^2} + 3r_gu^2\ }$$ $$\boxed{\ u'' + u = 3r_gu^2\ }$$

Step 5 — check. $r_g \to 0$: unit 08's Kepler equation ✓, and a straight line for light ✓.

Step 3 in full. $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varphi}(L^2u'^2) = 2L^2u'u''$, and the right side differentiates to $-2L^2uu' + 2r_g\sigma u' + 6r_gL^2u^2u'$. Divide by $2L^2u'$.

The turning points. Dividing by $u'$ fails where $u' = 0$, at perihelion and aphelion. The second-order equation obtained is nonetheless valid there too: both sides are continuous in $\varphi$, and they agree on a dense set, so they agree everywhere. (The same point arises in unit 00's energy method, which was where "multiply by $\dot x$ and integrate" first lost the equilibria.) The circular orbits, where $u' \equiv 0$, are recovered as the constant solutions of the second-order equation, which is exactly why one prefers it to the first-order one.

Dimensions. $[u''] = [u] = \mathsf{L^{-1}}$; $[GM/L^2] = \mathsf{L^3T^{-2}/L^4T^{-2}} = \mathsf{L^{-1}}$ ✓; $[r_gu^2] = \mathsf{L\cdot L^{-2}} = \mathsf{L^{-1}}$ ✓.

How exact is "exactly". No approximation has been made: this is the equatorial geodesic equation of the Schwarzschild metric, rewritten. All the approximating happens later, when it is solved. That is the reason to prefer this route to the weak-field one: the equation is right at every $r \gt 2r_g$, so section 2 can use it near a black hole and section 3 at Mercury with the same confidence.

Unit 08 predicted this. Its notes say "unit 37 gets $u'' + u = GM\mu^2/L^2 + 3GMu^2/c^2$". There $L$ was the total angular momentum of a body of mass $\mu$; here it is per unit mass, so $\mu$ has gone.

What the extra term is

Unit 08's Binet equation for a force $f(r)$ per unit mass is $u'' + u = -f(1/u)/(L^2u^2)$. So the orbit equation is Newton's, for the force

$$f(r) = -\frac{GM}{r^2} - \frac{3GML^2}{c^2r^4}.$$

An inverse-square law plus an inverse-fourth-power correction, attractive, proportional to $L^2$ — to the square of the transverse speed.

a reading, not a mechanism

No such force acts: the particle is in free fall. The reading is exact only for the shape $r(\varphi)$, and only with proper time as the clock.

the size, at Mercury

The ratio of the new term to Newton's is $3r_gu^2/(GM/L^2) = 3r_g\,p/r^2$, with $p = L^2/GM$:

The force reading, derived. Set $-f(1/u)/(L^2u^2) = GM/L^2 + 3r_gu^2$; then $f = -GMu^2 - 3r_gL^2u^4 = -GM/r^2 - 3GML^2/(c^2r^4)$.

Why "proportional to $L^2$" matters. A light ray has large $L$ relative to its energy scale, and the same term does all the bending in section 4. A planet moving slowly has small $L^2/c^2$ relative to $r^2$, and the term is tiny. The $u^2$-term therefore measures how fast things are moving sideways compared with light, as well as how deep they are in the potential: the ratio $3r_gp/r^2$ is of order $3(v/c)^2$ for a near-circular orbit, since $GM/r \approx v^2$.

The numbers. $r_g = 1476.625$ m, $p = 5.54604\times10^{10}$ m. At perihelion $r = a(1 - e) = 4.60012\times10^{10}$ m, ratio $3\times1476.625\times5.54604\times10^{10}/(4.60012\times10^{10})^2 = 1.161\times10^{-7}$. At $r = a$: $7.33\times10^{-8}$. At aphelion $a(1 + e) = 6.98169\times10^{10}$ m: $5.04\times10^{-8}$.

Bertrand's theorem. Unit 08 cited it: the only central forces all of whose bounded orbits close are the inverse square and the linear spring. The force here is neither, so bounded orbits do not close — the qualitative content of section 3 before a single number is computed.

Circular orbits come in pairs

Write $\ell = L/c$, a length. Then $V_{\rm eff} = c^2\big(-r_g/r + \ell^2/2r^2 - r_g\ell^2/r^3\big)$ and

$$V_{\rm eff}'(r) = \frac{c^2}{r^4}\big(r_g\,r^2 - \ell^2\,r + 3r_g\ell^2\big).$$

A circular orbit is a zero of the quadratic:

$$r_\pm = \frac{\ell^2 \pm \sqrt{\ell^4 - 12\,r_g^2\ell^2}}{2r_g}.$$

Newton's $V_{\rm eff}'$ has the bracket $r_gr - \ell^2$: one root, always a minimum.

circular orbits of Schwarzschild

(i) Circular orbits exist iff $\ell^2 \ge 12r_g^2$.
(ii) $r_+$ is a minimum of $V_{\rm eff}$ (stable), $r_-$ a maximum (unstable).
(iii) As $\ell$ runs from $\sqrt{12}\,r_g$ to $\infty$, $r_+$ increases from $6r_g$ to $\infty$ and $r_-$ decreases from $6r_g$ to $3r_g$.

full proof in the notes (N)

The next slide plots both roots against $\ell^2$.

Full proof

(i) The quadratic $r_gr^2 - \ell^2r + 3r_g\ell^2$ has real roots iff its discriminant $\ell^4 - 12r_g^2\ell^2 = \ell^2(\ell^2 - 12r_g^2)$ is non-negative. Both roots are positive (their sum $\ell^2/r_g$ and product $3\ell^2$ are), and both exceed $3r_g$, as (iii) shows, so they lie outside the horizon $2r_g$.

(ii) $V_{\rm eff}' = (c^2/r^4)\,q(r)$ with $q$ an upward parabola. For $r$ between the roots $q \lt 0$, outside them $q \gt 0$. So $V_{\rm eff}$ increases, decreases, increases: $r_-$ is a local maximum and $r_+$ a local minimum. When $\ell^2 = 12r_g^2$ the roots merge into a double root, $q \ge 0$ everywhere, and $V_{\rm eff}$ is monotone with an inflection at $6r_g$.

(iii) Put $s = \ell^2/r_g^2 \ge 12$. Then $r_\pm/r_g = \tfrac12\big(s \pm \sqrt{s^2 - 12s}\big)$. At $s = 12$ both equal 6. $r_+/r_g$ is a sum of increasing functions of $s$, so increases to $\infty$. For $r_-$: $r_-/r_g = \tfrac12\big(s - \sqrt{s^2 - 12s}\big) = \frac{6s}{s + \sqrt{s^2 - 12s}} = \frac{6}{1 + \sqrt{1 - 12/s}}$ (rationalise), which decreases from 6 at $s = 12$ towards 3 as $s \to \infty$. Every $r \gt 6r_g$ is some $r_+$ and every $r \in (3r_g, 6r_g)$ some $r_-$, by continuity and monotonicity. $\square$

the plunge

Newton's barrier $\ell^2/2r^2$ beats $-r_g/r$ as $r \to 0$ for any $\ell \ne 0$: nothing with angular momentum reaches the centre. Here $-r_g\ell^2/r^3$ beats both. A particle with $\ell^2 \lt 12r_g^2$, or with energy above the barrier's top, falls in.

The figure. Units $r_g = c = 1$. Newton: $-1/r + 8/r^2$ ($\ell = 4$), minimum at $r = 16$. Why the plunge has no Newtonian analogue. In Newton, the centrifugal barrier is $L^2/2r^2$ and gravity is $-GM/r$: at small $r$ the barrier wins, always. Only exactly radial motion ($L = 0$) reaches the centre. In Schwarzschild the extra term $-GML^2/c^2r^3$ is also proportional to $L^2$ and wins at small $r$, so more angular momentum helps the fall at small radius. Whether a particle survives is decided by whether its energy is below the top of the barrier, at $r_-$.

Where the ISCO matters. A thin accretion disc is a sequence of nearly circular orbits; gas spirals slowly inwards, radiating, until it reaches $6r_g$ and then plunges in a few orbits, radiating little more. The energy radiated is therefore the binding energy at the ISCO: $1 - \sqrt{8/9} = 5.72\%$ of the rest mass. The problem sheet's P3 derives it and compares it with what accreting black holes are observed to radiate.

Sizes. $6r_g = 8.86$ km for one solar mass: far inside the Sun, which is why none of this touches the solar system.

Nearly circular orbits precess, exactly

Put $u = u_c + \delta$ in the orbit equation, $u_c = 1/r_c$ a circular orbit, to first order in $\delta$:

$$\delta'' + \big(1 - 6r_g u_c\big)\,\delta = 0 .$$

So $r$ oscillates with frequency, in $\varphi$,

the apsidal frequency $$\nu = \sqrt{1 - \frac{6r_g}{r_c}},$$ $$\Delta\varphi_{\rm peri} = 2\pi\Big(\frac1\nu - 1\Big)\ \text{per orbit.}$$
three limits, one formula

$r_c \gg r_g$: $\Delta\varphi \approx 6\pi r_g/r_c$, section 3's answer at $e = 0$.
$r_c \to 6r_g$: $\nu \to 0$: at the ISCO the orbit stops returning to perihelion.
$r_c \lt 6r_g$: $\nu^2 \lt 0$: unstable.

The next slide plots the exact advance against its first-order approximation.

The linearisation. $(u_c + \delta)'' + u_c + \delta = GM/L^2 + 3r_g(u_c^2 + 2u_c\delta + \delta^2)$. The zeroth-order terms cancel by the circular-orbit condition, and $\delta^2$ is dropped: $\delta'' + \delta = 6r_gu_c\delta$. This is unit 02's linearisation about an equilibrium, and unit 08's "which power laws close" problem done for this force: there the frequency was $\sqrt{3 + n}$ for $f \propto r^n$.

From frequency to precession. $\delta = A\cos(\nu\varphi)$, so successive perihelia (maxima of $u$) are $\Delta\varphi = 2\pi/\nu$ apart, not $2\pi$. The excess is $2\pi(1/\nu - 1)$. For small $x = 6r_g/r_c$: $1/\sqrt{1 - x} = 1 + x/2 + \dots$, giving $\pi x = 6\pi r_g/r_c$.

Why this is worth having beside section 3's calculation: section 3 is first order in $r_g$ and all orders in $e$; this is all orders in $r_g$ and first order in $e$. They overlap in the corner $r_g \to 0$, $e \to 0$, and agree there. Two expansions agreeing where both are valid is the check unit 21 taught.

Light has a potential too: the photon sphere

For light ($\sigma = 0$), divide the radial equation by $L^2$:

$$u'^2 = \frac1{b^2} - W(u),$$ $$W(u) = u^2\big(1 - 2r_gu\big),$$

$b = cL/E$ is the impact parameter, the distance of the incoming line. Rescaling the affine parameter rescales $E$ and $L$ together, so $b$ is the only thing about a ray that either of them says. $W$ peaks at $u = 1/3r_g$, where $W = 1/27r_g^2$.

the photon sphere

Light can orbit — unstably — at $r = 3r_g$. A ray from infinity with $b \lt b_c = 3\sqrt3\,r_g$ is captured; with $b \gt b_c$ it escapes.

$W$ against $r = 1/u$, computed, with three rays' levels $1/b^2$. A ray moves where its level is above $W$, and turns where they meet. Above the peak, at $1/27r_g^2$, nothing stops it.

The null radial equation. With $\sigma = 0$, $\dot r^2 = E^2/c^2 - (1 - 2r_g/r)L^2/r^2$. Divide by $L^2$ and use $\dot r = -Lu'$ from worked example 1: $u'^2 = E^2/(c^2L^2) - u^2(1 - 2r_gu)$, and $E^2/(c^2L^2) = 1/b^2$. Far away, $u \to 0$ and $u'^2 \to 1/b^2$; the straight line $u = \sin\varphi/b$ of flat space has exactly this, so $b$ is the perpendicular distance of the incoming line.

Capture, proved. $u'^2 \ge 0$ forces $W(u) \le 1/b^2$ along the ray. $W$ rises from 0 at $u = 0$ to its maximum $1/27r_g^2$ at $u = 1/3r_g$ and falls to zero at the horizon $u = 1/2r_g$. If $1/b^2 \gt 1/27r_g^2$, i.e. $b \lt 3\sqrt3\,r_g$, no turning point exists on the way in and the ray crosses $r = 3r_g$ and reaches the horizon. If $b \gt 3\sqrt3\,r_g$ it turns at the first root of $W(u) = 1/b^2$, which is at $r \gt 3r_g$, and escapes. The circular orbit at the maximum of $W$ is unstable by the same argument as for $r_-$. Note $b_c \ne 3r_g$: the ray that just grazes the photon sphere came in from further out, because it was bent on the way.

Unit 21's scaling, $p = L^2/GM$ and $w = pu$:

$$w'' + w = 1 + \varepsilon w^2,\qquad \varepsilon = \frac{3r_g}{p}.$$

Unit 21's method: give the radial oscillation its own frequency, $\psi = \nu\varphi$ with $\nu = 1 + \varepsilon\nu_1 + \dots$, and demand $w$ be $2\pi$-periodic in $\psi$.

Order 1: $w_0 = 1 + e\cos\psi$, unit 08's ellipse.

Order $\varepsilon$: $\ddot w_1 + w_1 = w_0^2 - 2\nu_1\ddot w_0$, whose $\cos\psi$ coefficient is $2e + 2\nu_1e$.

A periodic $w_1$ exists only if that resonant coefficient vanishes (unit 21's solvability condition). So $\nu_1 = -1$.

the perihelion advance $$\nu = 1 - \varepsilon,\qquad \Delta\varpi = \frac{2\pi}{\nu} - 2\pi \approx 2\pi\varepsilon$$ $$\Delta\varpi = \frac{6\pi GM}{c^2a(1 - e^2)}\ \text{per orbit}$$

The order-$\varepsilon$ equation in full. Substitute $w = w_0 + \varepsilon w_1$ and $\nu^2 = 1 + 2\varepsilon\nu_1 + O(\varepsilon^2)$; a dot here is $\mathrm d/\mathrm d\psi$. Order $\varepsilon$: $\ddot w_1 + 2\nu_1\ddot w_0 + w_1 = w_0^2$. Now $w_0^2 = 1 + \tfrac12e^2 + 2e\cos\psi + \tfrac12e^2\cos2\psi$ and $-2\nu_1\ddot w_0 = 2\nu_1e\cos\psi$. So the $\cos\psi$ coefficient on the right is $2e + 2\nu_1e$. The solvability condition (unit 21, "$u'' + u = f$ has a $2\pi$-periodic solution iff $f$ is orthogonal to $\cos$ and $\sin$") forces $\nu_1 = -1$ whenever $e \ne 0$.

Why the secular term must not be removed

Expand naively instead, with $w_0 = 1 + e\cos\varphi$. The resonant forcing $2e\cos\varphi$ puts $e\,\varphi\sin\varphi$ into $w_1$: unit 21's secular term. And

$$\begin{aligned}&1 + e\cos\varphi + \varepsilon e\varphi\sin\varphi\\ &\quad= 1 + e\cos\big((1 - \varepsilon)\varphi\big) + O(\varepsilon^2\varphi^2).\end{aligned}$$

The secular term is the frequency shift, written as the first term of a Taylor series.

unit 21's warning, in its intended use

Deleting $\varepsilon e\varphi\sin\varphi$ "because secular terms are unphysical" predicts zero precession, and is inconsistent: at order $\varepsilon$ there is no $2\pi$-periodic solution when $e \ne 0$. The equation forbids the orbit to close after one turn. Poincaré–Lindstedt does not remove the term; it rewrites it as $\nu - 1$.

The identity. $\cos((1 - \varepsilon)\varphi) = \cos\varphi\cos\varepsilon\varphi + \sin\varphi\sin\varepsilon\varphi = \cos\varphi + \varepsilon\varphi\sin\varphi + O(\varepsilon^2\varphi^2)$. The naive series is the Taylor expansion in $\varepsilon$ at fixed $\varphi$ of a function whose $\varepsilon$-dependence is through $\varepsilon\varphi$; it is accurate while $\varepsilon\varphi$ is small and useless after.

Why this matters here more than for Duffing. In unit 21's Duffing oscillator the secular term encoded a frequency shift whose accumulated phase nobody measures. Here the accumulated phase is the observable: astronomers have measured the longitude of Mercury's perihelion for three centuries. The distinction is between removing a non-uniformity, which is good mathematics, and removing a physical effect, which is an error. This is the clause in the checkpoint.

The numbers. $\varepsilon = 7.9875\times10^{-8}$, so $1/\varepsilon = 1.252\times10^7$ rad, that is $1.99\times10^6$ orbits of $87.969$ days, $4.80\times10^5$ years. One century is $415.2$ orbits, $\varphi = 2609$ rad, $\varepsilon\varphi = 2.08\times10^{-4}$. The neglected term, $\tfrac12 e(\varepsilon\varphi)^2\cos\varphi$, is at most $4.5\times10^{-9}$ in $w$: a part in $10^4$ of the secular term, whose ratio to it is $\tfrac12\varepsilon\varphi$.

Bending by general relativity

The null orbit equation (worked example 1, $\sigma = 0$):

$$u'' + u = 3r_g\,u^2 .$$

No $GM/L^2$ term: light has no rest mass for Newton's $-GM/r$ to act on. All the bending is in the new term.

Order 0: $u_0 = \sin\varphi/b$, a line at distance $b$, coming in at $\varphi = 0$, leaving at $\varphi = \pi$.

Order $r_g$: $u_1'' + u_1 = 3r_g\sin^2\varphi/b^2 = \tfrac{3r_g}{2b^2}(1 - \cos2\varphi)$, solved by

$$u_1 = \frac{r_g}{b^2}\big(1 + \cos^2\varphi\big).$$

The ray $u_0 + u_1$ drawn with $r_g/b = 0.12$, fifty thousand times the Sun's, so the bend can be seen. Far away it runs along two straight lines, dashed: the asymptotes, each at $\delta$ to the undeflected line $u_0$. The next slide reads $\delta$ off $u_1$.

Solving for $u_1$. The constant part $3r_g/2b^2$ is solved by the constant $3r_g/2b^2$. The part $-\tfrac{3r_g}{2b^2}\cos2\varphi$ is solved by $C\cos2\varphi$ with $C(1 - 4) = -\tfrac{3r_g}{2b^2}$, so $C = r_g/2b^2$. Together: $\tfrac{r_g}{2b^2}(3 + \cos2\varphi) = \tfrac{r_g}{b^2}(1 + \cos^2\varphi)$. Check: $\big(\cos^2\varphi\big)'' = -2\cos2\varphi$, so $u_1'' + u_1 = \tfrac{r_g}{b^2}(1 + \cos^2\varphi - 2\cos2\varphi) = \tfrac{r_g}{b^2}(1 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cos2\varphi - 2\cos2\varphi) = \tfrac{3r_g}{2b^2}(1 - \cos2\varphi)$ ✓.

Why no homogeneous piece is added. A homogeneous solution $A\cos\varphi + B\sin\varphi$ would either break the symmetry about closest approach at $\varphi = \pi/2$ ($A$) or change the slope at infinity ($B$). The ray is symmetric about its point of closest approach — the metric is time-reversal invariant and spherically symmetric — so $A = 0$. And the first integral $u'^2 = 1/b^2 - u^2 + 2r_gu^3$ fixes $u' = 1/b$ where $u = 0$, which $u_0$ already has and $B\sin\varphi$ would spoil at order $r_g$, so $B = 0$. The distance of closest approach, at $\varphi = \pi/2$, is $1/u = b - r_g + O(r_g^2/b)$: it differs from $b$ by $r_g$, which moves the deflection only at second order.

What to look for. The order-0 ray $u_0 = \sin\varphi/b$ is the horizontal line at height $b$; $u_1$ is everywhere positive, so it raises $u$, lowers $r$ and pulls the ray towards the mass all along. Where $u_0$ alone would put the ends of the ray at $\varphi = 0$ and $\pi$, the corrected $u$ vanishes a little beyond them, at $\varphi = -\delta$ and $\pi + \delta$ — which is what turns the ends.

Full proof

From $\mathrm ds^2 = 0$: $c^2\mathrm dt^2 = \frac{1 + 2\gamma r_g/r}{1 - 2r_g/r}|\mathrm d\vec x|^2$, and the square root of the ratio is $1 + (1 + \gamma)r_g/r + O(r_g^2)$. The path differs from the straight line at order $r_g$, but by Fermat's principle — the coordinate travel time is stationary with respect to the path, proved for this metric below — a first-order change of path changes the time only at second order. So integrate along the line. One way: $\Delta t_{\rm one} = (1 + \gamma)\frac{r_g}{c}\int_{-x_1}^{x_2}\frac{\mathrm dx}{\sqrt{x^2 + b^2}} = (1 + \gamma)\frac{r_g}{c}\ln\frac{(x_2 + r_2)(x_1 + r_1)}{b^2}$, using $\operatorname{arsinh}(x/b) = \ln\big((x + \sqrt{x^2 + b^2})/b\big)$. For $b \ll r_i$, $x_i \approx r_i$ and the argument is $4r_1r_2/b^2$. Round trip doubles it, and $r_g/c = GM/c^3$. $\square$

Fermat's principle for this metric, proved. Write the metric as $-Ac^2\mathrm dt^2 + B|\mathrm d\vec x|^2$ and put $n = \sqrt{B/A}$. A null curve has $c\,\mathrm dt = n|\mathrm d\vec x|$, so its coordinate travel time is $T = \frac1c\int n\,|\dot{\vec x}|\,\mathrm d\lambda$, a functional of the spatial path alone: unit 11's optical functional with refractive index $n$. Its Euler–Lagrange equations are $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}\big(n\dot x_i/|\dot{\vec x}|\big) = |\dot{\vec x}|\,\partial_in$. A null geodesic satisfies them. With $E = Ac^2\dot t$ and the null condition $c\dot t = n|\dot{\vec x}|$, $n\dot x_i/|\dot{\vec x}| = B\dot x_i/(Ac\dot t) = (c/E)\,B\dot x_i$. The geodesic Lagrangian's $x^i$-equation (unit 32) is $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\lambda}(B\dot x_i) = \tfrac12\big(-c^2\dot t^2\,\partial_iA + |\dot{\vec x}|^2\partial_iB\big)$; multiply by the constant $c/E$ and use $B|\dot{\vec x}|^2 = Ac^2\dot t^2$ to get $\tfrac12c\dot t\,(\partial_iB/B - \partial_iA/A)$, which is $|\dot{\vec x}|\,\partial_in$ because $\partial_in = \tfrac n2(\partial_iB/B - \partial_iA/A)$ and $n|\dot{\vec x}| = c\dot t$. So the ray is a stationary point of $T$. $\square$

History. Irwin Shapiro proposed the test in 1964 (Phys. Rev. Lett. 13, 789), noting that it was the first new test of general relativity suggested since 1915. The first measurements, with radar echoes from Mercury and Venus using the Haystack and Arecibo antennas, came in 1967–1971 and agreed with general relativity to within about 5%. Radar cannot actually penetrate to $b = R_\odot$ at those frequencies because of the solar corona's plasma, which adds its own frequency-dependent delay; the experiments used larger $b$ and fitted the logarithm. The Viking landers on Mars (1976–77) reached $0.1\%$.

What was measured was the rate of change of the delay — a fractional frequency shift of order $10^{-10}$ — not the delay itself.

Where the geometry comes from. The distances were computed from JPL ephemerides for 21 June 2002: Earth $1.0162$ au, Cassini $7.4216$ au from the Sun. The minimum impact parameter $1.6R_\odot$ is the figure given in the paper, B. Bertotti, L. Iess and P. Tortora, Nature 425, 374 (2003).

Why a frequency. The Cassini experiment tracked the Doppler shift of a stable radio signal. The delay enters as $\mathrm d(\Delta t)/\mathrm dt$: as the Earth moves, $b$ changes by about three solar radii a day, and the delay's logarithm changes with it, giving a fractional frequency shift $y = \frac{\mathrm d\Delta t}{\mathrm dt} = -\frac{8GM}{c^3}\frac{\dot b}{b}$ (for $\gamma = 1$). P4's last part estimates its peak at about $5\times10^{-10}$. Frequency stability of $10^{-14}$ over the relevant times is what made the $10^{-5}$ level reachable.

Why the plasma matters. The solar corona delays radio waves by an amount proportional to $\nu^{-2}$; near the Sun at X band it is far larger than the relativistic effect. Two frequencies allow the $\nu^{-2}$ piece to be solved for and removed. Earlier missions without Ka band were limited by exactly this, at about $10^{-3}$.

Unit 35's statement, made good: Brans–Dicke theory predicts $\gamma = (1 + \omega)/(2 + \omega)$, so $1 - \gamma = 1/(2 + \omega) \gt 0$: Brans–Dicke always has $\gamma \lt 1$. Cassini's two-standard-error lower end is $\gamma - 1 \gt 2.1\times10^{-5} - 4.6\times10^{-5} = -2.5\times10^{-5}$, and $1/(2 + \omega) \lt 2.5\times10^{-5}$ requires $\omega \gt 4.0\times10^4$.

The redshift, closed exactly

A static observer at $r$ has four-velocity $U = \partial_t/\sqrt{A(r)}$, with $A = 1 - 2r_g/r$, so that $g(U, U) = -c^2$ (unit 27). A photon with wavevector $k$ has conserved $E_k = -g(\partial_t, k)$ (Killing, again). The frequency $\nu$ the observer measures — a light frequency now, not the apsidal $\nu$ of sections 2 and 3 — is proportional to $-g(k, U)$:

$$\nu(r) \propto -g(k, U) = \frac{E_k}{\sqrt{A(r)}} .$$
the exact gravitational redshift $$\frac{\nu_{\rm received}}{\nu_{\rm emitted}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - 2r_g/r_{\rm e}}{1 - 2r_g/r_{\rm r}}}$$

Light from a static emitter at $r_{\rm e}$ to a receiver far away, computed. Red: the exact ratio, which reaches zero at the horizon. Blue: unit 30's first-order formula, which still gives one half there. Far out they agree, and at the Sun's surface, $r/r_g = 4.7\times10^5$, they agree to $10^{-12}$.

The frequency an observer measures. For a photon of wavevector $k$ and an observer of four-velocity $U$ with $g(U, U) = -c^2$, the measured frequency is a fixed constant times $-g(k, U)$: unit 27's statement that energy is the time component of the four-momentum in the observer's rest frame, written invariantly. Here $g(\partial_t, \partial_t) = -Ac^2$, so $U = \partial_t/\sqrt A$ has $g(U, U) = -c^2$, and $-g(k, U) = -g(k, \partial_t)/\sqrt A = E_k/\sqrt A$. In the ratio of two such measurements, at emitter and receiver, the constant and $E_k$ cancel. $\square$

Limits. $r_{\rm e} = r_{\rm r}$: no shift ✓. $r_{\rm e} \to 2r_g$: infinite redshift — light from the horizon never arrives, as unit 36 found ✓. First order: expand each square root.

What to look for. The two curves part only where $r_g/r_{\rm e}$ is not small: at $r_{\rm e} = 3r_g$ the exact ratio is $0.577$ and the first-order one $0.667$. The exact one goes to zero at the horizon like $\sqrt{1 - 2r_g/r_{\rm e}}$, with a vertical tangent: a clock held just outside the horizon is seen from far away to tick arbitrarily slowly.

Why the gap matters.
