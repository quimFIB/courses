38 · linearise

A metric near Minkowski's

definition · linearisation

Let $g(\varepsilon)$ be a family of metrics on $\mathbb R^4$, smooth in $\varepsilon$, with $g(0) = \eta$. The metric perturbation is $$h = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0} g(\varepsilon),$$ a symmetric $(0,2)$-tensor field. The linearisation of any quantity $F[g]$ is $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varepsilon}F[g(\varepsilon)]\big|_0$ — its derivative at $\eta$ in the direction $h$.

Physics. Unit 35's weak field: $g = \eta + h$ with $|h_{\mu\nu}|\ll1$ in some chart, keeping terms linear in $h$ and dropping the rest. The definition says precisely what "dropping" means: it is taking a derivative.

lemma · the linear pieces

To first order, $g^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\nu} - h^{\mu\nu}$, and $$\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\eta^{\lambda\rho}\big(\partial_\mu h_{\rho\nu} + \partial_\nu h_{\rho\mu} - \partial_\rho h_{\mu\nu}\big).$$

proof

$(\eta^{\mu\lambda} - h^{\mu\lambda})(\eta_{\lambda\nu} + h_{\lambda\nu}) = \delta^\mu_\nu + O(h^2)$. In unit 32's formula for $\Gamma$ every term is $g^{-1}\partial g$; $\partial\eta = 0$, so only $\eta^{-1}\partial h$ survives at first order. $\square$

Check. $h$ is dimensionless, $[\Gamma] = \mathrm{m^{-1}}$ ✓. Static, with $h_{00} = -2\Phi/c^2$: $\Gamma^i_{00} = -\tfrac12\partial_ih_{00} = \partial_i\Phi/c^2$, unit 32's Newtonian limit ✓.

Why define it as a derivative. "Small $h$" is not a coordinate-free statement: the Minkowski metric in rotating coordinates has components that grow without bound, and a perfectly curved metric can be made to look like $\eta$ at a point. What is invariant is the family $g(\varepsilon)$ and its derivative at $\varepsilon = 0$. Everything this unit calls "first order" is a derivative in $\varepsilon$, and "$h$ is small" is shorthand for "we are computing that derivative". This is also why the linear theory is exact as a statement about tangent vectors to the space of solutions, even when no actual solution is exactly linear.

Indices. At first order an index on $h$ is raised with $\eta$, not $g$: the difference is $O(h^2)$. So $h^{\mu\nu} = \eta^{\mu\alpha}\eta^{\nu\beta}h_{\alpha\beta}$ and $h = \eta^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}$ is the trace. With the signature $(-,+,+,+)$, $h = -h_{00} + h_{11} + h_{22} + h_{33}$.

The $\Gamma\Gamma$ terms. Each Christoffel symbol is $O(\varepsilon)$, so any product of two is $O(\varepsilon^2)$ and has zero derivative at $\varepsilon = 0$. That is what makes the curvature linear in $h$ on the next slide: of unit 33's $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma\Gamma - \Gamma\Gamma$ only the two derivative terms survive.

The linearised field equations

definition · the trace-reversed perturbation

$\bar h_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h$. Its trace is $\bar h = -h$, and in four dimensions the operation is an involution: $\bar{\bar h}_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu}$.

theorem · the linearised Einstein tensor $$\begin{aligned}G^{(1)}_{\mu\nu} = -\tfrac12\Big(&\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} + \eta_{\mu\nu}\partial^\alpha\partial^\beta\bar h_{\alpha\beta}\\ &- \partial_\mu\partial^\alpha\bar h_{\nu\alpha} - \partial_\nu\partial^\alpha\bar h_{\mu\alpha}\Big)\end{aligned}$$

with $\Box = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu = -c^{-2}\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$. The field equations at first order are $G^{(1)}_{\mu\nu} = \dfrac{8\pi G}{c^4}T_{\mu\nu}$.

proof: linearise Ricci from the previous slide's $\Gamma$, contract, substitute — Full proof in the notes (N)

Why trace-reverse. In terms of $h$ the operator has six terms; in terms of $\bar h$ it has four, and three of them carry a divergence $\partial^\alpha\bar h_{\alpha\beta}$. The next section's gauge choice makes that divergence zero.

checks

Dimensions. $[\partial\partial\bar h] = \mathrm{m^{-2}} = [8\pi GT/c^4]$ ✓ (unit 35).

Static limit. $\bar h_{00} = -4\Phi/c^2$, others zero: the $00$ equation is $-\tfrac12\nabla^2\bar h_{00} = 8\pi G\rho/c^2$, i.e. $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$ — Poisson ✓.

Full proof

Step 1, Ricci. With unit 33's $R_{\mu\nu} = R^\lambda{}_{\mu\lambda\nu}$ and the $\Gamma\Gamma$ terms gone, $R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\lambda\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\lambda_{\lambda\mu}$. Insert the previous slide's $\Gamma$. The first term gives $\tfrac12(\partial_\lambda\partial_\nu h^\lambda{}_\mu + \partial_\lambda\partial_\mu h^\lambda{}_\nu - \Box h_{\mu\nu})$; for the second, $\Gamma^\lambda_{\lambda\mu} = \tfrac12\partial_\mu h$ because the other two terms cancel under the contraction. So $$R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\big(\partial_\lambda\partial_\mu h^\lambda{}_\nu + \partial_\lambda\partial_\nu h^\lambda{}_\mu - \Box h_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\mu\partial_\nu h\big).$$ Step 2, the scalar. $R^{(1)} = \eta^{\mu\nu}R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\lambda\partial_\mu h^{\lambda\mu} - \Box h$. Step 3, Einstein. $G^{(1)}_{\mu\nu} = R^{(1)}_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}R^{(1)}$ (the correction from $g_{\mu\nu}R$ versus $\eta_{\mu\nu}R$ is second order, since $R$ itself is first order). Step 4, substitute $h_{\mu\nu} = \bar h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h$ (the involution) and $h = -\bar h$. The terms in $\bar h$ with no divergence cancel: $-\tfrac12\Box h_{\mu\nu} = -\tfrac12\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} + \tfrac14\eta_{\mu\nu}\Box\bar h$, the $-\tfrac12\partial_\mu\partial_\nu h$ term is $+\tfrac12\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\bar h$ and cancels against the $-\tfrac12\partial_\mu\partial_\nu\bar h$ coming from the two $\partial_\lambda\partial h^\lambda$ terms, and the $\eta_{\mu\nu}\Box\bar h$ pieces cancel between Ricci and $-\tfrac12\eta R$. What is left is the slide's four terms. $\square$ The author checked the identity in full generality, symbolically, for arbitrary $h_{\mu\nu}(x)$; do it by hand once, with every index written out, because the cancellations are the content — problem P1 does the trace-reversal algebra they turn on.

The involution. $\bar h = \eta^{\mu\nu}(h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h) = h - 2h = -h$, using $\eta^{\mu\nu}\eta_{\mu\nu} = 4$. Then $\bar{\bar h}_{\mu\nu} = \bar h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}\bar h = h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h + \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h = h_{\mu\nu}$. In $n$ dimensions the trace is multiplied by $1 - n/2$ and the involution fails unless $n = 4$ (problem P1).

The name. $\bar h$ is related to $h$ exactly as $G_{\mu\nu}$ is related to $R_{\mu\nu}$: subtract half the trace times the metric. Unit 35's trace-reversed field equation is the same move.

Gauge: a diffeomorphism, infinitesimally

theorem · linearised gauge transformation

Let $\varphi_\varepsilon$ be the flow of a vector field $\xi$. Then $$\varphi_\varepsilon^*\big(g(\varepsilon)\big) = \eta + \varepsilon\big(h + \mathcal L_\xi\eta\big) + O(\varepsilon^2),$$ $$(\mathcal L_\xi\eta)_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu .$$ So $h$ and $h + \partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu$ are two descriptions of one spacetime.

proof

Differentiate $\varphi_\varepsilon^*g(\varepsilon)$ at $0$ by the product rule: $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\varepsilon}\varphi^*_\varepsilon\eta + \varphi_0^*h = \mathcal L_\xi\eta + h$ (unit 31's definition of $\mathcal L$). Unit 31's coordinate formula, with $\partial\eta = 0$, gives $\eta_{\lambda\nu}\partial_\mu\xi^\lambda + \eta_{\mu\lambda}\partial_\nu\xi^\lambda$. $\square$

Why it is the same spacetime. $\varphi_\varepsilon$ is an isometry from $(\mathbb R^4, \varphi_\varepsilon^*g)$ to $(\mathbb R^4, g)$. Every length, angle, geodesic and curvature invariant of one is the same number for the other. Unit 35 counted these four functions as the four that "change no physics".

For the trace-reversed field, with $\partial\cdot\xi = \partial^\lambda\xi_\lambda$:

$$\bar h_{\mu\nu}\ \to\ \bar h_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu - \eta_{\mu\nu}\,\partial\cdot\xi,$$ $$\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu}\ \to\ \partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu} + \Box\xi_\nu .$$
a sign you will meet

Unit 35 wrote $h\to h - \partial\xi - \partial\xi$: a coordinate change $x\to x + \xi$ rather than a flow. It is the same set of transformations, with $\xi\to-\xi$.

The product rule, carefully. $F(\varepsilon) = \varphi_\varepsilon^*(g(\varepsilon))$ is a function of $\varepsilon$ in which $\varepsilon$ appears twice. Write $F(\varepsilon) = \varphi_\varepsilon^*\eta + \varphi_\varepsilon^*(g(\varepsilon) - \eta)$. The first term's derivative at $0$ is $\mathcal L_\xi\eta$ by unit 31's definition. The second is $\varphi_\varepsilon^*$ applied to something $O(\varepsilon)$; its derivative at $0$ is $\varphi_0^*h = h$, since $\varphi_0$ is the identity and any correction is $O(\varepsilon^2)$.

The trace-reversed version. The change in $h$ is $\delta h_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\xi_\mu$, whose trace is $2\partial\cdot\xi$. So $\delta\bar h_{\mu\nu} = \delta h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}\cdot2\partial\cdot\xi$. Take $\partial^\mu$: $\Box\xi_\nu + \partial_\nu\partial\cdot\xi - \partial_\nu\partial\cdot\xi = \Box\xi_\nu$.

History. Einstein found the waves in 1916 and made an error in the radiated power; his 1918 paper corrected it and gave the quadrupole formula of section 3 (itself off by a factor of 2, fixed later by Eddington). In 1922 Eddington also showed that some of the "waves" in Einstein's 1916 treatment were pure coordinate effects that could be made to travel at any speed — they "propagate with the speed of thought", in his phrase. The theorem on this slide is how to tell the difference: a perturbation of the form $\mathcal L_\xi\eta$ is a coordinate effect, whatever it looks like. Section 2 finds what is left when all of those are removed.

Lorenz gauge, and the wave equation

lemma · Lorenz gauge can always be reached

Given $h$, solve $\Box\xi_\nu = -\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu}$ — four wave equations, solvable by unit 24's retarded solution. The new $\bar h$ has $\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$, the Lorenz gauge.

theorem · the linearised field equation

In Lorenz gauge,

$$\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{16\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}.$$

Proof. Three of the four terms of $G^{(1)}_{\mu\nu}$ carry $\partial^\alpha\bar h_{\alpha\beta} = 0$, leaving $-\tfrac12\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi GT_{\mu\nu}/c^4$. $\square$

unit 24's box has the other sign

Unit 24 wrote $\Box_{24} = c^{-2}\partial_t^2 - \nabla^2 = -\Box$, so that its retarded solution solves $\Box_{24}u = f$. In that convention the equation above is

$$\Box_{24}\,\bar h_{\mu\nu} = \frac{16\pi G}{c^4}\,T_{\mu\nu}.$$

Section 3 uses this form.

checks

Consistency. $\partial^\mu$ of the left side is $\Box\,\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$, so the source must obey $\partial^\mu T_{\mu\nu} = 0$ — the table's last row ✓.

Vacuum. $\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$: ten copies of unit 35's wave equation, every one travelling at $c$ ✓.

Why the gauge exists. The lemma asks for a solution of $\Box\xi = s$ with a given source $s = -\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu}$. Unit 24's retarded-solution theorem supplies one whenever $s$ is $C^2$ and vanishes before some time and outside a bounded set; for radiation from an isolated source that is the case, and for more general $h$ one adds a homogeneous solution. Unit 35 had to cite this step; this unit's prerequisites prove it.

Why "Lorenz". Ludvig Lorenz, Danish, 1867 — not Hendrik Lorentz, whose name is on the transformations. The gauge is often misattributed; both are in this course.

The running example, so far. The binary's field obeys this equation with $T_{\mu\nu}$ the stars' energy and momentum. Section 3 solves it. Before that, section 2 asks what the vacuum solutions are — what arrives at a detector far from the binary.

Counting to two, honestly

A vacuum plane wave in Lorenz gauge: $\bar h_{\mu\nu} = e_{\mu\nu}\cos(k_\lambda x^\lambda)$ with $e$ a constant symmetric matrix. $\Box\bar h = 0$ forces $k$ null (unit 35). Work in $V = \mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb R^4)^*$, $\dim V = 10$.

theorem · the degree-of-freedom count

Fix a null $k\ne0$. Let

$L = \{e\in V : k^\mu e_{\mu\nu} = 0\}$, the Lorenz-gauge amplitudes;
$P = \{k_\mu\epsilon_\nu + k_\nu\epsilon_\mu - \eta_{\mu\nu}(k\cdot\epsilon) : \epsilon\in\mathbb R^4\}$, the pure-gauge ones.

Then $P\subset L$, $\ \dim L = 6$, $\ \dim P = 4$, and the physical amplitudes $L/P$ form a space of dimension $\mathbf 2$.

proof, in outline

$\dim L = 6$: $e\mapsto k^\mu e_{\mu\nu}$ maps $V$ onto $\mathbb R^4$; $L$ is its kernel.
$P\subset L$: $k^\mu(k_\mu\epsilon_\nu + k_\nu\epsilon_\mu - \eta_{\mu\nu}k\cdot\epsilon) = 0 + k_\nu(k\cdot\epsilon) - k_\nu(k\cdot\epsilon) = 0$, using $k\cdot k = 0$.
$\dim P = 4$: $\epsilon\mapsto$ that tensor is injective. Details in the notes.

the count, in words

$10$ components $-\ 4$ Lorenz conditions $-\ 4$ residual gauge functions $= 2$. The residual four are the $\xi$ with $\Box\xi = 0$: a plane-wave $\xi = \epsilon\sin(k\cdot x)$ shifts $e$ by exactly an element of $P$.

Full proof

Surjectivity. Since $k\ne0$ pick a vector $a$ with $k\cdot a = 1$. Given any $v\in\mathbb R^4$ put $S_{\mu\nu} = a_\mu v_\nu + v_\mu a_\nu - (k\cdot v)\,a_\mu a_\nu$, which is symmetric. Then $k^\mu S_{\mu\nu} = v_\nu + (k\cdot v)a_\nu - (k\cdot v)a_\nu = v_\nu$. So the map is onto, and by rank–nullity $\dim L = 10 - 4 = 6$.

Injectivity of $\epsilon\mapsto p(\epsilon)$. Suppose $k_\mu\epsilon_\nu + k_\nu\epsilon_\mu - \eta_{\mu\nu}(k\cdot\epsilon) = 0$. Its trace is $2k\cdot\epsilon - 4k\cdot\epsilon = -2k\cdot\epsilon$, so $k\cdot\epsilon = 0$ and $k_\mu\epsilon_\nu + k_\nu\epsilon_\mu = 0$ for all $\mu,\nu$. Choose $\mu$ with $k_\mu\ne0$. Setting $\nu = \mu$ gives $\epsilon_\mu = 0$; then for any $\nu$, $k_\mu\epsilon_\nu = -k_\nu\epsilon_\mu = 0$ gives $\epsilon_\nu = 0$. So $\epsilon = 0$, the map is injective, and $\dim P = 4$.

The residual gauge, concretely. With $\xi_\mu = \epsilon_\mu\sin(k\cdot x)$, $\partial_\mu\xi_\nu = k_\mu\epsilon_\nu\cos(k\cdot x)$, so the transformation of the previous section shifts $\bar h$ by $p(\epsilon)\cos(k\cdot x)$. And $\Box\xi = -(k\cdot k)\,\xi = 0$, so it preserves the Lorenz condition, as it must, since $P\subset L$.

Against unit 35's count. Unit 35 wrote $10 - 4 - 4$ with the second four being constraint equations. Here the second four are residual gauge and the first four are the Lorenz conditions. These are two bookkeepings of one fact: in Lorenz gauge the four Lorenz conditions are what the constraints become, and the gauge has been spent in two instalments — four functions to reach Lorenz gauge, four more to exhaust it. Either way two remain. The electromagnetic count is $4 - 1 - 1 = 2$ by the same two routes.

One representative per class: TT gauge

Take the wave along $z$: $k_\mu = \frac\omega c(-1,0,0,1)$. Call $e\in L$ transverse-traceless gauge (TT) if $e_{0\mu} = 0$ and $e^\mu{}_\mu = 0$. Lorenz then forces $e_{3\mu} = 0$, and

$$e = \begin{pmatrix}0&0&0&0\\0&h_+&h_\times&0\\0&h_\times&-h_+&0\\0&0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.$$
theorem

$L = P\oplus TT$. Every Lorenz-gauge wave is gauge-equivalent to exactly one TT wave, and $$h_+ = \tfrac12(e_{11} - e_{22}),\qquad h_\times = e_{12}$$ are gauge invariant on $L$.

proof

For $p(\epsilon)\in P$: $p_{11} = p_{22} = -k\cdot\epsilon$ and $p_{12} = 0$, so $h_\pm$ are unchanged by gauge. If $p(\epsilon)\in TT$ then $p_{11} = -p_{22}$ forces $k\cdot\epsilon = 0$, and $p_{0\mu} = k_0\epsilon_\mu + k_\mu\epsilon_0 = 0$ forces $\epsilon = 0$. So $P\cap TT = 0$; dimensions $4 + 2 = 6 = \dim L$. $\square$

These are the plus and cross polarisations. In TT gauge $h = 0$, so $\bar h_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu}$ and the bar is dropped.

Check. Two free numbers, as counted ✓. Both components lie in the plane $\perp$ to the direction of travel: the wave is transverse, like light.

Lorenz forces $e_{3\mu} = 0$. With $k^\mu = \frac\omega c(1,0,0,1)$ (index raised with $\eta$), the Lorenz condition $k^\mu e_{\mu\nu} = 0$ reads $e_{0\nu} + e_{3\nu} = 0$. If $e_{0\nu} = 0$ then $e_{3\nu} = 0$. The remaining spatial block is $e_{11}, e_{12}, e_{22}$, and tracelessness $e_{11} + e_{22} = 0$ leaves two.

The proof's middle step, written out. $p_{0\mu} = k_0\epsilon_\mu + k_\mu\epsilon_0 - \eta_{0\mu}(k\cdot\epsilon)$ and $k\cdot\epsilon = 0$ by then. For $\mu = 0$: $2k_0\epsilon_0 = 0$, so $\epsilon_0 = 0$. For $\mu = 1, 2$: $k_0\epsilon_\mu = 0$. For $\mu = 3$: $k_0\epsilon_3 + k_3\epsilon_0 = k_0\epsilon_3 = 0$. Since $k_0 = -\omega/c\ne0$, all four vanish.

How to find the TT representative in practice. Problem P3 does it for a specific Lorenz-gauge amplitude, solving for $\epsilon$ component by component. The answer depends only on the gauge-invariant pair $(h_+, h_\times)$, which is the theorem.

The general function. By linearity and Fourier superposition (unit 20), a vacuum wave travelling along $z$ with any profile is $h_+(t - z/c)$ and $h_\times(t - z/c)$ in the two slots: two arbitrary functions of one variable, which is unit 35's "two free functions".

The transverse-traceless projector

definition · the TT projector

For a unit vector $n\in\mathbb R^3$ let $P = 1 - nn^{\mathsf T}$, the orthogonal projector onto $n^\perp$. On symmetric $3\times3$ matrices define $$\Lambda(S) = PSP - \tfrac12P\,\mathrm{tr}(PS),$$ in components $\Lambda_{ij,kl}S_{kl}$ with $\Lambda_{ij,kl} = P_{ik}P_{jl} - \tfrac12P_{ij}P_{kl}$.

lemma

$\Lambda(S)$ is symmetric, transverse ($\Lambda(S)n = 0$) and traceless; $\Lambda^2 = \Lambda$; $\Lambda$ is self-adjoint for $\langle A,B\rangle = \mathrm{tr}(AB)$; and $\Lambda(\mathbb 1) = 0$.

proof

$Pn = 0$ gives transversality. $\mathrm{tr}P = 2$ and $P^2 = P$ give $\mathrm{tr}\,\Lambda(S) = \mathrm{tr}(PS) - \mathrm{tr}(PS) = 0$. For a transverse traceless $A$, $PAP = A$ and $\mathrm{tr}(PA) = 0$, so $\Lambda(A) = A$: hence $\Lambda^2 = \Lambda$. $\Lambda(\mathbb 1) = P - \tfrac12P\cdot2 = 0$. Self-adjointness: $\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda(S)T)$ is symmetric in $S, T$ by cyclicity. $\square$

For $n = \hat z$: $\Lambda(S)$ has $\tfrac12(S_{11} - S_{22})$ and $S_{12}$ in the $h_+$, $h_\times$ slots — the gauge invariants of the last slide. So for a wave along $n$, $$h^{\rm TT}_{ij} = \Lambda_{ij,kl}\,\bar h_{kl} = \Lambda_{ij,kl}\,h_{kl}.$$

What it is for. Section 3 computes $\bar h_{ij}$ in Lorenz gauge from a source. That is not the TT field, but the TT field is its gauge-invariant part, and the projector extracts it without solving for any $\epsilon$. Far from the source the wave is locally a plane wave along $n = \vec x/r$, which is where the projector is applied.

Why $\Lambda h = \Lambda\bar h$. They differ by a multiple of $\delta_{ij}$ in the spatial block, and $\Lambda(\mathbb 1) = 0$. So it does not matter which one is projected.

Self-adjointness, in full. $\mathrm{tr}(\Lambda(S)T) = \mathrm{tr}(PSPT) - \tfrac12\mathrm{tr}(PS)\mathrm{tr}(PT)$. The first term equals $\mathrm{tr}(PTPS)$ by cyclicity and $P^{\mathsf T} = P$; the second is visibly symmetric. So $\Lambda$ is an orthogonal projector, and $\langle\Lambda S, \Lambda S\rangle = \langle S,\Lambda S\rangle$ — the identity that section 4 uses to compute the power.

Its image has dimension 2. In a basis with $n = e_3$, a transverse traceless symmetric matrix has its third row and column zero and a traceless symmetric $2\times2$ block: two parameters. The linear algebra of the last two slides and the projector agree.

The mathematics first, as ever. $\Lambda$ is a linear map on $\mathrm{Sym}^2(\mathbb R^3)$, and $\Lambda_{ij,kl}$ are its matrix entries in the standard basis. That it is a projector is a fact about the map; that its entries have the form above is a fact about the basis.

lemma · rest is a geodesic in TT gauge

In TT gauge the worldline $x^i = \text{const}$, $t = \tau$ is a geodesic.

proof

With $u = (c,0,0,0)$ the geodesic equation needs $\Gamma^\lambda_{00} = 0$. By the first slide's lemma, $\Gamma^i_{00} = \tfrac12(2\partial_0h_{i0} - \partial_ih_{00})$ and $\Gamma^0_{00} = -\tfrac12\partial_0h_{00}$; both vanish because $h_{0\mu} = 0$. $\square$

So TT coordinates are carried by the free masses. The coordinates do not move; the wave shows up in proper distance.

proper separation

Masses at the origin and at $L_0n$, with $L_0\ll\lambda$: $$L = \int_0^{L_0}\sqrt{1 + h_{ij}n^in^j}\,\mathrm ds = L_0\big(1 + \tfrac12h_{ij}n^in^j\big).$$ Along $x$ under $h_+$: $L_0(1 + \tfrac12h_+)$. Along $y$: $L_0(1 - \tfrac12h_+)$.

checks

$h\to0$: $L = L_0$ ✓. Along $z$, the direction of travel: $h_{3j} = 0$, no change — transverse ✓. The strain $\Delta L/L_0 = \tfrac12h$ is dimensionless ✓.

The gauge-invariant version. Proper distance is invariant, but the lemma was proved in a gauge. Spin two

theorem · the helicity of the wave

Rotate the transverse axes by $\psi$ about the direction of travel. The amplitudes transform as $$\begin{pmatrix}h_+'\\h_\times'\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\cos2\psi&\sin2\psi\\-\sin2\psi&\cos2\psi\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}h_+\\h_\times\end{pmatrix},$$ a rotation by $2\psi$.

proof

A bilinear form's matrix changes to $R^{\mathsf T}HR$ (unit 16). With $H = \begin{pmatrix}h_+&h_\times\\h_\times&-h_+\end{pmatrix}$ and $R$ the rotation by $\psi$, multiply out and use the double-angle formulas. $\square$

Read it.

  • $\psi = 45^\circ$ swaps $h_+$ and $h_\times$ (up to sign): the two polarisations are one pattern, turned by $45^\circ$ — the figure's two rows.
  • $\psi = 180^\circ$ changes nothing: the pattern is an ellipse, symmetric under a half turn.
  • Light's polarisation vector rotates by $\psi$, returning only after $360^\circ$ (unit 24). An angle multiplied by $s$ is helicity $s$: light has 1, gravity 2.
check

$h_+^2 + h_\times^2$ is invariant, as a rotation must leave it ✓. It is the combination that the energy flux of section 4 turns out to contain.

The multiplication. $R = \begin{pmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{pmatrix}$ with $c = \cos\psi$, $s = \sin\psi$. Then $(R^{\mathsf T}HR)_{11} = h_+(c^2 - s^2) + 2h_\times sc = h_+\cos2\psi + h_\times\sin2\psi$ and $(R^{\mathsf T}HR)_{12} = -2h_+sc + h_\times(c^2 - s^2) = -h_+\sin2\psi + h_\times\cos2\psi$. The $22$ entry is minus the $11$ entry, so the result is again of the TT form. The direction of the rotation (active or passive) only changes the sign of $\psi$.

Circular polarisation. Put $h_+ = h\cos\omega t$ and $h_\times = h\sin\omega t$. By the theorem this is the pure-plus pattern rotated by the angle $\psi$ with $2\psi = \omega t$: the ellipse keeps its shape and turns, at angular speed $\omega/2$. Section 5 shows that a binary seen face-on emits exactly this wave with $\omega = 2\Omega$ — so a ring of masses above the binary's pole has an ellipse that rotates with the binary. Problem P5.

The word "spin". In quantum theory helicity $s$ means the quanta carry angular momentum $s\hbar$ along their motion; the graviton, if it exists as a particle, has $s = 2$. This course is classical and makes no use of that. The classical content is the theorem: the transformation under rotations by $e^{\mp2i\psi}$ on $h_+\pm ih_\times$.

The retarded solution

theorem · the field of a source

Let $T_{\mu\nu}$ be $C^2$, of bounded spatial support, zero before some time, with $\partial^\mu T_{\mu\nu} = 0$. Then

$$\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t,\vec x) = \frac{4G}{c^4}\int\frac{T_{\mu\nu}(t_{\rm r},\vec y)}{|\vec x - \vec y|}\,\mathrm d^3y,$$

$t_{\rm r} = t - |\vec x - \vec y|/c$, solves the linearised equation in Lorenz gauge.

proof

Component by component, $\Box_{24}\bar h_{\mu\nu} = f$ with $f = 16\pi GT_{\mu\nu}/c^4$. Unit 24's retarded solution is $\int f(t_{\rm r})/(4\pi|\vec x - \vec y|)$, and $16\pi/4\pi = 4$. The Lorenz condition holds because $\partial^\mu T_{\mu\nu} = 0$, as unit 24's retarded potentials satisfy theirs because charge is conserved. $\square$

The kernel is unit 24's retarded Green's function $\delta(t - r/c)/4\pi r$, found there from unit 20's contour integral with the poles pushed below the axis. That choice — the field at $\vec x$ now reports the source as it was at the retarded time — is causality, and it is the only input here that is not the field equations.

checks

Static dust. $T_{00} = \rho c^2$: $\bar h_{00} = \frac{4G}{c^2}\int\frac{\rho}{|\vec x - \vec y|} = -\frac{4\Phi}{c^2}$, unit 35's result ✓.

Dimensions. $\frac{G}{c^4}\cdot\mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}\cdot\mathrm{m^2} = \frac{\mathrm N^{-1}\cdot\mathrm J}{\mathrm m} = 1$ ✓.

The Lorenz condition, in full. Write $\bar h_{\mu\nu} = \frac{4G}{c^4}\int T_{\mu\nu}(t - |\vec x - \vec y|/c, \vec y)\,|\vec x - \vec y|^{-1}\mathrm d^3y$ and change variables to $\vec z = \vec x - \vec y$. Then $\partial/\partial x^j$ acts only on the second argument of $T$ (and on nothing else), and $\partial_t$ on the first, so $\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu} = \frac{4G}{c^4}\int(\partial^\mu T_{\mu\nu})(t - |\vec z|/c, \vec x - \vec z)\,|\vec z|^{-1}\mathrm d^3z = 0$. This is unit 24's proof for its potentials, word for word, with $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\vec J = 0$ replaced by $\partial^\mu T_{\mu\nu} = 0$.

Why "zero before some time". Unit 24's theorem assumes it, so that the integral over the past converges and no radiation comes in from the infinite past. A binary that has existed for ever does not literally satisfy it; switch the source on in the distant past and the far-field result below does not depend on when.

The static check, in full. $\Phi(\vec x) = -G\int\rho(\vec y)|\vec x - \vec y|^{-1}\mathrm d^3y$ (unit 05's superposition), so $\frac{4G}{c^2}\int\rho/|\vec x - \vec y| = -4\Phi/c^2$, and unit 35 found $\bar h_{00} = -4\Phi/c^2$ from Poisson's equation. Two routes, one answer.

Far away and slow

Three lengths: the source's size $d$, the distance $r = |\vec x|$, and the reduced wavelength $\lambda/2\pi = c/\omega$. Put $n = \vec x/r$.

the wave zone and the slow-motion approximation

Far: $r\gg d$ and $r\gg\lambda/2\pi$. Then $|\vec x - \vec y| = r - n\cdot\vec y + O(d^2/r)$, and in the denominator $1/|\vec x - \vec y| = 1/r$ up to a relative $O(d/r)$.

Slow: $d\ll\lambda/2\pi$, which for a source of speed $v\sim\omega d$ is $v\ll c$. Then in the retarded time $$T\big(t - \tfrac rc + \tfrac{n\cdot\vec y}{c}\big) = \sum_{m\ge0}\frac1{m!}\Big(\frac{n\cdot\vec y}{c}\Big)^m\partial_t^mT\big(t - \tfrac rc\big),$$ and the $m$-th term is $O\big((v/c)^m\big)$ relative to the first.

leading order $$\bar h_{\mu\nu}(t,\vec x) \approx \frac{4G}{c^4r}\int T_{\mu\nu}\big(t - r/c,\ \vec y\big)\,\mathrm d^3y .$$

Every component falls as $1/r$ and depends on $t - r/c$: an outgoing wave.

The multipole ladder. The $m$-th term involves the $m$-th moment $\int T\,y^{i_1}\cdots y^{i_m}$ — unit 10's multipole expansion of $1/|\vec x - \vec y|$, with time added and each rung costing a factor $v/c$.

For the binary: $d = a$, $\omega = 2\Omega$, so $\dfrac{d}{\lambda/2\pi} = \dfrac{2\Omega a}{c} = \dfrac{2v}{c}$. For Hulse–Taylor, $v/c = 1.5\times10^{-3}$.

The expansion of the distance. $|\vec x - \vec y|^2 = r^2 - 2r\,n\cdot\vec y + |\vec y|^2$, so $|\vec x - \vec y| = r\sqrt{1 - 2n\cdot\vec y/r + |\vec y|^2/r^2} = r - n\cdot\vec y + O(d^2/r)$ by the binomial series. In the denominator the correction is relative $O(d/r)$ and is dropped: it gives terms falling faster than $1/r$, which carry no energy to infinity (unit 24's three zones). In the time argument it is multiplied by $\omega/c$ and becomes $\omega d/c$, which is not small because $r$ is large but small because the source is slow. That is why two different conditions are needed.

The Taylor series in time. It is Taylor's theorem in the first argument of $T$ with step $n\cdot\vec y/c$, valid when $T$ is smooth in time; the ratio of consecutive terms is $|n\cdot\vec y|\,|\partial_tT|/(c|T|)\sim d\omega/c$. Truncating after $m = 0$ is unit 21's order notation made concrete: the error is $O(v/c)$.

The Hulse–Taylor number. $v = \sqrt{GM/a}$ with $GM = 3.7531\times10^{20}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}}$ and $a = 1.949\times10^{9}$ m gives $v = 4.39\times10^{5}$ m/s, $v/c = 1.46\times10^{-3}$. The slow-motion expansion is superb there. For GW150914 near 150 Hz, $v/c\approx0.55$, and it is not.

Why gravity has no dipole radiation

For a slow source $T^{00} = \rho c^2$ and $T^{0i} = c\rho v^i$ at leading order. Then

$\bar h_{00}\approx\dfrac{4GM}{c^2r}$ with $M = \displaystyle\int\rho$, $\quad\bar h_{0i}\approx-\dfrac{4GP^i}{c^3r}$ with $P^i = \displaystyle\int\rho v^i$.

theorem · two conservation laws

$\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ gives $\dot M = 0$ and $\dot P^i = 0$; and the mass dipole $D^i = \int\rho\,y^i$ has $\dot D^i = P^i$, so $\ddot D^i = 0$.

proof

$\nu = 0$: $\partial_t\rho + \partial_k(\rho v^k) = 0$. Integrate: $\dot M = 0$. Multiply by $y^i$ and integrate by parts: $\dot D^i = \int\rho v^i = P^i$. $\nu = i$: $\partial_t T^{0i}/c = -\partial_kT^{ki}$ integrates to $\dot P^i = 0$. $\square$

electromagnetism is different

Unit 24's dipole $\vec p = \sum q_a\vec x_a$ has $\dot{\vec p} = \sum q_a\vec v_a$, which is not conserved unless every particle has the same $q/m$. Larmor radiation is $\ddot{\vec p}\ne0$.

the structural reason

Gravity's charge is inertial mass — the equivalence principle of unit 30. So its dipole's derivative is total momentum, conserved by Newton's third law (unit 13's Noether, for translations). Mass conservation kills the monopole; momentum conservation kills the dipole. The first multipole that can radiate is the quadrupole, and each rung up the ladder costs $(v/c)^2$ in power.

The monopole and dipole fields are static. $\bar h_{00} = 4GM/c^2r$ with constant $M$ is the Newtonian potential of the whole system, $-4\Phi/c^2$ with $\Phi = -GM/r$. $\bar h_{0i}$ with constant $P$ is the same potential seen from a frame moving with the centre of mass; in the centre-of-mass frame $P = 0$ and it vanishes. The next term in the slow-motion series gives $\bar h_{00}$ a piece proportional to $n\cdot\dot{\vec D}$, constant again. None of these fields changes with $t - r/c$, so none carries energy away.

The integration by parts. $\int y^i\partial_k(\rho v^k)\,\mathrm d^3y = -\int\delta^i_k\rho v^k\,\mathrm d^3y = -P^i$, the boundary term vanishing because the source has bounded support. Hence $\dot D^i = \int y^i\partial_t\rho = -\int y^i\partial_k(\rho v^k) = P^i$.

The magnetic dipole too. Electromagnetism has a second dipole, the magnetic one, $\tfrac12\sum q_a\vec x_a\times\vec v_a$. Its gravitational analogue is $\tfrac12\sum m_a\vec x_a\times\vec v_a$ — half the total angular momentum, conserved. So gravity's "current dipole" is silenced as well. What survives at the next order is the mass quadrupole, and at the order after it the current quadrupole and mass octupole.

Self-gravitating sources. For a binary, $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ in flat space is false — the stars accelerate. But the two conservation laws used here hold anyway, for any isolated Newtonian system: $M$ is conserved because mass is, and $P$ because of Newton's third law. The theorem survives; the next slides need more care, and the warning after the quadrupole formula says how much.

Lemma: the stresses are the second moment

The wave lives in $\bar h_{ij}$, which by the leading-order formula needs $\int T^{ij}$ — the source's stresses, including the kinetic $\rho v^iv^j$. Those are hard to know. The lemma trades them for the mass distribution.

lemma · tensor virial theorem

If $\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ and $T$ has bounded spatial support, then $$\int T^{ij}\,\mathrm d^3y = \frac12\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm dt^2}\int\rho\,y^iy^j\,\mathrm d^3y,\qquad\rho = T^{00}/c^2.$$

proof

1. From $\nu = 0$ and $\nu = k$, with $x^0 = ct$: $\partial_0^2T^{00} = -\partial_0\partial_kT^{k0} = \partial_k\partial_lT^{kl}$.

2. Multiply by $y^iy^j$ and integrate. Integrating by parts twice, with no boundary terms, $$\int y^iy^j\partial_k\partial_lT^{kl} = \int\partial_k\partial_l(y^iy^j)\,T^{kl} = 2\int T^{ij},$$ since $\partial_k\partial_l(y^iy^j) = \delta^i_k\delta^j_l + \delta^i_l\delta^j_k$ and $T$ is symmetric.

3. So $\frac1{c^2}\frac{\mathrm d^2}{\mathrm dt^2}\int T^{00}y^iy^j = 2\int T^{ij}$. $\square$

Check. Both sides are $\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}} = \mathrm J$ ✓.

Step 1 in full. The $\nu = 0$ law is $\partial_0T^{00} + \partial_kT^{k0} = 0$ and the $\nu = l$ law is $\partial_0T^{0l} + \partial_kT^{kl} = 0$. Differentiate the first by $\partial_0$ and use Clairaut and the symmetry $T^{k0} = T^{0k}$: $\partial_0^2T^{00} = -\partial_k\partial_0T^{0k} = \partial_k\partial_lT^{lk}$.

Step 2's derivatives. $\partial_l(y^iy^j) = \delta^i_ly^j + y^i\delta^j_l$, and $\partial_k$ of that is $\delta^i_l\delta^j_k + \delta^i_k\delta^j_l$. Contracting with symmetric $T^{kl}$ gives $T^{ji} + T^{ij} = 2T^{ij}$.

The name. Its trace is the scalar virial theorem: $\int T^{kk} = \tfrac12\ddot I$ with $I = \int\rho|\vec y|^2$. For a bound system in a steady state $\ddot I$ averages to zero, and $\int T^{kk}$ — kinetic energy twice plus the gravitational stresses — averages to zero too: unit 08's $2\langle T\rangle + \langle V\rangle = 0$, if you include in $T^{ij}$ the gravitational stresses of the next slide's warning.

An example. Two masses on a circle about their centre of mass: the left side is the kinetic stress $\sum m v^iv^j$ plus the tension of the gravitational "string" between them; the right side is half the second derivative of $\sum my^iy^j$. Problem P6 checks one component.

The quadrupole formula

theorem · the quadrupole formula

With $I_{ij}(t) = \int\rho\,y^iy^j\,\mathrm d^3y$ and its trace-free part $Q_{ij} = I_{ij} - \tfrac13\delta_{ij}I_{kk}$, far from a slow source

$$h^{\rm TT}_{ij}(t,\vec x) = \frac{2G}{c^4r}\,\Lambda_{ij,kl}(n)\,\ddot Q_{kl}\big(t - r/c\big).$$
proof

Leading order and the lemma give $\bar h_{ij} = \frac{4G}{c^4r}\cdot\frac12\ddot I_{ij}$. Project with $\Lambda(n)$. Since $\Lambda(\mathbb 1) = 0$, replacing $I$ by $Q$ changes nothing. $\square$

a factor of three

Unit 10's quadrupole moment was $\int\rho(3y^iy^j - |\vec y|^2\delta_{ij}) = 3Q_{ij}$. Both are the same symmetric bilinear form up to scale; the gravitational-wave literature uses $Q$. And $I_{ij}$ here is the second mass moment, not unit 16's inertia tensor $\int\rho(|\vec y|^2\delta_{ij} - y^iy^j)$.

checks

Dimensions. $\frac{G}{c^4r}\cdot\mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-2}} = \frac{\mathrm J}{\mathrm N\cdot\mathrm m} = 1$ ✓.

A pulsating sphere. Spherical symmetry makes $I_{ij}\propto\delta_{ij}$, so $Q = 0$: no quadrupole radiation, however violently it pulsates ✓.

A rotating axisymmetric body about its axis has constant $I_{ij}$: silent ✓. A spinning neutron star radiates only through its asymmetry.

Einstein, 1918, with the factor fixed later. Every gravitational-wave amplitude in this unit comes from this line.

Why the TT part is the whole story. Far away, the $\bar h_{00}$ and $\bar h_{0i}$ components are the static fields of the last slide but one, and the gauge-dependent parts of $\bar h_{ij}$ (anything of the form $p(\epsilon)$ in section 2's language) carry nothing physical. Locally the outgoing wave is a plane wave along $n$, and section 2 showed that the physical content of such a wave is its TT part. The projector extracts it.

The mathematics first. $I$ is a symmetric bilinear form on $\mathbb R^3$, $I(u,v) = \int\rho\,(u\cdot y)(v\cdot y)$, and $Q$ is its trace-free part; $\Lambda(n)$ is a linear map on symmetric forms. The formula is a statement about maps, and the components in any orthonormal basis follow from unit 16's change-of-basis theorem. That $Q$ is unit 10's moment divided by 3 is visible from the definitions.

History. Einstein's 1916 paper had an error; his 1918 paper "Über Gravitationswellen" derived the quadrupole formula, with an overall factor of 2 wrong, found by Eddington in 1922. From the 1950s to the early 1980s a "quadrupole formula controversy" asked whether the formula applies to self-gravitating systems at all; the next slide is that controversy in one box.

The binary breaks the hypothesis

the honest difficulty

The linear theory needs $\partial^\mu T_{\mu\nu} = 0$ in flat spacetime. For dust that means $\partial_t(\rho v^i) + \partial_k(\rho v^iv^k) = 0$: every particle moves in a straight line. A binary held together by gravity violates it. Strictly, the quadrupole formula has been derived for a source that cannot orbit.

What saves it is that the lemma only needs some conserved symmetric $T^{\mu\nu}$ whose $T^{00}$ is the mass density.

proposition · Newtonian gravity has a stress

With $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$, the gravitational stress $$t^{ij} = \frac1{4\pi G}\Big(\partial_i\Phi\,\partial_j\Phi - \tfrac12\delta_{ij}|\nabla\Phi|^2\Big)$$ has $\partial_jt^{ij} = \rho\,\partial_i\Phi$. So $\partial_t(\rho v^i) + \partial_j(\rho v^iv^j + t^{ij}) = 0$ holds for self-gravitating dust.

Cited. That the full nonlinear theory reproduces the quadrupole formula with this source, at leading post-Newtonian order, is a theorem of the post-Newtonian and post-Minkowskian programme: Blanchet, Living Reviews in Relativity 17 (2014) 2, §§1–3; Maggiore, Gravitational Waves vol. 1, ch. 5.

Proof of the proposition. $\partial_j(\partial_i\Phi\,\partial_j\Phi) = \partial_j\partial_i\Phi\,\partial_j\Phi + \partial_i\Phi\,\nabla^2\Phi$ and $\partial_j(\tfrac12\delta_{ij}|\nabla\Phi|^2) = \partial_j\Phi\,\partial_i\partial_j\Phi$. Subtract: the first terms cancel by Clairaut, leaving $\partial_i\Phi\,\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho\,\partial_i\Phi$. Divide by $4\pi G$. $\square$ Then Newton's law for dust, $\rho(\partial_t + v\cdot\nabla)v^i = -\rho\partial_i\Phi$, combined with continuity, is $\partial_t(\rho v^i) + \partial_j(\rho v^iv^j) = -\rho\partial_i\Phi = -\partial_jt^{ij}$. $\square$ It is the gravitational analogue of unit 24's Maxwell stress tensor, with the opposite sign because like charges attract.

So what is proved here, and what is cited. Proved: the virial lemma applies to the effective source $T^{ij} = \rho v^iv^j + t^{ij}$, and gives $\int T^{ij} = \tfrac12\ddot I_{ij}$ with the Newtonian $\rho$ only. So if the linear formula is applied with the effective source, the answer depends only on the mass distribution and is the quadrupole formula. Cited: that doing this is legitimate — that the nonlinear terms of the field equations act, at this order, as exactly this source and nothing more. It took until the early 1980s (Damour's 1983 treatment of binary motion, and the Walker–Will analysis) to settle, and the Hulse–Taylor measurement of section 6 was the experimental half of the settlement.

Where it certainly fails. When $v/c$ is not small, when the bodies are close enough that their own fields are strong, and at merger. Section 6 shows all three with numbers for GW150914.

38 · energy

No energy density at a point

proposition

Let $t_{\mu\nu}$ be a tensor built at each point from $g$ and $\partial g$ alone, vanishing whenever $\partial g = 0$ in some chart at that point. Then $t_{\mu\nu} = 0$ everywhere.

proof

At any point $p$, unit 32's normal coordinates give $\partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}(p) = 0$. So $t(p) = 0$ in that chart; a tensor zero in one basis is zero in every basis. $\square$

Physics. The equivalence principle (unit 30): a freely falling observer feels no gravitational field at a point. Whatever gravitational energy is, it cannot be a local density built from the field's first derivatives — the way $\tfrac12(\varepsilon_0E^2 + B^2/\mu_0)$ is for electromagnetism.

the phrase to be careful with

"The energy of the gravitational field" has no local meaning. Unit 35 said this about pseudotensors. The proposition is why.

what does survive

Curvature cannot be transformed away — it involves $\partial^2g$. A wave has curvature $R_{i0j0} = -\tfrac12\partial_0^2h_{ij}$, and a region several wavelengths across cannot be put in normal coordinates all at once.

So an energy for waves must be an average over a region, not a value at a point. The next slide is the average.

The hypothesis "vanishing whenever $\partial g = 0$". It is what "energy of the field" ought to satisfy: in flat spacetime in Cartesian coordinates, where the field is trivially absent, the energy is zero. A tensor with that property and no second derivatives is identically zero. This does not forbid quantities built from curvature (which involve $\partial^2g$), and it does not forbid non-tensorial quantities — the pseudotensors of unit 35 — whose values depend on the chart and which therefore do not answer the question "how much energy is here".

What the average buys. Normal coordinates remove $\partial g$ at one point, and in a neighbourhood whose size is small compared with the curvature radius. A wave of wavelength $\lambda$ has curvature radius comparable to $\lambda/\sqrt h$, and no chart flattens it over a region containing several wavelengths. Averaging over such a region produces a quantity that no choice of coordinates can remove.

Global energy. For an isolated system, the total mass measured at infinity — the ADM mass, and the Bondi mass at null infinity — is well defined, and the Bondi mass decreases by exactly the energy carried off by waves (cited: Bondi, van der Burg and Metzner 1962; Sachs 1962; see Wald, General Relativity, §11.2). That is the theorem behind the energy balance of section 5.

Gauge invariance of the average. Under a residual gauge transformation, the change in $\langle\partial_\mu h\,\partial_\nu h\rangle$ is a sum of terms each of which, after moving one derivative across inside the average, contains $\Box\xi = 0$ or $\partial^\alpha\bar h_{\alpha\beta} = 0$, plus total derivatives. The average of a total derivative over a region of size $\ell$ is smaller by $\lambda/\ell$ and is discarded. That is the precise sense in which averaging buys gauge invariance.

What the chirp looks like

$h(t)\propto f(t)^{2/3}\cos\Phi(t)$ from worked example 4, with $\mathcal M = 30.6\,M_\odot$ — GW150914's — from $0.2$ s before coalescence, where $f = 33$ Hz, until $f$ reaches $250$ Hz, where the drawing stops because the approximations have long since failed.

What rises. The frequency, as $(t_c - t)^{-3/8}$, and the amplitude, as $f^{2/3}\propto(t_c - t)^{-1/4}$. Both diverge at $t_c$, which the real signal does not: it saturates at merger and then rings down, and none of that is in this formula.

Heard as sound, the band from 35 to 250 Hz is audible: a rising "whoop" lasting a fifth of a second. The name chirp is literal.

How the figure was drawn. $f(t)$ from step 4 of worked example 4, inverted: $f = \frac1\pi\big(\frac{5}{256\,(t_c - t)}\big)^{3/8}\big(\frac{G\mathcal M}{c^3}\big)^{-5/8}$, with $G\mathcal M/c^3 = 1.507\times10^{-4}$ s. The phase is $\Phi = 2\pi\int f\,\mathrm dt$, integrated numerically; the amplitude is $f^{2/3}$ normalised to its final value. Between $0.2$ s before coalescence and the $250$ Hz cut there are about ten cycles. At $0.2$ s the formula gives $f = 32.6$ Hz.

Why the frequency is cut at 250 Hz. At a wave frequency $f$ the separation is $a = (GM)^{1/3}(\pi f)^{-2/3}$. With $M = 70.9\,M_\odot$ (the detector-frame total), $a = 920$ km at 35 Hz and $349$ km at 150 Hz — that is $8.8$ and $3.3$ times $GM/c^2$. By 250 Hz the "stars" would be closer than any black holes of that mass can be without merging, and the formula is describing nothing.

The exponents. $f\propto(t_c - t)^{-3/8}$ from step 4. $h\propto f^{2/3}$ from step 5. So $h\propto(t_c - t)^{-1/4}$: slower growth than the frequency, which is visible in the drawing as oscillations bunching up faster than they grow.

Why a parabola. Each orbit is shorter than the last by $\dot P_bP_b$. After $N$ orbits the accumulated lag is $\sum_{k\le N}k\,\dot P_bP_b\approx\tfrac12\dot P_bP_bN^2$, and $N = t/P_b$.

The detector is Michelson's

A beam splitter sends light down two perpendicular arms of length $L = 4$ km to mirrors hung as free masses, and recombines the returns. Any difference in round-trip time shows as a change in the interference.

round-trip times in TT gauge

Wave along $z$, arms along $x$ and $y$, $L\ll\lambda$. Light along $x$ has $0 = -c^2\mathrm dt^2 + (1 + h_+)\,\mathrm dx^2$, so $c\,\mathrm dt = (1 + \tfrac12h_+)\,\mathrm dx$, and by the lemma the mirrors stay at $x = 0, L$:

$$T_{x,y} = \frac{2L}c\big(1 \pm \tfrac12h_+\big).$$

The measured quantity. $T_x - T_y = \dfrac{2Lh_+}c$: the arms behave as if lengthened and shortened by $\tfrac12h_+L$ each, a differential length change $h_+L$.

For GW150914, $h = 1.0\times10^{-21}$: each arm $\pm2\times10^{-18}$ m, differential $4\times10^{-18}$ m, about $1/200$ of a proton radius.

the same experiment, twice

Michelson and Morley (1887) compared round-trip times along perpendicular arms, looking for the ether, and found no difference. LIGO compared round-trip times along perpendicular arms, and in 2015 found one — made by a wave that had travelled for over a billion years.

The round trip. Along the $x$ arm $h_+$ is effectively constant during one round trip ($2L/c = 27\ \mu$s, against a wave period of $7$ ms at 150 Hz), so $T_x = 2\int_0^L(1 + \tfrac12h_+)\,\mathrm dx/c = \frac{2L}c(1 + \tfrac12h_+)$. Along $y$, $g_{yy} = 1 - h_+$. The mirrors do not move in TT coordinates (the free-masses lemma); the light's coordinate speed changes instead. In a local lab frame one would say the mirrors moved and light's speed is $c$; the time difference is the same, being a comparison of two proper times at the beam splitter.

The proton. Its charge radius is $0.84\times10^{-15}$ m; $4\times10^{-18}/0.84\times10^{-15} = 0.0048$.

What the real instrument adds (cited: Saulson, Fundamentals of Interferometric Gravitational Wave Detectors). Each arm is a Fabry–Pérot cavity in which light makes hundreds of round trips, multiplying the phase shift; the mirrors are 40 kg fused-silica masses on multi-stage pendulums, free along the arm above a few hertz; and the laser power is recycled to reduce the photon counting noise. None of that changes the principle on the slide.

The light-travel time of the source. A luminosity distance of 410 Mpc at $z = 0.09$ corresponds to a light travel time of about 1.3 billion years (cited from the discovery paper; the conversion needs unit 39's cosmology).
