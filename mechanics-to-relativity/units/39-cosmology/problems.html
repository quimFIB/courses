Checkpoint. You derive the Friedmann equations from the FLRW metric and Einstein's equation without consulting a reference, solve for the scale factor in the matter-, radiation- and \(\Lambda\)-dominated eras, and compute the age of the universe for the Planck parameters (\(\Omega_{\mathrm m} = 0.315\), \(\Omega_\Lambda = 0.685\), \(H_0 = 67.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}Mpc^{-1}}\)) to within \(0.3\) Gyr of \(13.8\).

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Thirteen problems, about six hours. Conventions are the deck's: signature \((-,+,+,+)\), \(x^0 = ct\), unit 33's sign of the Riemann tensor with \(R_{\sigma\nu} = \partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}\), \(a(t_0) = 1\), dots are \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dt\), and \(E(a) = H(a)/H_0\).

Constants: \(c = 2.9979\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(G = 6.6743\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m^3\,kg^{-1}s^{-2}}\), \(m_p = 1.6726\times10^{-27}\) kg, \(1\ \mathrm{Mpc} = 3.0857\times10^{22}\) m \(= 3.2616\) million light years, \(1\ \mathrm{Gyr} = 3.1558\times10^{16}\) s. The running example: \(\Omega_{\mathrm m} = 0.315\), \(\Omega_\Lambda = 0.685\), \(H_0 = 67.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}Mpc^{-1}}\); radiation, where it is asked for, \(\Omega_{\mathrm r} = 9.21\times10^{-5}\); recombination at \(a_* = 1/1091\); \(T_0 = 2.7255\) K.

Four steps suffice for three figures once the integrand is made smooth.

Scales, and universes with one component

P1 Units and scales routine

exercises: the units of \(H_0\), the critical density, \(\Lambda\) read as a density, a number

  1. Express \(H_0 = 67.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}Mpc^{-1}}\) in \(\mathrm s^{-1}\). Give the Hubble time \(1/H_0\) in Gyr and the Hubble length \(c/H_0\) in Mpc.
  2. Compute the critical density \(\rho_{\mathrm c,0} = 3H_0^2/8\pi G\), and from it the present matter density and the density \(\rho_\Lambda\) of the cosmological constant, each in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and in proton masses per cubic metre.
  3. Compute \(\Lambda\) itself from \(\Omega_\Lambda = \Lambda c^2/3H_0^2\), in \(\mathrm m^{-2}\), and the length \(1/\sqrt\Lambda\) in Mpc. Check that \(\Lambda c^2/3\) has the units of \(H^2\).
  4. Galaxies carry peculiar velocities of about \(300\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) on top of the expansion. Beyond what distance is that less than one per cent of \(H_0 d\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every part is a unit conversion or a one-line formula evaluated. The answers are numbers with units; the only thing to get right is which Mpc cancels which metre.

Rung 2

\(\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}Mpc^{-1}}\) is a speed divided by a length: write both in metres and it is an inverse time. Each \(\Omega_i\) is \(\rho_i/\rho_{\mathrm c,0}\), so the densities follow from \(\rho_{\mathrm c,0}\) by multiplication.

Rung 3

First line: \(H_0 = 67.4\times10^{3}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} / 3.0857\times10^{22}\ \mathrm m\). For part 4, solve \(300 = 0.01\times67.4\,d\) with \(d\) in Mpc.

Worked solution

Idea. Everything in cosmology is measured in units of \(H_0\); this problem fixes those units once, so that the rest of the sheet can work in Hubble times and Hubble lengths.

1. \(H_0 = 6.74\times10^{4}/3.0857\times10^{22} = 2.184\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm s^{-1}\). Then \(1/H_0 = 4.578\times10^{17}\ \mathrm s = 14.51\) Gyr and \(c/H_0 = 1.373\times10^{26}\ \mathrm m = 4448\) Mpc (\(14.51\) billion light years, as it must be: a light-time of one Hubble time).

2. \[ \rho_{\mathrm c,0} = \frac{3(2.184\times10^{-18})^2}{8\pi(6.6743\times10^{-11})} = 8.53\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}, \] which is \(5.10\) protons per cubic metre. Matter: \(0.315\rho_{\mathrm c,0} = 2.69\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), \(1.61\) protons per \(\mathrm m^3\). \(\Lambda\): \(0.685\rho_{\mathrm c,0} = 5.85\times10^{-27}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), \(3.49\) protons per \(\mathrm m^3\). Today \(\rho_\Lambda / \rho_{\mathrm m} = 2.17\).

3. \(\Lambda = 3\Omega_\Lambda H_0^2/c^2 = 3(0.685)(2.184\times10^{-18})^2/(2.998\times10^8)^2 = 1.091\times10^{-52}\ \mathrm m^{-2}\), and \(1/\sqrt\Lambda = 9.57\times10^{25}\ \mathrm m = 3103\) Mpc. Units: \([\Lambda c^2] = \mathrm{m^{-2}\cdot m^2s^{-2}} = \mathrm s^{-2} = [H^2]\) ✓.

4. \(300 \lt 0.01\times67.4\,d\) requires \(d \gt 445\) Mpc. At the deck's \(100\) Mpc the contamination is \(300/6740 = 4.5\%\).

Check. \(\rho_\Lambda = \Lambda c^2/8\pi G\) directly: \(1.091\times10^{-52}\times (2.998\times10^8)^2/(8\pi\times6.6743\times10^{-11}) = 5.85\times10^{-27}\) ✓, the same as \(\Omega_\Lambda\rho_{\mathrm c,0}\), as the definitions require.

P2 One component, and the decay of peculiar motion routine

exercises: separable ODEs, the Friedmann equation for one component, geodesics through a cyclic coordinate

  1. A flat universe holds one fluid with \(p = w\rho c^2\), \(w\) constant. Solve the first Friedmann equation for \(a(t)\) with \(a(0) = 0\) when \(w \gt -1\), and for \(w = -1\). Give \(H(t)\) and the age \(t_0\), and evaluate \(t_0\) in Gyr for matter and for radiation.
  2. Show that the deceleration parameter \(q = -\ddot aa/\dot a^2\) is the constant \(\tfrac12(1+3w)\). Which \(w\) accelerate?
  3. A free massive particle moves in flat FLRW, \(\mathrm ds^2 = -c^2\mathrm dt^2 + a(t)^2(\mathrm dx^2 + \mathrm dy^2 + \mathrm dz^2)\). Using unit 32's geodesic Lagrangian, show that \(a^2\,\mathrm dx^i/\mathrm d\tau\) is constant, and deduce that the magnitude of its momentum measured by the comoving observers it passes falls as \(1/a\).
  4. A galaxy had a peculiar velocity of \(600\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) at \(z = 1\). What is it now, if nothing but the expansion acts on it?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 2 are a separable first-order ODE and its consequences; part 3 is a conservation law, and the object you want is a conserved momentum conjugate to a coordinate the Lagrangian does not contain.

Rung 2

With one component, \(\rho = \rho_0a^{-3(1+w)}\) and \(H^2 = H_0^2a^{-3(1+w)}\). For part 3, which coordinates are absent from \(L = -c^2\dot t^2 + a(t)^2\delta_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\)? For the momentum a comoving observer measures, you need a unit vector along \(\partial_x\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(\dot a = H_0a^{1-\frac32(1+w)}\), so \(a^{\frac32(1+w)-1}\,\mathrm da = H_0\,\mathrm dt\). First line of part 3: \(\partial L/\partial\dot x^i = 2a^2\dot x^i\) and \(\partial L/\partial x^i = 0\). The unit vector is \(a^{-1}\partial_x\).

Worked solution

Idea. One component makes the Friedmann equation separable, and the answer is a power law whose exponent is fixed by \(w\). The decay of peculiar momentum is Noether's theorem for the spatial translations, which remain symmetries of flat FLRW even though time translation is not.

1. The fluid equation gives \(\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}\), so \(H^2 = H_0^2a^{-3(1+w)}\) and \(\dot a = aH = H_0a^{1-\frac32(1+w)}\). Separate (unit 00) and integrate from \(a = 0\) at \(t = 0\): \[ \frac{a^{\frac32(1+w)}}{\tfrac32(1+w)} = H_0t,\qquad a(t) = \Bigl(\tfrac32(1+w)H_0t\Bigr)^{\frac{2}{3(1+w)}}. \] Then \(H = \dot a/a = \frac{2}{3(1+w)t}\), and \(a = 1\) at \(t_0 = \frac{2}{3(1+w)H_0}\). Matter, \(w = 0\): \(a \propto t^{2/3}\), \(t_0 = \tfrac23(14.51) = 9.67\) Gyr. Radiation, \(w = \tfrac13\): \(a \propto t^{1/2}\), \(t_0 = \tfrac12(14.51) = 7.25\) Gyr. For \(w = -1\), \(H = H_0\) is constant, \(\dot a = H_0a\), and \(a = e^{H_0(t-t_0)}\): no zero, no age.

2. With \(a = Ct^n\), \(n = \frac{2}{3(1+w)}\): \(\ddot aa/\dot a^2 = n(n-1)/n^2 = (n-1)/n\), so \(q = (1-n)/n = \frac{3(1+w)}{2} - 1 = \tfrac12(1+3w)\). For \(w = -1\), \(\ddot a a/\dot a^2 = 1\) and \(q = -1 = \tfrac12(1-3)\) ✓. Acceleration, \(q \lt 0\), exactly when \(w \lt -\tfrac13\) — the acceleration equation's \(\rho + 3p/c^2 \lt 0\).

3. Parametrise by proper time \(\tau\). Unit 32: geodesics are the stationary curves of \(\int L\,\mathrm d\tau\) with \(L = -c^2\dot t^2 + a(t)^2\delta_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\) (dots here \(\mathrm d/\mathrm d\tau\)). The coordinates \(x^i\) do not appear, so by unit 12's cyclic-coordinate rule \(p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot x^i = 2a^2\dot x^i\) is constant. A comoving observer's orthonormal spatial basis is \(e_{\hat i} = a^{-1}\partial_i\), since \(g(\partial_i,\partial_i) = a^2\). The particle's four-velocity has spatial part \(\dot x^i\partial_i = (a\dot x^i)e_{\hat i}\), so the momentum the observer measures has components \(ma\dot x^i = m\,p_i/(2a)\), and its magnitude falls as \(1/a\).

4. At \(600\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\) the motion is non-relativistic, so the momentum is \(m\) times the velocity, which also falls as \(1/a\). From \(a = \tfrac12\) to \(a = 1\) it halves: \(300\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}\).

Check. Dimensions: \(H_0t\) is dimensionless ✓. Limit: \(w \to -1^+\) sends \(t_0 \to \infty\), matching the exponential ✓. Part 3 for a photon: the same conservation law gives energy \(\propto 1/a\), which is the deck's cosmological redshift obtained a second way ✓. The same result from the Christoffels: \(\mathrm du^i/\mathrm d\tau + 2\Gamma^i_{0j}u^0u^j = 0\) with \(\Gamma^i_{0j} = (\dot a/ca)\delta^i_j\) and \(u^0 = c\,\mathrm dt/\mathrm d\tau\) gives \(\mathrm du^i/\mathrm dt = -2(\dot a/a)u^i\), so \(u^i \propto a^{-2}\) ✓.

The geometry and the equations

P3 Proof: what isotropy allows medium

exercises: invariant tensors under \(SO(3)\), polarisation, why FLRW and its source have the form they do

Let \((V, \gamma)\) be a three-dimensional real inner-product space and \(SO(3)\) its group of orientation-preserving isometries.

  1. Prove that a covector \(\omega\) with \(\omega(Qv) = \omega(v)\) for every \(Q\in SO(3)\) and every \(v\) is zero.
  2. Prove that a symmetric bilinear form \(B\) with \(B(Qv, Qw) = B(v,w)\) for all \(Q\in SO(3)\) is \(\lambda\gamma\) for some real \(\lambda\).
  3. Deduce, without computing any curvature, that in FLRW \(g_{0i} = 0\), \(T_{0i} = 0\), \(T_{ij} = p\,g_{ij}\) with \(p\) a function of \(t\) alone, and \(G_{0i} = 0\).
  4. Now suppose only the rotations about one axis are symmetries. Show that part 2 fails, and say what a universe isotropic about one axis only would look like.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two short proofs and two consequences. Everything rests on one fact about the group: how it acts on unit vectors.

Rung 2

\(SO(3)\) acts transitively on the unit sphere. For part 1, find one rotation that sends the dual vector of \(\omega\) to its negative. For part 2, look at the quadratic form \(q(v) = B(v,v)\) on the unit sphere.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: let \(n = \omega^\sharp\), and let \(Q\) be the rotation by \(\pi\) about any axis orthogonal to \(n\); then \(Qn = -n\). First line of part 2: \(q(Qv) = q(v)\), and every unit vector is \(Qe\) for a fixed unit \(e\).

Worked solution

Idea. A rotation-invariant object can single out no direction. A covector singles out its dual vector, so it must vanish; a symmetric form singles out its eigenvectors, so it must have only one eigenvalue.

Transitivity. Given unit vectors \(u \ne \pm v\), the rotation about the axis \(u\times v\) through the angle between them carries \(u\) to \(v\); for \(v = -u\), rotate by \(\pi\) about any axis orthogonal to \(u\); for \(v = u\), the identity.

1. Let \(n = \omega^\sharp\), so that \(\omega(v) = \gamma(n, v)\). If \(n \ne 0\), take \(Q\) the rotation by \(\pi\) about an axis orthogonal to \(n\); then \(Qn = -n\) and \(Q^{-1} = Q\). Invariance gives \(\gamma(n,n) = \omega(n) = \omega(Qn) = -\gamma(n,n)\), so \(\gamma(n,n) = 0\) and \(n = 0\), a contradiction. So \(\omega = 0\). \(\square\)

2. Let \(q(v) = B(v,v)\), and fix a unit vector \(e\); put \(\lambda = q(e)\). For any unit \(u\), transitivity gives \(Q\) with \(u = Qe\), so \(q(u) = q(Qe) = q(e) = \lambda\). By homogeneity of degree two, \(q(v) = \lambda\gamma(v,v)\) for all \(v\). Polarisation, \(B(v,w) = \tfrac12[q(v+w) - q(v) - q(w)]\), then gives \(B = \lambda\gamma\). \(\square\)

3. At an event \(p\), the cosmological principle supplies, for each \(Q\in SO(3)\), an isometry of spacetime fixing \(p\) and the observer's velocity \(u\) and acting on the slice's tangent space \(T_p\Sigma\) by \(Q\). Such an isometry preserves \(g\), and preserves every tensor built from \(g\) alone — the Riemann, Ricci and Einstein tensors. Field equations whose left side is so invariant force the same on \(T\). Now apply the lemmas on \(T_p\Sigma\):

  • \(v \mapsto g(u, v)\) is an invariant covector, so zero: the observers are orthogonal to the slices, \(g_{0i} = 0\). Likewise \(v\mapsto T(u,v)\) and \(v\mapsto G(u,v)\): \(T_{0i} = G_{0i} = 0\).
  • \(T\) restricted to \(T_p\Sigma\) is an invariant symmetric form, so \(T_{ij} = p\,g_{ij}\) there, for some number \(p\) at \(p\).
  • Homogeneity supplies isometries carrying any point of a slice to any other, so \(p\) and \(T_{00}\) are constant on each slice: functions of \(t\) alone.

4. With only the rotations about the \(z\)-axis, \(B = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda,\lambda,\mu)\) in an orthonormal basis with \(e_3\) along the axis is invariant for every \(\lambda, \mu\): each such rotation preserves the \(xy\)-plane with its inner product and fixes \(e_3\). So part 2 fails when \(\mu \ne \lambda\). A universe isotropic about one axis only can have different pressures along and across that axis, and a metric \(-c^2\mathrm dt^2 + a(t)^2(\mathrm dx^2 + \mathrm dy^2) + b(t)^2\mathrm dz^2\) with two scale factors, expanding at different rates in different directions. The CMB's isotropy to parts in \(10^5\) limits any such difference in the expansion rates severely.

Check. The proofs used orientation-preserving rotations only, so reflections were never needed ✓. Dimension three mattered only through transitivity, which holds for \(SO(n)\), \(n \ge 2\), on the unit sphere; so parts 1 and 2 hold in every dimension \(n \ge 2\) — the two-dimensional case being the rotation by \(\pi\) itself ✓.

P4 The curvature of the model spaces medium

exercises: Christoffel symbols by the Lagrangian shortcut, Ricci components term by term, constant curvature

Take \(\gamma = \dfrac{\mathrm dr^2}{1-kr^2} + r^2\,\mathrm d\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\phi^2\).

  1. Find every nonzero Christoffel symbol of \(\gamma\).
  2. Compute \(R_{rr}\) and \(R_{\theta\theta}\) term by term from unit 33's formula, and show that they equal \(2k\gamma_{rr}\) and \(2k\gamma_{\theta\theta}\).
  3. Give the scalar curvature, and check it against the constant-curvature form \(R_{ijkl} = k(\gamma_{ik}\gamma_{jl} - \gamma_{il}\gamma_{jk})\).
  4. For \(k \gt 0\) the sphere at geodesic distance \(\chi\) from the origin has area \(4\pi S_k(\chi)^2\). Expand it for small \(\chi\) and say what the sign of the correction measures.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a list of seven functions; part 2 is two sums of four groups of terms each; part 3 is one contraction. The answer you are aiming at is known in advance, which is the check.

Rung 2

Read the Christoffels off the Euler-Lagrange equations of \(L = \dot r^2/(1-kr^2) + r^2\dot\theta^2 + r^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\), as the deck's worked example did for FLRW. In the Ricci formula, the contraction \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r} = kr/(1-kr^2) + 2/r\) appears twice; compute it once.

Rung 3

First line: the \(r\) equation is \(\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm ds}\bigl(\frac{2\dot r}{1-kr^2}\bigr) - \frac{2kr\dot r^2}{(1-kr^2)^2} - 2r\dot\theta^2 - 2r\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2 = 0\). Expand the derivative and multiply through by \((1-kr^2)/2\).

Worked solution

Idea. Mechanical, and worth doing once: it is the spatial half of the deck's \(R_{ij}\), whose \(2kc^2\) comes entirely from here.

1. The \(r\) equation, expanded and multiplied by \((1-kr^2)/2\): \(\ddot r + \frac{kr}{1-kr^2}\dot r^2 - r(1-kr^2)\dot\theta^2 - r(1-kr^2)\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2 = 0\). The \(\theta\) equation: \(\ddot\theta + \frac2r\dot r\dot\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2 = 0\). The \(\phi\) equation: \(\ddot\phi + \frac2r\dot r\dot\phi + 2\cot\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\phi = 0\). Comparing with \(\ddot x^i + \Gamma^i_{jk}\dot x^j\dot x^k = 0\) (off-diagonal symbols appear twice):

\[ \Gamma^r_{rr} = \frac{kr}{1-kr^2},\ \ \Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = -r(1-kr^2),\ \ \Gamma^r_{\phi\phi} = -r(1-kr^2)\sin^2\theta, \] \[ \Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \Gamma^\phi_{r\phi} = \frac1r,\ \ \Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} = -\sin\theta\cos\theta,\ \ \Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi} = \cot\theta. \]

2. Write \(D = 1 - kr^2\). The four groups of \(R_{rr}\):

  • \(\partial_\mu\Gamma^\mu_{rr} = \partial_r\frac{kr}{D} = \frac{k(1+kr^2)}{D^2}\).
  • \(-\partial_r\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r} = -\partial_r\bigl(\frac{kr}{D} + \frac2r\bigr) = -\frac{k(1+kr^2)}{D^2} + \frac2{r^2}\).
  • \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{rr} = \bigl(\frac{kr}{D} + \frac2r\bigr)\frac{kr}{D} = \frac{k^2r^2}{D^2} + \frac{2k}{D}\).
  • \(-\Gamma^\mu_{r\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu r} = -\bigl[(\Gamma^r_{rr})^2 + (\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta})^2 + (\Gamma^\phi_{r\phi})^2\bigr] = -\frac{k^2r^2}{D^2} - \frac2{r^2}\).

Sum: \(R_{rr} = \frac{2k}{1-kr^2} = 2k\gamma_{rr}\) ✓. The four groups of \(R_{\theta\theta}\):

  • \(\partial_r\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = \partial_r(-r + kr^3) = -1 + 3kr^2\).
  • \(-\partial_\theta\Gamma^\mu_{\mu\theta} = -\partial_\theta\cot\theta = \frac1{\sin^2\theta}\).
  • \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu r}\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = \bigl(\frac{kr}{D} + \frac2r\bigr)(-rD) = -kr^2 - 2D = kr^2 - 2\).
  • \(-\Gamma^\mu_{\theta\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\theta}\): the pairs \((r,\theta)\) and \((\theta,r)\) each give \(-r D\cdot\frac1r = -D\), and \((\phi,\phi)\) gives \(\cot^2\theta\); with the sign, \(2D - \cot^2\theta = 2 - 2kr^2 - \cot^2\theta\).

Sum: \(-1 + 3kr^2 + kr^2 - 2 + 2 - 2kr^2 + \bigl(\frac1{\sin^2\theta} - \cot^2\theta\bigr) = 2kr^2 = 2k\gamma_{\theta\theta}\) ✓. \(R_{\phi\phi} = 2k\gamma_{\phi\phi}\) follows by the same computation, or by isotropy (P3 part 2), which also gives the off-diagonal components zero.

3. \({}^{(3)}R = \gamma^{ij}R_{ij} = 2k\cdot3 = 6k\). From the constant-curvature form, \(R_{jl} = \gamma^{ik}R_{ijkl} = k(3\gamma_{jl} - \gamma_{jl}) = 2k\gamma_{jl}\) ✓, and in three dimensions unit 33's decomposition makes Ricci determine Riemann, so the space has constant sectional curvature \(k\).

4. \(S_k(\chi) = \sin(\sqrt k\chi)/\sqrt k = \chi - k\chi^3/6 + O(\chi^5)\), so \[ 4\pi S_k^2 = 4\pi\chi^2\Bigl(1 - \frac{k\chi^2}{3} + O(\chi^4)\Bigr). \] Spheres are smaller than Euclidean ones by the fraction \(k\chi^2/3\): the deficit measures positive curvature, and the same expansion with \(\sinh\) shows an excess for \(k \lt 0\).

Check. Dimensions: \([k] = \mathrm m^{-2}\) and \([R_{rr}] = [k/(1-kr^2)] = \mathrm m^{-2}\) ✓. Limit \(k = 0\): every \(k\)-term vanishes and the list is that of flat spherical coordinates, with \(R_{ij} = 0\) ✓.

P5 Friedmann again, in conformal time hard

exercises: the whole Christoffel–Ricci–Einstein chain in new coordinates, conformal time, a closed-form two-component universe

Take flat FLRW in conformal time: \(\mathrm ds^2 = a(\eta)^2\bigl[-c^2\mathrm d\eta^2 + \mathrm dx^2 + \mathrm dy^2 + \mathrm dz^2\bigr]\), with \(x^0 = c\eta\), a prime meaning \(\mathrm d/\mathrm dx^0\), and \(\mathcal H = a'/a\).

  1. Find every nonzero Christoffel symbol.
  2. Show that \(R_{00} = -3\mathcal H'\), \(R_{ij} = (\mathcal H' + 2\mathcal H^2)\delta_{ij}\) and \(R = 6a''/a^3\).
  3. Find \(G_{00}\) and \(G_{ij}\); with a comoving perfect fluid, obtain the Friedmann equations in \(\eta\), and convert them to \(t\) to recover the deck's (with \(k = \Lambda = 0\)).
  4. For matter plus radiation, show \((\mathrm da/\mathrm d\eta)^2 = H_0^2(\Omega_{\mathrm m}a + \Omega_{\mathrm r})\) and solve it with \(a(0) = 0\). Find the comoving horizon \(c\eta\) and the time \(t\) at matter–radiation equality for the running example's \(\Omega_{\mathrm m}\) and \(\Omega_{\mathrm r}\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The metric is a function times the Minkowski metric, so only time derivatives of \(a\) can appear. Parts 1 to 3 are the deck's worked example in new clothes; part 4 is an ODE that turns out to be linear once differentiated.

Rung 2

Three Christoffels, each equal to \(\mathcal H\) times a delta. In the Ricci sums, \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu0} = 4\mathcal H\) and \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu i} = 0\). To convert, note that \(\mathrm dt = a\,\mathrm d\eta\), so \(\mathrm da/\mathrm d\eta = a\dot a\). For part 4, differentiate the ODE in \(\eta\).

Rung 3

First line: \(\Gamma^0_{00} = \tfrac12g^{00}\partial_0g_{00} = \tfrac12(-a^{-2})(-2aa') = \mathcal H\). For part 4: differentiating gives \(2a_\eta a_{\eta\eta} = H_0^2\Omega_{\mathrm m}a_\eta\), so \(a_{\eta\eta} = \tfrac12H_0^2\Omega_{\mathrm m}\), a constant.

Worked solution

Idea. A second derivation in different coordinates, which cannot be reproduced from memory of the first. Conformal time makes light simple and matter-plus-radiation solvable, and it trades \(\ddot a\) for \(\mathcal H'\).

1. \(g_{\mu\nu} = a^2\eta_{\mu\nu}\), \(g^{\mu\nu} = a^{-2}\eta^{\mu\nu}\), and only \(\partial_0 g_{\mu\nu} = 2aa'\eta_{\mu\nu}\) is nonzero. So \(\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} = \mathcal H\,(\delta^\lambda_\mu\delta^0_\nu + \delta^\lambda_\nu\delta^0_\mu - \eta^{\lambda0}\eta_{\mu\nu})\), that is

\[ \Gamma^0_{00} = \mathcal H,\qquad \Gamma^0_{ij} = \mathcal H\,\delta_{ij},\qquad \Gamma^i_{0j} = \mathcal H\,\delta^i_j, \]

and all others vanish (\(\Gamma^0_{0i}\), \(\Gamma^i_{00}\), \(\Gamma^i_{jk}\)). Contractions: \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu0} = 4\mathcal H\), \(\Gamma^\mu_{\mu i} = 0\).

Scalar: \(R = -a^{-2}R_{00} + a^{-2}\delta^{ij}R_{ij} = a^{-2}\bigl[3\mathcal H' + 3\mathcal H' + 6\mathcal H^2\bigr] = 6(\mathcal H' + \mathcal H^2)/a^2\), and \(\mathcal H' + \mathcal H^2 = a''/a\), so \(R = 6a''/a^3\).

Source: the fluid is at rest, \(u^\mu = \mathrm dx^\mu/\mathrm d\tau = (c/a, 0,0,0)\) since \(\mathrm d\tau = a\,\mathrm d\eta\); so \(u_0 = -ac\) and \(T_{00} = (\rho + p/c^2)a^2c^2 - pa^2 = \rho c^2a^2\), \(T_{ij} = pa^2\delta_{ij}\). Einstein's equation with \(\kappa = 8\pi G/c^4\):

Convert: \(\mathcal H = \frac1{ca}\frac{\mathrm da}{\mathrm d\eta} = \frac{a\dot a}{ca} = \frac{\dot a}{c}\), and \(\mathcal H' = \frac1c\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\eta}\frac{\dot a}c = \frac{a\ddot a}{c^2}\). Idea. Unit 08's picture: a particle rolls in \(V(a)\) with energy \(\Omega_k/2\) (in units of \(H_0^2\)). It turns round where its kinetic energy vanishes, which is where \(f = 0\). A positive \(k\) makes the energy negative, but \(\Lambda\) pulls the potential down without limit, so negative energy is not enough to be trapped.

1. The first Friedmann equation divided by \(H_0^2\) and multiplied by \(a^2\) is \(\dot a^2/H_0^2 = f(a)\). Also \(f = \Omega_k - 2V/H_0^2\) with \(V/H_0^2 = -\Omega_{\mathrm m}/2a - \Omega_\Lambda a^2/2\), so \(f = 0\) is "energy equals potential". Today \(f(1) = 1 \gt 0\). If \(f\) is negative somewhere beyond \(1\), it first vanishes at some \(a_1 \gt 1\) with \(f'(a_1) \lt 0\) — the zero is simple, because \(af(a) = \Omega_\Lambda a^3 + \Omega_ka + \Omega_{\mathrm m}\) is a cubic with at most two positive roots and each is a simple root unless the two coincide. Then \(\ddot a = -V'(a_1) = \tfrac12H_0^2f'(a_1) \lt 0\) at a moment when \(\dot a = 0\), and the universe turns round. Conversely, if \(f \ge 0\) on \([1,\infty)\) the universe never recollapses: the only way to touch zero without crossing is a double zero, approached in infinite time (part 4).

2. \(\Omega_k = 1 - 0.4 - 0.7 = -0.1 \lt 0\), so \(k \gt 0\): closed. It expands for ever. Its minimum is \(f(a_{\mathrm{top}}) = 0.6073 - 0.1 + 0.3037 = 0.811 \gt 0\), so \(\dot a\) never vanished in the past either: it began at \(a = 0\). In the deck's language the energy \(-0.05\) is far above the potential's top, \(V_{\max}/H_0^2 = -\tfrac34\Omega_{\mathrm m}/a_{\mathrm{top}} = -0.455\).

3. \(\Omega_k = -1 - \Omega_\Lambda\). Tangency at \(a_c\): \(f'(a_c) = 0\) gives \(\Omega_\Lambda a_c^3 = 1\); then \(f(a_c) = 2/a_c - (1+\Omega_\Lambda) + 1/a_c = 0\), so \(a_c = 3/(1+\Omega_\Lambda)\). Substituting into the first,

\[ 27\,\Omega_\Lambda = (1 + \Omega_\Lambda)^3. \]

Iterate \(x \mapsto (1+x)^3/27\) from \(x = 0\): \(0.03704,\ 0.04131,\ 0.04182,\ 0.04188,\ 0.04189\). 4. Check. \(\Omega_\Lambda = 0\), \(\Omega_{\mathrm m} = 2\): \(f = 2/a - 1\) vanishes at \(a = 2\), recollapse, as unit 08's bound orbit and P8 say ✓. Everything here is dimensionless ✓.

For the radiation correction, expand P5's \(\eta(a)\) for \(a \gg \Omega_{\mathrm r}/\Omega_{\mathrm m}\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\chi_{\mathrm p}(1) \approx \frac{c}{H_0}\int_0^1\frac{2\,\mathrm du}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm m} + \Omega_\Lambda u^6}}\), with Simpson's nodes at \(u = 0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, 1\).

Worked solution

Idea. The patch of the last-scattering surface that one point there could have heard from has comoving radius \(\chi_{\mathrm p}(a_*)\); we see it from comoving distance \(\chi_{\mathrm{ls}}\); in flat space the angle is the ratio. The whole problem is getting two integrals right.

1. \(\Lambda\) is negligible: at \(a_*\), \(\Omega_\Lambda/(\Omega_{\mathrm m}a_*^{-3}) \sim 2\times10^{-9}\). P5:

\[ \chi_{\mathrm p}(a_*) = \frac{2c}{\Omega_{\mathrm m}H_0}\Bigl(\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm m}a_* + \Omega_{\mathrm r}} - \sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm r}}\Bigr) = \frac{2}{0.315}(0.019515 - 0.009597)\frac c{H_0} = 0.06297\,\frac c{H_0}, \]

that is \(280\) Mpc comoving (at the time, \(280/1091 = 0.26\) Mpc proper).

2. Without radiation, \(\chi_{\mathrm p}(1) = \frac c{H_0}\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm da}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm m}a + \Omega_\Lambda a^4}}\); with \(a = u^2\), \(\mathrm da = 2u\,\mathrm du\) and the root is \(u\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm m} + \Omega_\Lambda u^6}\), so the integrand is \(f(u) = 2/\sqrt{0.315 + 0.685u^6}\). Values at \(u = 0, \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, 1\): \(3.5635,\ 3.5625,\ 3.5044,\ 3.0257,\ 2.0000\). Simpson with \(h = \tfrac14\):

\[ \tfrac1{12}\bigl[3.5635 + 4(3.5625) + 2(3.5044) + 4(3.0257) + 2.0000\bigr] = \tfrac{38.9255}{12} = 3.2438. \]

Radiation correction: for \(a \gg a_{\mathrm{eq}}\), P5 gives \(H_0\eta = \frac{2}{\Omega_{\mathrm m}} \bigl(\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm m}a}\sqrt{1 + \Omega_{\mathrm r}/\Omega_{\mathrm m}a} - \sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm r}}\bigr) = \frac{2\sqrt a}{\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm m}}} - \frac{2\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm r}}}{\Omega_{\mathrm m}} + O\bigl(\Omega_{\mathrm r}a^{-1/2}\bigr)\). The first term is the radiation-free horizon; so by the time radiation is negligible, it has left a constant deficit \(2\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm r}}/\Omega_{\mathrm m} = 0.0609\), and afterwards the two integrands agree. Hence \(\chi_{\mathrm p}(1) = (3.2438 - 0.0609)\,c/H_0 = 3.1829\,c/H_0\).

3. \(\chi_{\mathrm{ls}} = 3.1829 - 0.0630 = 3.1199\,c/H_0 = 13\,880\) Mpc. Then

\[ \theta_{\mathrm H} = \frac{0.06297}{3.1199} = 0.02018\ \text{rad} = 1.16°. \]

A cap of angular radius \(\theta_{\mathrm H}\) has solid angle \(\approx \pi\theta_{\mathrm H}^2\), so the sky holds \(4\pi/\pi\theta_{\mathrm H}^2 = 4/\theta_{\mathrm H}^2 \approx 9800\) of them. (Numerical quadrature gives \(3.2402\) for the radiation-free integral, so Simpson's rule is high by \(0.0036\); with radiation it gives \(\chi_{\mathrm{ls}} = 3.1170\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm H} = 0.02020\) rad. Four Simpson steps were enough for three figures in the angle.)

4. Two points on the last-scattering surface in opposite directions are \(2\chi_{\mathrm{ls}}\) apart in comoving distance. Their past light cones intersect — so that some event could have influenced both — only if that separation is below \(2\chi_{\mathrm p}(a_*)\). The ratio is \(\chi_{\mathrm{ls}}/\chi_{\mathrm p}(a_*) = 49.5\). So no signal, and hence no physical process, has ever connected them, nor any two points more than about \(2.3°\) apart; yet their temperatures agree to one part in \(10^5\). The model does not contradict this. It must assume it, as an initial condition imposed separately on some ten thousand causally disconnected regions. The problem is that the observed uniformity is put in by hand, not explained.

Check. \(H_0\) cancels from the angle, as it must ✓. Leaving out radiation altogether gives \(\chi_{\mathrm p}(a_*) = 2\sqrt{a_*/\Omega_{\mathrm m}} = 0.1079\) and \(\theta_{\mathrm H} = 1.97°\): radiation matters at the forty per cent level, because recombination is only \(3.1\) times later in \(a\) than equality ✓. The deck's Einstein-de Sitter estimate, \(1.79°\), differs in having \(\Omega_{\mathrm m} = 1\) and neither radiation nor \(\Lambda\); all three versions agree that the angle is of order a degree, which is all the argument of part 4 needs.

exercises: comoving distance by quadrature, recession rates versus Doppler, lookback time, luminosity distance

A galaxy in the running example is seen at \(z = 1\).

  1. Find its comoving distance \(\chi = \frac c{H_0}\int_{1/2}^1\frac{\mathrm da}{a^2E(a)}\), by the substitution \(a = u^2\) and Simpson's rule with four steps. Give it in Mpc and in light years.
  2. How fast does its proper distance grow now, and how fast did it grow when the light left? Compare both with the speed unit 26's Doppler formula would assign to \(z = 1\).
  3. How long ago did the light leave? Compare \(c\) times that with \(\chi\).
  4. Its luminosity distance, \(d_L = (1+z)\chi\) for flat space. How much fainter is it than the naive \(d = cz/H_0\) predicts?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Four distances or speeds, all in units of \(c/H_0\) or \(c\) until the last step. None of them is the same as any other, which is the point.

Rung 2

The substitution turns the integral into \(\int_{1/\sqrt2}^1 2\,\mathrm du/\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm m} + \Omega_\Lambda u^6}\) (radiation is negligible at \(z = 1\)). Proper distance is \(a\chi\), so its rate is \(\dot a\chi = aH\chi\). The lookback time uses the deck's \(t(a)\) in closed form.

Rung 3

First line: \(h = (1 - 0.70711)/4 = 0.073223\), nodes \(u = 0.70711, 0.78033, 0.85355, 0.92678, 1\). At emission the rate is \(\tfrac12E(\tfrac12)\chi\) in units of \(c\), with \(E(\tfrac12) = \sqrt{8\Omega_{\mathrm m} + \Omega_\Lambda}\).

Worked solution

Idea. In an expanding universe "the distance" and "the velocity" each split into several honest quantities. Computing them for one galaxy shows that none coincides with the special-relativistic reading of its redshift.

1. Integrand \(g(u) = 2/\sqrt{0.315 + 0.685u^6}\) at the five nodes: \(3.1598,\ 2.9184,\ 2.6264,\ 2.3109,\ 2.0000\). Simpson:

\[ \frac{0.073223}{3}\bigl[3.1598 + 4(2.9184) + 2(2.6264) + 4(2.3109) + 2.0000\bigr] = 0.024408\times31.3295 = 0.7647. \]

So \(\chi = 0.7647\times4448 = 3401\) Mpc \(= 11.09\) billion light years. (Quadrature: \(0.76468\).)

2. Now: \(\dot d = H_0\chi = 0.765c\). At emission: \(a = \tfrac12\), \(E(\tfrac12) = \sqrt{2.52 + 0.685} = 1.7903\), and \(\dot d = aH\chi = 0.5\times1.7903\times0.7647\,c = 0.684c\). Unit 26's Doppler formula, \(1 + z = \sqrt{(1+\beta)/(1-\beta)}\), gives \(\beta = \frac{(1+z)^2 - 1}{(1+z)^2 + 1} = \tfrac35 = 0.600\). Three different numbers, and no reason for any to agree: the redshift records only \(a(t_0)/ a(t_e) = 2\).

3. \(t(a) = \frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}}\operatorname{arsinh}\bigl(\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda/ \Omega_{\mathrm m}}\,a^{3/2}\bigr)\). At \(a = \tfrac12\): argument \(1.4747\times0.35355 = 0.52137\), \(\operatorname{arsinh} = 0.50024\), \(t = 0.8055\times0.50024\times14.51 = 5.85\) Gyr. Lookback \(13.80 - 5.85 = 7.95\) Gyr. The light travelled \(7.95\) billion light years of path, but the galaxy is now \(11.09\) billion light years away: the space behind the light kept stretching.

4. \(d_L = 2\times3401 = 6803\) Mpc, against \(cz/H_0 = 4448\) Mpc. Flux goes as \(d^{-2}\), so it is \((6803/4448)^2 = 2.34\) times fainter than the naive estimate. Comparisons of this kind, between \(d_L(z)\) and a decelerating model's prediction, are how acceleration was found in 1998.

Check. Small \(z\): \(\chi \approx cz/H_0\), \(d_L \approx \chi\), and all three speeds tend to \(cz\) ✓. Dimensions: \(\chi\) in units of \(c/H_0\) is dimensionless ✓. Radiation at \(a = \tfrac12\): \(\Omega_{\mathrm r}a^{-4} = 1.5\times10^{-3}\) against \(E^2 = 3.2\), negligible ✓.

exercises: the density parameter as a function of time, an unstable equilibrium, e-folds, a number

  1. With \(\Omega(t)\) the total density parameter (\(\Lambda\) included), show \(\Omega - 1 = kc^2/(a^2H^2)\) and hence \(|\Omega(a) - 1| = |\Omega_k|\,a^{-2}E(a)^{-2}\).
  2. Take \(|\Omega_k| = 0.002\) today, with matter, radiation and \(\Lambda\). Evaluate \(|\Omega - 1|\) at recombination and at nucleosynthesis, \(a = kT_0/(1\ \mathrm{MeV}) = 2.35\times10^{-10}\).
  3. During inflation \(H\) is nearly constant. Show that \(|\Omega - 1|\) then falls as \(e^{-2N}\) after \(N\) e-folds, and evaluate the factor for \(N = 60\). If inflation ends at \(kT = 10^{15}\) GeV, i.e. \(a_{\mathrm{end}} = 2.35\times10^{-28}\), how many e-folds bring an order-one \(|\Omega - 1|\) within today's bound?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

One identity and three evaluations. The identity is the first Friedmann equation divided by \(H^2\).

Rung 2

Write \(\rho_{\mathrm c}(t) = 3H^2/8\pi G\) and \(\Omega = \rho/\rho_{\mathrm c}\). Then \(kc^2/(a^2H^2)\) is \(-\Omega_k\) times \(H_0^2/(a^2H^2)\). At \(a_*\) and earlier, which term of \(E^2\) dominates?

Rung 3

First line: \(1 = \frac{8\pi G\rho}{3H^2} - \frac{kc^2}{a^2H^2} = \Omega - \frac{kc^2}{a^2H^2}\). At \(a_*\), \(E^2 = 9.21\times10^{-5}(1091)^4 + 0.315(1091)^3 + 0.685\).

Worked solution

Idea. \(\Omega = 1\) is an equilibrium, and in a decelerating universe a repelling one; so the nearer to \(1\) we find it now, the nearer it must have started.

1. Divide \(H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}3\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2}\) (with \(\rho_\Lambda\) in \(\rho\)) by \(H^2\): \(1 = \Omega - kc^2/(a^2H^2)\) ✓. And \(kc^2 = -\Omega_kH_0^2\), so \(\Omega - 1 = -\Omega_k/(a^2E^2)\).

2. At \(a_* = 1/1091\) the three terms of \(E^2\) are: radiation \(9.21\times10^{-5}\times 1091^4 = 9.21\times10^{-5}\times1.4168\times10^{12} = 1.305\times10^8\); matter \(0.315\times1091^3 = 4.091\times10^8\); \(\Lambda\), \(0.685\). So \(E^2 = 5.395\times10^8\) — radiation is still a quarter of it, the trap in this part — and with \(a_*^{-2} = 1.190\times10^6\), \(|\Omega - 1| = 0.002\times1.190\times10^6/5.395\times10^8 = 4.4\times10^{-6}\). At nucleosynthesis radiation dominates completely: \(E^2 \approx \Omega_{\mathrm r}a^{-4}\), so \(|\Omega - 1| \approx |\Omega_k|a^2/\Omega_{\mathrm r} = 0.002\times(2.35\times10^{-10})^2/9.21\times10^{-5} = 1.2\times10^{-18}\).

3. With \(H\) constant, \(a \propto e^{Ht}\) and \(a^2H^2 \propto e^{2Ht}\); after \(N\) e-folds, \(a\) has grown by \(e^N\), so \(|\Omega - 1| = |k|c^2/(a^2H^2)\) has shrunk by \(e^{-2N}\). For \(N = 60\): \(e^{-120} = 7.7\times10^{-53}\). From the end of inflation to today the universe is radiation-dominated almost throughout the growth of \(|\Omega - 1|\), so part 1's formula with \(E^2 \approx \Omega_{\mathrm r}a^{-4}\) gives the value today's bound demands at \(a_{\mathrm{end}}\): \(0.002\,a_{\mathrm{end}}^2/\Omega_{\mathrm r} = 0.002\times(2.35\times10^{-28})^2/9.21\times10^{-5} = 1.2\times10^{-54}\). Starting from order one, \(e^{-2N} \le 1.2\times10^{-54}\) needs \(N \ge \tfrac12\ln(8.3\times10^{53}) = 62\). The deck's horizon estimate for the same reheating temperature was \(59\): the two problems ask for the same number of e-folds, "about sixty", which is part of why one mechanism is offered for both.

Check. Dimensions: \([kc^2/(a^2H^2)] = \mathrm{m^{-2}m^2s^{-2}/s^{-2}} = 1\) ✓. Special case \(k = 0\): \(\Omega = 1\) at all times ✓. Single-component scaling: in the radiation era \(a^{-2}E^{-2} \propto a^2\), and in the matter era \(\propto a\), which is the deck's \(|\Omega - 1| \propto a^{1+3w}\) ✓.

P13 Helium from three numbers routine

exercises: a counting argument, exponential decay, sensitivity of a prediction to its inputs, a number

  1. If the neutron-to-proton ratio is \(x\) when nucleosynthesis happens and every neutron ends in \(^4\)He, prove that the helium mass fraction is \(Y = 2x/(1+x)\).
  2. At freeze-out, \(kT_f = 0.75\) MeV, the ratio is the Boltzmann factor \(x_f = e^{-\Delta mc^2/kT_f}\) with \(\Delta mc^2 = 1.293\) MeV (both cited: this course has no statistical mechanics). Free neutrons then decay, lifetime \(\tau_n = 878\) s, until nucleosynthesis at \(t \approx 200\) s. Compute \(Y\).
  3. Recompute \(Y\) for \(kT_f = 0.70\) and \(0.80\) MeV. Measured: \(Y_p = 0.245\pm0.003\). What does that say about the sensitivity of the prediction?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A counting argument, then an exponential applied to the right quantity. The right quantity is the fraction of nucleons that are neutrons, not the ratio \(x\).

Rung 2

Decay turns neutrons into protons, so it lowers \(X_n = n/(n+p) = x/(1+x)\) by \(e^{-t/\tau_n}\) while keeping the total. In terms of \(X_n\), \(Y = 2X_n\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(x_f = e^{-1.293/0.75} = 0.1784\), so \(X_n = 0.1784/1.1784\).

Worked solution

Idea. Helium is where the neutrons end up, so predicting \(Y\) is predicting the neutron fraction at about three minutes: a Boltzmann factor at freeze-out, then a radioactive decay.

1. With \(N_n\) neutrons and \(N_p\) protons, \(N_n/2\) helium nuclei form, of mass \(4\times N_n/2 = 2N_n\) nucleon masses, out of \(N_n + N_p\). So \(Y = 2N_n/(N_n + N_p) = 2x/(1+x)\), neglecting the neutron–proton mass difference and the binding energy, both under one per cent. \(\square\)

2. \(x_f = e^{-1.724} = 0.1784\), \(X_n = 0.1784/1.1784 = 0.1514\). Decay: \(e^{-200/878} = 0.7963\), so \(X_n = 0.1205\) and

\[ Y = 2X_n = 0.241. \]

(The textbook \(0.25\) rounds \(x\) to \(1/7\); here \(x = 0.1205/0.8795 = 0.137 = 1/7.3\).)

3. \(kT_f = 0.70\): \(x_f = 0.1577\), \(X_n = 0.1362\to0.1085\), \(Y = 0.217\). \(kT_f = 0.80\): \(x_f = 0.1986\), \(X_n = 0.1657\to0.1320\), \(Y = 0.264\). A \(7\%\) change in the freeze-out temperature moves \(Y\) by \(10\%\), because it sits in an exponent. The measurement, at about \(1\%\), therefore pins the freeze-out temperature, and with it the expansion rate at one second — which is how nucleosynthesis limits extra relativistic species. The full network calculation gives \(0.247\).

Check. Limits: \(x = 0\) gives \(Y = 0\), no neutrons, no helium; \(x = 1\) gives \(Y = 1\), all helium ✓. The exponents \(\Delta mc^2/kT\) and \(t/\tau_n\) are dimensionless ✓. A longer lifetime, \(\tau_n = 888\) s, raises \(Y\) only to \(0.2417\): the decay stage is the less sensitive one.
