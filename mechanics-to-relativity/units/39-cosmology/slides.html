Unit 35 ended by saying this unit drops two things at once: staticity and weakness. Unit 36 solves the field equations exactly by assuming spherical symmetry about one point. This unit solves them exactly by assuming far more symmetry — spatial homogeneity and isotropy — and the reward is correspondingly larger: the ten field equations become two ordinary differential equations, and one of them is the energy equation of unit 08.

This is a branch unit. Nothing later in the course depends on it, and it depends on nothing after unit 35. It also uses nothing from units 36 to 38: no Schwarzschild metric, no perturbation theory, no gravitational waves.

What is proved and what is assumed

The deck keeps three kinds of statement strictly apart. The cosmological principle is an assumption, and is labelled as one. The evidence for it is observational, and is stated separately, with its limits. Everything derived from it — the metric, the Friedmann equations, the age — is proved on the slides or in the notes, or labelled cited. Cosmology is a subject where these three are easily blurred, and blurring them is the most common way to believe more than has been shown.

39 · overview

One function of time

The universe on large scales, idealised as uniform: how old is it? The route, in four steps.

steps 1–2 · the geometry and its equation

1. Assume space looks the same everywhere and in every direction (the cosmological principle). Symmetry forces the FLRW metric: every distance between galaxies scales with one function $a(t)$, $a = 1$ today.

2. Einstein's equation for it (unit 33's machinery: Christoffels, Ricci, Einstein) collapses to one ODE, the first of the Friedmann equations, $H^2 = \tfrac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \tfrac{kc^2}{a^2} + \tfrac{\Lambda c^2}{3}$, with $H = \dot a/a$, $\rho$ the density, $k$ the curvature of space, $\Lambda$ unit 35's constant: unit 08's energy equation in disguise.

steps 3–4 · the numbers and the age

3. Energy conservation fixes how each density dilutes: matter as $a^{-3}$, $\Lambda$ not at all. With $\Omega_{\mathrm m}$, $\Omega_\Lambda$ their measured shares of today's density, flat space ($k = 0$) turns step 2 into $H(a) = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm m}a^{-3} + \Omega_\Lambda}$.

4. Since $\mathrm dt = \mathrm da/\dot a = \mathrm da/(aH)$, the age is one integral with a closed form: $$t_0 = \int_0^1\frac{\mathrm da}{aH(a)} = \frac{0.951}{H_0} = 13.8\ \text{Gyr}$$ for the Planck universe.

39 · assumption

The cosmological principle

the assumption

There is a family of fundamental observers — the mean motion of matter — and on large scales, at each instant of their common time, space is homogeneous (no point is special) and isotropic about each of them (no direction is special).

large scales only

It is false below roughly $100$ Mpc: galaxies, clusters, filaments and voids are not homogeneous. The claim is about averages over volumes larger than that, and the metric it produces describes the average, not the Milky Way.

the evidence, separately
  • The CMB: $T_0 = 2.7255$ K in every direction. Remove a dipole of $3.36$ mK — our own motion, $370\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ — and what is left varies by parts in $10^5$.
  • Galaxy surveys (SDSS, WiggleZ): counts in spheres approach the homogeneous $N \propto R^3$ above about $100$ Mpc.
  • Radio sources and X-ray background: isotropic to about a per cent.

Isotropy is observed only from here. Homogeneity is inferred: isotropy about every point implies it, and the Copernican assumption that we are not special supplies "every point".

Why the assumption is stated apart from the evidence. Observations are made from one point, on our past light cone. Isotropy about us is measured; homogeneity is not, because we cannot visit another galaxy's present. The logical chain is: isotropy observed here, plus the assumption that we are not privileged, gives isotropy about every fundamental observer; and isotropy about every point implies homogeneity (given two points $p, q$, take the midpoint $m$ of a geodesic joining them; the rotation by $\pi$ about $m$ in a plane containing the geodesic is an isometry swapping $p$ and $q$ — this is Weinberg, Gravitation and Cosmology (1972) §13.1, cited). So the only genuinely unobservable input is "we are typical".

The numbers. $T_0 = 2.7255 \pm 0.0006$ K (Fixsen 2009). The dipole amplitude $3.3621$ mK is a Doppler shift (unit 26's formula to first order: $\Delta T/T = v/c$), giving $v = c \times 3.3621\times10^{-3}/2.7255 = 369.8\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ for the Sun relative to the CMB rest frame. That frame is how "the fundamental observers" is made operational: they are the observers who see no dipole. The residual anisotropies are of order $10^{-5}$ in $\Delta T/T$ and are not noise: they are the seeds of structure, and their statistics are what Planck fitted to obtain the running example's parameters.

The homogeneity scale. Hogg et al. (2005) with SDSS luminous red galaxies and Scrimgeour et al. (2012) with WiggleZ found the fractal dimension of the galaxy distribution reaching $3$ within a per cent at about $70\,h^{-1}$ Mpc, which is $104$ Mpc for $h = 0.674$. "About $100$ Mpc" is that number rounded.

History. Einstein assumed homogeneity in 1917 for his static model, and Milne named the principle in 1933. It was a simplifying assumption for forty years before the CMB (Penzias and Wilson, 1965) made it an observational statement.

Maximally symmetric spaces

maximally symmetric space

A Riemannian $n$-manifold $(\Sigma, \gamma)$ whose Killing fields (unit 31) span the largest possible algebra, of dimension $n(n+1)/2$. Equivalently (cited): it is homogeneous, and at each $p$ every rotation of $T_p\Sigma$ is $\mathrm d\varphi_p$ for some isometry $\varphi$ fixing $p$.

Physics: the cosmological principle says each slice of constant time is one of these, with $n = 3$ and six Killing fields — three translations, three rotations.

theorem

A maximally symmetric space has constant sectional curvature: $$R_{ijkl} = K\,(\gamma_{ik}\gamma_{jl} - \gamma_{il}\gamma_{jk}),\quad K \text{ constant}.$$

proof

An isometry carries $R$ to $R$, since $R$ is built from $\gamma$ alone. At $p$, the isotropy isometries realise all of $SO(n)$ on $T_p\Sigma$, which acts transitively on 2-planes; so $K(\Pi)$ is the same for every plane at $p$, and unit 33's polarisation gives the displayed form. Homogeneity moves $p$ anywhere, so $K$ is constant. $\square$

Full proof. Three steps.

Isometries preserve curvature. If $\varphi$ is an isometry, $\varphi^*\gamma = \gamma$. The Levi-Civita connection is determined by $\gamma$ (unit 32's Koszul formula), so $\varphi$ carries it to itself, and hence carries $R$ to itself: $R_{\varphi(p)}(\mathrm d\varphi X, \mathrm d\varphi Y, \mathrm d\varphi Z, \mathrm d\varphi W) = R_p(X,Y,Z,W)$. Consequently sectional curvature satisfies $K(\mathrm d\varphi_p\Pi) = K(\Pi)$.

$SO(n)$ is transitive on 2-planes. Given planes $\Pi, \Pi'$ with orthonormal bases $(e_1, e_2)$ and $(e_1', e_2')$, extend each to an orthonormal basis of $T_p\Sigma$ and, if necessary, flip the sign of the last vector of one of them so both have the same orientation ($n \ge 3$; for $n = 2$ there is only one plane). The linear map sending one basis to the other is orthogonal with determinant $+1$ and carries $\Pi$ to $\Pi'$. So if every element of $SO(n)$ is realised by an isometry fixing $p$, all planes at $p$ have the same sectional curvature $K(p)$.

Polarisation. The tensor $K(p)(\gamma_{ik}\gamma_{jl} - \gamma_{il}\gamma_{jk})$ has all of unit 33's symmetries and sectional curvature $K(p)$ on every plane (unit 33's check). Two algebraic curvature tensors with the same sectional curvatures are equal (unit 33, cited there from do Carmo ch. 4, Lemma 3.3). So $R_p$ has the displayed form.

Constancy. Homogeneity gives, for any $p, q$, an isometry $\varphi$ with $\varphi(p) = q$, and then $K(q) = K(p)$ by the first step. $\square$

Cited, not proved. (i) The equivalence between "Killing algebra of dimension $n(n+1)/2$" and "homogeneous and isotropic": unit 31 cited the bound; the equivalence is Weinberg (1972) §13.1–13.2. (ii) The converse direction that matters for uniqueness: two Riemannian metrics of the same constant curvature $K$ and dimension are locally isometric (Riemann 1854; a proof via Cartan's theorem is in do Carmo, Riemannian Geometry, ch. 8, on space forms). So constant curvature fixes the geometry locally up to the one number $K$.

An aside worth having. For $n \ge 3$ homogeneity is not even needed: Schur's lemma (cited; it is among the exercises of do Carmo ch. 4) says that if $K(p)$ is independent of the plane at each point, the contracted Bianchi identity forces it to be independent of the point.

Three model spaces

Up to local isometry there is one maximally symmetric 3-space for each $K = k$. With $\chi$ the geodesic distance from a chosen point, $$\gamma = \mathrm d\chi^2 + S_k(\chi)^2\,\mathrm d\Omega^2,$$ with $S_k$ from the table; or, with $r = S_k(\chi)$, the form used on the FLRW metric: $\gamma = \mathrm dr^2/(1 - kr^2) + r^2\,\mathrm d\Omega^2$.

check

Unit 32's Christoffels give $R_{ij} = 2k\,\gamma_{ij}$ (P4); in three dimensions Ricci determines Riemann (unit 33), so the curvature is $k$. As $k \to 0$, $S_k(\chi) \to \chi$: Euclidean polar coordinates ✓.

Why $\mathrm d\chi^2 + S_k^2\,\mathrm d\Omega^2$. Choose a point and use geodesic polar coordinates about it: $\chi$ is geodesic distance, and the spheres $\chi = $ const are orbits of the rotations fixing the point, so by isotropy they are round, with some area $4\pi S(\chi)^2$. The metric is then $\mathrm d\chi^2 + S(\chi)^2\,\mathrm d\Omega^2$ (Gauss's lemma, that geodesic spheres are orthogonal to the radial geodesics, is cited from do Carmo ch. 3). The sectional curvature of the plane spanned by $\partial_\chi$ and $\partial_\theta$ is $-S''/S$: unit 32's formula gives $\Gamma^\chi_{\theta\theta} = -SS'$, $\Gamma^\theta_{\chi\theta} = S'/S$ and $\Gamma^\chi_{\chi\lambda} = 0$, so $R^\chi{}_{\theta\chi\theta} = \partial_\chi\Gamma^\chi_{\theta\theta} - \Gamma^\chi_{\theta\theta}\Gamma^\theta_{\chi\theta} = -(S'^2 + SS'') + S'^2 = -SS''$, and dividing by the Gram determinant $1\cdot S^2$ gives $K = -S''/S$. Constant curvature $k$ therefore means $S'' = -kS$ with $S(0) = 0$, $S'(0) = 1$ (so that the space is smooth at the centre). That is unit 06's constant- coefficient ODE, and its unique solution is the $S_k$ in the box.

The $r$ form. With $r = S_k(\chi)$, $\mathrm dr = S_k'(\chi)\,\mathrm d\chi$ and $S_k'^2 = 1 - kS_k^2$ in all three cases (for $k > 0$ this is $\cos^2 = 1 - \sin^2$). So $\mathrm d\chi^2 = \mathrm dr^2/(1 - kr^2)$. For $k > 0$ the $r$ coordinate covers only half the 3-sphere, since $r$ rises to $1/\sqrt k$ at the equator and falls again: the coordinate singularity at $kr^2 = 1$ is unit 05's lesson about spherical coordinates again, not a feature of the space.

Circles in the three model spaces

Two-dimensional analogues, schematic. From the centre walk a geodesic distance $\chi$ in every direction: the circle traced has circumference $2\pi S_k(\chi)$. On the sphere the radial geodesics reconverge, so the circle is short; on the saddle they spread apart, and the circle has to ruffle to fit.

local, not global

Curvature fixes the geometry locally. A flat 3-torus has $k = 0$ and finite volume. "Flat means infinite" is not a theorem.

What to look for. The three pictures are 2-dimensional slices of the 3-dimensional model spaces, which cannot themselves be drawn. In each, the dot is the chosen point, the straight-looking line is a radial geodesic of length $\chi$, and the thick curve is the set of points at geodesic distance $\chi$. Only the sphere is drawn exactly. The saddle is a surface of negative curvature only near its centre, and its curvature is not constant; no surface in ordinary 3-space is a complete hyperbolic plane (Hilbert's theorem, cited from do Carmo, Differential Geometry of Curves and Surfaces, §5-11), which is why hyperbolic space is always drawn by some distorted model.

The circumference column. A circle at geodesic distance $\chi$ has circumference $2\pi S_k(\chi)$, and $\sin x \lt x \lt \sinh x$ for $x > 0$. Compare unit 30's Ehrenfest disc, which had circumference greater than $2\pi\chi$: negative spatial curvature, forced there by rotation. This is also how curvature would be measured from inside, with no embedding: count the galaxies, or measure the angular sizes, out to a given distance, and compare with the Euclidean $4\pi\chi^2$ and $2\pi\chi$.

Topology. Observations constrain only $k$ and only within the observable region. A non-trivial topology smaller than the horizon would repeat patterns in the CMB, and searches for matched circles have found none, so any identification scale exceeds roughly the distance to the last-scattering surface (Planck 2015 XVIII, cited).

The FLRW metric, and why there is no other

theorem (FLRW metric)

If spacetime is foliated by spacelike slices orthogonal to the worldlines of fundamental observers, and each slice is homogeneous and isotropic about each of them, then there are coordinates in which $$\mathrm ds^2 = -c^2\,\mathrm dt^2 + a(t)^2\Bigl[\frac{\mathrm dr^2}{1-kr^2} + r^2\,\mathrm d\Omega^2\Bigr].$$

  • $t$ is cosmic time: proper time along every fundamental observer.
  • $(r, \theta, \phi)$ are comoving coordinates: each observer keeps fixed values.
  • $a(t)$ is the scale factor, dimensionless, $a(t_0) = 1$ today; $k$ is the curvature constant of the previous slides, in $\mathrm m^{-2}$; $\mathrm d\Omega^2 = \mathrm d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,\mathrm d\phi^2$.
  • The bracket is the constant-curvature metric $\gamma$ of Three model spaces, in its $r$ form. The whole of the time dependence is the one factor $a(t)^2$.

The proof is drawn on the next slide.

Reading the metric. Along a fundamental observer ($r, \theta, \phi$ fixed), $\mathrm ds^2 = -c^2\mathrm dt^2$, so $t$ is that observer's proper time. Within a slice ($t$ fixed), $\mathrm ds^2 = a(t)^2\gamma$: the same shape at every time, uniformly rescaled. Those two sentences are the theorem's content; the next slide proves it.

Cited. The same theorem, with the step "the symmetry group of spacetime acts on the slices" treated more carefully, is Wald, General Relativity (1984) §5.1, and Weinberg (1972) §14.1–14.2. Friedmann (1922, 1924) wrote the metric by assumption; Robertson (1935) and Walker (1936) proved independently that the symmetries force it, which is why the four names are attached. Lemaître (1927), the L in "FLRW", found the expanding solution independently and was the first to connect it with observed redshifts.

What the theorem does not say. It says nothing about $a(t)$ or the sign of $k$. Those are what the Einstein equations and the matter content decide, in section 2. The geometry has been reduced from ten functions of four variables to one function of one variable and one number.

Slices, observers and one scale factor

Schematic. Each observer keeps its comoving label from slice to slice; what changes is the spacing, by the same factor $a(t_2)/a(t_1)$ everywhere and in every direction.

proof, the three moves

1. Coordinates. Label each observer by where it crosses one slice, and each slice by the observers' proper time.

2. No cross terms. $g_{0i}$ would be a covector on the slice, and a rotation-invariant covector is zero: the worldlines meet the slices at right angles.

3. One factor. The slice metrics at two times differ by a map commuting with all rotations and translations: a constant factor, $a(t_2)^2/a(t_1)^2$.

Full proof in the notes.

What the figure shows. Two slices of constant cosmic time and nine fundamental observers, one dimension of space suppressed. The observers are drawn fanning out, which is how the separations look; in comoving coordinates the same worldlines are vertical, and that is move 1. Move 2 is that each worldline crosses each slice at a right angle, in the spacetime metric. Move 3 is the two brackets: one ratio, the same for every pair of neighbours and every direction.

Full proof. Let $u$ be the unit timelike field of fundamental observers, orthogonal to the slices $\Sigma$.

Step 1, coordinates. Pick one slice $\Sigma_*$ and coordinates $x^i$ on it; give every event the $x^i$ of the observer through it. Along one observer, proper time between two slices is the same for all observers, since an isometry of the slices (a symmetry of spacetime, by assumption) carries any observer to any other and preserves proper time; so label slices by that proper time $t$. Then $g_{00} = -1$: the observers' worldlines are the curves $x^i = $ const and $t$ is proper time along them.

Step 2, no cross terms. $g_{0i}\,\mathrm dx^i$ restricted to a slice is a covector field. Isotropy about a point $p$ means every rotation of $T_p\Sigma$ is induced by a symmetry fixing $p$ and $u$; such a symmetry preserves the covector, so $\omega_p$ is invariant under $SO(3)$. If $\omega_p \ne 0$, some rotation sends its dual vector $v$ to $-v$; invariance then gives $\omega_p = -\omega_p$. So $g_{0i} = 0$. (This is also simply "the observers are orthogonal to the slices", and problem P3 proves the lemma cleanly.)

Step 3, one scale factor. Now $g = -c^2\mathrm dt^2 + h_t$ with $h_t$ a metric on the comoving 3-space, maximally symmetric for each $t$. Compare $h_t$ and $h_{t'}$ at a point $p$: both are inner products on the same space $T_p$, both invariant under the same $SO(3)$ (the symmetries fix the comoving labels up to the isometry). The quadratic form $v \mapsto h_t(v,v)/ h_{t'}(v,v)$ is then constant on the $h_{t'}$-unit sphere, because $SO(3)$ is transitive on it; so $h_t = \lambda(t, t', p)\,h_{t'}$ by polarisation. Homogeneity makes $\lambda$ independent of $p$. Fix $t' = t_0$, write $\lambda = a(t)^2$ and $h_{t_0} = \gamma$, which by the previous slides is the constant-curvature metric with some $k$. $\square$

Proper distance, Hubble's law, conformal time

proper distance

The length, in the slice at time $t$, of the radial geodesic to a comoving object at $\chi$: $d(t) = a(t)\,\chi$.

Hubble's law, exact

$\dot d = \dot a\,\chi = H(t)\,d$, with Hubble parameter $H = \dot a/a$.

One line: $\chi$ is constant. $[H] = \mathrm s^{-1}$; for the running example $H_0 = 67.4\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}Mpc^{-1}} = 2.184\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm s^{-1}$.

not a velocity

$\dot d$ is a rate of change of distance within slices. It exceeds $c$ beyond $c/H_0 = 4448$ Mpc $= 14.5$ Gly, and nothing is violated: unit 26's speed limit is local.

Computed for the running example. Comoving galaxies (grey) separate as $d = a(t)\chi$, each with slope $\dot d = Hd$. On the dashed Hubble sphere, $d = c/H$, that slope is $c$. Our past light cone is a teardrop: light reaching us from far away left its source when the source was near, never more than $5.85$ Gly off.

Why $d = a\chi$. In the slice $t = $ const the metric is $a(t)^2\gamma$, and along a radial path $\gamma = \mathrm d\chi^2$. So the length is $\int_0^\chi a(t)\,\mathrm d\chi' = a(t)\chi$. It is the distance a chain of fundamental observers would measure by laying rulers end to end at one instant of cosmic time — a definition, not something anyone can do.

Dimensions and the unit of $H_0$. $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$ is a velocity per distance, so an inverse time: $67.4\times10^3\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} / 3.0857\times10^{22}\ \mathrm m = 2.184\times10^{-18}\ \mathrm s^{-1}$. Its reciprocal, the Hubble time, is $4.578\times10^{17}$ s $= 14.51$ Gyr, and $c/H_0 = 4448$ Mpc $= 14.51$ Gly.

How the figure was made. Time runs up and proper distance across, as in unit 26's spacetime diagrams; we sit on the vertical axis. The galaxies are $d = a(t)\chi$ with $a(t)$ the closed form of worked example 3 (notes there), for $\chi = 5, 10, \dots, 25$ Gly. The Hubble sphere is $c/H(t)$; it is $14.5$ Gly today and tends to $c/(H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}) = 17.5$ Gly. The light cone is $d = a(t)\,c\,[\eta(t_0) - \eta(t)]$, with the conformal time of the next slide. Its widest point, $5.85$ Gly at $z = 1.59$, $4.05$ Gyr after the Big Bang, is exactly where it crosses the Hubble sphere: there light moving towards us at $c$ through space that recedes at $c$ holds its distance.

Superluminal recession. Unit 26's statement is that no causal curve leaves the light cone at any event, and it is about vectors at one point. $\dot d$ compares a distance defined across a whole slice at two times, and there is no tangent vector at one event whose length it is. Section 5 returns to this, with numbers, when it says why redshift is not a Doppler shift.

Conformal time straightens light rays

conformal time

$\mathrm d\eta = \mathrm dt/a(t)$. Then $$\mathrm ds^2 = a(\eta)^2\bigl[-c^2\,\mathrm d\eta^2 + \mathrm d\chi^2 + S_k(\chi)^2\mathrm d\Omega^2\bigr]$$ and radial light rays obey $\mathrm d\chi = \pm c\,\mathrm d\eta$: straight lines at $45°$, as in unit 26.

The factor $a^2$ in front changes lengths, not which curves are null. So the causal structure — who can signal whom — is that of the static bracket, and it can be read off with a ruler.

Our past light cone, computed for the running example with radiation, drawn twice on one grid. Against $\eta$ it is straight. Against $t$ it is bent: light now arriving from $46$ Gly covered $19$ Gly of the way in the first billion years, when $a$ was small.

Conformal time. The substitution is the whole content: $-c^2\mathrm dt^2 = -c^2a^2 \mathrm d\eta^2$. The causal structure of FLRW is that of the static metric in the bracket, because a conformal factor does not change which curves are null. That is why horizons in section 5 are computed as $\eta$-intervals, and why a Penrose-style diagram of FLRW is easy to draw. With $\mathrm d\eta = \mathrm dt/a$, $\eta$ has units of time; some books use $c\,\mathrm dt/a$ and give it units of length.

How the figure was made. A radial ray arriving at $\chi = 0$ today has $\chi = c[\eta_0 - \eta]$, and $\eta(a) = \int_0^a \mathrm da'/(a'^2H)$ was integrated numerically with $\Omega_{\mathrm r} = 9.21\times10^{-5}$, giving $\eta_0 = 46.1$ Gyr; $t(a) = \int_0^a\mathrm da'/(a'H)$ the same way. Both curves are the same set of events, plotted against two different time coordinates measured in the same unit. The straight one is the one to draw by hand; the bent one is what the same ray looks like in the time clocks keep.

The $19$ Gly. $\eta$ at $t = 1$ Gyr ($a = 0.150$) is $19.2$ Gyr, $42\%$ of $\eta_0$: in the early universe $a$ was small, so each second of cosmic time was many seconds of conformal time, and light crossed a great deal of comoving distance.

The Christoffel symbols of FLRW

worked example 1 · from the metric to the Einstein tensor, part 1

Write $g_{00} = -1$, $g_{ij} = a^2\gamma_{ij}$, $x^0 = ct$, so $\partial_0 = c^{-1}\partial_t$. Unit 32: $\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12 g^{\lambda\sigma}(\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu})$.

$\lambda = 0$. $g^{00} = -1$ and $g_{0\nu}$ is constant, so only $-\partial_0 g_{\mu\nu}$ survives: $$\Gamma^0_{ij} = \tfrac12\partial_0(a^2\gamma_{ij}) = \frac{a\dot a}{c}\,\gamma_{ij},\quad \Gamma^0_{00} = \Gamma^0_{0i} = 0.$$

$\lambda = i$, one time index. $\Gamma^i_{0j} = \tfrac12 g^{ik}\partial_0 g_{kj} = \tfrac12 a^{-2}\gamma^{ik}\cdot\tfrac{2a\dot a}{c}\gamma_{kj}$: $$\Gamma^i_{0j} = \frac{\dot a}{ca}\,\delta^i_j,\qquad \Gamma^i_{00} = 0.$$

All spatial. The factor $a^2$ cancels between $g^{ik}$ and $\partial g$: $\Gamma^i_{jk} = {}^{(3)}\Gamma^i_{jk}$, the Christoffels of $\gamma$.

Check. $[\Gamma] = \mathrm m^{-1}$: $[\dot a/(ca)] = \mathrm s^{-1}/(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}})$ ✓. For $a = $ const every time-bearing symbol vanishes and we are left with a static space ✓. Consequence used later: $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu 0} = 3\dot a/(ca)$.

The shortcut, and a cross-check. Unit 32 proved that geodesics are the stationary curves of $\int g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu\,\mathrm d\lambda$, so the Christoffels can be read off the Euler-Lagrange equations of $L = -(\dot x^0)^2 + a(x^0)^2\gamma_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$ (dots now $\mathrm d/\mathrm d\lambda$). The source: a perfect fluid at rest in the slices

Unit 35's perfect fluid, with unit 22's stress $p\,\delta_{ij}$ as its spatial part: $$T_{\mu\nu} = \Bigl(\rho + \frac{p}{c^2}\Bigr)u_\mu u_\nu + p\,g_{\mu\nu}.$$ The fluid moves with the fundamental observers: $u^\mu = (c, 0, 0, 0)$, $u_0 = -c$. Hence $$T_{00} = \rho c^2,\quad T_{0i} = 0,\quad T_{ij} = p\,a^2\gamma_{ij},$$ $$T^\mu{}_\nu = \mathrm{diag}(-\rho c^2,\ p,\ p,\ p).$$

the form is forced

Any symmetric $T$ respecting the symmetries of FLRW has this form, with $\rho, p$ functions of $t$ only.

Why: $T_{0i}$ would be an invariant covector, so $0$. $T_{ij}$ restricted to $T_p\Sigma$ is an $SO(3)$-invariant symmetric form, hence $\lambda\gamma_{ij}$. Homogeneity makes $\lambda$ and $T_{00}$ constant on slices. (P3.)

dimensions of $\rho$

$\rho$ is a mass density, $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$, and $\rho c^2$ is the energy density; $p$ is in $\mathrm{Pa} = \mathrm{J\,m^{-3}}$. Books with $c = 1$ write $T^\mu{}_\nu = \mathrm{diag}(-\rho, p, p, p)$.

The isotropy lemma, in full. Let $B$ be a symmetric bilinear form on a 3-dimensional inner-product space $(V, \gamma)$ with $B(Qv, Qw) = B(v,w)$ for all $Q \in SO(3)$. Then the quadratic form $q(v) = B(v,v)$ takes one value $\lambda$ on the unit sphere, because $SO(3)$ acts transitively on it (any unit vector is carried to any other by a rotation about their common normal). So $q(v) = \lambda\gamma(v,v)$ for all $v$, and polarisation, $B(v,w) = \tfrac12[q(v+w) - q(v) - q(w)]$, gives $B = \lambda\gamma$. For a covector $\omega$: pick $Q$ the rotation by $\pi$ about an axis orthogonal to $\omega^\sharp$; then $Q\omega^\sharp = -\omega^\sharp$ and invariance gives $\omega = -\omega$. $\square$

Connection with unit 22. Unit 22 flagged that the triple (energy density, momentum density, momentum flux) would become $T^{\mu\nu}$. In the comoving frame the momentum density $T^{0i}/c$ is zero — nothing flows, on average — and the momentum flux is Cauchy's stress, which is isotropic, so it is a pressure: unit 22's "pressure as the isotropic part", which here is the only part.

The index positions. $T^\mu{}_\nu = g^{\mu\lambda}T_{\lambda\nu}$: $T^0{}_0 = g^{00} T_{00} = -\rho c^2$ and $T^i{}_j = a^{-2}\gamma^{ik}pa^2\gamma_{kj} = p\,\delta^i_j$. The mixed form is the one to remember because it has no factors of $a$.

What the "fluid" is. Galaxies are the molecules. For them $p$ is utterly negligible (random velocities of a few hundred $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$ give $p/\rho c^2 \sim 10^{-6}$), so they are dust. Radiation has $p = \rho c^2/3$, and a cosmological constant behaves as $p = -\rho c^2$; both are proved on the equation-of-state slide and in its notes. Several components add: $\rho = \sum\rho_i$, $p = \sum p_i$.

The Friedmann equations

Unit 35's $G_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = \kappa T_{\mu\nu}$, $\kappa = 8\pi G/c^4$. The $00$ component: $\frac{3}{c^2}\frac{\dot a^2 + kc^2}{a^2} - \Lambda = \frac{8\pi G}{c^2}\rho$. The $ij$ component, divided by $a^2\gamma_{ij}$: $-\frac{2a\ddot a + \dot a^2 + kc^2}{c^2a^2} + \Lambda = \frac{8\pi G}{c^4}p$. Rearranging the first, and subtracting it from the second:

the Friedmann equations $$H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}$$ $$\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\Bigl(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\Bigr) + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}$$

The second is the acceleration equation.

dimensions

$[H^2] = \mathrm s^{-2}$. $[G\rho] = \mathrm{m^3kg^{-1}s^{-2}\cdot kg\,m^{-3}} = \mathrm s^{-2}$ ✓. $[kc^2/a^2] = \mathrm{m^{-2}\,m^2s^{-2}}$ ✓. $[\Lambda c^2]$ ✓. $[Gp/c^2] = [G\rho]$ since $[p] = [\rho c^2]$ ✓.

limits

Empty, $\Lambda = 0$: $H^2 = -kc^2/a^2$, the Milne universe ✓. $\Lambda$ only, $k = 0$: $H = c\sqrt{\Lambda/3}$ constant, $a \propto e^{Ht}$, de Sitter (1917) ✓. Static, $\rho = p = \Lambda = k = 0$: Minkowski ✓.

The rearrangement, written out. First equation times $c^2/3$: $\dot a^2/a^2 + kc^2/a^2 - \Lambda c^2/3 = 8\pi G\rho/3$, which is the first box. Second: multiply by $c^2$ to get $2\ddot a/a + \dot a^2/a^2 + kc^2/a^2 = \Lambda c^2 - 8\pi Gp/c^2$. Replace $\dot a^2/a^2 + kc^2/a^2$ using the first: $2\ddot a/a = \Lambda c^2 - 8\pi Gp/c^2 - 8\pi G\rho/3 - \Lambda c^2/3$, so $\ddot a/a = \Lambda c^2/3 - (4\pi G/3)(\rho + 3p/c^2)$ ✓.

Only two equations. Ten components, but $G_{0i} = T_{0i} = 0$ identically and the three diagonal $ij$ equations coincide. Symmetry has done the work that makes a PDE system into ODEs.

Pressure gravitates. The combination $\rho + 3p/c^2$ in the acceleration equation is the trace-reversed source of unit 35's weak-field computation, $T_{00} - \tfrac12 Tg_{00}$ per unit $c^2$, now with pressure kept. A positive pressure decelerates expansion even though it pushes outward, because in general relativity pressure is a source of gravity, not a force on the boundary — there is no boundary. Negative pressure below $-\rho c^2/3$ accelerates.

The running example. For the Planck universe, $k = 0$, $p = 0$ for the matter, and the first equation today reads $H_0^2 = 8\pi G\rho_{\mathrm m,0}/3 + \Lambda c^2/3$. Section 3 turns this into the density parameters.

History. Friedmann found both equations in 1922 (for $k > 0$) and 1924 ($k \lt 0$); Einstein first published a note claiming an error, then retracted it. Lemaître rederived them independently in 1927 and added the connection to galaxy redshifts.

route 1 · $\nabla_\mu T^\mu{}_0 = 0$

Unit 32's divergence of a (1,1)-tensor: $\nabla_\mu T^\mu{}_\nu = \partial_\mu T^\mu{}_\nu + \Gamma^\mu_{\mu\lambda}T^\lambda{}_\nu - \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}T^\mu{}_\lambda$. For $\nu = 0$:

Differentiate $\dot a^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho a^2 - kc^2 + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}a^2$: $\ 2\dot a\ddot a = \frac{8\pi G}{3}(\dot\rho a^2 + 2\rho a\dot a) + \frac{2\Lambda c^2}{3}a\dot a$. why twice is not a coincidence

Unit 33's contracted Bianchi identity makes $\nabla^\mu G_{\mu\nu} = 0$ identically, so the field equations imply $\nabla_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0$. It is the first law: $\mathrm d(\rho c^2 a^3) = -p\,\mathrm d(a^3)$.

Route 1 in full. $\partial_\mu T^\mu{}_0$: only $\mu = 0$, since $T^i{}_0 = 0$ and nothing depends on $x^i$; it is $\frac1c\partial_t(-\rho c^2) = -c\dot\rho$. Second term: $\lambda$ must be $0$ because $T^i{}_0 = 0$, and $\Gamma^\mu_{\mu0} = \Gamma^0_{00} + \Gamma^i_{i0} = 0 + 3\dot a/(ca)$. Third term: $\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu0}$ is nonzero only for $\lambda = i$, $\mu = j$, where it is $(\dot a/ca)\delta^i_j$; then $T^j{}_i = p\delta^j_i$ and the sum over $i, j$ gives $3p\dot a/(ca)$. Route 2 in full. The acceleration equation times $2a\dot a$: $2\dot a\ddot a = -\frac{8\pi G}{3}(\rho + 3p/c^2)a\dot a + \frac{2\Lambda c^2}{3}a\dot a$. Equate with the derivative of the first equation; the $\Lambda$ terms are identical and cancel; $k$ disappeared on differentiating. Divide by $\frac{8\pi G}{3}a$: $\dot\rho a + 2\rho\dot a = -(\rho + 3p/c^2)\dot a$, i.e. $\dot\rho a = -3(\rho + p/c^2)\dot a$ ✓. This needs $\dot a \ne 0$; at a turning point route 1 still holds.

The first law. $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\rho c^2a^3) = c^2a^3(\dot\rho + 3H\rho) = -3Hpa^3 = -p\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}a^3$. A comoving volume $a^3$ of fluid does work $p\,\mathrm dV$ as it expands, and its energy falls by exactly that. For radiation this is why photons lose energy; for $\Lambda$, with $p = -\rho c^2$, the energy in a comoving volume grows as $a^3$, consistently with $\rho_\Lambda$ being constant. Where the energy "comes from" has no answer, because unit 35 showed there is no global energy conservation law in a spacetime without a timelike Killing field, and FLRW with $\dot a \ne 0$ has none.

Structure. So only two of the three equations (Friedmann, acceleration, fluid) are independent. In practice one uses the first Friedmann equation and the fluid equation, which are both first order.

A Newtonian ball of dust

A uniform ball of dust expanding as $R(t) = a(t)\,r_c$. By unit 05's shell theorem, a particle on its surface feels only the mass inside, $M = \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho a^3r_c^3$, constant as it expands. Schematic. Around any fundamental observer, a sphere of dust of radius $R = a\,r_c$; the shells outside pull equally every way and drop out, leaving the mass $M$ inside to decelerate the surface.

The argument, and why it is legitimate as motivation. This is Milne and McCrea (1934). Take any comoving sphere, of any radius $r_c$, centred on any fundamental observer. By the shell theorem the matter outside a spherical cavity exerts no force inside it, so the particle on the sphere's surface responds only to $M$. motivation, not derivation

What the potential says

  • $\Lambda = 0$. $V = -A/a$ rises monotonically to $0$. Energy $\ge 0$ ($k \le 0$): expand for ever. Energy $\lt 0$ ($k > 0$): reach a turning point and recollapse. Unit 08's unbound and bound orbits, exactly.

The Einstein static universe

Einstein static universe

$\dot a = \ddot a = 0$ with dust. The acceleration equation gives $\Lambda = 4\pi G\rho/c^2$; the first then gives $k/a^2 = 4\pi G\rho/c^2 = \Lambda$: closed, with curvature radius $1/\sqrt\Lambda$.

The sign of $k$ is decided by whether the energy exceeds $0$, not whether it exceeds the top: with $\Lambda > 0$, a closed universe can expand for ever.

That is the previous figure again: any line between the top and $0$ is closed ($k > 0$) and still clears the top.

deceleration parameter

Every dust-plus-$\Lambda$ model today. Above the solid line space is closed, below it open; above the dashed line the expansion accelerates. The Planck universe sits on the first line and well above the second.

Cosmological redshift

cosmological redshift

Light emitted by a comoving source at $t_e$ and received by a comoving observer at $t_0$ has $$1 + z \equiv \frac{\lambda_0}{\lambda_e} = \frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_e)} = \frac{1}{a(t_e)}.$$

proof

Put the observer at $\chi = 0$. A radial null geodesic has $\mathrm ds^2 = 0$: $c\,\mathrm dt = a\,\mathrm d\chi$. A crest leaving $\chi_e$ at $t_e$ arrives at $t_0$, the next leaves at $t_e + \delta t_e$ and arrives at $t_0 + \delta t_0$: $$\int_{t_e}^{t_0}\frac{c\,\mathrm dt}{a} = \chi_e = \int_{t_e+\delta t_e}^{t_0+\delta t_0}\frac{c\,\mathrm dt}{a}.$$ Subtract: $\delta t_0/a(t_0) = \delta t_e/a(t_e)$ to first order. Periods scale as $a$, and so do wavelengths. $\square$

checks

$a$ constant: $z = 0$ ✓. Dimensionless ✓. Small distances: $a(t_e) \approx 1 - H_0(t_0 - t_e)$, so $z \approx H_0 d/c$ — Hubble's 1929 law, $v = cz = H_0 d$ ✓.

The CMB: $z = 1090$, so it left at $T = 2.7255 \times 1091 = 2974$ K, when hydrogen recombined, $3.7\times10^5$ yr after the Big Bang.

Why a radial null curve is a geodesic. Unit 32's geodesic equation for $\theta$ is $\ddot\theta + \Gamma^\theta_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = 0$. On a curve with $\theta, \phi$ constant only $\dot x^0, \dot x^r$ are nonzero, and from worked example 1 every $\Gamma^\theta_{\mu \nu}$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{\mu\nu}$ with $\mu, \nu \in \{0, r\}$ vanishes. So $\theta = $ const, $\phi = $ const is consistent, and the remaining two equations are solved by $\mathrm ds^2 = 0$ with a suitable affine parameter (unit 32's null case). By homogeneity any comoving observer can be put at $\chi = 0$.

First order. $\int_{t_0}^{t_0+\delta t_0}c\,\mathrm dt/a - \int_{t_e}^{t_e+\delta t_e} c\,\mathrm dt/a = 0$ after cancelling the common range, and each integral is its integrand times its length plus $O(\delta t^2)$. For light, $\delta t \sim 10^{-15}$ s and $a$ changes on a scale of $10^{17}$ s, so the approximation is exact to $30$ figures.

Temperatures. A black-body spectrum stays black-body under uniform stretching of all wavelengths, with $T \propto 1/a$ (cited: the Planck distribution is a function of $\lambda T$ only). This is why the CMB is still a perfect black body, and why $T(z) = T_0(1+z)$. The release time $3.7\times10^5$ yr was computed by integrating $\mathrm dt = \mathrm da/(aH)$ with radiation included, to $a = 1/1091$; the commonly quoted "380 000 years" differs only in rounding and in the precise definition of the moment.

Massive particles too. The same $\Gamma^0_{ij}$ that stretches wavelengths makes the momentum of a free massive particle fall as $1/a$ (P2's last part): "peculiar" velocities decay, which is why galaxies are, to good approximation, comoving.

Why it is not a Doppler shift

Unit 26's Doppler shift compares two four-velocities at one event. Source and observer here are at different events in curved spacetime, and unit 32 says vectors at different points cannot be compared without choosing a path. There is no "relative velocity" for a Doppler formula to take.

The redshift depends only on $a(t_0)/a(t_e)$: how much space stretched while the light was in flight — not on how fast anything was moving at emission or reception.

in numbers, for the running example

A galaxy at $z = 1$. Unit 26's formula would say $\beta = \frac{(1+z)^2 - 1}{(1+z)^2 + 1} = 0.600$. Its proper distance grows now at $H_0d = 0.765c$, and grew at $0.684c$ when the light left. Three numbers, none equal.

The CMB. Doppler would need $\beta = 1 - 1.7\times10^{-6}$. The last-scattering surface's proper distance grows today at $3.1c$.

but locally, yes

Over a short step between neighbouring comoving observers the shift is a Doppler shift, to first order; the total is a product of these along the ray. The global shift is not a single Doppler shift.

The numbers. For $z = 1$ the comoving distance is $\chi = (c/H_0)\int_{0.5}^1\mathrm da /(a^2E(a))$ with $E = \sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm m}a^{-3} + \Omega_\Lambda}$; numerically $0.7647\,c/H_0 = 3401$ Mpc. Today $\dot d = H_0\chi = 0.765c$. At emission the proper distance was $\chi/2$ and $H = 1.790H_0$, so $\dot d = 1.790\times0.5\times0.7647\,c = 0.684c$. For the CMB, $\chi_{\mathrm{ls}} = 3.117\,c/H_0 = 13\,864$ Mpc (radiation included), and $H_0\chi_{\mathrm{ls}} = 3.12c$. The special- relativistic $\beta$ for $1 + z = 1091$ is $(1091^2 - 1)/(1091^2 + 1) = 1 - 1.68\times10^{-6}$.

The local statement, with its proof sketched. Two comoving observers a proper distance $\delta d$ apart are, to first order, in one local inertial frame (unit 32's normal coordinates) with relative velocity $H\,\delta d$. Light crossing between them takes $\delta t = \delta d/c$ and is Doppler-shifted by $\delta\lambda/\lambda = H\,\delta d/c = H\,\delta t = \delta a/a$. Integrating $\mathrm d\lambda/\lambda = \mathrm da/a$ recovers $\lambda \propto a$ ✓. So "infinitesimal Doppler shifts, parallel-transported" and "stretching" are the same computation (Bunn and Hogg, Am. J. Phys. 77, 688 (2009), argue for the first reading). What fails is a single Doppler shift with a single velocity, and that is what the slide denies.

Superluminal recession, again. We receive light today from objects whose proper distance has always grown faster than $c$ (everything beyond $z \approx 1.48$ is now receding faster than $c$, where $H_0\chi = c$). No contradiction: that light was, at emission, moving towards us through space that was carrying it away, and the Hubble sphere has since grown past it.

Horizons

particle horizon

The comoving distance light has travelled since $t = 0$: $$\chi_{\mathrm p}(t) = \int_0^t\frac{c\,\mathrm dt'}{a(t')} = c\,\eta(t).$$ Nothing beyond it can have influenced us yet. Finite if the integral converges at $0$.

event horizon

The comoving distance light emitted now will ever travel: $$\chi_{\mathrm e}(t) = \int_t^\infty\frac{c\,\mathrm dt'}{a(t')}.$$ Events beyond it now can never influence us. Finite only if expansion accelerates enough.

two cases in closed form

Einstein-de Sitter: $\chi_{\mathrm p} = \int_0^a\frac{c\,\mathrm da}{a^2H} = \frac{c}{H_0}\int_0^a a^{-1/2}\mathrm da = \frac{2c}{H_0}\sqrt a$. Today $2c/H_0 = 3ct_0$. No event horizon: $\int^\infty a^{-1/2}\mathrm da$ diverges.

de Sitter: $\chi_{\mathrm e} = \int_t^\infty c\,e^{-H(t'-t_0)}\mathrm dt'$; proper $a\chi_{\mathrm e} = c/H$, constant.

Both horizons of the running example are drawn on the next slide.

Why the integrals. A radial light ray has $\mathrm d\chi = c\,\mathrm dt/a$, so the comoving distance covered between $t_1$ and $t_2$ is $\int_{t_1}^{t_2}c\,\mathrm dt/a$. Changing variable to $a$ with $\mathrm dt = \mathrm da/(aH)$ gives the $\int\mathrm da/(a^2H)$ forms used for computation. Dimensions: $[c\,\mathrm dt/a] = $ m ✓.

Convergence at $t = 0$. Near the Big Bang with $a \propto t^n$, $\int_0 t^{-n}\mathrm dt$ converges iff $n \lt 1$, i.e. iff the expansion decelerates. So every decelerating early universe has a finite particle horizon — which is the root of the horizon problem in section 6, and what inflation (accelerating, $n > 1$ effectively) is designed to change.

The $3ct_0$. With $t_0 = 2/(3H_0)$, $2c/H_0 = 3ct_0$: in Einstein-de Sitter the most distant matter we could in principle see is now three light-travel-times away, because the space the light crossed has stretched since.

why one must go far

Galaxies have peculiar velocities of order $300\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$. At $100$ Mpc, $H_0d = 6740\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$, so the contamination is $4.5\%$; at $10$ Mpc it would be $45\%$. The ladder exists because the nearby universe is too lumpy to measure the expansion.

flux and distance

The FLRW flux law (proved here, flat case). Light emitted at $t_e$ from a comoving source at $\chi$ is spread today over a sphere of proper area $4\pi\chi^2$ (flat slices). Each photon's energy is down by $1+z$, and the arrival rate is down by another $1+z$ (the period stretching of the redshift slide). For $k \ne 0$, replace $\chi$ by $S_k(\chi)$.

Supernovae and acceleration. Measuring $d_L(z)$ out to $z \sim 1$ and comparing with the prediction for each $(\Omega_{\mathrm m}, \Omega_\Lambda)$ is exactly how $q_0 \lt 0$ was found in 1998: the distant supernovae were fainter (further) than a decelerating universe allows.

Big Bang nucleosynthesis

Run $a \to 0$ in the radiation era: $T \propto 1/a$, $a \propto t^{1/2}$. At $T \sim 1$ MeV ($t \sim 1$ s) weak reactions stop converting neutrons and protons; after about three minutes deuterium survives, and almost every free neutron ends in helium-4. This is Big Bang nucleosynthesis.

helium from the neutron fraction

If the neutron-to-proton ratio is $x = n/p$ and every neutron ends in $^4$He, the helium mass fraction is $$Y = \frac{2x}{1 + x}.$$

Why: each $^4$He takes two neutrons and two protons, so its mass is $2\times$ that of the neutrons; per $1 + x$ nucleons, $2x$ are in helium.

the number

Freeze-out at $kT \approx 0.75$ MeV gives $x = e^{-1.293/0.75} = 1/5.6$ (Boltzmann factor, cited); free decay ($\tau_n = 878$ s) until $t \approx 200$ s lowers it to $x \approx 1/7$. Then $$Y = \frac{2/7}{8/7} = 0.25.$$ Measured primordial helium: $Y_p = 0.245 \pm 0.003$.

why it is a success

It predicted a quarter of all ordinary matter is helium, everywhere, before this was measured; and the deuterium abundance fixes the baryon density to agree with the CMB's, measured at $3.7\times10^5$ yr instead of $3$ minutes.

What is cited and what is computed. The course has no statistical mechanics, so the Boltzmann factor $e^{-\Delta mc^2/kT}$ for the equilibrium ratio, the freeze-out temperature and the time-temperature relation are cited (Weinberg, Cosmology (2008) §3.2; Kolb and Turner, The Early Universe, ch. 4). What is computed here: $\Delta mc^2 = 1.293$ MeV, $e^{-1.293/0.75} = 0.178 = 1/5.6$; the neutron fraction $x/(1+x) = 0.151$ decays by $e^{-200/878} = 0.796$ to $0.120$, i.e. $x = 0.137 \approx 1/7.3$, and $Y = 2\times0.120 = 0.24$. Rounding $x$ to $1/7$ gives the textbook $0.25$. The sensitivity is to the freeze-out temperature, and the full network calculation with Planck's baryon density gives $Y_p = 0.247$.

The $Y$ formula. Mass fraction $=$ (mass in helium)/(total baryon mass), neglecting the neutron-proton mass difference and the binding energy (both below one per cent). With $N_n$ neutrons and $N_p$ protons, $N_n/2$ helium nuclei are formed, of total mass $4\times N_n/2 = 2N_n$ nucleon masses, out of $N_n + N_p$: $Y = 2N_n/(N_n + N_p) = 2x/(1+x)$ ✓. Limits: $x = 0$ gives $Y = 0$; $x = 1$ gives $Y = 1$, all helium ✓.

Measurements. $Y_p = 0.2453 \pm 0.0034$ from metal-poor extragalactic H II regions (Aver et al. 2021). Primordial D/H $= (2.527 \pm 0.030)\times10^{-5}$ from absorption in quasar spectra (Cooke et al. 2018), which fixes $\Omega_{\mathrm b}h^2 \approx 0.022$ to within a few per cent, the spread coming from the nuclear rate for deuterium burning; the CMB gives $0.02237 \pm 0.00015$.

History. Alpher, Bethe and Gamow (1948) and Alpher and Herman's prediction of a relic radiation at about 5 K; Hoyle and Tayler (1964) for the helium fraction; Wagoner, Fowler and Hoyle (1967) for the network. Lithium-7 is over-predicted by a factor of about three, an unresolved discrepancy usually attributed to stellar astrophysics.

The horizon problem

worked example 4 · the angle a causal patch subtends, Einstein-de Sitter version

In EdS, $\chi_{\mathrm p}(a) = \frac{2c}{H_0}\sqrt a$. At recombination, $a_* = 1/1091$: $$\chi_{\mathrm p}(a_*) = \frac{2c}{H_0}\sqrt{a_*}.$$ The comoving distance from us to the last-scattering surface is what light covered since: $$\chi_{\mathrm{ls}} = \chi_{\mathrm p}(1) - \chi_{\mathrm p}(a_*) = \frac{2c}{H_0}(1 - \sqrt{a_*}).$$

In flat space a comoving length $\ell$ at comoving distance $\chi$ subtends $\theta = \ell/\chi$: $$\theta_{\mathrm H} = \frac{\sqrt{a_*}}{1 - \sqrt{a_*}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1091} - 1} = 0.0312 = 1.79°.$$

Checks. $H_0$ cancels, as it must for an angle ✓. $a_* \to 1$ (recombination today) gives $\theta \to \infty$: everything in causal contact ✓. $a_* \to 0$: $\theta \to 0$ ✓.

So a causal patch on the CMB sky is under two degrees across, in a sky of $4\pi$ steradians. The next slide draws why that is a problem.

The problem, stated precisely. In any FLRW model whose expansion decelerated all the way back to $a = 0$, the particle horizon at recombination is finite and subtends about a degree on today's sky. Regions further apart than that shared no past: no signal, and hence no process whatever, could have brought them to the same temperature. Yet they are at the same temperature to one part in $10^5$. The model does not contradict this; it simply requires it as an initial condition, imposed independently on ten thousand causally disconnected regions. The "problem" is that the explanation is put in by hand.

The flatness problem

flatness is unstable

With $\Omega(t) = \rho/\rho_{\mathrm c}(t)$ (all components), the first Friedmann equation is $$\Omega(t) - 1 = \frac{kc^2}{a^2H^2}.$$ In an era dominated by one component with $w$, $a^2H^2 \propto a^{-(1+3w)}$, so $$|\Omega - 1| \propto a^{1+3w}:$$ growing as $a$ for matter, as $a^2$ for radiation.

An unstable equilibrium: $k = 0$ keeps $\Omega = 1$ exactly, but any $k \ne 0$ is amplified in every era with $1 + 3w > 0$ — every decelerating era. The next slide runs today's bound backwards.

This is the flatness problem; the one before was the horizon problem. Both are fine-tuning of initial data, not contradictions.

Proof. Divide $H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - kc^2/a^2$ (with $\Lambda$ absorbed into $\rho$ as $\rho_\Lambda$) by $H^2$: $1 = \Omega - kc^2/(a^2H^2)$ ✓. With one component, $H^2 \propto \rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}$ (the curvature term being subdominant, which is the regime of interest), so $a^2H^2 \propto a^{-1-3w}$ ✓. Dimensions: $[kc^2/(a^2H^2)] = \mathrm{m^{-2}m^2s^{-2}/s^{-2}}$, dimensionless ✓. Special case: $k = 0$ gives $\Omega = 1$ for all time ✓ — flatness is an equilibrium, but an unstable one whenever $1 + 3w > 0$.

The dynamical-systems reading. Unit 02's language: $\Omega = 1$ is an equilibrium of the flow of $\Omega$ in $\ln a$, and for $w > -\tfrac13$ it is a repeller. Observing a system very near a repeller after a long time requires it to have started extraordinarily near. For $w \lt -\tfrac13$ — accelerating expansion — it becomes an attractor. That is the observation inflation is built on.

An honest dissent. Some cosmologists (Penrose, and later Carroll and others) argue that "fine-tuning" requires a measure on initial conditions, which nobody has, so the flatness problem may not be a well-posed problem at all. The horizon problem is harder to dismiss, because it is about correlations between causally disconnected regions rather than about a single number.

Inflation, and its status

inflation

A hypothesised early era with $\ddot a > 0$, nearly de Sitter, driven by a field with $w \approx -1$, ending well before nucleosynthesis.

the mechanism in one line

$\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(aH) = \ddot a$. So $\ddot a > 0$ exactly when the comoving Hubble length $c/(aH)$ shrinks.

  • Horizon: regions now far apart were inside one Hubble length before inflation; $\chi_{\mathrm p}$ becomes large.
  • Flatness: $|\Omega - 1| = |k|c^2/(aH)^2$ is driven towards $0$ as $aH$ grows.

The comoving Hubble length, computed under the notes' assumptions ($60$ e-folds, factors of $e$ in $a$, at constant $H$, ending at $10^{15}$ GeV). A comoving scale is a horizontal line: inside the curve before inflation, pushed outside during it, back in long after.

Proof of the one line. $aH = \dot a$, so $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(aH) = \ddot a$ ✓. The comoving Hubble length $c/(aH)$ is the comoving distance over which expansion is "fast", and in a decelerating universe it grows, so regions keep entering causal contact for the first time. During inflation it shrinks, so a region that was causally connected is stretched beyond it. After inflation it grows again and regions re-enter, already correlated.

How many e-folds. The horizon problem is solved if today's comoving Hubble length was inside the Hubble length when inflation began: $(a H)_{\mathrm{begin}} \le a_0H_0$. During near-de Sitter inflation $H$ is almost constant, so $aH$ grows by $e^N$ over $N$ e-folds; we need $e^N \ge (a_{\mathrm{end}}H_{\mathrm{end}})/(a_0H_0)$. In the radiation era $H = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm r}}a^{-2}$, so $aH = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm r}}/a$. If inflation ends at $kT = 10^{15}$ GeV, then with $T \propto 1/a$ (ignoring order-one factors from particle counting), $a_{\mathrm{end}} = kT_0/10^{24}\ \mathrm{eV} = 2.35\times10^{-28}$, and $(a_{\mathrm{end}}H_{\mathrm{end}})/(a_0H_0) = \sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm r}}/a_{\mathrm{end}} = 4.1\times10^{25}$: $N \ge \ln(4.1\times10^{25}) = 59$. The standard "about 60" is this computation; a lower reheating temperature lowers it.

During it, $H$ is held at its value at the end, $H_{\mathrm{end}} = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_{\mathrm r}}/a_{\mathrm{end}}^2$, so $c/(aH) \propto 1/a$, for exactly $60$ e-folds before $a_{\mathrm{end}} = 2.35\times10^{-28}$. The Hubble length now shrinks again: under $\Lambda$, scales are leaving once more.

evidence for

Its generic predictions are borne out: $|\Omega_k| \lesssim 0.002$; nearly scale-invariant fluctuations with spectral index $n_s = 0.965 \pm 0.004$, slightly below $1$ as predicted; correlations on scales beyond $\theta_{\mathrm H}$.

unresolved

No identified field; the required $\sim 60$ e-folds depend on an unknown energy scale; gravitational waves from inflation not detected ($r \lt 0.036$); initial conditions and a measure are open. It is the leading hypothesis, not an established part of the model.

Numbers cited. $n_s = 0.9649 \pm 0.0042$ (Planck 2018 X). $r \lt 0.036$ at 95% (BICEP/Keck 2021). Guth (1981) proposed inflation to solve the monopole problem and the horizon and flatness problems; Linde (1982) and Albrecht and Steinhardt (1982) repaired its exit. The prediction of the fluctuation spectrum (Mukhanov and Chibisov 1981; several groups 1982) is the part with the most evidential weight, and it needs quantum field theory, which this course does not reach.

Why "unresolved" is the honest word. Inflation makes generic predictions that are confirmed, but alternatives (bouncing cosmologies, varying speed of light proposals) have been constructed to match the same data, and the inflationary framework can accommodate a wide range of outcomes. Whether it is falsifiable in its general form is itself debated (Ijjas, Steinhardt and Loeb 2017, and the reply signed by Guth, Linde and some thirty others).
