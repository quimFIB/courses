Calculus finds its period; analysis justifies its power series; linear algebra sees its solution as an orthogonal matrix; metric spaces prove it has exactly one solution; groups see the motion as a group acting on the plane. The point is that the five topics are not five separate prerequisites: they are five views of the same objects, and the course uses them together.

Smallest cases first. One spring, five readings

The spring: a mass on a spring, in units where both are $1$, obeys $\ddot x = -x$. Write its state as $u = (x, v)$, $v = \dot x$:

$$\dot u = Ju,\qquad J = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix};$$

its solution from $u(0) = u_0$ is

$$u(t) = R(t)\,u_0,\qquad R(t) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos t&\sin t\\-\sin t&\cos t\end{pmatrix}.$$
sectionwhat it says about the spring
1 calculus movesthe period is $2\pi$; the series for $R(t)$ is $\cos$ and $\sin$
2 analysisthat series converges uniformly on bounded times only — enough to differentiate it term by term at every $t$
3 linear algebra$R(t)$ is orthogonal; $J$ has no real eigenvector
4 metric spacesthere is exactly one solution, and iterating finds it
5 groups$t\mapsto R(t)$ is a homomorphism; the orbits are energy circles

The state moves clockwise round a circle of constant energy.

Where the equation comes from. A spring pulls back with a force proportional to the stretch, $m\ddot x = -kx$. Measuring time in units of $\sqrt{m/k}$ makes $m = k = 1$; unit 00 explains why that change of units is allowed. Nothing in this deck needs the physics beyond that one line: the spring is here because it is the simplest equation on which all five pieces of background do real work.

Checking the solution. Differentiate $R(t)$ entry by entry: $\dot R = \begin{pmatrix}-\sin t&\cos t\\-\cos t&-\sin t\end{pmatrix}$, and multiplying out $JR(t)$ gives the same matrix. At $t = 0$, $R = I$. So $u(t) = R(t)u_0$ solves $\dot u = Ju$ with $u(0) = u_0$. Its first row says $x(t) = x_0\cos t + v_0\sin t$.

The direction of motion. In the $(x, v)$ plane with $v$ upwards, a mass released at $x = 1$ starts moving left, so $v$ goes negative: the state runs clockwise. That sign is a common place to slip and is drawn in the figure.

Three integration moves, each in its smallest case

substitution: spot the inner derivative

$\displaystyle\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,\mathrm dx$. The $2x$ is the derivative of the $x^2$ inside, so put $u = x^2$:

$$\int\cos u\,\mathrm du = \sin(x^2).$$

Check: the chain rule gives back $2x\cos(x^2)$.

parts: differentiate the polynomial away

$\displaystyle\int x\,e^x\,\mathrm dx$. Let $x$ be differentiated (it becomes $1$) and $e^x$ integrated (it stays $e^x$):

$$\begin{aligned}&x\,e^x - \int e^x\,\mathrm dx\\ &= (x - 1)\,e^x .\end{aligned}$$

Check: $e^x + (x-1)e^x = x\,e^x$.

a trig substitution: area of a circle

$\displaystyle\int_0^1\sqrt{1-x^2}\,\mathrm dx$, with $x = \sin\theta$: $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos^2\theta\,\mathrm d\theta = \frac\pi4$.

What each example isolates. Substitution is the chain rule read backwards: it works when the integrand contains a function and that function's derivative as a factor. Parts is the product rule read backwards: it trades one integral for another, and the trade is good when one factor gets simpler on differentiation and the other does not get worse on integration. A trig substitution uses $1 - \sin^2\theta = \cos^2\theta$ to get rid of a square root.

The circle as a check. The integral is the area under the upper half of the unit circle over $[0,1]$, a quarter disc, so $\pi/4$ is known before computing. The computation uses $\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12(1 + \cos2\theta)$, whose integral over $[0,\pi/2]$ is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac\pi2$.

worked · the period of the spring

Released from $x = 1$ at rest, its energy gives $x^2 + v^2 = 1$, so $|\dot x| = \sqrt{1-x^2}$, and time is $\int\mathrm dx/|\dot x|$. A full swing is four quarter-swings from $x = 1$ to $0$. With $x = \sin\theta$, $\mathrm dx = \cos\theta\,\mathrm d\theta$ and $\sqrt{1-x^2} = \cos\theta$: $$T = 4\int_0^1\frac{\mathrm dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} = 4\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\cos\theta}{\cos\theta}\,\mathrm d\theta = 4\cdot\frac\pi2 = 2\pi .$$

The table is a first guess, not an algorithm. Real integrands combine shapes — a root inside a rational function, a polynomial times a log of a quadratic — and the skill is applying two moves in the right order. The sheet's P1 asks for exactly that: two moves, chained.

Where the energy line comes from. Multiply $\ddot x = -x$ by $\dot x$: the left side is $\tfrac12\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}\dot x^2$ and the right is $-\tfrac12\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}x^2$, so $\dot x^2 + x^2$ is constant. Released from $x = 1$ with $\dot x = 0$, the constant is $1$. This "multiply by $\dot x$" move is how unit 00 integrates every force that depends on position alone.

Check. The answer $2\pi$ agrees with $x = \cos t$, which returns to $1$ first at $t = 2\pi$. Dimensions: time was measured in units of $\sqrt{m/k}$, so in seconds $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$, the familiar formula.

The integral is improper. The integrand is infinite at $x = 1$. That it still has a finite value is a fact of analysis, settled on Improper integrals: absolute and conditional in section 2. The substitution hid the problem, because $\cos\theta/\cos\theta = 1$ is bounded; that is legitimate, since the substitution is a smooth bijection on the open interval and the limits are taken afterwards.

R · calculus

The period integral, drawn: infinite at the end, finite in area

The quarter-period is the shaded area. It is finite although the integrand is not.

At $x = 1$ the mass stops and turns round, so $\dot x = 0$ and the time per unit distance, $1/|\dot x|$, is infinite. The mass still gets there in finite time.

Near the end, $1 - x^2 = (1-x)(1+x) \approx 2(1-x)$, so the integrand behaves like $(1-x)^{-1/2}$: a power below $1$, which is integrable.

the commonest slip

The factor of $4$. A period is out, back, out the other way and back: four quarter-swings, not two.

The comparison, exactly. On $[0,1)$, $1 + x \ge 1$, so $1 - x^2 \ge 1 - x$ and $\frac1{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \le \frac1{\sqrt{1-x}}$. The right side integrates to $\big[-2\sqrt{1-x}\big]_0^1 = 2$, so the quarter-period is finite and at most $2$; it is $\pi/2 \approx 1.571$. This is the comparison test for improper integrals, the subject of Improper integrals: absolute and conditional in section 2.

Why the power $\tfrac12$ is the general case. At any turning point of any smooth potential with nonzero slope there, the kinetic energy vanishes linearly in the distance, so the speed vanishes like a square root and the integrand blows up like $(\text{distance})^{-1/2}$. Unit 00 uses this for the pendulum, and also shows what goes wrong at a maximum of the potential, where the slope is zero and the period becomes infinite.

R · calculus · smallest cases

Partial fractions and the arctangent, in their smallest cases

two real roots: two logarithms

$x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$. Cover-up: at $x = 1$ the other factor is $2$, at $x = -1$ it is $-2$:

$$\frac1{x^2-1} = \frac{1/2}{x-1} - \frac{1/2}{x+1}$$

so $\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm dx}{x^2-1} = \frac12\ln\left|\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right|$.

no real roots: an arctangent

$1 + x^2$ cannot be split over $\mathbb R$, and it does not need to be:

$$\int\frac{\mathrm dx}{1+x^2} = \arctan x .$$

This is where $\pi$ enters: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm dx}{1+x^2} = \pi$.

a scaled one: pull the scale out

$x^2 + 4 = 4\big(1 + (x/2)^2\big)$; put $x = 2s$:

$$\int\frac{\mathrm dx}{x^2+4} = \frac12\arctan\frac x2 .$$

Completing the square reduces every irreducible quadratic to this.

The pattern. A real root of the denominator gives a logarithm; a pair of complex roots — an irreducible quadratic — gives an arctangent, plus a logarithm when the numerator has an $x$ in it. Every rational integral is a sum of these two kinds of piece, and the next slide does one with both.

Checks. Differentiating $\tfrac12\ln|x-1| - \tfrac12\ln|x+1|$ gives $\tfrac12\big(\frac1{x-1} - \frac1{x+1}\big) = \frac{1}{x^2-1}$. For the scaled one, $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dx}\tfrac12\arctan\frac x2 = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\frac{1}{1 + x^2/4} = \frac1{4 + x^2}$.

The cover-up rule, in words: to find the coefficient of $\frac{1}{x - r}$, cover the factor $x - r$ in the denominator and evaluate what is left at $x = r$.

Parts, partial fractions, and the arctangent that completes the square

integration by parts

$$\int_a^b f\,g'\,\mathrm dx = \big[fg\big]_a^b - \int_a^b f'\,g\,\mathrm dx .$$ Make $f$ the factor that simplifies when differentiated. A polynomial of degree $n$ needs $n$ rounds.

partial fractions

Over $\mathbb R$ every polynomial factors into linear and irreducible quadratic factors, and $p/q$ with $\deg p \lt \deg q$ splits as $\sum \frac{A}{(x-r)^k} + \sum\frac{Bx + C}{(x^2+bx+c)^k}$. Cover-up: for a simple root $r$, $A = p(r)/q'(r)$.

worked · an irreducible quadratic

$\displaystyle\int\frac{x+3}{x^2+2x+5}\,\mathrm dx$. The denominator is $(x+1)^2 + 4$, no real root.

1. Split the numerator into a multiple of the denominator's derivative plus a constant: $x + 3 = \tfrac12(2x+2) + 2$.

2. The first piece is $\tfrac12\ln(x^2+2x+5)$.

3. The second: $\displaystyle\int\frac{2\,\mathrm dx}{(x+1)^2+4} = \arctan\frac{x+1}{2}$.

Check: differentiating $\tfrac12\ln(x^2+2x+5) + \arctan\frac{x+1}2$ gives $\frac{x+1}{x^2+2x+5} + \frac{2}{(x+1)^2+4} = \frac{x+3}{x^2+2x+5}$ ✓.

The two facts behind partial fractions. That a real polynomial factors into linear and irreducible quadratic factors follows from the fundamental theorem of algebra (cited; every complex root comes with its conjugate, and the pair multiplies to a real quadratic). That the split has the stated form, with exactly that many unknown constants, is linear algebra: the constants solve a square linear system, and that it always has a solution is cited (Spivak, Calculus, chapter 19, the sheet's book for this).

Why the cover-up rule works. If $q(x) = (x-r)s(x)$ with $s(r)\ne0$, multiply the split by $x - r$ and set $x = r$: every other term dies, leaving $A = p(r)/s(r)$. And $q'(r) = s(r)$ by the product rule.

The general irreducible quadratic. $\int\frac{\mathrm dx}{(x-\alpha)^2+\beta^2} = \frac1\beta\arctan\frac{x-\alpha}\beta$. That one formula, plus completing the square, is where every $\pi$ in a rational integral comes from.

A number to check against. Over $[0,1]$ the integral is $\tfrac12\ln\tfrac85 + \arctan1 - \arctan\tfrac12 = 0.2350 + 0.7854 - 0.4636 = 0.5568$; the integrand falls from $0.6$ to $0.5$, so the answer must lie between those, and it does.

The half-angle substitution and the parameter trick, each in one line

half-angle: a trig integral becomes $\int\mathrm dt$

$\displaystyle\int\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{1+\cos\theta}$. With $t = \tan\frac\theta2$, $1 + \cos\theta = \frac{2}{1+t^2}$ and $\mathrm d\theta = \frac{2\,\mathrm dt}{1+t^2}$, so the integrand is just $\mathrm dt$:

$$\int\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{1+\cos\theta} = \tan\frac\theta2 .$$

Check another way: $1 + \cos\theta = 2\cos^2\frac\theta2$, and $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d\theta}\tan\frac\theta2 = \frac1{2\cos^2(\theta/2)}$.

differentiate a parameter: one integral gives another

Start from something trivial: $\displaystyle\int_0^1x^a\,\mathrm dx = \frac1{a+1}$ for $a\gt-1$.

Differentiate both sides in $a$; since $\frac{\partial}{\partial a}x^a = x^a\ln x$,

$$\int_0^1x^a\ln x\,\mathrm dx = -\frac1{(a+1)^2},$$

and at $a = 0$: $\displaystyle\int_0^1\ln x\,\mathrm dx = -1$.

Check: $\big[x\ln x - x\big]_0^1 = -1$.

The idea of the half-angle substitution is that $\tan(\theta/2)$ parametrises the circle rationally: $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = \big(\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}, \frac{2t}{1+t^2}\big)$. Rational functions of a point on the circle become rational functions of $t$, which partial fractions can always finish.

The idea of the parameter trick is that an integral you cannot do may be the derivative, in some parameter, of one you can. The $\ln x$ was produced by differentiating $x^a = e^{a\ln x}$. The integral $\int_0^1\ln x$ is improper (the log is $-\infty$ at $0$) and the differentiation needs a licence; Differentiating under the integral sign, and what licenses it says when it is given. Here it is: for $a\ge-\tfrac12$, $|x^a\ln x|\le x^{-1/2}|\ln x|$, which is integrable on $(0,1]$.

Two substitutions worth knowing by heart, and differentiating a parameter

half-angle substitution

$t = \tan\frac\theta2$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$ gives $$\cos\theta = \frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\quad \sin\theta = \frac{2t}{1+t^2},\quad \mathrm d\theta = \frac{2\,\mathrm dt}{1+t^2}.$$ Any rational function of $\sin$ and $\cos$ becomes a rational function of $t$.

worked

$\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{\mathrm d\theta}{2+\cos\theta}$: $t$ runs from $0$ to $1$, and $2 + \cos\theta = \frac{3+t^2}{1+t^2}$, so the integral is $\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{2\,\mathrm dt}{3+t^2} = \frac{2}{\sqrt3}\arctan\frac1{\sqrt3} = \frac{\pi}{3\sqrt3}\approx 0.6046$.

differentiate under the integral sign

Turn one known integral into a family. From $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}\,\mathrm dx = \frac1a$ for $a \gt 0$, apply $-\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm da}$ $n$ times: $$\int_0^\infty x^n e^{-ax}\,\mathrm dx = \frac{n!}{a^{n+1}} .$$

The move needs a justification, and that is analysis: Differentiating under the integral sign, in section 2.

check

$n = 0$ gives back $1/a$. At $a = 1$, $n = 2$: parts twice gives $\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-x}\,\mathrm dx = 2 = 2!$ ✓. Dimensions: if $x$ is a length, $a$ is an inverse length and both sides are $(\text{length})^{n+1}$ ✓.

Why the range matters for $t = \tan(\theta/2)$. The tangent blows up at $\theta = \pi$. On $(-\pi, \pi)$ the substitution is a smooth increasing bijection onto $\mathbb R$; on $[0, 2\pi]$ it is not, and integrating straight through gives nonsense. For an integral over a full period, use periodicity to move it to $(-\pi,\pi)$ first.

Deriving the three formulas. With $c = \cos\frac\theta2$, $s = \sin\frac\theta2$: $\cos\theta = c^2 - s^2 = c^2(1-t^2)$ and $1 + t^2 = 1/c^2$, which gives the first; the second is $2sc = 2tc^2$; and $\mathrm dt = \tfrac12(1+t^2)\,\mathrm d\theta$ gives the third.

Check of the worked example. The integrand runs from $\tfrac13$ to $\tfrac12$ over an interval of length $\pi/2$, so the integral lies between $0.524$ and $0.785$; $0.6046$ is inside, and numerical quadrature agrees to all digits shown.

The $n!/a^{n+1}$ formula at $a = 1$ is $\int_0^\infty x^ne^{-x}\,\mathrm dx = n!$, the integral that defines the Gamma function: $\Gamma(n+1) = \int_0^\infty x^ne^{-x}\,\mathrm dx$ for any real $n\gt-1$, which is $n!$ at the integers. The course meets this integral again in unit 21, and $\Gamma$ itself in unit 09's Bessel functions.

A tangent line, and how wrong it is

$e^x\approx1+x$, with the error pinned down

Taylor to first order: $e^x = 1 + x + \frac{e^\xi}{2}x^2$ for some $\xi$ between $0$ and $x$.

At $x = 0.1$, $1\lt e^\xi\lt e^{0.1}$, so the error lies between $\frac{x^2}2 = 0.005$ and $\frac{x^2}{2}e^{0.1} = 0.00553$.

Truly: $e^{0.1} - 1.1 = 0.00517$ ✓.

$\sqrt{1.1}$, one more term at a time

$\sqrt{1+x} = 1 + \frac x2 - \frac{x^2}8 + \frac{x^3}{16} - \cdots$. At $x = 0.1$:

$1.05$ (error $1.2\times10^{-3}$), then $1.05 - 0.00125 = 1.04875$ (error $5.9\times10^{-5}$).

True value $1.0488088$. Each term buys about one more correct digit, and the first omitted term, $x^3/16 = 6.3\times10^{-5}$, is the size of the error.

the idea

The polynomial says what the approximation is; the remainder term says how far it can be trusted. The first term you dropped is, to leading order, the error you made.

Why "the first dropped term is the error". The Lagrange remainder is $\frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}x^{n+1}$, and for small $x$ the point $\xi$ is close to $0$, so the remainder is close to $\frac{f^{(n+1)}(0)}{(n+1)!}x^{n+1}$ — the next term of the series. That is the working physicist's rule; the theorem on the next slide says exactly how close.

The coefficients of $\sqrt{1+x}$ are the binomial series with $\alpha = \tfrac12$: $\binom{1/2}{2} = \frac{(1/2)(-1/2)}{2} = -\frac18$ and $\binom{1/2}{3} = \frac{(1/2)(-1/2)(-3/2)}{6} = \frac1{16}$.

Taylor's theorem, with the remainder that says how wrong you are

Taylor's theorem, Lagrange remainder

If $f$ is $n+1$ times differentiable on an interval containing $0$ and $x\ne0$, then for some $\xi$ strictly between them $$f(x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}x^k + \frac{f^{(n+1)}(\xi)}{(n+1)!}\,x^{n+1}.$$

worked · how good is $\cos t \approx 1 - t^2/2$?

The $t^3$ term is $0$, so take $n = 3$: the remainder is $\frac{\cos\xi}{24}t^4$ with $0\lt\xi\lt t$. For $0 \lt t \le \pi/2$, $0 \lt \cos\xi \lt 1$, so $0 \lt \cos t - (1 - \tfrac{t^2}2) \lt \tfrac{t^4}{24}$. At $t = 0.5$: the error is $0.0025826$, inside $(\cos(0.5)\tfrac{t^4}{24},\ \tfrac{t^4}{24}) = (0.0022854,\ 0.0026042)$ ✓.

Each polynomial hugs $\cos t$ near $0$; the remainder term says exactly how far out the hug holds.

Why the remainder is the useful part. A list of coefficients says what the approximation is; the remainder says when it is good enough. And when $f^{(n+1)}$ is monotone on the interval — as $\cos\xi$ is decreasing here — the remainder is bracketed between its values at the two ends, which gives a two-sided bound rather than just an order of magnitude.

The lower end of the bracket. Since $\xi \lt t$ and $\cos$ is decreasing on $[0, \pi/2]$, $\cos\xi \gt \cos t$, so the error exceeds $\cos t\cdot t^4/24$. At $t = 0.5$ that is $0.87758 \times 0.0026042 = 0.0022854$.

The proof (cited: Spivak, Calculus, chapter 20, the sheet's book for this) applies Rolle's theorem $n+1$ times to $f$ minus its Taylor polynomial minus a multiple of $x^{n+1}$ chosen to vanish at $x$. The integral form of the remainder, $\frac1{n!}\int_0^x f^{(n+1)}(s)(x-s)^n\,\mathrm ds$, follows from repeated integration by parts and is sometimes easier to bound.

For the spring, $1 - t^2/2$ is the position after a short time from release: the mass starts at rest, so the first motion is quadratic in $t$, with acceleration $-1$.

Build a series from one you know, in two moves

substitute into the geometric series

Put $-x^2$ in place of $x$ in $\frac1{1-x} = 1 + x + x^2 + \cdots$:

$$\frac1{1+x^2} = 1 - x^2 + x^4 - \cdots\quad(|x|\lt1).$$
substitute into $e^x$

$e^{-x^2} = 1 - x^2 + \frac{x^4}2 - \frac{x^6}{6} + \cdots$ — no derivative of $e^{-x^2}$ taken.

then integrate term by term

$\arctan x = \int_0^x\frac{\mathrm ds}{1+s^2}$, so

$$\arctan x = x - \frac{x^3}3 + \frac{x^5}5 - \cdots$$

and at $x = 1$: $\displaystyle\frac\pi4 = 1 - \frac13 + \frac15 - \frac17 + \cdots$

the idea

Six series, plus substituting, multiplying and integrating, give nearly every expansion the course needs.

Why integrating term by term is allowed. Inside its radius of convergence a power series converges uniformly on every smaller closed interval, and uniform convergence lets an integral pass through the sum — the licences of section 2.

The endpoint is a separate matter. The series for $\arctan$ was derived for $|x|\lt1$. At $x = 1$ it still converges (alternating, terms decreasing to $0$), and Abel's theorem (cited: Abbott, chapter 6) says that a power series converging at an endpoint converges there to the limit of the function. So the value at $1$ is $\arctan 1 = \pi/4$. It converges very slowly: a thousand terms give $0.78515$ against $0.78540$.

$\big(1 + x + \tfrac{x^2}2 + \tfrac{x^3}6\big)\big(x - \tfrac{x^3}6\big)$, keeping powers up to $4$: $$x + x^2 + \big(\tfrac12 - \tfrac16\big)x^3 + \big(\tfrac16 - \tfrac16\big)x^4 = x + x^2 + \tfrac13x^3 + 0\cdot x^4 .$$

worked · the spring's series

$J^2 = -I$, so the powers of $J$ cycle $I, J, -I, -J$. Split $\sum (tJ)^n/n!$ into even and odd $n$: $$\begin{aligned}R(t) &= I\big(1 - \tfrac{t^2}{2!} + \cdots\big) + J\big(t - \tfrac{t^3}{3!} + \cdots\big)\\ &= I\cos t + J\sin t .\end{aligned}$$

Check of the $e^x\sin x$ expansion. Its derivative is $e^x(\sin x + \cos x)$, whose series starts $1 + 2x + x^2 + \cdots$; integrating term by term gives $x + x^2 + \tfrac13x^3$, as found. (The vanishing $x^4$ coefficient is not a coincidence: unit 06 introduces complex numbers, and there $e^x\sin x$ is the imaginary part of $e^{(1+i)x}$, with $(1+i)^4 = -4$ real.)

The spring's series, entry by entry. $I\cos t + J\sin t = \begin{pmatrix}\cos t & \sin t\\ -\sin t& \cos t\end{pmatrix}$, which is the $R(t)$ of the overview. Here the series was rearranged into its even and odd parts; that is allowed because the series converges absolutely, entry by entry (each entry is bounded by $|t|^n/n!$), and absolutely convergent series may be rearranged (cited: Abbott, Understanding Analysis, chapter 2).

The binomial series is the one most used in the course's relativity units: with $\alpha = -\tfrac12$ it expands the Lorentz factor. The sheet's P3 does that with its remainder. The table's ranges are the radii of convergence, the subject of the next slide; at the edge, behaviour must be checked case by case — $\ln(1+x)$ converges at $x = 1$ and not at $x = -1$.

R · calculus · smallest cases

How small the terms are decides everything

compare with a series you know

$\displaystyle\sum\frac1{n^2+1}$: each term is below $\frac1{n^2}$, whose sum converges. So this one converges.

where the ratio test goes blind

$\sum\frac1n$ diverges and $\sum\frac1{n^2}$ converges, but both have $\big|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\big|\to1$. The ratio test sees only geometric decay; for powers of $n$, compare.

cancellation can rescue a divergent size

$1 - \frac12 + \frac13 - \frac14 + \cdots$ converges (to $\ln 2$), though $\sum\frac1n$ diverges: conditional convergence.

three radii of convergence: $1$, $\infty$, $0$

$\sum x^n$: ratio of terms $|x|$; converges for $|x|\lt1$. Radius $1$.

$\sum\frac{x^n}{n!}$: ratio $\frac{|x|}{n+1}\to0$; converges for every $x$. Radius $\infty$.

$\sum n!\,x^n$: ratio $(n+1)|x|\to\infty$ unless $x = 0$. Radius $0$.

the idea

A factorial beats every geometric factor $x^n$, whether it sits in the denominator (radius $\infty$) or the numerator (radius $0$); with no factorial, the geometric factor alone sets the radius ($1$).

The ladder of growth. For large $n$: $\ln n \ll n^p \ll c^n \ll n! \ll n^n$ (for $p \gt 0$, $c \gt 1$). Almost every convergence question reduces to placing the terms on this ladder. The ratio test distinguishes rungs from $c^n$ upward; comparison with $n^{-p}$ distinguishes the lower rungs.

$\ln 2$. The alternating harmonic series is $\ln(1+x)$ at $x = 1$, an endpoint of its interval of convergence; the value $\ln 2$ is Abel's theorem again, as on Build a series from one you know.

R · calculus

Series: sum it if you can, decide it by the size of the terms if you cannot

summing

Integrate a geometric series. $\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{x^n}{n} = \int_0^x\frac{\mathrm dt}{1-t} = -\ln(1-x)$ for $|x|\lt1$, so $\sum\frac1{n\,2^n} = \ln2$.

Telescope. $\frac1{n(n+1)} = \frac1n - \frac1{n+1}$, so the partial sums are $1 - \frac1{N+1}\to1$.

radius of convergence

$\sum a_nx^n$ converges absolutely for $|x|\lt R$ and diverges for $|x|\gt R$, where $1/R = \limsup|a_n|^{1/n}$ — or $\lim|a_{n+1}/a_n|$ when that exists.

deciding, by the size of $a_n$

comparison test: $0\le a_n\le b_n$ and $\sum b_n$ converges ⇒ $\sum a_n$ does. The benchmark is $\sum n^{-p}$: converges iff $p \gt 1$.

ratio test: $|a_{n+1}/a_n|\to L$; $L\lt1$ converges, $L\gt1$ diverges, $L = 1$ says nothing.

alternating: $a_n\ge0$ decreasing to $0$ ⇒ $\sum(-1)^na_n$ converges.

first, expand: find $a_n$'s leading behaviour with a one-term Taylor expansion, e.g. $\sin\frac1{n^2}\sim\frac1{n^2}$: converges.

The working habit. Almost every convergence question in the course is settled by finding how big the $n$-th term is for large $n$, which is a Taylor expansion in $1/n$, and comparing with a $p$-series. The named tests are packaging for that one idea. Limit comparison makes it precise: if $a_n, b_n \gt 0$ and $a_n/b_n \to c \in (0,\infty)$, the two series converge or diverge together.

Why the benchmark is $p \gt 1$. Compare with $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,\mathrm dx$ (the integral test): finite exactly when $p \gt 1$. The harmonic series, $p = 1$, diverges, as does $\sum 1/(n\ln n)$, which is smaller still — the integral of $1/(x\ln x)$ is $\ln\ln x$, unbounded, but very slowly.

Radii, quickly. $\sum x^n/n$: the ratio is $n/(n+1)\to1$, so $R = 1$. $\sum x^n/n!$: the ratio is $1/(n+1)\to0$, so $R = \infty$, which is why the spring's series converges for every $t$. Inside the radius a power series may be differentiated and integrated term by term; that is a theorem about uniform convergence, on What uniform convergence buys, and how to get it cheaply.

The tests are cited: comparison, ratio, the alternating-series test and the radius formula are proved in Spivak, Calculus, the chapters on infinite series and on uniform convergence and power series.

Check of $\sum 1/(n\,2^n) = \ln 2$. The partial sums are $0.5, 0.625, 0.667, 0.682,\dots$, rising towards $0.693$. Differentiating a geometric series, instead of integrating it, is the other move worth knowing; the sheet's P4 uses it.

section 2

Real analysis: when may two limits be swapped?

Nearly every theorem a physicist uses without thinking is a licence to swap a limit with a sum, an integral or a derivative. Uniform convergence is the licence.

R · analysis · smallest cases

The simplest sequence that converges, but not uniformly

$f_n(x) = x/n$

At every fixed $x$, $x/n\to0$. So $f_n\to0$ pointwise on all of $\mathbb R$.

On $[0,1]$: $\sup|x/n| = 1/n\to0$. Uniform.

On $\mathbb R$: $\sup|x/n| = \infty$ for every $n$. Not uniform: far enough out, every line is still far from the axis.

the idea

Uniform is a property of the sequence and the set. The same functions converge uniformly on a bounded piece and not on the whole line.

The lines flatten towards the axis. Over the shaded $[0,1]$ they are all close to it at once; over $\mathbb R$ none is.

The quantifiers, in this example. Pointwise: given $x$ and $\varepsilon$, take $N \gt |x|/\varepsilon$; that $N$ grows with $|x|$. Uniform on $[0,1]$: $N \gt 1/\varepsilon$ works for every $x$ in the interval at once. On $\mathbb R$ no single $N$ can work, because $|x|/\varepsilon$ is unbounded.

This is the same structure as The spring's series, later in this section: uniform on every bounded interval, not on the whole line. The next slide shows a sharper failure, on a bounded interval, where the limit is not even continuous.

R · analysis

Pointwise and uniform: the order of two quantifiers

pointwise convergence

$f_n\to f$ on $S$ if for each $x\in S$ and each $\varepsilon\gt0$ there is an $N$ — which may depend on $x$ — with $|f_n(x) - f(x)|\lt\varepsilon$ for $n\ge N$.

uniform convergence

One $N$ serves every $x$: equivalently $$\sup_{x\in S}|f_n(x) - f(x)|\to0 .$$

$x^n$ on $[0,1]$: continuous functions, converging pointwise to a step. The limit is not continuous, so the convergence cannot be uniform.

The example in detail. For $0\le x\lt1$, $x^n\to0$; at $x = 1$, $x^n = 1$. So the pointwise limit is $0$ on $[0,1)$ and $1$ at $1$. The convergence is not uniform: $\sup_{[0,1)}|x^n - 0| = 1$ for every $n$, since $x^n$ gets as close to $1$ as you like near $x = 1$. The $N$ that works at $x$ grows without bound as $x\to1$: to make $x^n\lt\tfrac12$ you need $n \gt \ln2/\ln(1/x)$.

On any $[0, a]$ with $a\lt1$ it is uniform: $\sup_{[0,a]}x^n = a^n\to0$. The failure is concentrated at one end. That is typical, and the sheet's P6 asks you to find the same structure in another family.

The picture to keep. Uniform convergence means the graph of $f_n$ eventually lies inside a band of width $2\varepsilon$ around the graph of $f$ — the whole graph at once. Pointwise convergence lets the graph escape the band somewhere, as long as the place it escapes moves.

R · analysis

Why it matters: a bump that keeps its area while it vanishes

$f_n(x) = n^2x\,e^{-nx}$ on $[0,\infty)$. At each fixed $x \gt 0$ the exponential wins and $f_n(x)\to0$; also $f_n(0) = 0$.

But by $\int_0^\infty x^ke^{-ax}\,\mathrm dx = k!/a^{k+1}$ from Two substitutions worth knowing by heart, with $k = 1$, $a = n$:

$$\int_0^\infty f_n = n^2\cdot\frac{1!}{n^2} = 1\quad\text{for every }n .$$
so

$\displaystyle\lim_n\int f_n = 1 \ne 0 = \int\lim_n f_n$. The limit and the integral do not commute. The bump peaks at $x = 1/n$ with height $n/e$: it escapes every band.

The bump narrows and rises; its area stays $1$; its pointwise limit is $0$.

The peak. $f_n'(x) = n^2e^{-nx}(1 - nx)$, which vanishes at $x = 1/n$, where $f_n = n^2\cdot\frac1n e^{-1} = n/e$. So $\sup|f_n - 0| = n/e\to\infty$: far from uniform.

Why the physicist should care. Every time a derivation says "take the limit inside the integral", it is assuming this does not happen. In the course it is the step that turns a sum over a chain of masses into an integral (unit 18), that differentiates a Fourier transform (unit 20), and that the Picard iteration of unit 01 relies on. In each place the course says which theorem licenses the swap; this slide is why it has to.

The general tool, for reference. The theorem that handles unbounded intervals and integrands most cleanly is Lebesgue's dominated convergence theorem: if $f_n\to f$ pointwise and $|f_n|\le g$ with $\int g\lt\infty$, then $\int f_n\to\int f$. Here no such $g$ exists — any $g$ above every bump has infinite integral near $0$. The course does not assume Lebesgue integration. Mostly it uses uniform convergence and the explicit bounds of the next slides; where it needs dominated convergence, as for unit 20's derivative of a Fourier transform, it cites it.

R · analysis · smallest cases

Each swap, broken in the smallest way

limit and continuity

$x^n$ on $[0,1]$: each continuous, the limit a step — so the convergence is not uniform. See Pointwise and uniform.

limit and integral

$n^2x\,e^{-nx}$: each has area $1$, the limit has area $0$. A bump that keeps its area.

limit and derivative

$f_n(x) = \frac{\sin(n^2x)}{n}$: $\sup|f_n| = \frac1n\to0$, uniformly. But $f_n'(0) = n\to\infty$.

and a series where the M-test gives everything

$\displaystyle\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\sin nx}{n^2}$: each term is at most $\frac1{n^2}$ in size, for every $x$, and $\sum\frac1{n^2}\lt\infty$. So the series converges uniformly on all of $\mathbb R$, and its sum is continuous. Its derivative series, $\sum\frac{\cos nx}{n}$, has no such bound — and no licence.

The pattern. Each of the three swaps can fail, and each failure has a smallest example. The derivative one is the most surprising: the functions converge uniformly to $0$, as flat as you like, but they wiggle faster and faster, so their slopes do not settle. That is why the derivative licence on the next slide puts its hypothesis on the derivatives.

The M-test example. The bound $|\sin nx/n^2|\le1/n^2$ does not depend on $x$, which is exactly what the M-test needs. The derivative series $\sum\cos(nx)/n$ is bounded only by $\sum 1/n$, which diverges; at $x = 0$ it is the harmonic series and diverges outright.

R · analysis

What uniform convergence buys, and how to get it cheaply

three licences (on a bounded interval $[a,b]$)

1. continuity: a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous.

2. integrals: $f_n\to f$ uniformly ⇒ $\int_a^bf_n\to\int_a^bf$. Proof: $|\int(f_n-f)|\le(b-a)\sup|f_n - f|$.

3. derivatives: if $f_n'\to g$ uniformly and $f_n(a)$ converges, then $f_n\to f$ uniformly with $f' = g$.

Weierstrass M-test

If $|g_n(x)|\le M_n$ for all $x\in S$ and $\sum M_n\lt\infty$, then $\sum g_n$ converges uniformly (and absolutely) on $S$. Proof: the tail is at most $\sum_{n\gt N}M_n\to0$, whatever $x$ is.

consequence: power series

Inside its radius $R$, on every $|x|\le r\lt R$, a power series converges uniformly, and so does its term-by-term derivative. So it may be differentiated and integrated term by term on $|x|\lt R$.

Licence 3 is the subtle one. Uniform convergence of $f_n$ alone does not make the derivatives converge: $f_n(x) = \sin(n^2x)/n\to0$ uniformly, yet $f_n'(x) = n\cos(n^2x)$ does not converge at all. The hypothesis has to be on the derivatives. When each $f_n'$ is continuous the proof writes $f_n(x) = f_n(a) + \int_a^xf_n'$ and applies licence 2: then $f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^xg$, and the fundamental theorem gives $f' = g$. The general case uses the mean value theorem instead.

The power-series consequence, proved. Pick $r \lt \rho \lt R$. The series converges at $\rho$, so its terms are bounded, $|a_n|\rho^n\le C$. On $|x|\le r$, $|a_nx^n|\le C(r/\rho)^n$, a convergent geometric series: the M-test applies. For the derivative series, $|na_nx^{n-1}|\le (C/\rho)\,n(r/\rho)^{n-1}$, also summable. Then licence 3.

The proofs of the three licences are cited: Abbott, Understanding Analysis, chapter 6 — the sheet's book for this group. Licences 1 and 2 are short enough to reconstruct; if licence 3's proof is unfamiliar, that is the chapter to reread.

R · analysis

The spring's series: uniform on bounded times, not on all of them

worked · the series for $R(t)$ may be differentiated

1. Each entry of $(tJ)^n/n!$ is $0$ or $\pm t^n/n!$. On $|t|\le T$ it is at most $T^n/n!$, and $\sum T^n/n! = e^T\lt\infty$. M-test: uniform.

2. The derivative of the $n$-th term is $J(tJ)^{n-1}/(n-1)!$: the same series again, times $J$. Uniform too.

3. Licence 3: $\dot R(t) = JR(t)$ for $|t|\lt T$; $T$ was arbitrary.

but not uniformly on $\mathbb R$

Let $S_N$ be the partial sum up to $t^N$. For $N\ge2$ it is a non-constant polynomial, unbounded; $\cos t$ is bounded. So $\sup_{\mathbb R}|S_N - \cos| = \infty$.

More terms buy a longer stretch of agreement; no finite number buys all of $\mathbb R$.

Why this is enough. Differentiability at a point is local. To show $\dot R = JR$ at a given $t_0$, choose $T \gt |t_0|$; on $(-T, T)$ the series converges uniformly with its derivative, so licence 3 gives the derivative there. That every $t_0$ has such a $T$ is the whole argument. Uniform convergence on every closed bounded interval inside the radius — "locally uniform" — is what power series have, and it is all the licences need.

The figure's numbers. The partial sums up to $t^4, t^8, t^{12}, t^{16}, t^{20}$ stay within $0.2$ of $\cos t$ up to $t\approx 2.3, 3.9, 5.4, 7.0, 8.5$ respectively: each four extra powers buy about $1.5$ more units of time. That is Stirling's formula at work — the terms $t^n/n!$ stay small until $n$ is comparable with $t$.

The same caution recurs. An expansion is a statement about a bounded region. Unit 21 is about what to do when that region is not the one you care about.

R · analysis · smallest cases

Two curves that look alike: one area finite, one not

tails out to infinity

$\displaystyle\int_1^b\frac{\mathrm dx}{x^2} = 1 - \frac1b\to1$, but $\displaystyle\int_1^b\frac{\mathrm dx}{x} = \ln b\to\infty$.

a spike at a point

$\displaystyle\int_\varepsilon^1\frac{\mathrm dx}{\sqrt x} = 2 - 2\sqrt\varepsilon\to2$: infinitely tall, finite area.

the idea

Out at infinity the integrand must fall faster than $1/x$; at a spike it must rise slower than $1/x$. The power $1$ is the borderline both ways.

On $[1,\infty)$ the area under $1/x^2$ is $1$; under $1/x$ it never stops growing.

Why the two look alike and are not. Both curves go to $0$; the question is how fast. $\ln b$ does grow without bound, but very slowly: the area under $1/x$ out to $b = 10^6$ is only about $13.8$. A numerical integration cut off at a large $b$ cannot tell these two apart — only the computation of the limit can.

The series analogue is the previous section's $\sum 1/n$ against $\sum 1/n^2$; the integral test makes the two statements the same statement.

R · analysis

Improper integrals: absolute and conditional

improper integral

$\int_a^\infty f = \lim_{b\to\infty}\int_a^bf$, and $\int_a^bf = \lim_{c\to b^-}\int_a^cf$ when $f$ blows up at $b$ — when the limit exists.

the benchmarks

$\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,\mathrm dx$ converges iff $p\gt1$; $\int_0^1x^{-p}\,\mathrm dx$ converges iff $p\lt1$. Then compare: $0\le f\le g$ and $\int g\lt\infty$ ⇒ $\int f\lt\infty$.

absolute convergence and conditional convergence

$\int|f|\lt\infty$ implies $\int f$ converges (apply comparison to $|f| + f$ and $|f|$): absolute. $\int f$ can converge while $\int|f| = \infty$: conditional — the cancellations between positive and negative parts do the work, and are fragile.

worked · the spring's quarter-period

$1 - x^2 = (1-x)(1+x)\ge1-x$ on $[0,1)$, so $\frac1{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\le(1-x)^{-1/2}$: power $\tfrac12\lt1$ at the end. Absolutely convergent, and at most $\big[-2\sqrt{1-x}\big]_0^1 = 2$; its value is $\pi/2$.

The standard conditional example is $\int_0^\infty\frac{\sin x}{x}\,\mathrm dx$: it converges, and its absolute version does not. The sheet's P7 asks you to prove both and then find its value, so the details are left there.

Why "fragile". A conditionally convergent integral can change its value under operations that would be harmless for an absolutely convergent one — rearranging, swapping with a limit, a change of variables that reorders the cancellations. Every swap theorem in the course has absolute convergence, or a uniform bound, among its hypotheses.

The benchmarks, proved. For $p\ne1$, $\int_1^bx^{-p}\,\mathrm dx = \frac{b^{1-p} - 1}{1-p}$, which has a finite limit as $b\to\infty$ iff $p\gt1$; $p = 1$ gives $\ln b\to\infty$. The other benchmark is the same computation near $0$.

R · analysis · smallest cases

Differentiating under the integral sign: once licensed, once not

licensed: everything bounded and continuous

$F(a) = \displaystyle\int_0^1e^{ax}\,\mathrm dx$. Differentiate inside: $F'(0) = \displaystyle\int_0^1x\,\mathrm dx = \frac12$.

Directly: $F(a) = \frac{e^a - 1}{a} = 1 + \frac a2 + \frac{a^2}6 + \cdots$, so $F'(0) = \frac12$ ✓.

A bounded interval and a continuous $\partial_a(e^{ax}) = xe^{ax}$: the proper-integral licence applies.

not licensed: no bound uniform in $a$, and already the limit fails

$F(a) = \displaystyle\int_0^\infty a\,e^{-ax}\,\mathrm dx = 1$ for every $a\gt0$, but $F(0) = 0$.

So $\lim_{a\to0}F(a) = 1$, while the integrand tends to $0$ at every $x$. The limit cannot pass inside: the mass of $a e^{-ax}$ spreads out to infinity as $a\to0$. So $F$ is not even continuous at $0$, let alone differentiable.

the idea

On a bounded interval with continuous integrands, swap freely. On an unbounded one, find a single integrable function that bounds the integrand for all the parameter values at once. That one hypothesis licenses both the limit and the derivative; if none exists, distrust either swap.

Why no dominating function exists in the second example. For fixed $x \gt 0$, $a e^{-ax}$ is largest at $a = 1/x$, where it equals $1/(ex)$. Any $g$ above all of them must be at least $1/(ex)$, which is not integrable on $[1,\infty)$. This is the bump of Why it matters: a bump that keeps its area while it vanishes, run the other way: there the mass piled up near $0$, here it escapes to infinity. Either way, area goes missing in the limit.

The first example's series. $\frac{e^a-1}a = \sum_{k\ge0}\frac{a^k}{(k+1)!}$, whose linear coefficient is $\frac1{2!} = \frac12$.

R · analysis

Differentiating under the integral sign, and what licenses it

differentiating under the integral sign

Proper. If $f$ and $\partial f/\partial a$ are continuous on $[\alpha,\beta]\times[c,d]$, then $\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm da}\int_c^df(a,x)\,\mathrm dx = \int_c^d\frac{\partial f}{\partial a}(a,x)\,\mathrm dx$ for $a\in[\alpha,\beta]$.

Improper. On $[c,\infty)$, or on $(c,d]$ with the integrand unbounded at $c$, add a dominating function: $\int f(a,x)\,\mathrm dx$ converges for one $a$, and $|\partial_af(a,x)|\le g(x)$ for all $a\in[\alpha,\beta]$, with $\int g\lt\infty$ over the range.

worked · licensing $\int_0^\infty x^ne^{-ax} = n!/a^{n+1}$

Fix $\delta\gt0$ and take $a\ge\delta$. The $k$-th $a$-derivative of $e^{-ax}$ is $(-x)^ke^{-ax}$, and $|x^ke^{-ax}|\le x^ke^{-\delta x}$, which is integrable on $[0,\infty)$. So each differentiation is licensed for $a\ge\delta$; $\delta$ was arbitrary, so the formula holds for every $a\gt0$.

where it fails

At $\delta\to0$ the dominating function $x^k$ is not integrable, and the formula indeed blows up as $a\to0^+$. No bound uniform in $a$, no licence.

The proper case, proved. The difference quotient is $\int_c^d\frac{f(a+h,x) - f(a,x)}{h}\,\mathrm dx = \int_c^d\partial_af(a + \theta h, x)\,\mathrm dx$ for some $\theta = \theta(x)\in(0,1)$, by the mean value theorem. $\partial_af$ is continuous on a compact rectangle, hence uniformly continuous (a fact from section 4), so the integrand converges to $\partial_af(a,x)$ uniformly in $x$ as $h\to0$; licence 2 does the rest.

The improper case is cited: Abbott, Understanding Analysis, the section on the factorial function in chapter 8 — the place the sheet names. The idea is the proper case on $[c, b]$, plus the dominating function to make the tail $\int_b^\infty$ small uniformly in $a$.

Why the integrability of $x^ke^{-\delta x}$. For large $x$ it is below $x^{-2}$ (the exponential beats any power), and $\int^\infty x^{-2}$ converges. The sheet's P2 and P7 both turn on this kind of bound; P7 is where the dominating function is hardest to find.

section 3

Linear algebra: maps, forms, and the two ways a matrix can change

The course needs linear algebra in its basis-free form, because a tensor is a multilinear map. The one fact to carry out of this section: a linear map and a bilinear form both have matrices, and those matrices change differently.

R · linear algebra · smallest cases

Double one basis vector, and watch what changes

coordinates shrink when the basis grows

New basis $b_1 = 2e_1$, $b_2 = e_2$. Write $P = \mathrm{diag}(2, 1)$, the matrix whose columns are the new basis vectors.

The vector $v = (2, 3)$ is $1\cdot b_1 + 3\cdot b_2$: new coordinates $(1, 3) = P^{-1}(2,3)$.

Measure in a unit twice as long and the number halves — like metres to kilometres.

some things do not change at all

$D = \mathrm{diag}(2,3)$ stretches $x$ by $2$ and $y$ by $3$. In the new basis its matrix is $P^{-1}DP$ (the next slide says why), and that is $D$ again.

The spring's $J$, in any basis whatever, has trace $0$ and determinant $1$: those belong to the map, not to the basis.

the idea

A matrix is the map's description in one particular basis. Change the basis and the description changes; the map, and its invariants, do not.

Why $D$ is unchanged. Diagonal matrices commute with each other, so $P^{-1}DP = P^{-1}PD = D$. In general the matrix does change: the next slide uses a basis that is sheared rather than stretched, and there it would.

"Coordinates shrink when the basis grows" is the word contravariant, which the course uses from unit 18 on (unit 16 states the same rule as "each upper index contracts with $S^{-1}$"): vector components vary against the basis. The dual-space slides show the quantities that vary with it.

R · linear algebra

A matrix is a map written in a basis

change of basis

Let $P$ have the new basis vectors $b_j$ as its columns, written in the old coordinates. A vector with old coordinates $x$ and new coordinates $x'$ has $x = Px'$, so $x' = P^{-1}x$.

A linear map with matrix $A$ in the old basis has matrix $\;A' = P^{-1}AP\;$ in the new one.

worked

$b_1 = (1,0)$, $b_2 = (1,1)$: $P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $P^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. For $v = (3,2)$: $x' = P^{-1}x = (1, 2)$, and indeed $b_1 + 2b_2 = (3,2)$ ✓.

Invariants — the same in every basis: $\det A$, $\operatorname{tr}A$, the characteristic polynomial, the eigenvalues.

The same arrow, two grids: $v = 3e_1 + 2e_2 = b_1 + 2b_2$.

Deriving $P^{-1}AP$. In new coordinates, the map sends $x'$ to: convert to old coordinates ($Px'$), apply $A$, convert back ($P^{-1}$). That is $P^{-1}AP\,x'$. Reading it right to left in that way is the reliable method; memorising which side the inverse goes on is not.

Why the invariants are invariant. $\det(P^{-1}AP) = \det P^{-1}\det A\det P = \det A$, and $\det(P^{-1}AP - \lambda I) = \det(P^{-1}(A - \lambda I)P)$, so the characteristic polynomial, and with it its roots and its coefficients (trace and determinant among them), do not depend on the basis. They belong to the map, not to the matrix.

The meaning of the determinant: the factor by which the map scales areas (volumes in higher dimensions), with a sign for orientation. The spring's $R(t)$ has $\det R = \cos^2t + \sin^2t = 1$: the flow preserves area in the phase plane, which unit 14 will turn into Liouville's theorem.

R · linear algebra · smallest cases

Eigenvectors are the directions a map keeps

a stretch: two kept directions

$\mathrm{diag}(2,3)$: the $x$-axis is stretched by $2$, the $y$-axis by $3$. Eigenvalues $2, 3$; already diagonal.

a projection: one direction killed

$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, the shadow on the $x$-axis: the $x$-axis is kept ($\lambda = 1$), the $y$-axis is crushed to $0$ ($\lambda = 0$).

a shear: too few directions

$\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$: double eigenvalue $1$, but only the $x$-axis is kept. One line of eigenvectors in a plane: not diagonalisable.

The shear keeps the horizontal axis and tips every other direction over.

The idea. An eigenvector is a direction the map sends to itself, and the eigenvalue is the factor it multiplies lengths by along it. Diagonalising means finding enough such directions to make a basis; in that basis the map does nothing but stretch each axis.

The shear, in detail. $\det(A - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)^2$, so $\lambda = 1$ twice, but $A - I = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ has a one-dimensional kernel, the $x$-axis. The shear is the smallest Jordan block, the one on the next slide's warning box. The rotation $J$ is a third kind of failure: it keeps no real direction at all.

Eigenvalues, diagonalising, and the matrices that will not

eigenvalue

$Av = \lambda v$ with $v\ne0$; the eigenvalues are the roots of $\det(A - \lambda I)$.

Diagonalising. If there is a basis of eigenvectors $v_1,\dots,v_n$, let $P$ be the matrix with those eigenvectors as its columns. Then $P^{-1}AP = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$, because $A$ sends column $i$ of $P$ to $\lambda_i$ times itself. So $A^n = P\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_i^n)\,P^{-1}$.

Jordan form (statement only)

Over $\mathbb C$, every matrix is similar to a block diagonal matrix of blocks $\lambda I + N$, where $N$ has $1$s just above the diagonal and $N^k = 0$ for a block of size $k$. Diagonalisable means every block has size $1$.

worked · the spring's $J$ has no real eigenvector

$\det(J - \lambda I) = \det\begin{pmatrix}-\lambda&1\\-1&-\lambda\end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 1$, no real root.

Geometrically: $J$ turns every vector through a right angle, so no line through $0$ is sent to itself. Nor is any by $R(t)$, unless $t$ is a multiple of $\pi$.

two different failures

$J$ fails to diagonalise over $\mathbb R$ for lack of real roots, but does over $\mathbb C$. $\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}$ fails everywhere: a double root with only a line of eigenvectors.

What $P$ is. It is the change-of-basis matrix of A matrix is a map written in a basis, for one particular new basis: the eigenvectors. In that basis the map only stretches each axis, so its matrix $P^{-1}AP$ is diagonal. Column by column, $AP = P\,\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_i)$ says $Av_i = \lambda_iv_i$; multiply on the left by $P^{-1}$. For example $A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvectors $(1,1)$ and $(1,-1)$ for $3$ and $1$, so $P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ and $P^{-1}AP = \mathrm{diag}(3,1)$. Writing $D = \mathrm{diag}(\lambda_i)$, the powers follow because the inner $P^{-1}P$ factors cancel: $A^n = (PDP^{-1})^n = PD^nP^{-1}$.

Over $\mathbb C$, $J$ has eigenvalues $\pm i$ and is diagonalisable; unit 06 introduces the complex numbers properly, and this is where the $e^{\pm it}$ of oscillation theory comes from. For now the real picture is the important one: a rotation has no real direction it keeps.

Why the Jordan block fails. $\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix} - \lambda I$ has rank $1$, so the eigenspace is one-dimensional, and two independent eigenvectors do not exist. In the course this is critically damped motion (units 06 and 07), whose solution $t\,e^{-t}$ has the polynomial factor that the nilpotent part of the block produces. The sheet's P10 asks for the powers of such a block (it calls the block $J$; here $J$ is the spring's).

The Jordan theorem is cited: Axler, Linear Algebra Done Right, 4th edition, chapter 8 — as a statement; the course never needs its proof.

Orthogonal maps and symmetric matrices, in their smallest cases

a reflection is orthogonal too

$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ flips $y$. It preserves lengths and angles: with $Q$ this matrix, $Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$. But $\det = -1$: it reverses orientation, so it is orthogonal but not a rotation (a rotation also has $\det = 1$).

symmetric: perpendicular eigenvectors

$\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ swaps the coordinates. $(1,1)$ is kept ($\lambda = 1$), $(1,-1)$ is flipped ($\lambda = -1$), and $(1,1)\cdot(1,-1) = 0$ ✓.

not symmetric: no such luck

$\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvectors $(1,0)$ for $\lambda = 1$ and $(1,1)$ for $\lambda = 2$. They are not perpendicular: $(1,0)\cdot(1,1) = 1$.

the idea

Orthogonal means "moves things rigidly". Symmetric means "stretches along perpendicular axes" — that is the spectral theorem, and the third box shows it is a property of symmetry, not of all matrices.

The checks. For the swap, $\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}(1,1) = (1,1)$ and $(1,-1)\mapsto(-1,1) = -(1,-1)$. For the third matrix, $(A - 2I) = \begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ kills $(1,1)$.

Where the course uses the spectral theorem. Unit 07: the normal modes of coupled oscillators are eigenvectors of a symmetric problem, perpendicular in the inner product the masses define, $u^{\mathsf T}Mv$, not in the plain dot product. Unit 16: the principal axes of a rigid body. Each time, the symmetry of the matrix comes from physics — a potential's second derivatives, an inertia tensor — and the perpendicularity is the payoff.

Inner products, orthogonal matrices, and the spectral theorem

orthogonal matrix

$Q^{\mathsf T}Q = I$: equivalently $Q$ preserves the dot product, $(Qx)\cdot(Qy) = x\cdot y$; its columns are orthonormal. $R(t)$ is one: $R^{\mathsf T}R = \begin{pmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}c&s\\-s&c\end{pmatrix} = I$.

spectral theorem (real symmetric)

If $A = A^{\mathsf T}$, there is an orthogonal $Q$ with $Q^{\mathsf T}AQ$ diagonal: an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors, real eigenvalues.

the one-line part

Eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix for different eigenvalues are perpendicular. The reason is one line, using $Au\cdot w = u\cdot Aw$; finding it is the sheet's P10, part 2.

$A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$: eigenvalues $3$ and $1$. The level set $x^{\mathsf T}Ax = 1$ is an ellipse whose semi-axes lie along the eigenvectors, of lengths $1/\sqrt3$ and $1$.

The simplest covector: "take the $x$-coordinate"

a covector eats a vector, returns a number

$e^1(x, y) = x$ is linear, so it is a covector. So is any $\omega(x,y) = ax + by$; $(a, b)$ are its components.

With $b_1 = 2e_1$, $b_2 = e_2$ and $v = (2,3)$:

vector components: $(2,3)\to(1,3)$ — halved along $b_1$.

$\omega = e^1$'s components $\omega(b_i)$: $(1,0)\to(2,0)$ — doubled along $b_1$.

the idea

A vector is an amount of something; a covector is a rate per something. Measure lengths in a unit twice as big: a distance becomes half the number, a gradient per unit length twice the number. Their product — the actual change — is the same.

the dual basis, here

The covectors $\beta^i$ with $\beta^i(b_j) = 1$ if $i = j$ and $0$ otherwise: $\beta^1 = \tfrac12e^1$ (so that $\beta^1(b_1) = 1$), $\beta^2 = e^2$.

The physical reading. A temperature gradient in kelvin per metre is a covector: fed a displacement, it returns a temperature change. Switch to kilometres and the displacement's number shrinks by $1000$ while the gradient's grows by $1000$. That is all "transforms the other way" means, and it is why unit 16 can say a tensor's transformation law is a theorem rather than a definition.

Check the dual basis. $\beta^1(b_1) = \tfrac12e^1(2e_1) = 1$, $\beta^1(b_2) = \tfrac12e^1(e_2) = 0$, $\beta^2(b_1) = 0$, $\beta^2(b_2) = 1$. Equivalently: the rows of $P^{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(\tfrac12, 1)$.

The dual space: covectors, and components that change the other way

dual space, dual basis

$V^*$ is the space of linear maps $V\to\mathbb R$; its elements are covectors. Given a basis $b_j$ of $V$, the dual basis $\beta^i$ is defined by $\beta^i(b_j) = \delta^i_j$ ($1$ if $i = j$, else $0$). With $P$ the matrix whose columns are the $b_j$, the $\beta^i$, written as rows, are the rows of $P^{-1}$.

opposite rules

Vector components change by $P^{-1}$: $x' = P^{-1}x$. Covector components change by $P$: $\omega'_i = \omega(b_i)$, i.e. $\omega' = \omega P$ as a row. So $\omega(v) = \omega'x' = \omega PP^{-1}x$ is the same number in every basis.

worked · the basis $b_1 = (1,0)$, $b_2 = (1,1)$ again

Rows of $P^{-1}$: $\beta^1 = e^1 - e^2$, $\beta^2 = e^2$. Check: $\beta^1(b_2) = 1 - 1 = 0$ ✓.

$\omega = 2e^1 + e^2$ has new components $(\omega(b_1), \omega(b_2)) = (2, 3)$. With $v' = (1,2)$ from A matrix is a map, $2\cdot1 + 3\cdot2 = 8$; in the old basis, $2\cdot3 + 1\cdot2 = 8$ ✓.

The picture. A covector on the plane is determined by its level lines $\omega = 0, 1, 2, \dots$: a family of parallel, evenly spaced lines. $\omega(v)$ counts how many of them the arrow $v$ crosses. Make the basis vectors longer and the vector components get smaller while the covector components — the number of lines each basis vector crosses — get bigger. That is "change the other way" without any algebra.

Why the dual basis is the rows of $P^{-1}$. $P^{-1}P = I$ says exactly that row $i$ of $P^{-1}$, applied to column $j$ of $P$ — that is, to $b_j$ — gives $\delta^i_j$.

Why the course cares. In unit 16 a tensor is a multilinear map on vectors and covectors, and its components transform with one factor of $P$ or $P^{-1}$ per slot — a theorem, from exactly the two rules on this slide. The gradient of a function is naturally a covector, not a vector, and becomes a vector only once an inner product is chosen. The sheet's P8 does this slide in three dimensions.

Scale every basis vector by 2: the map stays, the form quadruples

a linear map: $P^{-1}AP$

$P = 2I$. Then $P^{-1}AP = \tfrac12\cdot A\cdot2 = A$. A map's matrix does not notice a uniform change of scale: "double the input" is still "double the input" in any unit.

a bilinear form: $P^{\mathsf T}BP$

With $B$ the form's matrix, $B_{ij} = B(e_i, e_j)$: $P^{\mathsf T}BP = 2\cdot B\cdot2 = 4B$. The dot product in the basis $2e_1, 2e_2$ has matrix $4I$: each new basis vector has squared length $4$.

the idea

A form takes two vectors, so it picks up the scale twice; a map takes one vector and returns one, so the scale cancels. Same square array of numbers, different objects.

In units. If lengths are measured in a unit twice as long, an area — the dot product of two lengths — is measured in a unit four times as large, and its matrix in the new basis is four times larger to compensate. A ratio of lengths, which is what a linear map of the plane to itself is, carries no units at all and does not change.

R · linear algebra

A bilinear form is not a linear map, though both have matrices

bilinear form

$B: V\times V\to\mathbb R$, linear in each slot. Its matrix in a basis is $B_{ij} = B(b_i, b_j)$, so $B(x,y) = x^{\mathsf T}By$.

the rule that matters

With $P$ the new basis vectors as columns, as before: $$\text{form: }\ B' = P^{\mathsf T}BP\qquad\qquad\text{map: }\ A' = P^{-1}AP$$ For an orthogonal change of basis, $P^{\mathsf T} = P^{-1}$, the two coincide — which is why the difference hides in elementary work.

worked · the same matrix $I$, read two ways

With $P = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$:

as the identity map: $P^{-1}IP = I$. The identity is the identity in every basis.

as the dot product: $P^{\mathsf T}IP = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ — the table of $b_i\cdot b_j$: $b_1\cdot b_1 = 1$, $b_1\cdot b_2 = 1$, $b_2\cdot b_2 = 2$ ✓.

the trap

Between orthonormal bases every change of basis is orthogonal, so the two rules coincide and you can go years without noticing there are two. The first oblique basis or curved coordinate shows it.

Deriving $P^{\mathsf T}BP$. $B(x, y) = x^{\mathsf T}By$ with $x = Px'$, $y = Py'$ gives $x'^{\mathsf T}(P^{\mathsf T}BP)y'$. Or directly: the new matrix's entries are $B(b_i, b_j)$, and $b_i$ is column $i$ of $P$.

The tensor reading. A linear map $V\to V$ eats a vector and returns one: one covector slot and one vector slot, one $P$ and one $P^{-1}$. A bilinear form eats two vectors: two vector slots (two lower indices), two factors of $P$. That is unit 16's transformation theorem in its smallest case. The metric of relativity, from unit 26 on, is a bilinear form, and treating it as a map is the classic first error in index gymnastics.

Why the two agree for orthogonal $P$. If $P^{\mathsf T}P = I$ then $P^{\mathsf T} = P^{-1}$ and the two formulas are the same formula. That is why the distinction is invisible in elementary work, where every change of basis is a rotation.

Signatures you can read off at a glance

$(2, 0)$: a bowl

$x^2 + y^2$, and also $4x^2 + 9y^2$. Different numbers, same signs: positive in every direction. Level sets are ellipses.

$(1, 1)$: a saddle

$x^2 - y^2$: up along $x$, down along $y$, zero along the lines $y = \pm x$. Level sets are hyperbolas.

$(1, 0)$: a trough

$(x+y)^2$: only one square, so rank $1$. It vanishes on the whole line $y = -x$. Its matrix $\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $2$ and $0$.

the idea

The signature counts the directions in which the form goes up and the directions in which it goes down. Rescaling the axes changes how steeply, never which way — and Sylvester's law says the same survives any change of basis, even one that mixes the variables.

Why the numbers can change but not the signs. $4x^2 + 9y^2$ becomes $X^2 + Y^2$ under $X = 2x$, $Y = 3y$. No real change of variables can turn a positive square into a negative one, since a square of a real number is never negative. Sylvester's law is that intuition made into a theorem for arbitrary changes of basis, which mix the variables.

Why these matter in the course. The second derivative of a potential at an equilibrium is a quadratic form: a bowl is a stable equilibrium, a saddle an unstable one (unit 04). And spacetime's interval (unit 26) has one negative and three positive squares: signature $(3,1)$ in this deck's (positive, negative) order; unit 26 writes it $(-,+,+,+)$ and its group $O(1,3)$. Its zero set is the light cone — the higher-dimensional version of the saddle's lines $y = \pm x$.

Quadratic forms, signature, and Sylvester's law

quadratic form, signature

$q(x) = B(x,x)$ for symmetric $B$. In a suitable basis $q = y_1^2 + \cdots + y_p^2 - y_{p+1}^2 - \cdots - y_{p+n}^2$; the signature is $(p, n)$.

Sylvester's law of inertia

$p$ and $n$ do not depend on how $q$ was diagonalised — completing squares, eigenvalues, anything. $p$ is the largest dimension of a subspace on which $q$ is positive definite.

worked · two routes, one answer

$q = 2x^2 + 2xy - y^2 = 2(x + \tfrac y2)^2 - \tfrac32y^2$: signature $(1,1)$. Eigenvalues of $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$: $\frac{1\pm\sqrt{13}}2 = 2.30,\ -1.30$ — one of each sign ✓.

The spring's energy is positive definite. Flip the force, $\ddot x = +x$, and the conserved quantity becomes $v^2 - x^2$: signature $(1,1)$, level sets hyperbolas.

Why the two routes must agree. Completing squares is a change of basis $P$, giving $P^{\mathsf T}BP$ diagonal; the eigenvalue route is the special case $P = Q$ orthogonal. They give different diagonal entries — $2$ and $-\tfrac32$ against $2.30$ and $-1.30$ — but Sylvester says the signs must match. The product of the entries changes by $\det(P)^2\gt0$, so its sign is fixed: here $-3$ and $-3$, negative, one of each sign.

The proof of Sylvester is cited: Hoffman and Kunze, Linear Algebra, the chapter on bilinear forms — the reference the sheet names, since Axler does not dwell on it. Its idea is the characterisation of $p$ on the slide: a subspace where $q\gt0$ meets the span of the negative and zero directions only in $0$, which bounds its dimension.

The flipped spring. $\ddot x = x$ is a ball balanced on a hilltop. Multiplying by $\dot x$ as before gives $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}(\dot x^2 - x^2) = 0$. The form $v^2 - x^2$ has signature $(1,1)$, and its null lines $v = \pm x$ carry the motions that roll exactly to the top, from either side, or exactly away. Unit 26's Minkowski space has one negative and three positive squares, $(3,1)$ in this deck's order, and its null cone is the light cone. R · metric spaces · smallest cases

Three distances on the plane, one on functions, one hole

a distance between two functions

On $[0,1]$ the sup distance $d_\infty$ between the functions $x\mapsto x$ and $x\mapsto x^2$ is $\max(x - x^2) = \frac14$, at $x = \frac12$: the widest gap between the graphs.

a Cauchy sequence with nowhere to go

In $\mathbb Q$: $1,\ 1.4,\ 1.41,\ 1.414,\ \dots$ (decimals of $\sqrt2$). The terms bunch together — Cauchy — but $\sqrt2\notin\mathbb Q$. So $\mathbb Q$ is not complete; $\mathbb R$ fills the hole.

Three honest metrics on $\mathbb R^2$, three different unit "circles". All three agree on which sequences converge.

The three metrics. Euclidean $\sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}$, taxicab $|\Delta x| + |\Delta y|$, and max $\max(|\Delta x|, |\Delta y|)$. Each satisfies the triangle inequality. Their unit balls are nested (diamond inside circle inside square): $d_{\max}\le d_{\mathrm E}\le d_{\mathrm{taxi}}\le2\,d_{\max}$ on $\mathbb R^2$, so a sequence converging in one converges in all three. (On $\mathbb R^n$ every norm behaves this way.) On spaces of functions that fails, and the choice of metric matters — the next slide's table has an example.

The sup metric is the max metric with one coordinate for every point $x$ of $[0,1]$: the distance between two functions is the largest difference between their values.

The Cauchy bound. Two truncations of $\sqrt2$ to $m$ and $n$ decimals differ by less than $10^{-\min(m,n)}$.

metric space

A set with $d(x,y)\ge0$, zero iff $x = y$, symmetric, and $d(x,z)\le d(x,y) + d(y,z)$.

On $C[a,b]$, the continuous functions on $[a,b]$: $d_\infty(f,g) = \sup_{[a,b]}|f - g|$. Then $d_\infty(f_n, f)\to0$ is uniform convergence.

$(x_n)$ is Cauchy if $d(x_m,x_n)\to0$ as $m,n\to\infty$. The space is complete if every Cauchy sequence converges in it.

Why $(C[a,b], d_\infty)$ is complete — the one proof worth reconstructing. If $(f_n)$ is Cauchy in $d_\infty$, then for each $x$, $(f_n(x))$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb R$, so it converges to some $f(x)$. The same functions, a different metric, a different answer. Under $d_1(f,g) = \int|f-g|$, continuous ramps rising from $0$ to $1$ over shorter and shorter intervals are Cauchy, but their limit is a step, not continuous. Completeness belongs to the pair (set, metric), not to the set.

Vector-valued functions, such as the spring's state $u(t)\in\mathbb R^2$, work the same way with $|\cdot|$ the Euclidean length: $C([0,\tau];\mathbb R^2)$ with $\sup_t|u(t) - w(t)|$ is complete, by the same proof applied componentwise.

R · metric spaces · smallest cases

Three ways for a maximum not to exist

an open end

$f(x) = x$ on $(0,1)$: values climb towards $1$ and never reach it. No maximum. The missing point $1$ is the trouble: not closed.

a missing end, blowing up

$f(x) = 1/x$ on $(0,1]$: continuous, unbounded. The missing $0$ again.

running off

$f(x) = x$ on $\mathbb R$ (closed, not bounded): no maximum. And $x_n = n$ has no convergent subsequence.

the idea

On $[0,1]$ none of these can happen: closed (no missing endpoint) and bounded (nowhere to run off to). Compactness is the name for "no escape route", and a continuous function on a compact set attains its max.

Each failure is an escaping sequence. In the first two, a sequence heading to the missing endpoint has no limit in the set; in the third, a sequence runs off to infinity. Compactness — every sequence has a convergent subsequence with its limit in the set — is exactly the absence of such escapes.

The proof that the max exists follows the same picture: take points where $f$ approaches its supremum, extract a convergent subsequence by compactness, and continuity says $f$ at the limit is the supremum.

R · metric spaces

Compactness: what makes a maximum exist

compact

In a metric space: every sequence has a subsequence converging to a point of the set.

Heine–Borel theorem

A subset of $\mathbb R^n$ is compact iff it is closed and bounded.

what compactness buys

A continuous $f$ on a compact set is bounded, attains its max and min, and is uniformly continuous.

Heine–Borel is about $\mathbb R^n$ only

In $(C[0,1], d_\infty)$ the closed unit ball is closed and bounded but not compact. The sequence $x^n$ lies in it, and no subsequence converges uniformly: any subsequence converges pointwise to the discontinuous step of section 2, and a uniform limit would have to be that step.

where the course uses it

"$|f'|$ has a maximum on $[a,b]$", for $f'$ continuous — every Lipschitz constant, every contraction constant $q$, every error bound. The maximum exists because $[a,b]$ is compact.

Uniform continuity is the property that one $\delta$ serves every point: $d(x,y)\lt\delta\Rightarrow|f(x) - f(y)|\lt\varepsilon$. On a compact set every continuous function has it; $1/x$ on $(0,1]$, which is not compact, does not. It was used in the notes to Differentiating under the integral sign, and what licenses it, to prove differentiation under the integral sign.

The proofs are cited: Sutherland, Introduction to Metric and Topological Spaces, the chapters on compactness and on sequential compactness — the sheet's book for this group. Halving, and three ways to break it

the smallest contraction

$T(x) = x/2$ on $\mathbb R$: every distance halves, shrink factor $q = \frac12$. From any start the iterates $x_0, \frac{x_0}2, \frac{x_0}4, \dots$ go to the one fixed point, $0$.

no completeness

$x/2$ on $(0,1]$: still a contraction, but the point it heads for, $0$, is not in the space. No fixed point.

not into itself

$x/2 + 1$ on $[0,1]$: sends $1$ to $1.5$, outside. Its fixed point, $2$, is outside too.

$q = 1$

$x + 1$ on $\mathbb R$: distances are kept, not shrunk. No fixed point; the iterates march off.

The idea. A contraction pulls everything together by a fixed factor each time, so the iterates bunch up (they are Cauchy); completeness supplies the point they bunch up to; and "into itself" keeps the iterates where the theorem can see them. Each warning box removes exactly one of the three and the conclusion fails.

There is a fourth, subtler failure — distances that shrink, but not by any fixed factor — and the readiness sheet's P11 gives one and asks which hypothesis it breaks, so it is not given here.

The contraction mapping theorem

contraction mapping theorem (Banach)

$(X,d)$ complete and nonempty, $T: X\to X$ a contraction: $d(Tx,Ty)\le q\,d(x,y)$ for one $q\lt1$ and all $x,y$. Then $T$ has exactly one fixed point $x^*$, and from any $x_0$ the iterates $x_{n+1} = Tx_n$ converge to it, with $$d(x_n, x^*)\le\frac{q^n}{1-q}\,d(x_1,x_0).$$

proof

$d(x_{k+1},x_k)\le q^kd(x_1,x_0)$, so $d(x_n,x_m)\le\sum_{k\ge n}q^kd(x_1,x_0) = \frac{q^n}{1-q}d(x_1,x_0)$: Cauchy. Complete: $x_n\to x^*$, and letting $m\to\infty$ gives the bound. $T$ is continuous, so $Tx^* = x^*$. Two fixed points: $d(x^*,y^*)\le q\,d(x^*,y^*)$ forces $d = 0$.

$T(x) = 1 + 1/x$ on $[1.5, 2]$, $q = 4/9$. The iterates spiral in to $\varphi$.

The example checked. On $[1.5, 2]$, $T$ is decreasing, with $T(1.5) = 1.667$ and $T(2) = 1.5$, so $T$ maps the interval into itself; $[1.5, 2]$ is closed in $\mathbb R$, hence complete. By the mean value theorem $|Tx - Ty| = |T'(\xi)||x - y|$ with $|T'(\xi)| = 1/\xi^2\le1/2.25 = 4/9$. The fixed point solves $x^2 = x + 1$: $\varphi = (1 + \sqrt5)/2 = 1.6180$.

The bound against the truth. From $x_0 = 2$, $x_1 = 1.5$, so the bound is $0.9\,(4/9)^n$, which is below $10^{-6}$ once $n\ge17$. In fact $|x_n - \varphi|\lt10^{-6}$ already at $n = 14$. Why the bound is so pessimistic is what the sheet's P11 asks, for $\cos x$; try answering it here first, for $1 + 1/x$.

Every hypothesis is doing something. Drop completeness, drop "into itself", or weaken the single $q\lt1$, and the theorem fails; each has a counterexample, and P11 asks for one. The proof shows where each is used: $q\lt1$ in the geometric series, completeness in "$x_n\to x^*$", and "into itself" in "the iterates are defined at all".

R · metric spaces · smallest cases

Picard's iteration on the simplest ODE: $\dot x = x$

integrate, substitute, repeat

$\dot x = x$, $x(0) = 1$ is the same as $x(t) = 1 + \int_0^tx(s)\,\mathrm ds$. Start from the constant guess $x_0 = 1$:

$$\begin{aligned}x_1 &= 1 + t,\\ x_2 &= 1 + t + \tfrac{t^2}{2},\\ x_3 &= 1 + t + \tfrac{t^2}{2} + \tfrac{t^3}{6},\ \dots\end{aligned}$$

— the Taylor polynomials of $e^t$, the one solution.

the idea

Turn the differential equation into an integral equation, $x = \Phi(x)$, where $(\Phi x)(t) = 1 + \int_0^tx(s)\,\mathrm ds$. Integrating smooths and shrinks differences, so on a short enough interval $\Phi$ is a contraction, and the contraction mapping theorem gives existence, uniqueness, and an algorithm, all at once.

The next slide does the same for the spring, with vectors in place of numbers.

The contraction estimate here. $|(\Phi x - \Phi y)(t)| = \big|\int_0^t(x - y)\big|\le t\,\sup|x - y|$, so on $[0,\tau]$ with $\tau \lt 1$ the map shrinks sup-distances by the factor $\tau$. Exactly the spring's estimate, one dimension down.

Why the integral form. Differentiation magnifies differences — two functions close in value can have very different slopes, as the $\sin(n^2x)/n$ example on Each swap, broken in the smallest way showed — while integration shrinks them. That asymmetry is why every existence proof for ODEs works with the integral equation.

R · metric spaces

The spring has exactly one motion, and iterating finds it

Integrate $\dot u = Ju$: a solution with $u(0) = u_0$ is a fixed point of $$(\Phi u)(t) = u_0 + \int_0^tJu(s)\,\mathrm ds\quad\text{on } X = C([0,\tau];\mathbb R^2),\ d_\infty .$$

worked · a contraction for $\tau\lt1$

$J$ turns vectors without stretching them: $|Jw| = |w|$. So $|(\Phi u - \Phi w)(t)|\le\int_0^t|u - w|\le \tau\,d_\infty(u,w)$. With $q = \tau\lt1$ and $X$ complete, there is exactly one solution on $[0,\tau]$.

the iterates are the Taylor polynomials

From the constant $u_0$: $\Phi u_0 = (I + tJ)u_0$, then $(I + tJ + \tfrac{t^2}2J^2)u_0$, … — the partial sums of $R(t)u_0$, by induction.

Picard's iterates for release from $x = 1$ at rest, closing in on $\cos t$.

The induction. If $u_k(s) = \sum_{n\le k}\frac{(sJ)^n}{n!}u_0$, then $u_0 + \int_0^tJu_k = u_0 + \sum_{n\le k}\frac{t^{n+1}J^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}u_0 = \sum_{n\le k+1}\frac{(tJ)^n}{n!}u_0$. With $u_0 = (1, 0)$ the position component gains a term only at every second step, which is why the figure's $x_0 = x_1$ and $x_2 = x_3$.

The restriction $\tau\lt1$ is an artefact. The iterates are the Taylor partial sums, and those converge on any interval. With the plain sup norm the contraction constant is $\tau$, which is only below $1$ for short times; unit 01 replaces it by a weighted norm, $\sup_t e^{-\lambda|t|}|u(t)|$ with a suitable $\lambda$, under which $\Phi$ is a contraction on every bounded interval. That is the Picard–Lindelöf theorem, unit 01's central proof.

What this bought. Uniqueness is the real content: the spring released from a given state has one future. Unit 01 is titled Determinism for that reason, and it also shows a force law for which uniqueness fails, because the contraction estimate fails.

Groups: symmetry as an algebraic object

The course uses only the first chapter of group theory: groups, homomorphisms, and groups acting on sets. Its payoff, in unit 13, is that every conservation law comes from a symmetry.

R · groups · smallest cases

Groups, non-groups, and maps that respect the product

groups

$(\mathbb Z, +)$; $\{1, -1\}$ under multiplication; the four rotations of a square by multiples of $90^\circ$.

not groups

$(\mathbb N, +)$: no inverses ($-3\notin\mathbb N$). $(\mathbb Z, -)$: not associative, $(5 - 3) - 1 \ne 5 - (3 - 1)$.

homomorphisms

Parity, $n\mapsto(-1)^n$ from $(\mathbb Z,+)$ to $\{\pm1\}$: $(-1)^{m+n} = (-1)^m(-1)^n$. Kernel: the even integers. Not injective.

The exponential, $a\mapsto e^a$ from $(\mathbb R,+)$ to $(\mathbb R_{\gt0},\times)$: $e^{a+b} = e^ae^b$. Kernel: $\{0\}$. A bijection — an isomorphism: adding and multiplying positive numbers are the same group.

The idea of a homomorphism: a map that turns one group's product into the other's, so that calculations can be done on either side. Logarithm tables were exactly this isomorphism, used to turn multiplication into addition.

The kernel measures what is forgotten. Parity forgets everything about $n$ but whether it is even; its kernel, the even integers, is everything sent to "no change". The spring's homomorphism on the next slide forgets multiples of $2\pi$ — the motion repeats — and its kernel is $2\pi\mathbb Z$.

Groups and homomorphisms, and the spring's group

group, homomorphism

A set with an associative product, an identity and inverses. A homomorphism $\phi$ has $\phi(gh) = \phi(g)\phi(h)$; its kernel $\phi^{-1}(e)$, with $e$ the identity, is a subgroup, and it is trivial iff $\phi$ is injective, since $\phi(g) = \phi(h)$ iff $\phi(gh^{-1}) = e$.

worked · $t\mapsto R(t)$ is a homomorphism $(\mathbb R,+)\to SO(2)$

Claim: $R(s)R(t) = R(s+t)$. Fix $s$. Both $t\mapsto R(s)R(t)u_0$ and $t\mapsto R(s+t)u_0$ solve $\dot u = Ju$ (as $J$ commutes with $R(s)$), and both equal $R(s)u_0$ at $t = 0$. One solution only (section 4; unit 01 extends it to all $t$): they agree.

Kernel: $R(t) = I$ iff $\cos t = 1$: $t\in2\pi\mathbb Z$. Not injective — the motion repeats.

A continuous homomorphism from $(\mathbb R,+)$ is a one-parameter group. Unit 13 builds on exactly these.

The other proof of $R(s)R(t) = R(s+t)$ is to multiply out and use the addition formulas for $\cos$ and $\sin$. The uniqueness proof is the better habit, because it works for every linear ODE with constant coefficients, where no addition formula is available: "both sides solve the same equation with the same start" proves $e^{(s+t)A} = e^{sA}e^{tA}$ for any square matrix $A$, which unit 07 needs. The step "$J$ commutes with $R(s)$" holds because $R(s)$ is a power series in $J$.

Onto $SO(2)$. A $2\times2$ matrix with orthonormal columns and determinant $1$ has the form $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\-b&a\end{pmatrix}$ with $a^2 + b^2 = 1$, and every such pair is $(\cos t, \sin t)$ for some $t$. So the homomorphism is onto, and $SO(2)$ is the line $\mathbb R$ wrapped round a circle: $\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z$.

Check the axioms on $SO(n)$. If $Q_1, Q_2$ are orthogonal, $(Q_1Q_2)^{\mathsf T}Q_1Q_2 = Q_2^{\mathsf T}Q_2 = I$, and $\det(Q_1Q_2) = 1$; the inverse is $Q^{\mathsf T}$. It is a subgroup of $GL(n)$, and $\det: O(n)\to\{\pm1\}$ is a homomorphism with kernel $SO(n)$.

R · groups · smallest cases

A group acting on three objects, and one acting on a line

$S_3$ permuting $\{1, 2, 3\}$

Orbit of $1$: every permutation can send $1$ anywhere, so all of $\{1,2,3\}$ — size $3$.

Stabiliser of $1$: the permutations leaving $1$ alone, $\{e, (2\,3)\}$, where $(2\,3)$ swaps $2$ and $3$ — size $2$.

$|S_3| = 6 = 3\times2$ ✓.

$\mathbb Z$ shifting $\mathbb R$: $n\cdot x = x + n$

Orbit of $0.3$: $\{\dots, -0.7, 0.3, 1.3, \dots\}$. Each orbit has exactly one point in $[0, 1)$.

Stabiliser: only $0$, since $x + n = x$ forces $n = 0$.

The set of orbits is $[0,1)$ with its ends glued: a circle.

the idea

An orbit is everywhere a point can be moved to; a stabiliser is the moves that leave it alone. The more moves leave a point fixed, the fewer places it can go: for finite $G$, $|G| = |\text{orbit}|\times|\text{stabiliser}|$.

Why the counting works, in the $S_3$ case. The six permutations split into three pairs according to where they send $1$; each pair is a coset of the stabiliser $\{e, (2\,3)\}$. Three cosets, three places, two moves per place.

The circle of orbits is the same circle as the spring's: $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ here, $\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z$ there. A line wrapped round by a group of shifts is a circle; that is the shape of every periodic motion.

Groups acting: orbits, stabilisers, and the counting theorem

group action, orbit, stabiliser

An action of $G$ on $X$: $e\cdot x = x$ and $g\cdot(h\cdot x) = (gh)\cdot x$. The orbit of $x$ is $G\cdot x = \{g\cdot x\}$; its stabiliser is $G_x = \{g : g\cdot x = x\}$, a subgroup. Orbits partition $X$: "in the same orbit" is an equivalence relation.

orbit–stabiliser theorem

For finite $G$: $|G| = |G\cdot x|\cdot|G_x|$. Proof: $gG_x\mapsto g\cdot x$ is a well-defined bijection from the cosets of $G_x$ onto the orbit.

$D_4$ acting on the square's vertices: one orbit of $4$, stabilisers of order $2$.

The square, in full. $D_4$ has four rotations ($e, r, r^2, r^3$) and four reflections (two in the diagonals, two in the lines through mid-edges). Rotations alone carry vertex 1 to every vertex, so the orbit has $4$ elements. The only symmetries fixing vertex 1 are $e$ and the reflection $s$ in the diagonal through 1 and 3. And $8 = 4\times2$.

Why "well-defined". If $gG_x = hG_x$ then $h^{-1}g\in G_x$, so $h^{-1}g\cdot x = x$ and $g\cdot x = h\cdot x$. Read backwards that is injectivity; surjectivity is the definition of the orbit.

The proofs and examples are in Armstrong, Groups and Symmetry, up to and including the chapter on actions, orbits and stabilisers — the sheet's book for this group, and all the group theory the course assumes.

The spring's group acting on its phase plane: orbits are energies

worked · $SO(2)$ acting on $\mathbb R^2$

Orbit of $u\ne0$: every rotation of $u$ — the circle of radius $|u|$. Orbit of $0$: just $\{0\}$.

Stabiliser of $u\ne0$: only $I$ (no rotation but the trivial one fixes a nonzero vector). Of $0$: all of $SO(2)$.

Orbit–stabiliser in its continuous form: the circle is a copy of $SO(2)/\{I\} = SO(2)$ itself.

the point of it

The motion is the group acting: $u(t) = R(t)u_0$. So the motion never leaves the orbit it starts on — and the orbits are the circles of constant energy. The conserved quantity labels the orbits.

The same figure as the overview, read as a group acting: each circle is an orbit.

Why no nonzero vector is fixed. $R(t)u = u$ means $u$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$; but section 3 showed $R(t)$ has no real eigenvector unless $t$ is a multiple of $\pi$, and at odd multiples $R = -I$. So only $R = I$ fixes a nonzero vector.

A preview of unit 13, not a theorem here. On this slide the conserved quantity happens to label the orbits of the group that is the motion. Noether's theorem is a different and deeper statement: a symmetry of the laws — a group acting on the motions that leaves the equations unchanged — produces a conserved quantity. Energy comes from time translation, momentum from space translation, angular momentum from rotation. This slide is only the first sign that groups and conservation laws belong together.

The flipped spring again. For $\ddot x = +x$ the flow is a one-parameter group of hyperbolic rotations, preserving $v^2 - x^2$, the form of Quadratic forms, signature, and Sylvester's law. Which sets are its orbits, and whether the motion ever repeats, is the sheet's P12; in unit 26 that group is the Lorentz boosts.
