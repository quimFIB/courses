Quantum Computing

Twenty-seven units from the question “why complex numbers?” to a surface-code error budget, in which every postulate arrives as mathematics with the experiment that forced it beside it, and every speedup is stated next to the best classical algorithm and what is proved about it. Finite-dimensional throughout; no physics assumed, and no physics course required first.

The premise

Four commitments shape this curriculum. The first two say what gets taught and how; the third is about honesty, which is the subject's chronic weakness; the fourth is about whether the graph actually closes.

Linear algebra carries the definition and the experiment carries the motivation, and the postulates are not handed down. Most courses open with “a qubit is a unit vector in ℂ²” and never say why ℂ. This one makes it the first theorem: three Stern–Gerlach directions are pairwise unbiased — prepare along one, measure along another, get a fair coin — and ℝ² can hold at most two mutually unbiased bases while ℂ² holds three. Unit 00 proves both halves; unit 02 proves that three is the maximum. Each later postulate is introduced the same way: the experiment first, as a puzzle; then the mathematical object, defined properly; then the theorem that shows the object does the job. A composite system is a tensor product, defined by its universal property, because that is what makes dimension multiply — and dimension multiplying is the entire reason quantum computing is interesting. The physics is always there, because it is why the objects exist; it never stands in for a definition.

You have the maturity and the complexity theory, so the course uses both. Nothing is drilled, everything load-bearing is proved, and every claim is proved on the slide, proved in the notes, or labelled cited with where the proof lives. The spectral theorem for normal operators is proved in unit 00, Stinespring and Choi in unit 21, Knill–Laflamme in unit 22, Gottesman–Knill in unit 23. And because you already own Turing machines, reductions and Cook–Levin, unit 12 can place BQP precisely — BPP ⊆ BQP ⊆ PP ⊆ PSPACE, each inclusion proved — rather than wave at it, and unit 18 can prove the lower bounds that most courses only quote. Where the proof is a research paper (Solovay–Kitaev's exponent, the full threshold theorem, Shor–Preskill security, strong subadditivity), the statement and its hypotheses are given exactly and the proof is cited, not gestured at.

No speedup without its classical baseline, and what quantum computers cannot do is a spine unit. The subject's literature is full of comparisons against the wrong classical algorithm. So every algorithm unit names the best known classical method and what is proved about it, and says plainly when the separation is weaker than it looks: Deutsch–Jozsa beats deterministic algorithms exponentially and randomised ones not at all, which is why Simon (unit 14) and not Deutsch–Jozsa is the first real separation. Unit 18 proves that Grover is optimal and that total functions admit at most polynomial speedups, so that “quantum computers solve NP-complete problems by trying everything at once” is refuted by a theorem rather than a disclaimer. The course ends not at Shor's algorithm but at an error budget: how many physical qubits, at what error rate, for how many hours, to factor a 2048-bit modulus — derived by you, then checked against the published estimates. Branch 20 adds the quantum Cook–Levin theorem: finding ground energies is QMA-complete, so there are physical questions even a quantum computer cannot answer efficiently.

It is a dependency graph, and both short routes are closed. There are two trunks above a shared foundation: the algorithmic line (circuits, then queries, Fourier and Shor, Grover and its lower bounds) and the noise line (channels, codes, stabilisers, fault tolerance). Both descend from Part A through unit 10's circuits, and neither needs the other until the end, where unit 26 and the capstone join them. Five branch units — 08, 09, 20, 25, 26 — are worth taking and on no spine unit's path. The express route to Shor and Grover is ten units with every needs satisfied on the route, and a second closed route reaches the threshold theorem in eleven. What each costs is named below rather than hidden.

What a unit is

The same contract as Mechanics to Relativity, so the tooling and the habits carry over — plus one optional addition that the subject earns and that course did not need. A unit is done when the checkpoint is true, not when you have read the chapter.

Theory

A deck of 25–45 slides, speaker notes on every content slide. Mathematics carries the definition, the experiment carries the motivation, and every claim is proved or labelled cited. Drawn whenever it can be drawn: the Bloch sphere, the circuit, Grover's plane, the surface-code lattice — a slide is split rather than a figure dropped.

Worked examples

One running example per unit, introduced on the overview slide, returned to in every section and closed at the end, plus three or four fully worked examples. Every amplitude is normalised and every probability summed to one on the slide, which is this subject's version of checking the dimensions.

Problems and solutions

8–14 problems, at least one a proof. Every problem has a hint ladder, rung by rung from “what kind of object is the answer” to the first line, and a complete worked solution folded under it. You cannot ask a question, so no step is “clearly”.

Checkpoint

One falsifiable claim about what you can now do, usually ending in a number: 0.8536 for Helstrom, 2√2 for Tsirelson, 804 Grover iterations for a million items, 881 physical qubits per logical one. If you cannot make it true, you know exactly what is missing.

Referee — optional

qclib, a small NumPy simulator shipped with the course — uv sync once. It checks any amplitude, density matrix or circuit you computed by hand, and it does not care how convincing the algebra felt. Every checkpoint is reachable on paper without it.

How long. 2–3 h of slides and 4–6 h of problems a unit, so 6–9 h. Summed over all 27 units that is 72 h of slides and 130.5 h of problems — 202.5 hours, plus 15–20 h for the capstone. By part: A 37 h, B 35 h, C 23 h, D 60 h, E 47.5 h. Each card gives its own estimate. Take it as a scale, not a schedule. Paper is the whole cost of entry; the referee adds a uv-managed virtualenv and nothing else.

Who this assumes you are

Everything on the left is used hard and never re-taught. Everything on the right is taught here, including all of the physics. Nothing from Mechanics to Relativity is a prerequisite; three of its units are cross-referenced where they shorten the way, and each reference is optional.

Assumed

  • Mathematical maturity. You read and write proofs and habitually ask which hypothesis a theorem actually needs. Assumed everywhere.
  • Python, only if you use the referee. Enough to call a library and read a traceback.
  • Linear algebra, properly — over ℝ is enough. Vector spaces, bases, linear maps, dual spaces, determinants, eigenvalues, inner products, the singular value decomposition. The complex theory is built on this in unit 00; the SVD is cashed in hard as the Schmidt decomposition in 03.
  • Elementary group theory. Groups, subgroups, cosets, quotients, homomorphisms, Lagrange. Used for SU(2) → SO(3) in 02, the Pauli and Clifford groups in 11 and 23, and hidden subgroups in 16.
  • Classical complexity theory. Turing machines, P, NP, reductions, Cook–Levin, uniform circuit families, and BPP with its amplification. You have proved a quasilinear Cook–Levin; unit 12 assumes you can read one.
  • Discrete probability. Expectation, independence, union bound, Chernoff–Hoeffding stated. Measurement outcomes are random variables from unit 01 onwards.
  • Elementary number theory. Modular arithmetic, Euclid's algorithm, the Chinese remainder theorem, Euler's theorem. Shor (16) uses all four on one slide.

Not assumed — all taught here

  • Any physics whatsoever, and in particular any quantum mechanics. The four postulates are unit 01.
  • Complex inner-product spaces and the spectral theorem for normal operators — unit 00, with functional calculus.
  • SU(2), SO(3) and the double cover — unit 02.
  • Tensor products — unit 03, by universal property. (Mechanics to Relativity unit 16 does the same from multilinear maps; either way in.)
  • Positive operators, trace, partial trace, convexity — unit 04.
  • Shannon and von Neumann entropy — unit 08.
  • Fourier analysis on finite abelian groups — unit 15. (Mechanics to Relativity unit 20 is the continuous version, not needed.)
  • Continued fractions — unit 16, Legendre's theorem proved.
  • Completely positive maps — unit 21.
  • Classical linear codes over 𝔽₂ — unit 22, as far as Hamming.
  • Lie–Trotter product formulas — unit 19. (Mechanics to Relativity unit 14's Hamiltonian flow is the classical picture of the same generator.)

Part A — a finite quantum system, mathematics first

Five units that turn four experiments into a definition, and prove the definition is the only one that works

Stern–Gerlach forces complex amplitudes, the Mach–Zehnder interferometer forces relative phase, the precessing spin forces unitary evolution, and two photons from one crystal force the tensor product. Part A ends with the density operator, the object that says what a subsystem knows when it is entangled with something you cannot see. Five units, 37 h.

00

Why complex numbers: unbiased bases, and the spectral theorem for normal operators

spine

needs nothing2.5 + 4.5 h

Theory
The Stern–Gerlach experiment as a puzzle with no theory attached: silver atoms through an inhomogeneous field split into exactly two beams; filter one, measure along a perpendicular axis, and the split is 50/50; measure along the original axis again and the first result is gone. Take the minimal model — states are unit vectors, outcome probabilities are |⟨u, v⟩|² — and ask whether it fits in ℝ². It does not: the three spatial axes give three pairwise mutually unbiased bases, and ℝ² has room for two.
Tools
Complex inner-product spaces, sesquilinearity, the adjoint. Dirac notation as notation for the Riesz isomorphism V → V*: a bra is a dual vector, nothing more, and |u⟩⟨v| is a rank-one map. Hermitian, unitary and normal operators. Schur triangularisation, then the spectral theorem for normal operators proved from it; simultaneous diagonalisation of commuting normal operators. Functional calculus: f(A) for normal A, and eiH unitary for Hermitian H.
Problems
The slides prove that ℝ² admits at most two mutually unbiased bases — every unit vector unbiased to the standard basis lies at ±45°, and there is only one such basis — and exhibit three in ℂ² (the eigenbases of what unit 02 will call σz, σx, σy). That three is the maximum in ℂ² is left as a debt for unit 02. The sheet computes eiθσx = cos θ · I + i sin θ · σx three ways (series, spectral, Cayley–Hamilton), shows a non-normal matrix with no orthonormal eigenbasis, proves that unitaries preserve unbiasedness, and states (cited: Wootters–Fields 1989) that ℂd holds d + 1 mutually unbiased bases when d is a prime power.
Checkpoint. You prove the spectral theorem for normal operators from Schur, and prove that ℝ² holds at most two mutually unbiased bases while ℂ² holds at least three — so that “amplitudes are complex” is, for you, a theorem about an experiment.
01

The postulates, as mathematics: states, measurement, and evolution

spine

needs 002.5 + 4.5 h

Theory
The running example is the Mach–Zehnder interferometer: one photon, two beam splitters, a phase shifter in one arm, and a detector that never clicks when the arms are balanced. Probabilities cannot add here; amplitudes do. From it, the postulates: a state is a ray in a complex Hilbert space (unit vector modulo global phase, and why the global phase is unobservable while the relative one runs the interferometer); an observable is a self-adjoint operator; the Born rule and projective measurement with its post-measurement state; and closed-system evolution is unitary.
Tools
Spectral projections and the Born rule as a probability measure on the spectrum; expectation and variance as ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩ and its square. Stone's theorem in finite dimension, proved: every continuous one-parameter group of unitaries is e−iHt for a unique Hermitian H — so the Schrödinger equation is not a separate postulate but the generator of the evolution postulate. The Robertson uncertainty relation ΔA ΔB ≥ ½|⟨[A, B]⟩| from Cauchy–Schwarz, and why it is a statement about preparations, not about disturbance.
Problems
The slides compute the Mach–Zehnder detector probabilities as cos²(φ/2) and sin²(φ/2), and then insert a which-path detector and watch the fringes vanish. The sheet works the Elitzur–Vaidman bomb tester — a live bomb certified without being touched — to its single-pass numbers, then shows that a chain of N weakly-coupled stages pushes the certification probability to 1 − O(1/N), which is the quantum Zeno effect arriving as an exercise. Also: prove two states differing by a global phase give identical statistics for every observable, and that a relative phase does not.
Checkpoint. For the Elitzur–Vaidman setup with a live bomb you derive the three outcome probabilities — ½ explodes, ¼ certified untouched, ¼ inconclusive — and you prove finite-dimensional Stone's theorem.
02

One qubit: the Bloch sphere, and the group that rotates it twice

spine

needs 012.5 + 5 h

Theory
A spin in a magnetic field precesses, and the precession is the whole of single-qubit dynamics. The pure states of a qubit are ℂP¹, which is a sphere: the Bloch map, proved a bijection onto S², with orthogonal states at antipodes. Pauli matrices, the Pauli vector, and e−iθ n·σ/2 as rotation by θ about n. Larmor precession and Rabi oscillation as the two pictures every hardware platform is controlled with; the rotating-wave approximation stated with its condition.
Tools
SU(2) and SO(3); the homomorphism SU(2) → SO(3) via conjugation of the Pauli vector, proved surjective with kernel {±I}. Consequently a 2π rotation multiplies a spinor by −1, which is observable in interference with an unrotated reference (neutron interferometry, 1975, cited). Every single-qubit unitary is eiαRz(β)Ry(γ)Rz(δ), proved.
Problems
The slides run a proton in an MRI magnet as the example: gyromagnetic ratio γ/2π = 42.58 MHz/T, so 63.87 MHz at 1.5 T. The sheet pays unit 00's debt — two bases of ℂ² are mutually unbiased if and only if their Bloch axes are orthogonal, so ℂ² holds exactly three — finds the axis and angle of given 2 × 2 unitaries, produces ZYZ angles, and designs a π-pulse of given Rabi frequency.
Checkpoint. Given any 2 × 2 unitary you produce its rotation axis and angle on the Bloch sphere and its ZYZ decomposition, and you prove SU(2) → SO(3) is surjective with kernel {±I}.
03

Two systems: the tensor product, and the states that are not products

spine

needs 012.5 + 5 h

Theory
A nonlinear crystal turns one photon into two whose polarisations, measured in the same basis, always disagree — in every basis — a fact no assignment of separate states to each photon reproduces. The composite-system postulate: the joint space is HA ⊗ HB, not HA ⊕ HB, and the difference is the difference between 2n and 2n dimensions for n qubits. Product and entangled states; the four Bell states; measuring one subsystem as the projective measurement P ⊗ I.
Tools
The tensor product defined by its universal property — the bilinear map V × W → V ⊗ W through which every bilinear map factors uniquely — then constructed, with uniqueness up to unique isomorphism proved. Bases and dimension; A ⊗ B on operators and the Kronecker matrix. The Schmidt decomposition, proved from the singular value decomposition, and Schmidt rank as the criterion: a pure bipartite state is entangled if and only if its rank exceeds one.
Problems
The slides show the dimension count that makes the subject: product states of n qubits form a 2n-real-parameter family inside a space of 2n+1 − 2. The sheet: a two-qubit state is entangled exactly when the determinant of its 2 × 2 coefficient matrix is non-zero; Schmidt-decompose given states; prove |Φ⁺⟩ is invariant under U ⊗ Ū for every unitary U, which is the algebraic heart of unit 07.
Checkpoint. You prove the universal property determines V ⊗ W up to unique isomorphism, and for any two-qubit state you decide entanglement and compute the Schmidt coefficients.
04

Density operators: what a subsystem knows, and why Alice cannot signal

spine

needs 03 023 + 5 h

Theory
Half of a Bell pair is not in any pure state, so the state space must grow. Density operators: positive, trace one, convex; pure if and only if tr ρ² = 1. For a qubit, the Bloch ball, with the maximally mixed state at the centre. Two different preparation stories — |0⟩ or |1⟩ with probability ½, and |+⟩ or |−⟩ with probability ½ — give the same operator and therefore identical statistics for every measurement: the operator is the state, the story is not.
Tools
The partial trace, defined as the unique linear map with tr((A ⊗ I)ρ) = tr(A ρA) for all A, and uniqueness proved. Purification, proved to exist. The Schrödinger–HJW theorem: two ensembles give the same density operator if and only if they are related by an isometry, proved. The no-signalling theorem: nothing Alice does locally — unitary or measurement — changes ρB, proved in two lines from the definition of partial trace.
Problems
The slides take the partial trace of each Bell state and get I/2 every time. The sheet: purify a given qubit state; find the unitary relating two given ensembles; show a pure bipartite state is entangled if and only if its reduced state is mixed; prove the Bloch ball is exactly the set of 2 × 2 density operators.
Checkpoint. You prove the no-signalling theorem from the defining property of the partial trace, and exhibit the HJW isometry between two given ensembles for the maximally mixed qubit.

Part B — correlations: what entanglement can and cannot do

Entanglement is stronger than any classical correlation and still cannot send a message, and both halves are theorems

No-cloning and imperfect distinguishability say what a single unknown state withholds; Bell and Tsirelson bound what shared entanglement gives, from both sides; teleportation and dense coding spend it as a resource with exact exchange rates. Two branches take it further — into information theory and into cryptography. Five units, 35 h.

05

No-cloning, and how well two states can be told apart

spine

needs 032 + 4 h

Theory
Opened by history, because here the history is the argument: Herbert's 1982 FLASH proposal for faster-than-light signalling, which needed a copier, and the two papers that killed it within months. The no-cloning theorem proved twice — once from linearity, once from preservation of inner products, where ⟨ψ|φ⟩ = ⟨ψ|φ⟩² forces 0 or 1. Orthogonal states copy freely (CNOT is a classical copier), so the theorem is precisely about non-orthogonality.
Tools
Non-orthogonal states cannot be perfectly distinguished, proved. The Helstrom bound for two equiprobable pure states, ½(1 + √(1 − |⟨ψ|φ⟩|²)), proved by reducing to the plane the two states span. The optimal universal cloner's fidelity 5/6 stated (Bužek–Hillery, cited). Unambiguous discrimination stated here and proved in unit 21, when POVMs exist.
Problems
The slides distinguish |0⟩ from |+⟩: overlap 1/√2, optimal success ½(1 + 1/√2) = 0.8536, measuring in the basis halfway between them. The sheet: show that a perfect cloner would let Alice signal through a shared Bell pair, completing the FLASH refutation from unit 03's no-signalling for projective measurements; prove no-deleting; compute Helstrom for states at Bloch angle θ.
Checkpoint. You prove no-cloning both ways and prove the Helstrom bound, producing 0.8536 and the optimal measurement for |0⟩ versus |+⟩.
06

Bell, CHSH and Tsirelson: the classical bound, the quantum bound, and the gap between

spine

needs 043 + 5 h

Theory
A local hidden-variable model defined as mathematics — a probability space Λ and response functions for each party depending only on their own setting — so that “local realism” is a hypothesis with content. The CHSH quantity, bounded by 2 in every such model, proved. The singlet with the right four angles reaches 2√2. The experiments: Aspect 1982; the 2015 loophole-free tests, Delft's at S = 2.42 ± 0.20 over 1.3 km; the 2022 Nobel prize.
Tools
Tsirelson's bound, proved: for ±1-valued observables C² = 4I − [A, A′][B, B′], so ‖C‖ ≤ 2√2. The CHSH game as a nonlocal game: 3/4 classically, cos²(π/8) quantumly. The Popescu–Rohrlich box reaches 4 while respecting no-signalling — so no-signalling is not what stops quantum mechanics at 2√2, and why it stops there remains a live question.
Problems
The slides derive the optimal angles and the value 2√2 from the singlet's correlation −cos(θa − θb). The sheet: prove the classical bound by reducing to deterministic strategies; show the PR box is no-signalling; prove the CHSH game value cos²(π/8) ≈ 0.854 is optimal; and read Delft's S = 2.42 ± 0.20 critically — how many standard deviations above 2, and why the authors reported a p-value of 0.039 rather than a sigma count.
Checkpoint. You prove CHSH ≤ 2 for every local hidden-variable model and ≤ 2√2 for every quantum strategy, and produce measurement directions on the singlet that attain 2√2.
07

Teleportation and dense coding: the exchange rate between qubits, bits and ebits

spine

needs 04 052 + 4 h

Theory
Teleportation derived as one algebraic identity: |ψ⟩ ⊗ |Φ⁺⟩ rewritten in the Bell basis on Alice's side. One ebit and two classical bits move one qubit; the original is destroyed, so no-cloning survives; and before the bits arrive Bob holds I/2, so no-signalling survives. Superdense coding runs the trade the other way. Entanglement swapping as teleportation of half a pair. Experiments: Innsbruck 1997; Micius 2017, ground to satellite.
Tools
Resource inequalities as bookkeeping: 1 ebit + 2 cbits ≥ 1 qubit; 1 ebit + 1 qubit ≥ 2 cbits. The Holevo bound stated (cited here, proved in branch 08): one qubit alone never carries more than one bit, so dense coding is optimal. Average fidelity over the Bloch sphere as the figure of merit, and the classical measure-and-prepare benchmark of 2/3.
Problems
The slides draw the teleportation circuit and trace all four branches. The sheet: show the naive measure-in-Z-and-resend strategy has average fidelity 1 − ½⟨sin²θ⟩ = 2/3, and state (cited, Massar–Popescu 1995) that no classical strategy does better — so a measured fidelity above 2/3 certifies that something quantum happened; verify entanglement swapping; teleport through a noisy pair and compute the fidelity as a function of the noise.
Checkpoint. You derive teleportation with all four corrections, prove Bob's state is I/2 until the bits arrive, and compute the 2/3 classical benchmark.
08

Entropy and the Holevo bound: how many bits a qubit holds

branch

needs 073 + 5 h

Theory
A qubit has a continuum of states and yields one bit. Shannon entropy as the answer to “how many bits” for a classical source; von Neumann entropy as its quantum successor, equal to the Shannon entropy of the spectrum. Concavity, subadditivity, the Araki–Lieb inequality, proved; strong subadditivity stated (Lieb–Ruskai 1973, cited) and used. The Holevo bound on accessible information, proved from strong subadditivity in the form that discarding a subsystem never increases quantum mutual information — the case of the monotonicity of relative entropy that strong subadditivity is.
Tools
Relative entropy and Klein's inequality, proved. Typical sequences and typical subspaces; Schumacher compression stated with its rate S(ρ) and the proof's structure given. Entanglement entropy of a pure bipartite state as the unique measure of pure-state entanglement (stated).
Problems
The sheet: compute the Holevo quantity for |0⟩ and |+⟩ sent with equal probability — the average state has eigenvalues cos²(π/8) and sin²(π/8), so χ = 0.601 bits, against one bit for orthogonal states; prove superdense coding saturates Holevo with an ebit; show that the whole can have less entropy than a part, S(AB) < S(A), for an entangled state and never for a separable one.
Checkpoint. You prove the Holevo bound from strong subadditivity and compute χ = 0.601 bits for the |0⟩/|+⟩ ensemble.
09

Quantum key distribution: disturbance as a security guarantee

branch

needs 06 052.5 + 4.5 h

Theory
No-cloning turned from a limitation into a weapon. BB84: random bases, sifting, error estimation, then classical error correction and privacy amplification, each step's job named. E91: the same key from a Bell pair, with a CHSH test as the eavesdropper alarm — the seed of device-independent cryptography, where security rests on unit 06's inequality rather than on trusting the hardware. Micius 2017, satellite-to-ground over 1200 km.
Tools
The information–disturbance trade-off for BB84 states, proved for intercept-resend. The Shor–Preskill key rate 1 − 2h(Q) for quantum bit error rate Q, stated with its proof cited, and its reading: error correction costs h(Q), privacy amplification costs another. What QKD does not do: authenticate, which still needs a shared secret.
Problems
The sheet: show intercept-resend in a random basis yields a 25 per cent error rate on the sifted key; compute the key rate at Q = 5 per cent (0.427 bits per sifted bit); find where it reaches zero (Q ≈ 11.0 per cent); show that a CHSH value of 2√2 leaves an eavesdropper no information in E91.
Checkpoint. You prove intercept-resend causes 25 per cent error, and produce the 11.0 per cent threshold from the Shor–Preskill rate.

Part C — circuits, and the complexity class they define

A quantum computer is a uniform family of circuits, and three units are enough to say exactly what that buys

Exact universality first, because it is pure linear algebra; then approximate universality, which is where the cost of finite gate sets is paid and where the T gate becomes special; then BQP, placed between BPP and PP with every inclusion proved. Three units, 23 h.

10

Circuits: reversible classical computation, and exact universality

spine

needs 03 022.5 + 5 h

Theory
The circuit model: qubits as wires, gates as unitaries on few of them, measurement at the end. The principle of deferred measurement, proved, so that end-only measurement loses nothing. Classical computation inside it: Toffoli is universal for reversible computation, and Bennett's uncomputation removes the garbage, proved — so every polynomial-time f yields a polynomial circuit |x⟩|y⟩ ↦ |x⟩|y ⊕ f(x)⟩, which is what every oracle in Part D is.
Tools
Every unitary on ℂd is a product of two-level unitaries, proved; Gray codes move each onto adjacent basis states; so CNOT plus single-qubit gates is exactly universal, proved (Barenco et al. 1995). Controlled-U from single-qubit gates and two CNOTs via the ZYZ form of unit 02. The counting lower bound: a generic n-qubit unitary has 4n − 1 real parameters, so exact synthesis needs Ω(4n) gates — most unitaries are not efficiently implementable, and the interesting ones are the exceptions.
Problems
The slides build Toffoli from six CNOTs, two Hadamards and seven T or T† gates, and verify it on all eight inputs. The sheet: build controlled-controlled-U; compile a given two-qubit unitary; prove deferred measurement; uncompute a garbage register for a reversible adder. Optionally, write your own statevector simulator — the tensor trick is ten lines — and test it against qclib on random circuits.
Checkpoint. You prove CNOT plus single-qubit gates exactly universal, and build and verify the seven-T Toffoli circuit.
11

Finite gate sets: density, Solovay–Kitaev, and why the T gate is the expensive one

spine

needs 103 + 5 h

Theory
A real machine has finitely many gates, so universality must be approximate. Operator-norm distance, and errors add along a circuit: ‖Un⋯U₁ − Vn⋯V₁‖ ≤ Σ‖Ui − Vi‖, proved. {H, T} generates a dense subgroup of SU(2) up to phase: T · HTH is a rotation by an angle θ with cos(θ/2) = cos²(π/8), an irrational multiple of π — density proved, the irrationality too, in four lines of algebraic integers. The Clifford group as the normaliser of the Pauli group; Clifford + T as the standard universal set.
Tools
The Solovay–Kitaev theorem: any gate to accuracy ε in O(logc(1/ε)) gates, c ≈ 4 in the Dawson–Nielsen version, with the balanced group-commutator step proved and the recursion's analysis cited. Exact synthesis over Clifford + T (Kliuchnikov–Maslov–Mosca: entries in ℤ[1/√2, i]) and optimal z-rotations at about 3 log₂(1/ε) T gates (Ross–Selinger), both cited — the number fault tolerance will later charge for.
Problems
The sheet: given a circuit of 10⁹ gates and a 1 per cent total error budget, find the per-gate accuracy (10⁻¹¹) and the T-count of each arbitrary rotation (about 110); show Clifford gates map Paulis to Paulis; prove the subadditivity bound is tight up to constants.
Checkpoint. You prove the error-accumulation bound and the density of ⟨H, T⟩, and carry a whole-circuit error budget down to a per-rotation T-count.
12

BQP, and where it sits among the classes you already know

spine

needs 112.5 + 5 h

Theory
BQP defined with uniform circuit families and error ⅓; independence of the gate set, by unit 11. Amplification by majority vote, proved with Hoeffding. Then the placement: BPP ⊆ BQP (coins from Hadamards, reversibility from unit 10), BQP ⊆ PSPACE (Feynman's sum over paths, evaluated depth-first in polynomial space), and BQP ⊆ PP (Adleman–DeMarrais–Huang 1997: acceptance probability as a difference of two #P functions), all proved. BQP is low for itself, stated. Its Merlin–Arthur analogue, QMA, is branch 20.
Tools
Path sums and GapP functions; restricting to gates with rational or dyadic entries without loss. What is not known: BQP versus NP in either direction. What is known relative to oracles: BQP ⊄ PH for some oracle (Raz–Tal 2018, cited), and unit 18's lower bound gives an oracle with NP ⊄ BQP. The honest summary: quantum computing is a hypothesis about BQP ≠ BPP, and no proof of it is in sight, since it would separate P from PSPACE.
Problems
The sheet: how many repetitions push error from ⅓ to 2−100 (about 1250 by Hoeffding); prove BQP closed under complement; show that postselected quantum computation contains NP, as the first step toward Aaronson's PostBQP = PP (cited).

Simon's problem: the first exponential separation, and the algorithm Shor read

spine

needs 132 + 4.5 h

Theory
A function on n bits that is two-to-one with a hidden XOR-period s. Quantumly: each run yields a uniformly random y with y · s = 0, and O(n) runs plus Gaussian elimination over 𝔽₂ recover s. Classically: any randomised algorithm needs Ω(2n/2) queries, proved by a collision argument. Relative to this oracle, BPP ≠ BQP. Simon's 1994 paper was the one Shor read, and the history is the argument: replace ℤ₂n by ℤN and you are most of the way to factoring.
Tools
Linear algebra over 𝔽₂: orthogonal complements, rank, elimination. The probability that n − 1 uniform vectors span an (n−1)-dimensional space, bounded below by ∏(1 − 2−k) ≈ 0.289, proved; expected number of runs below n + 1.
Problems
The sheet: run Simon by hand on n = 3 for a given s; prove the collision lower bound; show the infinite product converges to 0.2888.
Checkpoint. You prove the quantum upper bound O(n) and the classical randomised lower bound Ω(2n/2), and compute 0.2888.

Fourier on finite abelian groups, the quantum Fourier transform, and phase estimation

spine

needs 143 + 5 h

Theory
Characters of a finite abelian group, their orthogonality, and the Fourier transform as a unitary change to the character basis — ℤ₂n from unit 13 and ℤN as two instances of one theorem. On ℤ2n the transform of a basis state is a product state, proved, and that product formula is the circuit: n Hadamards and n(n−1)/2 controlled phases, against O(n2n) for the classical FFT on the same vector — with the caveat, stated loudly, that you cannot read the output vector.
Tools
The approximate QFT: dropping rotations below 2π/2m costs at most O(n2−m), so O(n log n) gates suffice (Coppersmith 1994, proved). Phase estimation: given U and an eigenvector, t ancillas give the eigenphase to the nearest t-bit fraction with probability at least 4/π² ≈ 0.405, and n + ⌈log₂(2 + 1/2ε)⌉ ancillas give n bits with probability 1 − ε, both proved.
Problems

Shor: factoring is order finding, and order finding is phase estimation

spine

needs 153 + 6 h

Theory
The reduction, proved: for odd N with at least two distinct prime factors, a random a coprime to N has even order r with ar/2 ≢ −1 with probability at least ½ — a CRT argument — and then gcd(ar/2 ± 1, N) splits N. Order finding as phase estimation on x ↦ ax mod N, whose eigenphases are s/r. Modular exponentiation by repeated squaring as a reversible circuit of O(n³) gates. The same machinery solves discrete logarithms, which is why elliptic-curve cryptography falls with RSA.
Tools
Continued fractions, and Legendre's theorem, proved: if |x − p/q| < 1/(2q²) then p/q is a convergent of x. Recovering r when gcd(s, r) > 1, by repetition and least common multiples. The hidden subgroup problem as the frame for Simon, Shor and discrete log; the abelian case solved, the dihedral and symmetric cases — lattices and graph isomorphism — stated as open.
Problems
The slides factor 15 with a = 7: order 4, 7² = 49 ≡ 4, gcd(3, 15) = 3 and gcd(5, 15) = 5. The sheet does 21 in full with a = 2 and nine counting qubits (21² ≤ 2⁹ < 2 · 21²): the outcome distribution peaks at 0, 85, 171, 256, 341 and 427; 427/512 has convergent 5/6, giving r = 6, then 2³ = 8 and gcd(7, 21), gcd(9, 21) give 7 and 3. Also: compute the probability that one run yields r = 6 directly (0.308), and explain what the other peaks give.
Checkpoint. You prove the order-finding reduction and Legendre's theorem, and factor 21 by hand from the measured distribution, including the runs that fail and why.
17

Grover: search as a rotation in a plane

spine

needs 132.5 + 4.5 h

Theory
The whole algorithm lives in the two-dimensional plane spanned by the uniform superposition and the marked states, and one Grover iteration — two reflections — is a rotation by 2θ with sin θ = √(M/N), proved and drawn. Hence ≈ (π/4)√(N/M) iterations, success probability at least 1 − M/N, and the overshoot when you iterate too long. Amplitude amplification: the same rotation boosts any algorithm with success probability p to near certainty in O(1/√p) runs.
Tools
Unknown M: exponentially growing random iteration counts reach expected O(√(N/M)) (Boyer–Brassard–Høyer–Tapp 1998, proved). Quantum counting as phase estimation on the Grover rotation, joining this unit to unit 15. Applied to key search: AES-128 in about 2⁶⁴ sequential iterations, which is why the response is longer keys rather than new ciphers — and why the sequential depth, not the count, is the real obstacle.
Problems
The sheet: for N = 2²⁰ and one marked item, find the optimal iteration count (804) and its success probability (above 0.999 999 7); find N for which exactly one iteration succeeds with certainty (N = 4); prove the overshoot is periodic.
Checkpoint. You prove the two-reflection rotation picture, and produce 804 iterations and the success probability for a million-item search.

Lower bounds: what quantum computers provably cannot do

spine

needs 17 123 + 5.5 h

Theory
The hybrid argument (Bennett–Bernstein–Brassard–Vazirani 1997), proved: any algorithm finding a marked item among N needs Ω(√N) queries, so Grover is optimal and brute-force search over NP witnesses gains a square root and no more. Relative to a random oracle, NP ⊄ BQP. The polynomial method (Beals–Buhrman–Cleve–Mosca–de Wolf 1998), proved: after T queries every acceptance probability is a polynomial of degree at most 2T in the input bits.
Tools
Symmetrisation (Minsky–Papert), proved, reducing symmetric functions to univariate polynomials. Approximate degree of OR at Θ(√N) (Nisan–Szegedy, Paturi, cited). For total functions, deterministic query complexity is at most a fourth power of quantum (Aaronson–Ben-David–Kothari–Rao–Tal 2021, cited): exponential speedups need a promise — which is exactly what Simon and Shor have and search does not.
Problems
The slides draw the hybrid argument as a sum of small perturbations. The sheet: show parity of N bits takes exactly N/2 quantum queries — the upper bound from Deutsch's algorithm on pairs, the lower bound from degree; prove the BBBV bound with explicit constants.
Checkpoint. You prove Ω(√N) for search by the hybrid argument and exactly N/2 for parity by the polynomial method.

Hamiltonian simulation: Feynman's reason, made into an algorithm

spine

needs 15 112.5 + 4.5 h

Theory
Feynman's 1981 argument: simulating n spins classically tracks 2n amplitudes, so build the simulator out of spins. Local Hamiltonians as the physically relevant class; the transverse-field Ising chain as the running example. Lie–Trotter: for non-commuting A, B, (e−iAt/ne−iBt/n)n approaches e−i(A+B)t with error at most ‖[A, B]‖t²/2n, proved; Strang's symmetric splitting to second order. Phase estimation on the simulated evolution reads off energies.
Tools
Commutator scaling of Trotter error (Childs–Su–Tran–Wiebe–Zhu 2021, cited). Gate counts for a k-local Hamiltonian on n qubits. Qubitisation and quantum signal processing named as the optimal methods. Branch 20 is the other side of the coin: dynamics is easy for a quantum computer, ground energies are not. (Mechanics to Relativity unit 14 is the classical picture: a Hamiltonian generates a flow, and splitting it is how symplectic integrators work.)
Problems
The sheet: for H = X + Z on one qubit at t = 1, compute the actual first-order Trotter error at n = 10 (0.070) against the bound ‖[X, Z]‖/2n = 0.1; count the Trotter steps for an Ising chain to given accuracy.

QMA and the local Hamiltonian problem: Cook–Levin, quantum

branch

needs 12 193 + 5 h

Theory
Merlin sends a quantum proof; Arthur checks it with a BQP circuit. QMA defined with completeness ⅔ and soundness ⅓ — and amplification is no longer free, because the witness cannot be copied. Parallel repetition on a product of witnesses works and entangled cheating witnesses do not help, proved; Marriott–Watrous in-place amplification stated (cited). The k-local Hamiltonian problem: given H = ΣHj, each term on k qubits with ‖Hj‖ ≤ 1, decide whether the ground energy is at most a or at least b, with b − a ≥ 1/poly. The classical case first: a k-SAT formula is a diagonal k-local Hamiltonian counting violated clauses, so the problem is NP-hard before anything quantum happens. Unit 19 showed a quantum computer simulates dynamics efficiently; this unit shows that finding a ground state — what a chemist most wants — is hard in the worst case even for one.
Tools
Membership: Arthur measures one randomly chosen term on Merlin's state, proved. Hardness, Kitaev's theorem that 5-local Hamiltonian is QMA-complete, proved: the Feynman–Kitaev history state Σt Ut⋯U₁|ξ⟩ ⊗ |t⟩ with a unary clock; the input, output, clock and propagation terms; the propagation term unitarily equivalent to the Laplacian of a path, with spectral gap 1 − cos(π/(T+1)) = Θ(1/T²), proved; soundness from the geometric lemma on the angle between two null spaces, proved. 2-local completeness (Kempe–Kitaev–Regev 2006) and one-dimensional chains (Aharonov–Gottesman–Irani–Kempe 2009) cited; the quantum PCP conjecture and the NLTS theorem (Anshu–Breuckmann–Nirkhe 2023) named.
Problems
The slides set Cook–Levin's tableau beside the history state, row for clock step: a classical tableau's consistency is checked locally, a history state's is enforced by energy penalties, and the price is a promise gap of 1/poly instead of a constant. The sheet: write a 3-SAT instance as a diagonal 3-local Hamiltonian and read off its ground energy; build the propagation term for a nine-gate circuit and find its gap, 1 − cos(π/10) ≈ 0.0489; show the history state of an accepting computation has zero propagation energy; prove membership in QMA.
Checkpoint. You prove the local Hamiltonian problem is in QMA, construct the Feynman–Kitaev Hamiltonian for a given circuit, and prove its propagation term has gap 1 − cos(π/(T+1)) — 0.0489 for nine gates.

Part E — noise, error correction, and the honest state of the art

Every obstacle to quantum error correction looks fundamental, and each one is removed by a theorem

Noise as completely positive maps; codes that correct continuous errors by correcting four discrete ones; the stabiliser formalism, which makes codes computable and makes Clifford circuits classically simulable; and the threshold theorem, which turns all of it into arithmetic about physical error rates. Theory

The physical qubit: driving a transmon, and the third level that will not stay empty

branch

needs 21 022.5 + 5 h

Theory
Every earlier unit treats a qubit as ℂ²; a superconducting qubit is not one. It is a weakly anharmonic oscillator — levels at 0, ω and 2ω + α, with ω/2π near 5 GHz and anharmonicity α/2π near −250 MHz — and the qubit is its lowest two levels, with nothing but α keeping the third away. The ladder is taken as given, with its origin cited (Koch et al. 2007): deriving it means quantising a circuit, which needs infinite-dimensional quantum mechanics (open question 5). Everything after it is finite-dimensional. A microwave drive in the lab frame; the rotating frame as a time-dependent change of basis, with the transformed Hamiltonian derived; the rotating-wave approximation, stated with its condition; Rabi oscillation and the π-pulse as unit 02's rotations made physical.
Tools
Time-dependent Hamiltonians, evolved as a product of short-time exponentials with the error of the piecewise-constant approximation bounded. The truncated ladder operator, whose |1⟩ → |2⟩ element is √2: the drive couples to the leakage transition more strongly than to the qubit one, detuned only by α. DRAG (Motzoi–Gambetta–Rebentrost–Wilhelm 2009): a second quadrature proportional to the envelope's derivative over α, derived to first order. The drive also shifts the qubit's own frequency — an AC Stark shift — repaired by a detuning proportional to Ω²/α; the correction that minimises leakage is not the one that minimises gate error (Gambetta et al. 2011, cited). Unit 21's amplitude damping as a gate error: average infidelity t/3T₁ for short gates, proved.
Problems
The slides drive a 10 ns Gaussian π-pulse into a three-level transmon at α/2π = −250 MHz and plot the populations through the pulse: 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ ends in |2⟩; at 4 ns, a quarter of the population leaks. The sheet: derive the rotating-frame Hamiltonian; add the derivative correction and watch leakage fall to about 4 × 10⁻⁶ while the rotation still misses |1⟩ by 2.6 per cent; find the detuning that repairs most of that; then compare with decoherence — a 20 ns gate at T₁ = 100 µs has average infidelity 6.7 × 10⁻⁵, so on these numbers control, not T₁, sets the floor. (Mechanics to Relativity's unit 21b solves the harmonic oscillator by ladder operators — the ladder this unit's transmon starts from.)
Checkpoint. You derive the rotating-frame Hamiltonian under the rotating-wave approximation, compute the 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ leakage of a 10 ns Gaussian π-pulse and what the derivative correction does to it, and say which of leakage, Stark shift and T₁ dominates the gate error.

Capstone — one modulus, one number, and an estimate that must survive the published ones

Mechanics to Relativity ends by closing Mercury's arcsecond budget. This course ends by closing a qubit-and-hour budget: what it would cost to factor a 2048-bit RSA modulus. Estimated 15–20 h.

The brief

Small and exact first. Factor 21 with the simulator from unit 10 — every gate, every measured distribution — and check it against your paper derivation from unit 16. Then add depolarising noise per gate and find the error rate at which the order is no longer recovered.

Then large and approximate, every line derived rather than looked up. Logical qubits for 2048-bit modular exponentiation (16); its Toffoli count, and the T-count that implies (10, 11); the code distance for a 1 per cent total failure probability at a physical error rate of 10⁻³ (24); magic-state factory footprint (24); wall-clock time at a one-microsecond surface-code cycle. Assemble the budget — physical qubits and hours — and defend each line.

Then confront the literature. Gidney and Ekerå (2019) estimated about 20 million noisy qubits for eight hours; Gidney (2025) brought it under a million for under a week. Your naive number will be worse by orders of magnitude. Account for the difference factor by factor — windowed arithmetic, better circuits for exponentiation, cheaper magic states, denser storage — until the gap is explained or you have found a mistake. The gap is the lesson, as it was for the solver comparisons in the optimization course.

  • Quantum machine learning and HHL. Dequantisation is unit 26's cautionary example; linear-systems algorithms would follow unit 19 and are not taken.
  • Post-quantum cryptography. Shor is why it exists; lattices are a different course.
  • Quantum Shannon theory beyond Holevo: channel capacities, entanglement distillation rates, the HSW theorem.
  • Interpretations of quantum mechanics. Unit 06 takes Bell's theorem as far as its hypotheses and stops; what one should believe afterwards is not a mathematical question.
  • Programming frameworks. Qiskit, Cirq and their successors change faster than a course does. The referee depends on NumPy and nothing else, and uses textbook qubit order, which Qiskit reverses.
