## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
The learner knows linear algebra over ℝ well and has never met quantum mechanics. Every textbook opens by announcing that states live in a complex vector space; this unit makes that a theorem about an experiment instead of an announcement. One puzzle drives it: a minimal model of the Stern–Gerlach experiments needs three pairwise mutually unbiased bases of a two-dimensional space, ℝ² has room for two, ℂ² has room for three. Then the unit builds the complex linear algebra the rest of the course stands on, ending with the spectral theorem for normal operators and functional calculus.

It must not become a linear algebra course. No Jordan form, no infinite dimensions, no tensor products (unit 03), no Bloch sphere or SU(2) (unit 02), no postulates stated in full generality (unit 01). The matrices X, Y, Z may be written down as matrices — the eigenbases of the three Stern–Gerlach axes need them — but are not yet "Pauli operators" or "spin": unit 02 earns those names. Say on the overview slide what is deliberately left to units 01 and 02.

## Physics
The Stern–Gerlach experiment (Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach, Frankfurt, 1922): silver atoms from an oven, collimated into a beam, pass between magnet poles shaped to make the field inhomogeneous, and land on a glass plate. Classically the deflection should smear continuously; the beam splits into exactly two. Describe the apparatus from nothing — the learner does not know what a magnetic moment is, and needs only "the atom has some internal direction, and the magnet sorts atoms by it along the magnet's axis". Then the sequential experiments, as an idealisation: block one beam of a z-oriented magnet, send the survivors through a second z magnet — all go up again; through an x magnet instead — 50/50; select x-up and measure z again — 50/50, the earlier z result is gone. Same for any pair among x, y, z. Be honest about the history: the 1922 experiment was a single magnet; the sequential version is the textbook idealisation (Feynman *Lectures* vol. III ch. 5–6, Sakurai §1.1), whose predictions later experiments with sequential fields confirm. Do not attribute the sequential runs to Stern and Gerlach. The postcard to Bohr ("Bohr is right after all") is fair history for the notes, with the caveat that Bohr's model was right for the wrong reason (the split is spin, unit 02).

## The minimal model, and the puzzle
Take as hypotheses, for this unit only, the least model that could describe it: a measurement with k outcomes is an orthonormal basis of the state space; a state is a unit vector; outcome i occurs with probability |⟨eᵢ|ψ⟩|²; afterwards the state is eᵢ. Unit 01 makes these precise and general; say so. Two outcomes per magnet makes the space two-dimensional (repeatability plus two outcomes). "50/50 between z and x" says the z basis and the x basis are *mutually unbiased*: every |⟨eᵢ|fⱼ⟩|² = 1/2. Three axes, pairwise 50/50, demand three pairwise mutually unbiased bases.

Theorem, proved on a slide: ℝ² holds at most two. A unit vector unbiased to the standard basis has both coordinates ±1/√2, so up to signs there is exactly one such basis, {(1,1)/√2, (1,−1)/√2}; any third basis's vectors would again be (±1,±1)/√2, whose overlaps with the second basis are 0 or 1, never 1/2. Then exhibit three in ℂ²: the standard basis, (|0⟩ ± |1⟩)/√2, and (|0⟩ ± i|1⟩)/√2, with all twelve overlaps computed (each 1/2). That three is the most ℂ² allows is a debt paid in unit 02 (MUBs become orthogonal axes of the Bloch sphere) — say so explicitly.

The honest caveat, for the notes and one sentence on a slide: the argument shows the *minimal* model must be complex. A real model on a larger space (Stueckelberg, 1960) reproduces single-system statistics; what rules real quantum theory out is a multi-party experiment (Renou et al., *Nature* 2021, and its 2022 experimental tests), cited, with a pointer to unit 06 where Bell-type arguments are built. Do not overclaim.

## Mathematical tools
Complex inner-product spaces: the course convention (AUTHORING.md) — conjugate-linear in the first argument, linear in the second — stated with a warning box, since the learner's real-inner-product habits are symmetric and Mechanics to Relativity uses the opposite convention. Sesquilinearity, conjugate symmetry, Cauchy–Schwarz (the real proof carries over; say where conjugation enters). The complex polarisation identity, and its consequence that over ℂ an operator with ⟨v|Av⟩ = 0 for all v is zero — false over ℝ (the quarter-turn), which is the first concrete place the field matters. The adjoint, defined by ⟨u|Av⟩ = ⟨A†u|v⟩; in an orthonormal basis, the conjugate transpose.

Dirac notation introduced as notation for the Riesz isomorphism V → V*, v ↦ ⟨v|·⟩, which is conjugate-linear: a bra is a dual vector and nothing more. |u⟩⟨v| as the rank-one map w ↦ ⟨v|w⟩u; its adjoint |v⟩⟨u|; the orthogonal projector |e⟩⟨e| onto a unit vector; the resolution of the identity Σ|eᵢ⟩⟨eᵢ| = I for an orthonormal basis.

Hermitian (A† = A), unitary (U†U = I, equivalently norm-preserving, via polarisation), and normal (A†A = AA†) operators. Schur triangularisation: every operator on a finite-dimensional complex inner-product space is upper triangular in some orthonormal basis — by induction, using that an eigenvalue exists over ℂ (fundamental theorem of algebra, cited) and Gram–Schmidt. The spectral theorem for normal operators proved from Schur: an upper-triangular normal matrix is diagonal (compare the squared norm of the first row with that of the first column, then induct). Corollaries: Hermitian ⇔ normal with real spectrum; unitary ⇔ normal with spectrum on the unit circle; eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues orthogonal. Spectral decomposition A = Σ λ P_λ with P_λ the orthogonal projectors onto eigenspaces. Simultaneous diagonalisation of commuting normal operators (each preserves the other's eigenspaces). Functional calculus: f(A) := Σ f(λ) P_λ for normal A; agreement with polynomials and with the exponential series; e^{iH} unitary for Hermitian H, with (e^{iH})† = e^{−iH}. The warning that e^{A}e^{B} ≠ e^{A+B} unless A and B commute, with a 2×2 instance.

## The running example
**The three Stern–Gerlach bases.** Introduced on the overview slide as the puzzle; the ℝ² impossibility in section 1; the three bases in ℂ² with all overlaps in section 2; in section 3 (adjoints, Dirac notation) the projectors |±⟩⟨±| for each axis written out and summed to I; in section 4 (normal operators) the x and y bases revealed as the eigenbases of the matrices X = [[0,1],[1,0]] and Y = [[0,−i],[i,0]] — and the y basis appearing as the complex eigenvectors of the real quarter-turn [[0,−1],[1,0]], whose eigenvalues are ±i: the y axis is exactly what a real matrix with no real eigenvectors gives you; in section 5 (functional calculus) e^{−iθX/2} rotating the z basis toward the y basis continuously, previewing unit 02. Closed on the last content slide: the puzzle restated, and the answer — complex amplitudes are the price of a third unbiased direction.

## Worked examples (three to five, in `box ex`)
1. ℝ² holds at most two mutually unbiased bases — every step.
2. Three mutually unbiased bases in ℂ², all twelve overlaps computed.
3. A state (3|0⟩ + 4i|1⟩)/5 measured along each axis: z gives 9/25, 16/25; x gives 1/2, 1/2; y gives 49/50, 1/50 — each pair summed to one on the slide.
4. The spectral decomposition of the quarter-turn over ℂ: eigenvalues ±i, eigenvectors (1, ∓i)/√2, projectors, the decomposition checked by multiplying out.
5. (optional) e^{iθX} by the spectral calculus, giving cos θ · I + i sin θ · X, checked at θ = 0 and θ = π/2.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems. At least one proof and one that ends in a number; at least two "With the referee" checks where natural (e.g. the overlaps, the exponential).
- "e^{iθX} three ways": by the power series (X² = I), by the spectral decomposition, and by Cayley–Hamilton; the three agree.
- "Normal is exactly what you need": a matrix with an eigenbasis that is not orthonormal ([[1,1],[0,2]]) and one with no eigenbasis at all ([[0,1],[0,0]]); prove A is unitarily diagonalisable iff normal.
- "Unitaries preserve unbiasedness": prove it; then find a unitary that cycles the three Stern–Gerlach bases z → x → y → z (one answer: U = (I − iX − iY − iZ)/2, which conjugates Z to X, X to Y and Y to Z — with Z = diag(1, −1); verify).
- "Where the field matters": prove that ⟨v|Av⟩ = 0 for all v forces A = 0 over ℂ, and that ⟨v|Av⟩ real for all v forces A Hermitian over ℂ; give the real counterexample for each.
- "Rank-one calculus": (|u⟩⟨v|)† = |v⟩⟨u|, composition, trace ⟨v|u⟩, and when |u⟩⟨v| is a projector.
- "Commuting is not enough without normality": simultaneous diagonalisation of two commuting normal 3×3 matrices, done; and two commuting matrices with no common orthonormal eigenbasis.
- "Exponentials do not add": compute e^{iθX}e^{iθZ} and e^{iθ(X+Z)} and show they differ for θ = π/4 (give the difference as a number, e.g. an entry or an operator norm, computed).
- "Square roots": a positive operator has exactly one positive square root, by functional calculus plus uniqueness.
- "Real MUBs": prove that the bound in ℝ² is two from scratch in a second way (e.g. via the angle: unbiased means 45°, and only one line sits at 45° to both axes up to reflection), and state, cited, that ℝ^d holds at most d/2 + 1 real MUBs.
- "How many in ℂ^d": state (cited: Wootters and Fields 1989) that ℂ^d holds d + 1 mutually unbiased bases when d is a prime power, and exhibit four in ℂ³ (the standard basis and the three bases built from the Fourier matrix and its diagonal phase twists), checking some overlaps equal 1/3. Note that d = 6 is open: three are known, whether four exist is not.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
You prove the spectral theorem for normal operators from Schur triangularisation, and prove that ℝ² holds at most two mutually unbiased bases while ℂ² holds at least three — so that "amplitudes are complex" is, for you, a theorem about an experiment.
