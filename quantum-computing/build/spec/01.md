## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 00 used a "least model" as a working hypothesis — a measurement is an orthonormal basis, probabilities are squared overlaps — and said that unit 01 makes it precise. This unit does: three postulates (states, measurement, closed-system evolution), each stated as mathematics, each forced by an experiment, with the composite-system postulate named and deferred to unit 03. The interferometer is the experiment that unit 00's Stern–Gerlach could not supply: one where amplitudes visibly cancel, and where a *relative* phase is the thing being measured.

It must not become a tour of interpretations (the curriculum excludes them), a physics course on optics (a beam splitter is a 2×2 unitary, described from nothing), or a second pass at unit 00's linear algebra. Density operators and mixed states are unit 04's; generalised measurements (POVMs) are unit 21's — say so where the postulates look narrower than they will end up. The Bloch sphere is unit 02's; a qubit here is ℂ² with the z basis, nothing more.

## Physics
The Mach–Zehnder interferometer, from nothing: a half-silvered mirror (beam splitter) sends light two ways; two mirrors recombine the paths at a second beam splitter; two detectors watch the outputs. Turn the source down until one photon is in the apparatus at a time — an experiment that was actually done with single photons (Grangier, Roger and Aspect, *Europhys. Lett.* 1, 173, 1986), which showed that a single photon is not split between the detectors and still interferes; say so, and say that the earlier faint-light experiments (Taylor 1909) did not establish single-photon behaviour. With balanced arms one detector never clicks. A photon that "took one path or the other" with probability ½ each would light both detectors half the time; probabilities cannot add here, amplitudes do. A phase shifter (a sliver of glass) in one arm changes the detector statistics as cos²(φ/2) and sin²(φ/2). Insert a which-path detector in one arm and the fringes vanish: 50/50 at both outputs for every φ.

The convention: model a beam splitter as B = (1/√2)[[1, i],[i, 1]] (unitary — check it on the slide), path 0 and path 1 as the z basis of ℂ², the phase shifter as diag(1, e^{iφ}). With this B, balanced arms send the photon to detector 1 with certainty (B² = iX); detector 0 is the dark one. Compute the general case on a slide: P(D0) = sin²(φ/2), P(D1) = cos²(φ/2) — sum checked. The which-path detector is a measurement in the z basis between the splitters; compute that it gives ½, ½ independent of φ.

ħ: state the course convention (AUTHORING.md): ħ = 1 throughout, so evolution is e^{−iHt} with H measured as an angular frequency; physics writes e^{−iHt/ħ} with H an energy and ħ = 1.054571817 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, and units 02, 25 and 26 restore it.

## The postulates, as the unit states them
1. **States.** A state of a closed system is a ray in a finite-dimensional complex Hilbert space: a unit vector modulo a global phase. Prove on a slide that ψ and e^{iα}ψ give identical statistics for every observable, and show with the interferometer that a *relative* phase (between path components) changes them.
2. **Observables and measurement.** An observable is a Hermitian operator A = Σ λ P_λ (unit 00's spectral theorem). Measuring it yields λ with probability ⟨ψ|P_λ|ψ⟩ = ‖P_λψ‖² — the Born rule (Born 1926), now a probability measure on the spectrum, summing to one because Σ P_λ = I — and leaves the state P_λψ/‖P_λψ‖ (Lüders' rule, 1951, for degenerate eigenvalues; von Neumann 1932). Repeatability as a consequence. Expectation ⟨A⟩ = ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩ and variance (ΔA)² = ⟨A²⟩ − ⟨A⟩², both derived from the measure, not postulated. Measuring in an orthonormal basis is the special case of a non-degenerate observable — unit 00's least model recovered.
3. **Evolution.** Between measurements a closed system evolves by a unitary U(t), with U(t + s) = U(t)U(s), U(0) = I, and t ↦ U(t) continuous. Then Stone's theorem, finite-dimensional, proved (see tools): U(t) = e^{−iHt} for a unique Hermitian H, so the Schrödinger equation i dψ/dt = Hψ is not a separate postulate but the generator of the evolution postulate.
4. **Composite systems** — named, and deferred to unit 03.

Why unitary, motivated not proved: evolution must map states to states and preserve the total probability of every measurement, and Wigner's theorem (1931, cited — Bargmann 1964 for a proof) says a symmetry preserving all transition probabilities is unitary or antiunitary; continuity in t rules out the antiunitary case. State this as cited.

## Mathematical tools
The Born rule as a probability measure on the spectrum: for each ψ, λ ↦ ⟨ψ|P_λ|ψ⟩ is a probability distribution on spec(A); expectation and variance computed from it equal ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩ and ⟨ψ|A²|ψ⟩ − ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩². Compatible observables: commuting Hermitian operators share a spectral decomposition (unit 00's simultaneous diagonalisation), so their joint measurement is defined; non-commuting ones do not.

Stone's theorem in finite dimension, proved in full (in the notes under **Full proof**, with the idea on the slide): let U be a continuous homomorphism from (ℝ, +) to the unitary group. For small ε the average A_ε = (1/ε)∫₀^ε U(s) ds is close to I, hence invertible; U(t)A_ε = (1/ε)∫_t^{t+ε} U(s) ds is differentiable in t, so U is differentiable; set H = iU′(0), show H is Hermitian by differentiating U(t)†U(t) = I; then U′(t) = −iHU(t), and d/dt(e^{iHt}U(t)) = 0 gives U(t) = e^{−iHt} (the derivative of e^{−iHt} from unit 00's spectral form Σ e^{−iλt}P_λ). Uniqueness of H. Integration of a continuous matrix-valued function entrywise is the only analysis needed; say that.

The Robertson uncertainty relation ΔA ΔB ≥ ½|⟨[A, B]⟩| from Cauchy–Schwarz (Robertson 1929), with the equality case. Say what it is and is not: a statement about the spread of outcomes over many identically prepared systems, measured separately — not about one measurement disturbing another (Heisenberg's 1927 disturbance reading is a different claim; Ozawa 2003 cited for a precise disturbance relation; no development). Instance: [X, Y] = 2iZ for unit 00's matrices, so ΔX ΔY ≥ |⟨Z⟩|, with equality at |0⟩ (ΔX = ΔY = 1, ⟨Z⟩ = 1) — computed on the slide.

## The running example
**The Mach–Zehnder interferometer.** Overview: the dark detector, posed as a puzzle. Section 1 (states): the two paths as the z basis; the phase shifter changes only the relative phase; global phase invisible. Section 2 (measurement): the detectors as a measurement in the z basis after the second splitter; P(D0) = sin²(φ/2), P(D1) = cos²(φ/2); the photon-number-in-arm-0 observable as a projector, and its expectation. Section 3 (which-path): a measurement between the splitters destroys the interference — computed via the post-measurement state. Section 4 (evolution): the whole interferometer as one unitary B·diag(1, e^{iφ})·B, and a beam splitter as e^{−iHt} for H ∝ X run for the right time (B = e^{iπX/4}; check). Section 5 (uncertainty): the path observable Z and the "which output" observable compared via Robertson. Close: why the dark detector forces amplitudes rather than probabilities.

## Worked examples (three to five, in `box ex`)
1. The interferometer with phase φ: amplitudes through both splitters, P(D0) = sin²(φ/2), P(D1) = cos²(φ/2), sum checked, φ = 0 and φ = π checked.
2. The which-path detector: post-measurement states, then the second splitter; ½, ½ for every φ.
3. Born rule for a degenerate observable: a three-level example (ℂ³ with A = diag(1, 1, −1)), probabilities, Lüders post-measurement state, normalisation checked.
4. Stone in one instance: U(t) = e^{−iXt} recovered from its derivative at 0 (H = X); and the beam splitter as B = e^{iπX/4}.
5. (optional) Robertson for X and Y on a general state (cos(θ/2), e^{iφ} sin(θ/2)), and where equality holds.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and one ending in a number; referee checks where natural (the interferometer, the bomb chain).
- "The bomb tester" (Elitzur and Vaidman, *Found. Phys.* 23, 987, 1993; realised by Kwiat et al., *PRL* 74, 4763, 1995): a live bomb in one arm absorbs the photon if it goes that way. With the 50/50 splitters: ½ explodes, ¼ certified live without the photon having touched it (the dark detector clicks), ¼ inconclusive. Then the improved scheme: N stages, each rotating the photon by π/(2N) toward the bomb arm; with a live bomb the photon survives all N with probability cos^{2N}(π/2N) = 1 − π²/(4N) + O(1/N²), e.g. 0.7806 at N = 10 and 0.9756 at N = 100 — the quantum Zeno effect arriving as an exercise (Misra and Sudarshan 1977, cited).
- "Phases you can and cannot see": prove two states differing by a global phase give identical statistics for every observable, and construct, for any two states differing by a relative phase, an observable that tells them apart.
- "Stone's theorem needs continuity": an example of a homomorphism ℝ → U(1) that is not continuous (via a Hamel basis, cited — the axiom of choice) and so is not e^{−iht}; and a proof that a measurable one is automatically continuous is *not* required — say it is cited.
- "Expectation from the measure": derive ⟨A⟩ = ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩ and the variance formula from the Born probabilities.
- "Repeatability": prove that measuring the same observable twice in succession gives the same outcome with certainty.
- "Commuting observables": joint distribution of two commuting observables on a given state in ℂ⁴ (or ℂ³), and why the construction fails for X and Z on ℂ².
- "Robertson's equality case": find all qubit states saturating ΔX ΔY ≥ |⟨Z⟩|.
- "Uncertainty is not disturbance": a short essay-proof problem: show the Robertson bound can be non-trivial for a state on which no measurement has yet been made, and so cannot be about disturbance.
- "A detector that never clicks": replace the first splitter by an unbalanced one, (t, ir) on input 0 with t, r real and t² + r² = 1, keeping the second balanced. Show P(D0) = (1 − 2tr cos φ)/2, so a perfectly dark output needs t = r, and the fringe visibility is 2tr — ending in a number for t² = 0.7 (visibility 0.9165).

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
For the Elitzur–Vaidman setup with a live bomb you derive the three outcome probabilities — ½ explodes, ¼ certified untouched, ¼ inconclusive — and you prove finite-dimensional Stone's theorem.
