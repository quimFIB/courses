## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 10's universality used every single-qubit gate, a continuum. A real machine — and above all a fault-tolerant one — has finitely many, so universality must be approximate, and this unit prices the approximation: errors add along a circuit; two gates, H and T, generate a dense subgroup; Solovay–Kitaev says the overhead is only polylogarithmic; and modern exact synthesis says an arbitrary rotation costs about 3 log₂(1/ε) T gates. The Clifford group appears as the set of gates that map Paulis to Paulis — the gates that will turn out cheap — and T as the one that is not.

It must not become a survey of synthesis algorithms or a proof of the full Solovay–Kitaev recursion's constants. Why Clifford gates are cheap and T is expensive in a fault-tolerant machine is unit 24's (magic states); this unit may say so as a forward pointer and must not argue it. The Gottesman–Knill theorem is unit 23's.

## Mathematical tools
The operator norm ‖A‖ = max over unit v of ‖Av‖ (with the spectral-theorem formula for normal A, unit 00); distance between unitaries, and its operational meaning: for any state and any measurement, outcome probabilities under U and V differ by at most 2‖U − V‖, proved. Errors add along a circuit: ‖U_n⋯U₁ − V_n⋯V₁‖ ≤ Σ‖Uᵢ − Vᵢ‖, proved by telescoping. Global phase: distances are taken up to phase where it matters, and the unit says when.

Density of ⟨H, T⟩: the product T·HTH is, up to phase, a rotation by an angle θ with cos(θ/2) = cos²(π/8) about an axis n (computed); H(T·HTH)H is the same rotation about a different axis m, not parallel to n (computed). θ/π is irrational — proved, citing only that algebraic integers form a ring: if θ/π were rational, e^{iθ/2} would be a root of unity and 2cos(θ/2) = ζ + ζ̄ an algebraic integer; but 2cos(θ/2) = 2cos²(π/8) = 1 + √2/2, whose minimal polynomial x² − 2x + ½ is not integral. (The card said "irrationality cited"; this short proof is better — use it, and cite Boykin et al., *Inf. Proc. Lett.* 75, 101, 2000, for the gate set.) Powers of a rotation by an irrational multiple of π are dense in the rotations about that axis (pigeonhole, proved). Rotations about two non-parallel axes generate SU(2) up to phase (proved by an Euler-angle argument generalising unit 02's, or cited from Nielsen and Chuang Ex. 4.11). So ⟨H, T⟩ is dense in SU(2) up to phase, and with CNOT the set {H, T, CNOT} is approximately universal by unit 10.

The Pauli group on n qubits and the Clifford group as its normaliser in the unitary group (up to phase); H, S, CNOT generate it (Gottesman 1998, cited); for one qubit, 24 elements up to phase — the rotation group of the cube, since a Clifford gate permutes ±x, ±y, ±z on the Bloch sphere (drawn; counted). T is not Clifford: TXT† = (X + Y)/√2, not a Pauli. Clifford + T as the standard universal set.

The Solovay–Kitaev theorem (Kitaev 1997; Solovay, unpublished; Dawson and Nielsen, *QIC* 6, 81, 2006): for a finite set generating a dense subgroup of SU(2) and closed under inverses, every gate is approximable to ε by a sequence of length O(log^c(1/ε)), c = log 5 / log(3/2) ≈ 3.97 in the Dawson–Nielsen version. Proved: the balanced group-commutator step — a unitary within ε of I is a group commutator VWV†W† of unitaries within O(√ε) of I, and approximating V and W to ε^{3/2}-ish accuracy yields the commutator to O(ε^{3/2}) (the error-squaring that drives the recursion). Cited: the recursion's full analysis and the constants. Then what replaced it in practice: exact synthesis over Clifford + T (Kliuchnikov, Maslov and Mosca, *QIC* 13, 607, 2013: a unitary is exactly a Clifford + T circuit iff its entries lie in ℤ[1/√2, i]) and optimal z-rotations at about 3 log₂(1/ε) T gates (Ross and Selinger, *QIC* 16, 901, 2016), both cited — the number fault tolerance will later charge for.

## The running example
**One arbitrary rotation inside a 10⁹-gate circuit.** Overview: the budget — 1 per cent total error — and the question "how many T gates does each rotation cost?". Section 1 (distance and accumulation): the bound, and the per-gate budget 10⁻²/10⁹ = 10⁻¹¹. Section 2 (density): ⟨H, T⟩ reaches everything; the irrational angle; a few powers of T·HTH plotted as points marching round its axis on the Bloch sphere (computed, drawn). Section 3 (the Clifford group): the 24 cube rotations; T is outside. Section 4 (Solovay–Kitaev): the commutator step, and what O(log^{3.97}(1/ε)) means at ε = 10⁻¹¹. Section 5 (exact synthesis): 3 log₂(10¹¹) ≈ 110 T gates per rotation. Close: the circuit's rotations times 110 is its T-count, the quantity unit 24 converts into physical qubits and hours.

## Worked examples (three to five, in `box ex`)
1. Error accumulation proved by telescoping, then applied: 10⁹ gates and a 1 per cent budget give 10⁻¹¹ per gate.
2. T·HTH computed: its trace, cos(θ/2) = cos²(π/8) ≈ 0.8536, θ ≈ 0.349π, and its axis; the second axis from conjugating by H.
3. The irrationality proof via algebraic integers, every step.
4. TXT† = (X + Y)/√2 and HXH = Z, SXS† = Y: Clifford gates permute Paulis, T does not.
5. (optional) One balanced-commutator step done numerically: a small rotation about z written as a commutator of two rotations about x and y.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and one ending in a number; referee checks where natural (operator norms, conjugations, the T·HTH angle).
- "The budget": a circuit of 10⁹ gates and a 1 per cent error budget; the per-gate accuracy (10⁻¹¹) and the T-count of each arbitrary rotation (about 110).
- "Clifford maps Paulis to Paulis": prove it for H, S, CNOT on all generators, and that T does not.
- "The bound is tight up to constants": a circuit of n identical small rotations by δ whose errors align, so the total error grows as nδ (for n = 1000, δ = 10⁻³: total distance ≈ 0.495 against the bound 0.500 — computed).
- "Probabilities from distance": prove outcome probabilities differ by at most 2‖U − V‖.
- "Density of powers": prove the powers of a rotation by an irrational multiple of 2π are dense in the circle.
- "The single-qubit Clifford group has 24 elements": by counting where ±x, ±y, ±z can go.
- "Exact synthesis, a first look": show that HT has entries in ℤ[1/√2, i], and that a rotation R_z(π/16) does not — so it can only be approximated.
- "Solovay–Kitaev's exponent": from the recursion ε_k ≈ C ε_{k−1}^{3/2} and length l_k = 5 l_{k−1}, derive c = log 5/log(3/2) ≈ 3.97.
- "How bad would naive search be": the number of distinct H, T words of length L up to phase grows like 2^L at most; what L does a counting argument require for ε = 10⁻¹¹ (volume of SU(2) against balls of radius ε: about 3 log₂(1/ε) — the reason Ross–Selinger's 3 log₂(1/ε) is optimal up to constants)?

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
You prove the error-accumulation bound and the density of ⟨H, T⟩, and carry a whole-circuit error budget down to a per-rotation T-count.
