## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Unit 13 ended on an honest scorecard: no exponential separation against randomised algorithms yet. This unit supplies one. Simon's function is two-to-one with a hidden XOR-period s; a quantum algorithm finds s with O(n) queries and some linear algebra over 𝔽₂, and every randomised classical algorithm needs Ω(2^{n/2}) — both proved. The history matters: Simon's 1994 paper was the one Shor read, and replacing ℤ₂ⁿ by ℤ_N is most of the way to factoring; the deck says so and the next two units cash it.

It must not become the hidden subgroup problem in general (unit 16 frames it) or a linear-algebra course over finite fields — only what Simon's algorithm needs.

## Mathematical tools
Simon's problem (Simon, FOCS 1994; *SIAM J. Comput.* 26, 1474, 1997): f: {0,1}ⁿ → {0,1}ⁿ with f(x) = f(x′) iff x′ ∈ {x, x ⊕ s}, s ≠ 0 unknown. The quantum algorithm: H^{⊗n}, one query into a second register, H^{⊗n} again, measure; the first register's distribution is uniform on {y : y·s = 0} — proved, by computing the amplitude of y as 2^{−n} Σ_x (−1)^{x·y} over a coset {x, x ⊕ s}, which cancels unless y·s = 0. (Measuring the second register first, or not, changes nothing — unit 10's deferred measurement.)

Linear algebra over 𝔽₂: the vectors y with y·s = 0 form s^⊥, a subspace of dimension n − 1; its orthogonal complement is {0, s}; n − 1 linearly independent samples determine s by Gaussian elimination over 𝔽₂ (the elimination worked on a slide; note that "orthogonal" over 𝔽₂ allows y·y = 0 for y ≠ 0, which does not matter here). The probability that n − 1 uniform samples from an (n − 1)-dimensional space over 𝔽₂ are independent is ∏_{k=1}^{n−1}(1 − 2^{−k}) ≥ ∏_{k≥1}(1 − 2^{−k}) ≈ 0.2888, proved; the expected number of samples to reach a basis is less than n + 1, proved (sum of geometric waiting times with success probabilities 1 − 2^{k−(n−1)}).

The classical lower bound, proved: any randomised algorithm making q queries finds s (or distinguishes the problem from a one-to-one f) with probability at most about q²/2ⁿ, because until it sees a collision f(x) = f(x′) its view is the same as for a random injective function, and with q queries the probability of a collision against a uniformly random s is at most q(q − 1)/2 · 1/(2ⁿ − 1). So Ω(2^{n/2}) queries are needed for constant success.

## The running example
**n = 3, s = 110.** Overview: a table of f on eight inputs, paired by s, and the question "find s". Section 1 (the problem): the pairing drawn as eight points in four pairs. Section 2 (the circuit): one run, the post-query state, and the outcome distribution uniform on {000, 001, 110, 111} (the y with y·s = 0), each with probability ¼ — computed. Section 3 (linear algebra): two independent samples (e.g. 001 and 110), the elimination, s = 110. Section 4 (classical): how many classical queries until two inputs collide — about 2^{3/2}, trivial here, astronomical at n = 100. ## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and one ending in a number; referee checks where natural (the distribution for given s).
- "Simon by hand": n = 3 for a given s different from the deck's; every run's state.
- "The collision lower bound": prove Ω(2^{n/2}) for randomised algorithms.
- "0.2888": prove the product converges and bound its tail; show it is above ¼.
- "Expected runs": prove the expected number of runs is below n + 1.
- "Las Vegas": make the algorithm zero-error with expected O(n) queries (check the candidate s with two more queries).
- "Deterministic Simon": the classical deterministic lower bound, and a remark that exact quantum algorithms for Simon exist (Brassard and Høyer 1997, cited).
- "What if s = 0 is allowed": show the algorithm distinguishes one-to-one f from two-to-one f.
- "Over ℤ₂ⁿ the characters are ±1": show that replacing the group by ℤ₄ breaks the H^{⊗n} step and needs the Fourier transform of unit 15.
- "Numbers at n = 100": quantum queries about 100 (and the elimination's 100³ bit operations), classical queries about 2⁵⁰ ≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁵ for constant success.

## Checkpoint (falsifiable, and the last slide)
You prove the quantum upper bound O(n) and the classical randomised lower bound Ω(2^{n/2}), and compute 0.2888.
