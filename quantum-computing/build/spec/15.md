## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Units 13 and 14 used the Fourier transform over ℤ₂ⁿ without the name. This unit builds the general object — characters of a finite abelian group and the Fourier transform as the unitary change to the character basis — and shows that ℤ₂ⁿ and ℤ_N are two instances of one theorem. On ℤ_{2ⁿ} the transform has an efficient circuit, derived from a product formula; and phase estimation turns that circuit into the engine of Shor, quantum counting and Hamiltonian-simulation energy readout. The caveat is stated loudly and early: the QFT is exponentially faster than the FFT at a task nobody wants, because you cannot read out the transformed vector. What it is good for is producing samples concentrated on a few outcomes.

It must not become harmonic analysis on non-abelian groups (named in unit 16 for the hidden subgroup problem) or signal processing.

## Mathematical tools
Characters of a finite abelian group G: homomorphisms χ: G → U(1); for ℤ_N they are χ_k(j) = e^{2πijk/N} (the course's sign convention, AUTHORING.md, first fixed in unit 00's sheet); for ℤ₂ⁿ they are (−1)^{x·y} (unit 13). Orthogonality of characters, proved (Σ_g χ(g) = 0 for non-trivial χ by translation invariance). The dual group Ĝ ≅ G for finite abelian G — proved for cyclic groups and products of them, with the structure theorem for finite abelian groups cited. The Fourier transform F_G: |g⟩ ↦ |G|^{−1/2} Σ_χ χ(g)|χ⟩ is unitary, proved; on ℤ_N it is the matrix (ω^{jk})/√N, ω = e^{2πi/N} (matching qclib's `qft_matrix`); on ℤ₂ⁿ it is H^{⊗n}.

The product formula on ℤ_{2ⁿ}: F|j⟩ = 2^{−n/2} ⊗_{l=1}^{n} (|0⟩ + e^{2πi 0.j_{n−l+1}…j_n}|1⟩), proved by expanding k in binary — the transform of a basis state is a product state. That product formula *is* the circuit: on each qubit a Hadamard then controlled phase rotations R_k = diag(1, e^{2πi/2^k}) from the less significant qubits, then a reversal of qubit order by ⌊n/2⌋ swaps — n Hadamards and n(n − 1)/2 controlled phases, O(n²) gates, against O(n2ⁿ) operations for the classical FFT on a 2ⁿ-vector. With the course's qubit order (qubit 0 most significant) and sign convention, derive exactly where the swaps go and say that sources differ.

The approximate QFT (Coppersmith, IBM report RC 19642, 1994): dropping every R_k with k > m changes the transform by at most O(n 2^{−m}) in operator norm, proved (each dropped gate is within 2π/2^k of the identity; errors add); m ≈ log₂ n + O(1) keeps the error constant with O(n log n) gates.

Phase estimation (Kitaev 1995; Cleve, Ekert, Macchiavello and Mosca 1998): given a controlled-U^{2^j} for each j and an eigenvector |u⟩ with U|u⟩ = e^{2πiφ}|u⟩, t counting qubits in |+⟩, the controlled powers kick back the phases, and the inverse QFT concentrates the counting register near 2^tφ. Proved: if 2^tφ is an integer the outcome is exact; otherwise the nearest t-bit fraction appears with probability at least 4/π² ≈ 0.405 (via |sin(πδ)/(2^t sin(πδ/2^t))|² with |δ| ≤ ½ and sin x ≥ 2x/π); and with t = n + ⌈log₂(2 + 1/(2ε))⌉ counting qubits the first n bits are correct with probability at least 1 − ε (Nielsen and Chuang's bound, proved). When |u⟩ is a superposition of eigenvectors, the outcome is a sample of one of their phases, with the superposition's weights — the fact Shor uses.

## The running example
**Phase estimation of φ = 1/3 with three counting qubits.** Overview: U = diag(1, e^{2πi/3}) on one qubit with eigenvector |1⟩; "estimate φ to three bits". Section 1 (characters): the eight characters of ℤ₈ as eighth roots of unity on a circle (drawn). Section 2 (the transform): F₈'s matrix; the product formula for one input, e.g. |5⟩. Section 3 (the circuit): the three-qubit QFT drawn — three H, three controlled phases, one swap — and checked against the matrix. Section 4 (phase estimation): the full outcome distribution for φ = 1/3, the peak at 3/8 (0.375 against 0.333) with probability 0.688 and the neighbour 2/8 at 0.175 — all eight probabilities tabulated and summed to one; drawn as a bar chart. Section 5 (precision): adding counting qubits sharpens the peak; the 4/π² worst case at φ halfway between fractions. Close: 0.688 ≥ 0.405, and why phase estimation is the engine of unit 16.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and one ending in a number; referee checks where natural (QFT circuit versus `library.qft_matrix`, phase-estimation distributions).
- "Three ancillas, phase 1/3": the full outcome distribution; the nearest fraction 3/8 with probability 0.688.
- "Derive the product formula" in general.
- "The approximate-QFT bound": prove O(n 2^{−m}).
- "4/π²": prove the bound, and show it is approached as φ approaches the midpoint between two t-bit fractions and t grows.
- "Counting qubits for n bits with confidence 1 − ε": for n = 10 and ε = 0.01, t = 10 + ⌈log₂(2 + 50)⌉ = 16.
- "Characters of ℤ₂ × ℤ₄": list them, and the Fourier matrix.
- "Superposition input": phase estimation on (|u₁⟩ + |u₂⟩)/√2 with phases 1/4 and 3/4 and t = 2 — the outcome distribution.
- "QFT versus FFT": the gate count for n = 30 (465 controlled phases and 30 Hadamards) against the FFT's ~30 × 2³⁰ ≈ 3.2 × 10¹⁰ operations — and why the comparison is misleading.
- "Inverse QFT": its circuit as the reverse of the QFT with conjugated phases.
