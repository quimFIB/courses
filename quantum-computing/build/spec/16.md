## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
The payoff of Part D. Factoring reduces classically to order finding; order finding is phase estimation on the map x ↦ ax mod N, whose eigenphases are s/r; continued fractions recover r from a phase measured to 2 log₂ N bits. Every step is proved, and the unit factors 21 by hand from the measured distribution — including the runs that fail and why. The same machinery solves discrete logarithms, so elliptic-curve cryptography falls with RSA. The hidden subgroup problem frames Simon, Shor and discrete log as one problem over abelian groups, and names the non-abelian cases that remain open.

It must not become a resource estimate — what it costs to factor RSA-2048 on real hardware is unit 24's and the capstone's; this unit may give the gate count O(n³) and stop. Post-quantum cryptography is named, not taught.

## Mathematical tools
The classical reduction (Miller 1976 for the randomised form), proved: for odd N with at least two distinct prime factors and a uniformly random a coprime to N, the order r of a is even and a^{r/2} ≢ −1 (mod N) with probability at least ½ — a CRT argument on the 2-adic valuations of the orders modulo each prime power (proved for N = pq and in general, or the general case cited after the pq proof). Then (a^{r/2} − 1)(a^{r/2} + 1) ≡ 0 with neither factor ≡ 0, so gcd(a^{r/2} ± 1, N) are non-trivial factors. Even N and prime powers N = p^k are handled classically first (detect p^k by integer root extraction) — say so.

Order finding as phase estimation: U_a|y⟩ = |ay mod N⟩ for y < N (identity above) is a permutation, hence unitary (qclib's `library.multiply_mod`); its eigenvectors |u_s⟩ = r^{−1/2} Σ_k e^{−2πisk/r}|a^k mod N⟩ have eigenphases s/r, and Σ_s |u_s⟩/√r = |1⟩, so starting the work register in |1⟩ samples s uniformly from {0, …, r − 1} — proved. Controlled U_a^{2^j} = U_{a^{2^j} mod N}, computed classically by repeated squaring, so each costs one modular multiplication; modular exponentiation as a reversible circuit of O(n³) gates for n = ⌈log₂ N⌉ (schoolbook multiplication, made reversible by unit 10's uncomputation), stated with the count derived. Counting register of t = 2n + 1 qubits (or t with N² ≤ 2^t < 2N²), so that the measured y/2^t is within 1/(2N²) of s/r.

Continued fractions: the expansion, convergents p_k/q_k, the recurrences, and Legendre's theorem, proved: if |x − p/q| < 1/(2q²) then p/q is a convergent of x. Hence s/r (in lowest terms) is a convergent of y/2^t, found in O(n³) time. If gcd(s, r) > 1 the algorithm finds a divisor of r: repeat and take least common multiples, or check candidates; the probability that s is coprime to r is Ω(1/log log r) (cited), and in practice a few runs suffice.

Discrete logarithms: given g and h = g^ℓ in a cyclic group, the two-register period-finding version finds ℓ; this breaks Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography — stated with the construction sketched. The hidden subgroup problem: f on a group G constant on cosets of a hidden H and distinct on different cosets; Simon (G = ℤ₂ⁿ), order finding (G = ℤ), discrete log (G = ℤ_r × ℤ_r) as instances; the abelian case solved in general (Kitaev 1995, cited); the dihedral case (related to lattice problems, Regev 2004) and the symmetric group (graph isomorphism) open — named.

## The running example
**Factoring 21 with a = 2.** Overview: 21 = 3 × 7 pretended unknown; the plan. Section 1 (the reduction): the order of 2 mod 21 is 6 (2⁶ = 64 ≡ 1), even, and 2³ = 8 ≢ −1; gcd(7, 21) = 7 and gcd(9, 21) = 3. Warm-up on 15 with a = 7: order 4, 7² = 49 ≡ 4, gcd(3, 15) = 3 and gcd(5, 15) = 5. Section 2 (order finding as phase estimation): U_2 on five work qubits; its eigenphases s/6. Section 3 (the circuit): nine counting qubits (21² = 441 ≤ 512 < 882), controlled U_{2^{2^j} mod 21}, the inverse QFT, fourteen qubits in all. Section 4 (the distribution): peaks at y = 0, 85, 171, 256, 341, 427 — heights 0.167 at 0 and 256, 0.114 at the other four (computed), drawn as a bar chart over 512 outcomes. Section 5 (continued fractions): 427/512 = [0; 1, 5, 42, 2] with convergent 5/6, so r = 6; 85/512 also gives 1/6; 171 and 341 give 1/3 and 2/3 (r = 3, a divisor), 256 gives 1/2 (r = 2, a divisor), 0 gives nothing — the runs that fail and why. Close: the probability one run yields r = 6 directly is 0.308; with the divisor runs combined by lcm, almost every pair of runs succeeds.

## Worked examples (three to five, in `box ex`)
1. The reduction on 15 (a = 7) and on 21 (a = 2), every gcd.
2. The eigenvectors |u_s⟩ of U_a and Σ_s|u_s⟩ = √r |1⟩, verified for a = 2, N = 21 (r = 6).
3. The continued-fraction expansion of 427/512, its convergents, and Legendre's condition checked.
4. The outcome classes of the 21 distribution: which y give r = 6, which give divisors, which give nothing; probabilities summed (r = 6 directly: 0.308; r = 3: 0.321; r = 2: 0.167; nothing useful from y = 0: 0.167).
5. (optional) The CRT argument for N = pq.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and one ending in a number; referee checks where natural (the full 21 distribution via `library.multiply_mod`, as `tests/test_known_results.py` does — but the sheet's snippet is its own).
- "Factor 21 in full": a = 2, nine counting qubits, the outcome distribution peaking at 0, 85, 171, 256, 341, 427; 427/512 → 5/6 → r = 6; 2³ = 8; gcd(7, 21) and gcd(9, 21) give 7 and 3.
- "One run's success": compute the probability that one run yields r = 6 directly (0.308), and what the other peaks give.
- "Legendre's theorem": prove it.
- "The reduction's ½": prove the bound for N = pq.
- "A bad a": find an a coprime to 21 for which the reduction fails (r odd, or a^{r/2} ≡ −1), and the fraction of bad a over all 12 values coprime to 21: six fail (a = 1, 4, 16 with odd order; 5, 17, 20 with a^{r/2} ≡ −1), exactly the ½ the theorem allows.
- "Counting qubits": why N² ≤ 2^t; what goes wrong with t = 5 for N = 21.
- "Modular exponentiation's cost": derive O(n³) for schoolbook arithmetic; for n = 2048, the order of magnitude of the gate count before any optimisation.
- "Discrete log on a toy group": in ℤ₁₁^×, find ℓ with 2^ℓ ≡ 7 by the two-register method's classical post-processing, given idealised measurement outcomes.
- "Hidden subgroups": state Simon's problem and order finding as hidden subgroup problems, naming G and H.
- "Prime powers": show the algorithm's reduction fails for N = p^k and give the classical test.
