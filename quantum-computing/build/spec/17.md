## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Shor exploits structure; Grover exploits none. Search among N items with M marked takes about (π/4)√(N/M) queries, and the whole algorithm lives in a two-dimensional plane where one iteration — two reflections — is a rotation by 2θ with sin θ = √(M/N). That picture, proved and drawn, gives the iteration count, the success probability, the overshoot when you iterate too long, and amplitude amplification as the general tool. Unit 18 proves √N is optimal; this unit says so as a pointer.

It must not oversell: a quadratic speedup on unstructured search does not make NP easy, and for key search the sequential depth, not the query count, is the obstacle. Say both.

## Mathematical tools
The oracle as a phase oracle O_f = I − 2Σ_{marked}|x⟩⟨x| (unit 13's kickback); the diffusion operator D = 2|s⟩⟨s| − I with |s⟩ = H^{⊗n}|0ⁿ⟩, built as H^{⊗n}(2|0⟩⟨0| − I)H^{⊗n} (the reflection about |0ⁿ⟩ as a multiply-controlled Z, unit 10). Both are reflections; the plane spanned by |s⟩ and the normalised marked superposition |m⟩ is invariant under both, proved; in that plane O_f reflects about |m^⊥⟩ and D about |s⟩, so G = D O_f is a rotation by 2θ where sin θ = ⟨m|s⟩ = √(M/N), proved (composition of two reflections is a rotation by twice the angle between their axes). After k iterations the success probability is sin²((2k + 1)θ), proved. The optimal k = ⌊π/(4θ)⌋ (or the nearest integer to π/(4θ) − ½), success at least 1 − M/N (proved), and the overshoot: success is periodic in k with period about π/θ, proved.

Unknown M (Boyer, Brassard, Høyer and Tapp, *Fortschr. Phys.* 46, 493, 1998), proved: choose the iteration count uniformly from an exponentially growing range; expected queries O(√(N/M)); the lemma that a uniformly random k in [0, m) succeeds with probability at least ¼ once m ≥ 1/sin 2θ, proved. Amplitude amplification (Brassard, Høyer, Mosca and Tapp 2002): for any algorithm A succeeding with probability p, the same two-reflection rotation (reflecting about A|0⟩ instead of |s⟩) reaches near certainty in O(1/√p) runs of A and A^{−1}, proved as the same theorem with |s⟩ replaced by A|0⟩. Quantum counting (Brassard, Høyer and Tapp 1998), stated: estimating M to relative error ε with O(√(N/M)/ε) queries, by phase estimation on G whose eigenphases are ±2θ — with unit 15 named as where phase estimation is built.

Key search, honestly: AES-128 by Grover needs about (π/4)2⁶⁴ ≈ 1.4 × 10¹⁹ sequential iterations, each an AES evaluation as a reversible circuit; parallelising over P machines buys only √P (Zalka 1999, cited), so the depth stays enormous; the response is longer keys (AES-256), not new ciphers. Name NIST's post-quantum guidance in one sentence, cited, without dates that might be stale.

## The running example
**A million-item search: N = 2²⁰, one marked item.** Overview: the problem and the classical cost (about N/2 queries on average). Section 1 (the plane): |s⟩, |m⟩, θ = arcsin(2^{−10}) ≈ 9.77 × 10⁻⁴ rad, drawn. Section 2 (two reflections): one iteration as a rotation by 2θ, drawn. Section 3 (how many): k = 804, success sin²(1609θ) ≈ 0.99999976 (computed; failure about 2.4 × 10⁻⁷). Section 4 (overshoot): at k = 1608 the success probability is about 10⁻¹⁰ — iterating twice as long is the worst thing to do; the sinusoid drawn. Section 5 (amplitude amplification and unknown M): the same picture for any algorithm. Close: 804 queries against 524 288 expected classically, and the pointer to unit 18's proof that nothing quantum does better than order √N.

## Worked examples (three to five, in `box ex`)
1. Two reflections make a rotation by twice the angle between their axes — proved in the plane, then applied.
2. N = 4, one marked item: θ = π/6, one iteration gives sin²(π/2) = 1 — certainty, every amplitude shown.
3. N = 2²⁰: θ, k = 804, the success probability — the arithmetic shown.
4. The six-qubit check: N = 64, marked item 42, success sin²((2k + 1)θ) for k = 0…7 tabulated (θ = arcsin(1/8)), peak at k = 6.
5. (optional) Amplitude amplification of a classical sampler with p = 1/100: sin θ = 0.1, so about π/(4θ) − ½ ≈ 7.3, i.e. 7 rounds, against about 100 classical repetitions.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and one ending in a number; referee checks where natural (success probabilities against `Circuit` runs).
- "A million items": the optimal iteration count (804) and its success probability (above 0.999 999 7).
- "Exactly one iteration": find N for which one iteration succeeds with certainty for M = 1 (N = 4), and all (N, M) for which some iteration count gives certainty exactly.
- "Overshoot is periodic": prove it and give the period in iterations.
- "Many marked items": M = N/4 — one iteration suffices exactly; M > N/2 — why iterating hurts and what to do instead.
- "Unknown M": prove the BBHT ¼ lemma.
- "Amplitude amplification": prove it from the two-reflection picture.
- "The diffusion operator as a circuit": its gate count from unit 10's multiply-controlled Z.
- "AES-128": the iteration count (π/4)2⁶⁴ ≈ 1.45 × 10¹⁹; at a hypothetical 10⁹ iterations per second, the time (about 460 years); with 10⁶ parallel machines, why the gain is only √10⁶ = 1000.
- "Grover is not a sorting oracle": show that with M = N/2 random guessing already succeeds with probability ½, and Grover's first iteration gives exactly the same.
