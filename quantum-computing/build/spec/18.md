## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Every speedup so far exploited structure. This unit proves that without structure there is almost nothing to exploit: any quantum algorithm for unstructured search needs Ω(√N) queries (so Grover is optimal), and the polynomial method shows that T queries produce acceptance probabilities that are polynomials of degree at most 2T — which turns query lower bounds into questions about polynomial degree. Consequences proved or cited: relative to a random oracle NP ⊄ BQP; for total functions quantum query complexity is at most polynomially smaller than deterministic, so exponential speedups need a promise — which is exactly what Simon and Shor have and search does not.

It must not become a survey of lower-bound methods. The adversary method is named (Ambainis 2002) and the negative-weight adversary's tightness cited (Reichardt 2011); only the hybrid argument and the polynomial method are proved.

## Mathematical tools
The hybrid argument (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani, *SIAM J. Comput.* 26, 1510, 1997), proved: run a T-query algorithm with the empty oracle and record the query magnitudes q_x = Σ_t ‖P_x|ψ_t⟩‖²; they sum to T, so some x has q_x ≤ T/N; replacing the oracle by one marking x changes the final state by at most Σ_t 2‖P_x|ψ_t⟩‖ ≤ 2√(T q_x) ≤ 2T/√N (Cauchy–Schwarz), drawn as a sum of small perturbations; distinguishing the two oracles with constant probability needs this distance to be a constant, so T = Ω(√N). Explicit constants derived (with success probability ⅔ for every marked x, T ≥ c√N for an explicit c — derive it; the sheet asks for it). Consequence, stated with the proof sketched: relative to a random oracle, NP ⊄ BQP (BBBV), and brute-force search over NP witnesses gains a square root and no more.

The polynomial method (Beals, Buhrman, Cleve, Mosca and de Wolf, *J. ACM* 48, 778, 2001), proved: after T queries to x ∈ {0,1}^N each amplitude is a multilinear polynomial of degree at most T in x₁, …, x_N (induction: a query multiplies by (−1)^{x_i} = 1 − 2x_i or acts as the bit oracle, raising degree by at most one), so every acceptance probability is a real multilinear polynomial of degree at most 2T. Hence T ≥ deg(f)/2 for exact algorithms and T ≥ ãdeg(f)/2 for bounded error, with ãdeg the ⅓-approximate degree. Symmetrisation (Minsky and Papert 1969), proved: averaging a multilinear polynomial over permutations of the inputs gives a univariate polynomial in |x| of no larger degree. Approximate degree of OR is Θ(√N) (Nisan and Szegedy 1994; Paturi 1992), cited — with the Markov-brothers inequality named as what the proof uses — giving Grover's optimality a second time. Parity: degree N, so any exact algorithm needs N/2 queries, and N/2 suffice (Deutsch on pairs, unit 13) — exactly N/2, proved both ways.

Total functions: for every total Boolean function, D(f) = O(Q(f)⁴) (Aaronson, Ben-David, Kothari, Rao and Tal, STOC 2021, cited; building on Huang's sensitivity theorem, 2019) and the fourth power is tight up to log factors for some function (Ambainis et al. 2016, cited). So no exponential speedup exists for total functions; Simon's and Shor's speedups need their promises.

## The running example
**OR on N bits — "is anything marked?" — set against parity.** Overview: two functions of N bits; Grover computes OR in O(√N) queries; Deutsch on pairs computes parity in N/2. Section 1 (hybrid argument): the query magnitudes for OR, the perturbation sum, Ω(√N), drawn. Section 2 (polynomials): after T queries, degree at most 2T; OR as a polynomial (1 − ∏(1 − x_i), degree N). Section 3 (symmetrisation): OR's symmetrised approximating polynomial p(k) with p(0) ≤ ⅓, p(k) ≥ ⅔ for k ≥ 1, drawn as a univariate curve; degree Θ(√N). Section 4 (parity): degree exactly N, hence N/2 queries, and Deutsch's algorithm meeting it. Section 5 (what this says): no exponential speedup for total functions; NP ⊄ BQP relative to an oracle. Close: OR's √N and parity's N/2 — one has a quadratic speedup and the other only a factor of two.

## Worked examples (three to five, in `box ex`)

4. Parity on N = 4 in two queries by Deutsch on pairs, and why one query is impossible (degree).

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and one ending in a number; referee checks where natural (small-N optimal query values via Grover runs).
- "Parity takes exactly N/2": the upper bound from Deutsch's algorithm on pairs, the lower bound from degree.
- "BBBV with explicit constants": with success ⅔ on every oracle, derive T ≥ c√N for an explicit c; evaluate for N = 2²⁰ and compare with Grover's 804.
- "Symmetrisation": prove it.
- "Degree of AND, OR, majority": exact degrees N, N, N; what that says about exact quantum algorithms for each.
- "Grover is optimal twice": derive the √N bound again from ãdeg(OR) = Θ(√N) (cited).
- "Random oracles": sketch why, relative to a random oracle, the NP language {1ⁿ : some string of length n is marked} is not in BQP.
- "Promises matter": show Deutsch–Jozsa's promise problem has a constant-degree approximating polynomial on its promise set but not on all inputs.
- "Total functions": state D(f) = O(Q(f)⁴) and use it to show no total function has an exponential quantum speedup.
- "Element distinctness", stated: Θ(N^{2/3}) quantum queries (Ambainis 2004 upper bound; Aaronson and Shi 2004 lower bound), against N classically — a polynomial speedup between Grover's and none.
