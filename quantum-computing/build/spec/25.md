## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
Every earlier unit treats a qubit as ℂ². A superconducting qubit is not one: it is a weakly anharmonic oscillator, and the qubit is its lowest two levels with nothing but the anharmonicity α keeping the third away. This unit drives one with a microwave pulse and finds the price: the drive couples to the leakage transition more strongly than to the qubit transition, detuned only by α, so a fast π-pulse leaks; a derivative correction (DRAG) removes most of the leakage but leaves the rotation wrong by a few per cent, because the drive also shifts the qubit's own frequency (an AC Stark shift), and a detuning repairs that. Then decoherence: a short gate's average infidelity from T₁ is t/3T₁ — and on realistic numbers, control error, not T₁, sets the floor. This is the physics behind unit 24's "p = 10⁻³".

It must not become circuit quantisation. The transmon's ladder of levels — 0, ω, 2ω + α — is taken as given, its origin cited (Koch et al., *PRA* 76, 042319, 2007): deriving it means quantising a circuit, which needs infinite-dimensional quantum mechanics, excluded from this course (Mechanics to Relativity's unit 21b solves the harmonic oscillator by ladder operators, the ladder this unit starts from — an optional cross-reference). Everything after the ladder is finite-dimensional: three levels.

## Physics
The transmon, from nothing: a superconducting circuit on a chip, cooled to millikelvin (unit 26 explains why), behaving as an oscillator whose energy levels are not quite evenly spaced — ω/2π ≈ 5 GHz between the lowest two, α/2π ≈ −250 MHz less for the next. A microwave pulse at the qubit frequency drives transitions; the qubit is |0⟩, |1⟩; |2⟩ is leakage, an error outside the code space that unit 22's codes do not see. Typical numbers: gates of 10–50 ns, T₁ of tens to hundreds of microseconds, stated as orders of magnitude.

## Mathematical tools
The three-level model: H₀ = ω n + (α/2) n(n − 1) on span{|0⟩, |1⟩, |2⟩} (levels 0, ω, 2ω + α), and the truncated lowering operator b with b|1⟩ = |0⟩, b|2⟩ = √2|1⟩ — the √2 is why the drive couples more strongly to the leakage transition. Drive H_d(t) = Ω(t) cos(ωt + ϕ)(b + b†). The rotating frame as the time-dependent change of basis |ψ̃⟩ = e^{iωnt}|ψ⟩, with the transformed Hamiltonian H̃ = e^{iωnt}(H₀ + H_d)e^{−iωnt} − ωn derived (the extra term from differentiating the change of basis); dropping the terms rotating at 2ω — the rotating-wave approximation, stated with its condition Ω, |α| ≪ ω (unit 02's condition, now with a third level), with its error named (Bloch–Siegert, unit 02) — gives H̃ = α|2⟩⟨2| + (Ω_x(t)/2)(b + b†) + (Ω_y(t)/2)(ib† − ib) for in-phase and quadrature envelopes. On the two lowest levels this is unit 02's Rabi drive; a π-pulse has area ∫Ω_x dt = π.

Time-dependent Hamiltonians: evolution as a time-ordered product approximated by piecewise-constant steps, U ≈ ∏ e^{−iH̃(t_k)Δt}, with the error of the approximation bounded by O(Δt · max‖dH̃/dt‖ · T) — proved in the simple form needed (each step's error from the variation of H over the step; errors add as in unit 11's lemma, reproved here since unit 11 is not on the path).

Leakage: for a Gaussian π-pulse (σ = duration/4, offset so it starts at zero) at α/2π = −250 MHz, the final |2⟩ population is about 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ for a 10 ns pulse and about a quarter for 4 ns (computed by the piecewise-constant evolution — the referee reproduces both). DRAG (Motzoi, Gambetta, Rebentrost and Wilhelm, *PRL* 103, 110501, 2009): a quadrature Ω_y = −λΩ̇_x/α, derived to first order in 1/α by an adiabatic-elimination / frame-change argument (with the sign fixed by the course's α < 0 convention — say which), cancels the leading leakage; with λ = 1 leakage falls to about 4 × 10⁻⁶. But the rotation still misses |1⟩ by about 2.6–2.8 per cent: the drive's coupling to |2⟩ shifts the qubit's frequency during the pulse (an AC Stark shift of order Ω²/α), so the π-pulse is off-resonant. A detuning proportional to Ω²/α repairs most of it: with λ = 0.5 and the detuning coefficient near −0.03 (a small grid search, computed), leakage and miss together fall to about 4 × 10⁻⁴. The correction that minimises leakage is not the one that minimises gate error (Gambetta, Motzoi, Merkel and Wilhelm, *PRA* 83, 012308, 2011, cited).

Decoherence as gate error: unit 21's amplitude damping with γ = 1 − e^{−t/T₁}; the average gate infidelity over input states (average over the Bloch sphere — via ⟨z²⟩ = ⅓ by symmetry, reproved; or via the entanglement fidelity and the formula F_avg = (2F_e + 1)/3 for a qubit, Horodecki et al. 1999 / Nielsen 2002, cited) is t/(3T₁) to first order, proved. A 20 ns gate at T₁ = 100 µs: 6.7 × 10⁻⁵ — below the control errors above, so on these numbers control, not T₁, sets the floor.

## The running example
**A 10 ns Gaussian π-pulse on a three-level transmon at α/2π = −250 MHz.** Overview: the ladder and the pulse; "flip |0⟩ to |1⟩ — what goes wrong?". Section 1 (the model): the three levels, b with its √2, drawn as a ladder with the two transitions and their detunings. Section 2 (the rotating frame): H̃ derived, the RWA made. Section 3 (leakage): populations of |0⟩, |1⟩, |2⟩ through the pulse, computed and plotted; 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ ends in |2⟩; at 4 ns a quarter leaks — the duration trade-off plotted (leakage against pulse length). Section 4 (DRAG and the Stark shift): leakage to 4 × 10⁻⁶; the 2.6 per cent miss; the detuning that repairs it. Section 5 (T₁): t/3T₁ = 6.7 × 10⁻⁵ for 20 ns at 100 µs. Close: which error dominates at which duration — a plot of leakage, control miss and T₁ infidelity against gate time, and the verdict.

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and one ending in a number; referee checks where natural (each pulse by piecewise-constant evolution — `tests/test_known_results.py`'s `transmon_pi_pulse` does this, but the sheet's snippet is its own; amplitude damping via `channels.amplitude_damping`).
- "Derive the rotating-frame Hamiltonian" for the three-level transmon, exactly, then make the RWA.
- "Leakage against duration": compute the |2⟩ population for 4, 6, 8, 10, 20 ns Gaussian π-pulses (about 0.25, 0.047, 1.9 × 10⁻³, 3.0 × 10⁻⁴, 2.7 × 10⁻⁵) and explain the trend (the pulse's spectral width against |α|).
- "DRAG": add the derivative correction and watch leakage fall to about 4 × 10⁻⁶ while the rotation still misses |1⟩ by 2.6–2.8 per cent; explain the miss as an AC Stark shift.
- "The detuning": find the detuning (proportional to Ω²/α) that repairs most of the miss, by a small search; report leakage and miss together.
- "t/3T₁": prove the average infidelity of amplitude damping for short times; a 20 ns gate at T₁ = 100 µs has 6.7 × 10⁻⁵.
- "Which dominates": for gate times 5–100 ns, compare control error (from the previous parts) with T₁ infidelity, and find the crossover.
- "Piecewise-constant error": bound the error of the stepwise evolution and choose a step size for 10⁻⁶ accuracy on the 10 ns pulse.
- "A harmonic oscillator cannot be a qubit": with α = 0, show a π-pulse on |0⟩ never reaches |1⟩ alone — the population spreads up the ladder (three levels suffice to see it; the full oscillator via the cross-reference, optional).
- "Two levels are not enough": compare the two-level and three-level predictions for the 10 ns pulse.
