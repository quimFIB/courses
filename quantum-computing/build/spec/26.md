## Why this unit exists, and what it must not become
The course has proved what a fault-tolerant quantum computer could do and what it would cost. This unit asks what exists, platform by platform, and what it has actually shown — with the same standard of proof applied to claims as to theorems. DiVincenzo's criteria are the lens. Three case studies of "advantage" are read critically: random-circuit sampling (a real separation claim whose classical baseline moved), variational optimisation (QAOA on Max-Cut, analysed exactly at depth one and set against the classical algorithms it competes with), and dequantisation (a claimed exponential speedup that was a classical algorithm waiting to be found). The unit's tone is the curriculum's: the honest state of the art.

It must not become a vendor survey, a roadmap, or a list of qubit counts that will be stale in a year. Every hardware number is an order of magnitude or a cited figure with its reference and date ("as reported in …"); nothing is called "current". Platforms, not companies.

## Physics
DiVincenzo's criteria (DiVincenzo, *Fortschr. Phys.* 48, 771, 2000): scalable well-characterised qubits; initialisation; long coherence relative to gate time; a universal gate set; qubit-specific measurement (plus the two networking criteria, named). Applied to four platforms, each with what limits its T₁, its gate fidelity and its connectivity, as orders of magnitude:
- **Superconducting circuits** (transmons — unit 25 for the learner who took it): GHz frequencies, ~10–100 ns gates, T₁ ~ 10–100s of µs, fixed nearest-neighbour coupling on a chip; why they need a dilution refrigerator, as a Boltzmann factor (below).
- **Trapped ions**: qubits in atomic levels, gates by lasers or microwaves, coherence of seconds or more, all-to-all connectivity within a trap, slow gates (µs–ms).
- **Neutral atoms**: atoms held in optical tweezers, entangled via Rydberg states, reconfigurable connectivity by moving atoms.
- **Photons**: qubits that do not decohere in flight but do not interact, so two-qubit gates are probabilistic or measurement-based; loss is the dominant error.

Thermal population, derived from nothing: a two-level system at temperature T in equilibrium has excited population e^{−x}/(1 + e^{−x}) with x = hf/k_BT (Boltzmann's distribution stated as the physical input). For a 5 GHz qubit: at 20 mK, x ≈ 12.0 and the population ≈ 6.2 × 10⁻⁶; at 50 mK, 8.2 × 10⁻³; at 100 mK, 0.083; at 4 K (liquid helium), x ≈ 0.06 and the populations are 0.515 and 0.485 — nearly maximally mixed (unit 04's centre of the Bloch ball). Hence the dilution refrigerator. Trapped ions and atoms avoid this because their qubit transitions are optical or hyperfine with the atoms laser-cooled, a different route — one sentence.

## Mathematical tools
Random-circuit sampling as a benchmark: sample from the output distribution of a random circuit; the linear cross-entropy benchmark (XEB) as the figure of merit, defined (F_XEB = 2ⁿ⟨p(x_i)⟩ − 1 over observed samples x_i), with its value ≈ 1 for an ideal circuit and 0 for uniform noise, proved for the Porter–Thomas distribution in the large-n limit (stated: output probabilities of random circuits are approximately exponentially distributed, cited). The episode: the 2019 claim (Arute et al., *Nature* 574, 505, 2019: 53 qubits, 200 seconds against an estimated 10,000 years classically); the classical responses that narrowed it (Pednault et al. 2019's estimate of days with disk storage; Pan, Chen and Zhang, *PRL* 129, 090502, 2022, tensor-network simulation in hours on GPUs); later, larger experiments (cited as reported). What the episode says about moving baselines: an advantage claim is a claim about the best *known* classical algorithm, and the baseline is part of the claim.

QAOA (Farhi, Goldstone and Gutmann, arXiv:1411.4028, 2014) on Max-Cut: the cost Hamiltonian C = Σ_{edges}(1 − Z_uZ_v)/2, the mixer B = Σ X_v, the depth-one state e^{−iβB}e^{−iγC}|+⟩^{⊗n}. For 3-regular triangle-free graphs, analysed exactly: each edge's expectation depends only on its local neighbourhood (a tree of depth one: the edge plus two other neighbours at each end), computed in closed form; maximised at γ = arctan(1/√2), β = π/8, giving an expected cut fraction ½ + 1/(3√3) ≈ 0.6925 — derived, with the cube graph as the instance (the referee confirms 0.6925 by direct simulation). Against the classical side: Goemans–Williamson's semidefinite rounding guarantees 0.878 on every graph (Goemans and Williamson, *J. ACM* 42, 1115, 1995 — the sister course's unit 25 builds it), and for graphs of maximum degree 3, 0.9326 is achievable classically (Halperin, Livnat and Zwick, *J. Algorithms* 53, 169, 2004, cited); simple local classical algorithms match or beat depth-one QAOA on these graphs (Hastings 2019; Barak and Marwaha 2022, cited). Higher depth named, with the honest summary that no proven advantage exists.

Dequantisation (Tang, STOC 2019): Kerenidis and Prakash's quantum recommendation algorithm (2017) appeared exponentially faster than any classical one; Tang found a classical algorithm with comparable sampling access, polylogarithmic in the matrix dimension. The lesson: an exponential speedup claim must specify the input model, and quantum-inspired classical algorithms are part of the baseline.

## The running example
**Max-Cut on the cube graph with depth-one QAOA.** Overview: the eight-vertex cube (3-regular, triangle-free, bipartite — its maximum cut is all 12 edges), and "what would running QAOA on it on real hardware involve, and what would it show?". Section 1 (criteria and platforms): eight qubits on each platform; connectivity matching the cube or not. Section 2 (the fridge): the thermal population of each qubit at 20 mK and at 4 K. Section 3 (the algorithm): the depth-one state; the edge expectation derived for triangle-free 3-regular graphs; γ, β; 0.6925 of the edges cut in expectation — 8.3 of 12, against a maximum of 12. Section 4 (the baselines): Goemans–Williamson's 0.878 guarantee, 0.9326 for degree-3 graphs, and the trivial random cut's ½; drawn as a number line. Section 5 (advantage claims): random-circuit sampling and dequantisation as the two cautionary tales. Close: on the cube graph, a classical computer finds the maximum cut (bipartite: all 12 edges) instantly; the running example is chosen to make the point.

## Worked examples (three to five, in `box ex`)
1. Thermal populations at 20 mK, 50 mK, 100 mK and 4 K for a 5 GHz qubit, from x = hf/k_BT, each summed with the ground population to one.
2. QAOA's depth-one edge expectation for a triangle-free 3-regular graph, derived; the maximisation; ½ + 1/(3√3) ≈ 0.6925.
3. XEB: F_XEB for an ideal sampler and for uniform noise, from the exponential distribution of output probabilities (stated).
4. (optional) The cube graph's maximum cut found classically (it is bipartite), and the expected cut of QAOA-1 on it (8.31 edges).

## What the problem sheet exercises
Ten to twelve problems; at least one proof and one ending in a number; referee checks where natural (thermal numbers, QAOA on the cube as `tests/test_known_results.py` does — but the sheet's snippet is its own).
- "The fridge": thermal excited-state population of a 5 GHz qubit — hf/k_BT ≈ 12 at 20 mK, about 6 × 10⁻⁶; 0.06 at 4 K, nearly maximally mixed — and the temperature at which the population is 10⁻³ (x = ln 999, about 34.7 mK).
- "QAOA's edge expectation": derive it for triangle-free 3-regular graphs and maximise over γ, β.
- "A claimed advantage against its baseline": for one published claim (random-circuit sampling 2019, or a QAOA experiment), state exactly which classical algorithm it was measured against, and what later classical work did to the comparison.
- "Random cuts": prove a uniformly random cut cuts half the edges in expectation, and that a greedy local search reaches at least half deterministically.
- "Triangles matter": show where the depth-one analysis uses triangle-freeness, and what changes on a triangle.
- "DiVincenzo for photons": which criteria are hard, and why measurement-based schemes sidestep the two-qubit gate.
- "Connectivity cost": embedding the cube graph's 12 interactions on a 2×4 nearest-neighbour grid — how many SWAPs a depth-one circuit needs (find a schedule and count).
- "XEB": prove F_XEB = 0 for a uniform sampler and ≈ 1 for an ideal one under the exponential-distribution assumption.
- "Dequantisation, in outline": state the sampling-access input model and why it makes the comparison fair.
- "Fault-tolerance arithmetic, revisited" (joining unit 24): with 881 physical qubits per logical qubit, how many physical qubits for 100 logical qubits plus distillation, to an order of magnitude — and how that compares with the largest devices reported in the references the unit cites.
