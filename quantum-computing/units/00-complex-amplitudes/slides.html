00 · the experiment

Stern–Gerlach, from nothing

Silver atoms leave an oven, are narrowed to a beam, cross a magnet whose field is stronger near one pole, and land on a plate. Two spots, not a smear.

What a magnet does. A small magnet — a compass needle — feels a net push only where the field is stronger on one side. The push is along the field, and its size depends on how the needle points: along the field, one way; against it, the other; across it, not at all.

The atom. Assume only that a silver atom has some internal direction, like a needle. Atoms from a hot oven point every which way, so the pushes should take every value between the extremes.

the Stern–Gerlach experiment, Frankfurt, 1922

Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach found exactly two spots. The magnet sorts atoms into two classes along its axis — call them up and down — and nothing in between.

The apparatus. Silver is heated in an oven until it evaporates; atoms escape through a small hole, and two narrow slits in a row keep only those travelling in one direction, so what reaches the magnet is a thin beam. The magnet's pole pieces are shaped differently — one is a knife edge, the other flat or grooved — so the field is much stronger near the edge than near the flat face. The atoms then land on a glass plate, where after some hours they leave a visible deposit.

Why only a non-uniform field pushes. A compass needle in a uniform field feels a twist (it turns to line up) but no net push: its two ends are pulled equally hard in opposite directions. If the field is stronger at one end than the other, the pulls no longer cancel, and the needle is pushed toward the stronger or weaker region depending on which way it points along the field. That is all the physics this unit needs. What the "internal direction" of an atom actually is — the electron's spin — is unit 02's business, and nothing here depends on knowing it.

The expected smear. If the internal directions were random and the push depended continuously on the angle between that direction and the field, the deflections would fill an interval: a vertical line on the plate. The two separated spots say the measured quantity takes two values only.

History. The experiment was done in Frankfurt in February 1922. Gerlach mailed Niels Bohr a postcard of the split, congratulating him on the confirmation of his theory, which had predicted a discrete set of orientations. Bohr's model was right for the wrong reason: silver's ground state has no orbital angular momentum at all, and the two beams come from the outermost electron's spin, an idea from 1925 (unit 02). The deposit was at first too faint to see and became visible, the story goes, when sulphur from Stern's cheap cigar darkened it; Friedrich and Herschbach tell the story in Physics Today, December 2003.

00 · the experiment

Magnets in sequence

Boxes are magnets labelled by their axis; a red bar blocks a beam. Row 3: the $x$ magnet has erased the earlier $z$ result.

Repeat: keep the up beam of a $z$ magnet; a second $z$ magnet sends all of it up.

Turn: a perpendicular $x$ magnet splits it half and half — and so does an $x$ magnet after the down beam.

Return: keep the $+$ beam from $x$, measure $z$ again: half and half. The first $z$ result is gone.

The same holds for every pair among three perpendicular axes $x, y, z$.

an idealisation, honestly labelled

Stern and Gerlach used one magnet. The sequence is the textbook idealisation (Feynman, Lectures III ch. 5–6; Sakurai §1.1), whose predictions later experiments with sequential fields confirm.

Three axes from a beam that travels along one of them. The beam must cross the magnet, so a magnet's axis is always perpendicular to the beam. With the beam flying along one direction, only two perpendicular axes are available. To measure along the third, send the atoms in along a different line, as the textbook accounts do. That the atom's internal direction does not care which way the atom flies is part of the idealisation, and is borne out by experiment.

What "half" means. Each atom goes one way or the other; it is the fraction, over many atoms, that is one half. Nothing in the experiment says which way a given atom will go, and the minimal model on the next slide does not claim to either: it predicts probabilities.

Attribution. The sequential arrangement is not something Stern and Gerlach did; it is the standard teaching idealisation, popularised by Feynman's Lectures on Physics, volume III, chapters 5 and 6, and by the first section of Sakurai's Modern Quantum Mechanics. Its predictions are what every later experiment on two-outcome systems — atoms, neutrons, the polarisation of light — finds.

Why this is strange. If an atom carried a definite $z$ value and a definite $x$ value, the third row would return all atoms up. It returns half. Either the $x$ magnet disturbs the $z$ value, or there was never a $z$ value to keep. The model that follows does not decide between these readings; it just reproduces the numbers.

00 · the experiment

The least model that could describe it

the minimal model, hypotheses for this unit only
  1. A state is a unit vector $\psi$ of a finite-dimensional inner-product space $V$.
  2. A measurement with $k$ outcomes is an orthonormal basis $e_1, \dots, e_k$ of $V$.
  3. Outcome $i$ has probability $p_i = |\braket{e_i|\psi}|^2$.
  4. Afterwards the state is $e_i$.

Normalise and check. $\sum_i p_i = \sum_i |\braket{e_i|\psi}|^2 = \lVert\psi\rVert^2 = 1$ by Parseval (proved for $\mathbb C$ in section 2).

Two outcomes, so two dimensions. A measurement is a basis, and a basis has $\dim V$ elements; a magnet has two outcomes, so $\dim V = 2$.

Repeatability — row 1 — is built in: after outcome $i$ the state is $e_i$, and measuring again gives $i$ with probability $|\braket{e_i|e_i}|^2 = 1$.

over which field?

Nothing yet says whether $V$ is real or complex. Try $\mathbb R^2$ first — the experiment is about directions in ordinary space, so a real model is the natural guess.

Unit 01 makes these hypotheses general: measurements with repeated outcomes, and what happens between measurements.

Why "minimal". This is the smallest structure that could possibly fit: one vector per preparation, one basis per magnet, probabilities from overlaps. It is not yet quantum mechanics. Unit 01 replaces "a basis" by a projection-valued measure, so that an outcome can correspond to a subspace bigger than a line, and adds the rule for evolution between measurements.

Why squares of overlaps. The rule $p_i = |\braket{e_i|\psi}|^2$ is the simplest assignment that (a) gives probability one to the outcome the state already is, (b) sums to one over any orthonormal basis, and (c) depends only on the two vectors. Gleason's theorem (cited: A. M. Gleason, J. Math. Mech. 6, 885 (1957); not needed in this course) says that in dimension at least three, any reasonable probability assignment to measurements has this form or is a weighted average of such — unit 04's density operators. In dimension two it is a hypothesis.

Why the dimension is exactly two. If $V$ were bigger, a two-outcome magnet would have to be a basis with more than two elements, which hypothesis 2 forbids. Unit 01's more general measurements lift this restriction — and that is precisely the door through which the real model of the slide What the real failure does and does not show escapes.

00 · the experiment

Mutually unbiased bases

mutually unbiased bases

Orthonormal bases $\{e_i\}$ and $\{f_j\}$ of a $d$-dimensional space are mutually unbiased if

$$|\braket{e_i|f_j}|^2 = \frac1d \quad\text{for all } i, j.$$

Prepared in any vector of one basis, a measurement in the other is uniformly random.

Check: why $1/d$. By Parseval, $\sum_j|\braket{e_i|f_j}|^2 = \lVert e_i\rVert^2 = 1$; $d$ equal terms make $1/d$ each. Only the equality is a condition.

Translate the experiment. A $z$ magnet is a basis $z_\uparrow, z_\downarrow$; an $x$ magnet a basis $x_+, x_-$. Row 2: from $z_\uparrow$ or $z_\downarrow$, each $x$ outcome has probability $\tfrac12$:

$$|\braket{x_\pm|z_\uparrow}|^2 = |\braket{x_\pm|z_\downarrow}|^2 = \tfrac12 .$$

Row 3 says the same from the other side. So $z$ and $x$ are unbiased, and so are $z, y$ and $x, y$.

the puzzle, stated

Find three orthonormal bases of a two-dimensional space, each pair mutually unbiased.

Symmetry of the definition. $|\braket{e_i|f_j}| = |\braket{f_j|e_i}|$, so "unbiased" does not care which basis came first; that is why rows 2 and 3 of the experiment say the same thing. (Over $\mathbb C$ the two inner products are complex conjugates, as section 2 shows, and have the same modulus.)

Unbiased is the opposite of equal. Two equal bases (up to signs or phases) have overlaps 0 and 1: measuring in one tells you everything about the other. Unbiased bases have all overlaps equal: knowing the result in one tells you nothing about the other. A measurement in the $x$ basis of a $z$ state is a fair coin.

Where this goes. Mutually unbiased bases are the backbone of quantum key distribution (unit 09: an eavesdropper who measures in the wrong basis learns nothing and leaves a trace) and of optimal state estimation (Wootters and Fields, 1989, problem P2).

00 · the experiment · example

Worked: the real plane holds at most two

in $\mathbb R^2$, with $\braket{u|v} = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2$

1. Unbiased to the standard basis. A unit vector $u = (a, b)$ has $|\braket{e_1|u}|^2 = a^2$ and $|\braket{e_2|u}|^2 = b^2$. Both equal $\tfrac12$ iff

$$u = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\pm1, \pm1).$$

2. Only one such basis. Up to sign — which changes no square — there are two such vectors, $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, -1)$. They are orthogonal: so exactly one basis, call it $x$, is unbiased to $z = \{e_1, e_2\}$.

3. No third. A third basis unbiased to $z$ is, by 1–2, again made of $\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, \pm1)$. Its overlaps with $x$:

$$\Big|\tfrac12(1, 1)\cdot(1, 1)\Big|^2 = 1, \qquad \Big|\tfrac12(1, 1)\cdot(1, -1)\Big|^2 = 0.$$

Never $\tfrac12$. At most two mutually unbiased bases in $\mathbb R^2$. $\blacksquare$

Unbiased lines in $\mathbb R^2$ meet at $45^\circ$. The diagonals are the only lines at $45^\circ$ to $z$, and they are at $0^\circ$ or $90^\circ$ to themselves.

Each step. Step 1: $\lVert u\rVert = 1$ means $a^2 + b^2 = 1$; unbiased means $a^2 = b^2 = \tfrac12$, so $a, b \in \{\pm1/\sqrt2\}$, four vectors in all. Step 2: replacing $u$ by $-u$ changes no probability, so the four come in two sign-classes, represented by $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)$ and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)$; their inner product is $\tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0$, so they form an orthonormal basis, and any orthonormal basis unbiased to $z$ must consist of one vector from each class. Step 3: the third basis's first vector is $\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, \pm1)$, whose inner products with the two $x$ vectors are $\pm1$ and $0$ in some order. Squared: $1$ and $0$.

Check on $x$ itself. Its overlaps with $z$ are all $(1/\sqrt2)^2 = \tfrac12$ ✓, so two is attained.

The same argument as geometry. In $\mathbb R^2$, $|\braket{u|v}|^2 = \cos^2\angle(u,v)$, and $\cos^2 = \tfrac12$ means the lines meet at $45^\circ$. Lines at $45^\circ$ to both axes of $z$ are the diagonals; a line at $45^\circ$ to both diagonals is an axis. So a third basis would have to be $z$ and $x$ at once. Problem P1 asks for this version in full.

In higher dimension. $\mathbb R^d$ holds at most $d/2 + 1$ real mutually unbiased bases (cited: Delsarte, Goethals and Seidel, Philips Research Reports 30, 91 (1975)); for $d = 2$ that is two, the bound proved here.

What the real failure does and does not show

what it shows

The minimal model over $\mathbb R$ cannot reproduce three magnets: it has room for two unbiased bases and the experiment needs three. If the minimal model is to survive, its field is not $\mathbb R$.

what it does not show

That no real theory could work. Drop "dimension two" and one can: Stueckelberg (1960) built a real model on a doubled space that reproduces every single-system statistic.

What does rule out real quantum theory is an experiment with several parties sharing systems from independent sources: Renou et al., Nature 600, 625 (2021), proposed it; two experiments in 2022 (Phys. Rev. Lett. 128, 040402 and 040403) performed it, and the real theory lost. Cited.

The reasoning is Bell-type — about correlations between distant parties — and unit 06 builds that kind of argument.

So, read precisely: the minimal model forces $\mathbb C$; the full theory needs a multi-party experiment to force it. Both point the same way.

How Stueckelberg's model escapes (E. C. G. Stueckelberg, Helv. Phys. Acta 33, 727 (1960), cited). Write $\mathbb C^d$ as $\mathbb R^{2d}$ (real and imaginary parts). Multiplication by $i$ becomes a real matrix $\mathcal J$ with $\mathcal J^2 = -I$. Every complex measurement basis vector $e$ becomes a real plane, spanned by $e$ and $\mathcal Je$, and the outcome probability is the squared length of the projection onto that plane — which equals the complex $|\braket{e|\psi}|^2$. So the real model's measurements are rank-two projections, not bases, and the minimal model's hypothesis 2 fails for it. It survives single-system tests by carrying a hidden copy of the complex structure.

Why it fails with several parties. Combining two such real systems the natural real way gives a space that is "too big": it contains states that complex quantum theory does not, and it lacks some correlations that complex quantum theory produces when two parties each share a system with a third, from independent sources. Renou and collaborators found a network experiment whose quantum prediction no real model of that kind can match; the 2022 experiments (one with photons, one with superconducting qubits) measured past the real bound. The argument needs tensor products (unit 03) and Bell-type reasoning (unit 06) and is not developed in this course.

Why bother with the minimal-model argument, then? Because it is where the complex numbers first become necessary rather than convenient, it uses nothing but one experiment and one page of linear algebra, and the third basis it finds — the $y$ basis — keeps reappearing in this unit.

Complex numbers: the facts this unit uses

For $z = a + ib$ with $a, b$ real:

  • complex conjugate $\bar z = a - ib$; $\ \overline{zw} = \bar z\bar w$, $\ \overline{z + w} = \bar z + \bar w$;
  • modulus $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, so $|z|^2 = \bar zz$; $\ |zw| = |z||w|$; $\ \mathrm{Re}\,z \le |z|$;
  • $z + \bar z = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,z$, $\ z - \bar z = 2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,z$: $z$ is real iff $z = \bar z$.
phase

$e^{i\varphi} := \cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi$ for real $\varphi$. A phase is a complex number of modulus one; every one is $e^{i\varphi}$.

A phase is a point of the unit circle: $e^{i\varphi}$ at angle $\varphi$, its conjugate $e^{-i\varphi}$ the mirror image, and $z = |z|e^{i\varphi}$ further out on the same ray. The black points are the checks of Three facts about phases.

Why this slide. The course assumes $\mathbb C$ as a field and nothing more. Conjugation, modulus and polar form are used on every slide from here, so they are fixed now, with the exponential notation defined rather than assumed. That $\cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi$ really is $\sum (i\varphi)^n/n!$ is proved in section 5, as the one-dimensional case of the matrix exponential.

The inequality $\mathrm{Re}\,z \le |z|$ is $a \le \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$; it is where the triangle inequality comes from, in the notes to Cauchy–Schwarz, by projection.

The figure. $z = a + ib$ is the point $(a, b)$ of the plane, $|z|$ its distance from $0$, and $\bar z$ its reflection in the real axis. A phase $e^{i\varphi} = \cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi$ is the point $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$, at angle $\varphi$ on the unit circle, and polar form says every $z \ne 0$ lies on the ray through exactly one such point. The multiplication rule of the next slide then says that multiplying by $e^{i\alpha}$ turns the circle by $\alpha$; the marked points are the quarter-, half- and third-turns, and three third-turns make a whole one, which is why $e^{2\pi i/3}$ cubes to $1$.

Three facts about phases

three facts $$|e^{i\varphi}| = 1, \qquad \overline{e^{i\varphi}} = e^{-i\varphi},$$ $$e^{i\alpha}e^{i\beta} = e^{i(\alpha + \beta)},$$

and every $z \ne 0$ is $|z|e^{i\varphi}$ for some $\varphi$ (its polar form).

proof

$\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$; multiply out and use the addition formulae for $\cos(\alpha + \beta)$ and $\sin(\alpha + \beta)$; $\sin$ is odd. Polar form: $z/|z|$ has modulus one, so is $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$ on the unit circle.

Check: $e^{i\pi/2} = i$, $\ e^{i\pi} = -1$, $\ e^{2\pi i/3}$ is a cube root of $1$. Section 5 shows $e^{i\varphi}$ is the exponential series.

Full proof of the multiplication rule. $(\cos\alpha + i\sin\alpha)(\cos\beta + i\sin\beta) = (\cos\alpha\cos\beta - \sin\alpha\sin\beta) + i(\sin\alpha\cos\beta + \cos\alpha\sin\beta) = \cos(\alpha + \beta) + i\sin(\alpha + \beta)$. And $\overline{e^{i\varphi}} = \cos\varphi - i\sin\varphi = \cos(-\varphi) + i\sin(-\varphi)$.

The cube root. $(e^{2\pi i/3})^3 = e^{2\pi i} = \cos2\pi + i\sin2\pi = 1$ by the multiplication rule; it reappears in problem P2, where $\mathbb C^3$ holds four mutually unbiased bases built from it. On the circle of the previous slide it is the point a third of a turn round.

The inner product, and its convention

a complex inner product

On a complex vector space $V$, a map $\braket{\cdot|\cdot}: V\times V \to \mathbb C$ with

  1. linear in the second slot: $\braket{u|av + bw} = a\braket{u|v} + b\braket{u|w}$;
  2. conjugate symmetry: $\braket{v|u} = \overline{\braket{u|v}}$;
  3. positive: $\braket{v|v} \gt 0$ for $v \ne 0$.

Then $\braket{au + bw|v} = \bar a\braket{u|v} + \bar b\braket{w|v}$: sesquilinear. The norm is $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt{\braket{v|v}}$.

On $\mathbb C^d$: $\ \braket{u|v} = \sum_i \bar u_iv_i$. Check: $\big\lVert\tfrac15(3, 4i)\big\rVert^2 = \tfrac{9 + 16}{25} = 1$.

the course convention

Conjugate-linear in the first slot, linear in the second — the physicists' convention, fixed for the whole course. Many mathematics texts (Axler), and Mechanics to Relativity unit 19, conjugate the second slot instead. Translating: swap the arguments.

why a conjugate at all

A form that is bilinear over $\mathbb C$ cannot be positive: it would give

$$\braket{iv|iv} = i\cdot i\,\braket{v|v} = -\braket{v|v} \lt 0 .$$

Conjugating one slot repairs it: $\braket{iv|iv} = \bar i\,i\braket{v|v} = \braket{v|v}$.

Full proof of conjugate-linearity in the first slot. By conjugate symmetry and linearity in the second slot, $\braket{au + bw|v} = \overline{\braket{v|au + bw}} = \overline{a\braket{v|u} + b\braket{v|w}} = \bar a\,\overline{\braket{v|u}} + \bar b\,\overline{\braket{v|w}} = \bar a\braket{u|v} + \bar b\braket{w|v}$. "Sesquilinear" means "one-and-a-half linear".

The standard example. On $\mathbb C^d$, $\sum_i\bar u_iv_i$ is linear in $v$, conjugate symmetric because $\overline{\sum\bar u_iv_i} = \sum u_i\bar v_i = \sum\bar v_iu_i$, and positive because $\braket{v|v} = \sum|v_i|^2$. In particular $\braket{v|v}$ is always real, as conjugate symmetry with $u = v$ forces.

Why the convention matters in practice. Expanding a vector in an orthonormal basis, the coefficient of $e_i$ in $v$ is $\braket{e_i|v}$ in this convention and $\braket{v, e_i}$ in the other. Get it backwards and you conjugate every amplitude — which, Worked: one state, three magnets shows, swaps the two outcomes of a $y$ measurement. The physicists' convention has one real advantage: a bra, section 3, is then a linear functional.

A dimension count, for later. $\mathbb C^d$ is a real vector space of dimension $2d$. As a real space with $\mathrm{Re}\braket{u|v}$, it is just $\mathbb R^{2d}$ with the dot product; the imaginary part of the inner product is the extra structure, and it is what Stueckelberg's real model, section 1, has to carry by hand.

Cauchy–Schwarz, by projection

Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $$|\braket{u|v}| \le \lVert u\rVert\,\lVert v\rVert,$$

with equality iff $u, v$ are linearly dependent.

proof

For $u \ne 0$, let $w = v - \frac{\braket{u|v}}{\lVert u\rVert^2}u$. Then $\braket{u|w} = 0$, and Pythagoras gives

$$\lVert v\rVert^2 = \frac{|\braket{u|v}|^2}{\lVert u\rVert^2} + \lVert w\rVert^2 .$$

Equality iff $w = 0$. The conjugate enters once: $\lVert cu\rVert^2 = \bar cc\lVert u\rVert^2 = |c|^2\lVert u\rVert^2$.

Drawn in the real plane. The leg along $u$ has length $|c|\,\lVert u\rVert = |\braket{u|v}|/\lVert u\rVert$ and the hypotenuse is $\lVert v\rVert$. A leg is never longer than the hypotenuse — that is the inequality — and equals it only when $w = 0$.

Full proof of Cauchy–Schwarz. If $u = 0$ both sides vanish. Otherwise set $c = \braket{u|v}/\lVert u\rVert^2$ and $w = v - cu$. Then $\braket{u|w} = \braket{u|v} - c\braket{u|u} = 0$. Write $v = cu + w$ and expand, using $\braket{u|w} = \braket{w|u} = 0$: $\lVert v\rVert^2 = \braket{cu + w|cu + w} = \bar cc\lVert u\rVert^2 + \lVert w\rVert^2 = |\braket{u|v}|^2/\lVert u\rVert^2 + \lVert w\rVert^2$. Dropping $\lVert w\rVert^2 \ge 0$ gives the inequality; equality holds iff $w = 0$, iff $v$ is a multiple of $u$.

The figure, over $\mathbb C$. The plane drawn is real, so $c$ is a real number there. Over $\mathbb C$ the coefficient $c$ is complex and $cu$ is not a point of a drawable line, but nothing in the proof looks at the picture: it uses only $\braket{u|w} = 0$ and the Pythagorean expansion above, which hold verbatim. The figure is the shape of the argument, not a model of $\mathbb C^d$.

Check in a known case. $u = \ket0$, $v = \psi = \tfrac15(3, 4i)$: $|\braket{0|\psi}| = \tfrac35 \le 1\cdot1$, strict, as it must be since $\psi$ is not a multiple of $\ket0$; $v = 2i\ket0$ gives $|2i| = 2 = 1\cdot2$, equality for a dependent pair ✓.

Where the real proof breaks. The other common real proof expands $\lVert u + tv\rVert^2 \ge 0$ for real $t$ and reads off a discriminant. Over $\mathbb C$ that gives $\lVert u\rVert^2 + 2t\,\mathrm{Re}\braket{u|v} + t^2\lVert v\rVert^2 \ge 0$, which bounds only $|\mathrm{Re}\braket{u|v}|$. Repair: first multiply $v$ by a phase making $\braket{u|v}$ real and non-negative. The projection proof above needs no repair.

Triangle inequality. $\lVert u + v\rVert^2 = \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\braket{u|v} + \lVert v\rVert^2 \le \lVert u\rVert^2 + 2\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert + \lVert v\rVert^2$, using $\mathrm{Re}\,z \le |z|$ and Cauchy–Schwarz.

Orthonormal bases, and Parseval

Orthonormal bases. Gram–Schmidt runs exactly as over $\mathbb R$, subtracting $\braket{e_j|v}e_j$ — with the basis vector on the left. So every finite-dimensional complex inner-product space has an orthonormal basis, and

$$v = \sum_i \braket{e_i|v}\,e_i, \qquad \braket{u|v} = \sum_i \overline{\braket{e_i|u}}\,\braket{e_i|v}.$$
Parseval's identity

$\lVert v\rVert^2 = \sum_i|\braket{e_i|v}|^2$. So the minimal model's probabilities sum to one for every unit vector.

One step, drawn. Each Gram–Schmidt step is the picture of Cauchy–Schwarz, by projection: $w_k$ is $v_k$ minus its projections on the earlier $e_j$, so it is the perpendicular leg of that triangle, and $e_k$ is that leg scaled to length one.

Gram–Schmidt and the expansions. Given independent $v_1, \dots, v_n$, set $e_1 = v_1/\lVert v_1\rVert$ and $e_k = w_k/\lVert w_k\rVert$ with $w_k = v_k - \sum_{j\lt k}\braket{e_j|v_k}e_j$; then $\braket{e_j|w_k} = \braket{e_j|v_k} - \braket{e_j|v_k} = 0$ for $j \lt k$, by linearity in the second slot. Writing $\braket{v_k|e_j}e_j$ instead would subtract the wrong (conjugated) amount. For the expansion: if $v = \sum c_ie_i$ then $\braket{e_j|v} = c_j$. For the inner product: $\braket{u|v} = \braket{\sum_i a_ie_i|\sum_j c_je_j} = \sum_{i}\bar a_ic_i$, with $a_i = \braket{e_i|u}$, $c_i = \braket{e_i|v}$. Parseval is the case $u = v$.

Global phase and relative phase

global phase is invisible

For every basis vector $e$: $\ |\braket{e|e^{i\alpha}\psi}|^2 = |e^{i\alpha}|^2|\braket{e|\psi}|^2 = |\braket{e|\psi}|^2$.

So $\psi$ and $e^{i\alpha}\psi$ give the same probabilities in every measurement. The course drops such a factor only with the words up to global phase.

A relative phase is not. Every unit vector unbiased to $z$ is, up to global phase,

$$\ket{\varphi} := \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\ket1\big),$$

(norm $\tfrac12(1 + |e^{i\varphi}|^2) = 1$), and its overlap with $\ket+$ is

$$|\braket{+|\varphi}|^2 = \tfrac14|1 + e^{i\varphi}|^2 = \tfrac12(1 + \cos\varphi).$$

Each point $e^{i\varphi}$ of the circle is a state unbiased to $z$. Real amplitudes reach only the two teal points on the real axis; the warm points are the $y$ basis.

the third basis is forced

Unbiased to $x$ as well iff $\cos\varphi = 0$, iff $\varphi = \pm\tfrac\pi2$: the $y$ basis, and nothing else.

Full proof that every unit vector unbiased to $z$ has this form. Let $\psi = a\ket0 + b\ket1$ with $|a|^2 = |b|^2 = \tfrac12$. Write $a = e^{i\alpha}/\sqrt2$ and $b = e^{i\beta}/\sqrt2$ (polar form). Then $\psi = e^{i\alpha}\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{i(\beta - \alpha)}\ket1)$, which is $\ket{\varphi}$ with $\varphi = \beta - \alpha$, up to global phase. Normalised: $\tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1$.

The overlap. $\braket{+|\varphi} = \tfrac12(1 + e^{i\varphi})$ and $|1 + e^{i\varphi}|^2 = (1 + \cos\varphi)^2 + \sin^2\varphi = 2 + 2\cos\varphi$. Checks: $\varphi = 0$ gives $1$, and the state is $\ket+$ itself ✓; $\varphi = \pi$ gives $0$, and the state is $\ket-$, orthogonal to $\ket+$ ✓; $\varphi = \pm\pi/2$ gives $\tfrac12$ ✓.

What this says about $\mathbb R^2$ again. Over $\mathbb R$ the "phase" $e^{i\varphi}$ can only be $\pm1$, so the circle collapses to its two real points, which are the $x$ basis. Worked: the real plane holds at most two was this picture with the imaginary axis missing. Over $\mathbb C$ the circle is all there, and the two points a quarter-turn from $x$ are the $y$ basis.

How close this is to "at most three". A fourth basis unbiased to $z$, $x$ and $y$ would have to lie, by this slide, at $\varphi = \pm\pi/2$: that is $y$ itself, whose overlaps with $y$ are $0$ and $1$. So with $z$ and $x$ fixed there is no fourth. The missing step — that any two unbiased bases of $\mathbb C^2$ can be moved onto $z$ and $x$ by a unitary — is left to unit 02, as promised.

Why "relative". Multiplying the whole state by $e^{i\alpha}$ changes nothing; changing the phase of one component relative to the other changes the $x$ and $y$ statistics while leaving the $z$ ones alone. That relative phase is a third real parameter beyond the two $z$ probabilities, and it is the room a third unbiased basis needs.

A bra is a dual vector and nothing more. An outer product is a rank-one map. The adjoint is what moves an operator across the bracket.

00 · bras and kets

A bra is a dual vector

the dual space

$V^*$ is the space of linear maps $V \to \mathbb C$. Each $v \in V$ gives one: $\ w \mapsto \braket{v|w}$, linear because the second slot is.

the Riesz isomorphism

On a finite-dimensional inner-product space, every $\phi \in V^*$ is $\braket{v|\cdot}$ for exactly one $v$. The map $R: v \mapsto \braket{v|\cdot}$ is a bijection $V \to V^*$, and conjugate-linear: $R(cv) = \bar c\,R(v)$.

proof

Take $v = \sum_i\overline{\phi(e_i)}\,e_i$ for an orthonormal basis; then $\braket{v|w} = \sum_i\phi(e_i)\braket{e_i|w} = \phi(w)$. If $\braket{v|\cdot} = \braket{v'|\cdot}$, put $w = v - v'$: $\lVert v - v'\rVert^2 = 0$.

Dirac notation

A ket $\ket v$ is the vector $v$. A bra $\bra v := R(v)$ is its dual vector. The bracket is a bra applied to a ket:

$$\bra u\big(\ket v\big) = \braket{u|v}.$$

On $\mathbb C^d$: a ket is a column, its bra the conjugate-transposed row, and the bracket is row times column.

the one rule to remember

$\bra{cv} = \bar c\bra v$. So $\bra{+i} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, -i)$: the conjugation the test state's $y$ amplitudes needed.

Full proof of the Riesz isomorphism. Existence: with $v$ as on the slide, $\braket{v|w} = \sum_i\overline{\overline{\phi(e_i)}}\braket{e_i|w} = \sum_i\phi(e_i)\braket{e_i|w}$, by conjugate-linearity in the first slot; and $w = \sum_i\braket{e_i|w}e_i$, so by linearity of $\phi$ this is $\phi(w)$. Uniqueness is on the slide. Conjugate-linearity: $R(cv)(w) = \braket{cv|w} = \bar c\braket{v|w}$. So $R$ is injective and conjugate-linear, and surjective by existence. (Over $\mathbb R$ the same proof gives the familiar linear isomorphism $V\cong V^*$.)

"Nothing more". Dirac notation can look like a new formalism. It is not: $\bra v$ is the linear functional $\braket{v|\cdot}$, and $\braket{u|v}$ written as a single bracket is the same number as $\bra u$ applied to $\ket v$. What the notation buys is that every expression reads left to right as a composition of maps, and the next slide's $\ket u\!\bra v$ makes sense as written.

Infinite dimensions are not this course's business, but for orientation: there the Riesz theorem needs completeness and continuity of $\phi$, and kets like "$\ket x$" for a position are not vectors at all. Every space in this course is finite-dimensional.

Why the physicists' slot convention is natural here. With linearity in the second slot, $\bra v$ is a linear functional of the ket it eats. In the other convention, $\braket{\cdot, v}$ is linear in its first argument and the bracket would have to be read right to left.

Outer products are rank-one maps

the outer product

For $u, v \in V$, the linear map

$$\ket u\!\bra v : \ w \mapsto \braket{v|w}\,u .$$

The notation reads itself: $\ket u\!\bra v\,\ket w = \ket u\braket{v|w}$. For $u, v \ne 0$ it has rank one: image $\mathrm{span}(u)$, kernel $v^\perp$. On $\mathbb C^d$ its matrix is $u\,\bar v^{\mathsf T}$, column times conjugate row.

calculus $$\big(\ket u\!\bra v\big)\big(\ket w\!\bra x\big) = \braket{v|w}\,\ket u\!\bra x,$$ $$\operatorname{tr}\ket u\!\bra v = \braket{v|u}.$$

In the real plane: the bra $\bra0$ reads one number, constant along each line parallel to the kernel; the ket says where to put it. $\ket+\!\bra0$ puts $0.8$ on the line of $\ket+$; $\ket0\!\bra0$ drops $w$ straight down.

a small one

$\ket+\!\bra0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ sends $\ket0 \mapsto \ket+$ and $\ket1 \mapsto 0$. Trace $\tfrac1{\sqrt2} = \braket{0|+}$ ✓.

Full proof of the calculus. Composition: apply both sides to $y$. Left: $\ket u\!\bra v\,(\braket{x|y}w) = \braket{x|y}\braket{v|w}u$. Right: $\braket{v|w}\braket{x|y}u$. Equal. Trace: in an orthonormal basis, $\operatorname{tr}\ket u\!\bra v = \sum_i\braket{e_i|u}\braket{v|e_i} = \braket{v|\sum_i\braket{e_i|u}e_i} = \braket{v|u}$, using linearity of the second slot and the expansion of $u$. Equivalently: the trace of $u\bar v^{\mathsf T}$ is $\sum_iu_i\bar v_i$.

Rank one. The image of $w\mapsto\braket{v|w}u$ is contained in $\mathrm{span}(u)$ and contains $u$ (take $w = v/\lVert v\rVert^2$); its kernel is $\{w : \braket{v|w} = 0\} = v^\perp$. Conversely every rank-one map is an outer product: if the image is $\mathrm{span}(u)$, then $Aw = \phi(w)u$ for a linear $\phi$, and $\phi = \bra v$ by Riesz.

Orthogonal projectors

orthogonal projector

An operator $P$ with $P^2 = P$ and $P^\dagger = P$ (adjoint: the slide The adjoint). For a unit vector $e$,

$$P_e = \ket e\!\bra e, \qquad P_ew = \braket{e|w}\,e,$$

is the orthogonal projector onto $\mathrm{span}(e)$: $P_e^2 = \ket e\braket{e|e}\bra e = P_e$, by the composition rule of Outer products are rank-one maps.

$P_e$ keeps the part of $w$ along $e$ and kills the part in $e^\perp$. The two parts are perpendicular, and a vector already on the line stays where it is: $P_e^2 = P_e$.

Why $P_e$ is orthogonal. $w - P_ew$ is orthogonal to $e$: $\braket{e|w - \braket{e|w}e} = \braket{e|w} - \braket{e|w} = 0$. So $P_e$ splits $w$ into a part along $e$ and a part perpendicular to it — the picture of projection from the real plane, unchanged. Problem P4 asks when a general $\ket u\!\bra v$ is a projector, and finds projectors that are idempotent but not orthogonal.

Resolving the identity

resolution of the identity

For any orthonormal basis $e_1, \dots, e_d$:

$$\sum_i\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i} = I .$$
proof

Applied to $w$, the left side gives $\sum_i\braket{e_i|w}e_i = w$, the expansion in the basis.

Each measurement of the minimal model splits $I$ into one projector per outcome; the probabilities $\braket{\psi|P_{e_i}\psi}$ sum to $\braket{\psi|\psi} = 1$.

One $w$, two orthonormal bases of the real plane. Teal: $w = P_0w + P_1w$. Warm: $w = P_+w + P_-w$. Either way the pieces are perpendicular and add back to $w$. The $y$ basis needs complex coordinates; The three bases, resolved does it with matrices.

The figure. $P_e$ is $\ket e\!\bra e$, so $P_0w$ and $P_1w$ are the coordinates of $w$ along the axes and $P_\pm w = \braket{\pm|w}\ket\pm$ its coordinates along the diagonals. With $w = (0.75, 0.5)$: $\braket{+|w} = 1.25/\sqrt2$ and $\braket{-|w} = 0.25/\sqrt2$, so $P_+w = (0.625, 0.625)$ and $P_-w = (0.125, -0.125)$, which add to $(0.75, 0.5)$ ✓. The two rectangles share the diagonal $w$: a resolution of the identity is a way of writing every vector as a sum of perpendicular pieces, one per basis vector.

The trick everybody uses. Inserting $I = \sum\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}$ between two factors is how a basis change is written in Dirac notation: $\braket{u|v} = \sum_i\braket{u|e_i}\braket{e_i|v}$ is Parseval again, and $\bra u A\ket v = \sum_{i,j}\braket{u|e_i}\bra{e_i}A\ket{e_j}\braket{e_j|v}$ is matrix multiplication.

The adjoint

the adjoint

For a linear $A: V \to W$, the unique linear $A^\dagger: W \to V$ with

$$\braket{u|Av} = \braket{A^\dagger u|v}\quad\text{for all } u \in W,\ v \in V.$$

Exists, uniquely: for fixed $u$, $v \mapsto \braket{u|Av}$ is in $V^*$, so by Riesz it is $\braket{w|\cdot}$ for exactly one $w =: A^\dagger u$.

in an orthonormal basis: the conjugate transpose $$(A^\dagger)_{ij} = \overline{A_{ji}}.$$
rules $$\begin{aligned}(AB)^\dagger &= B^\dagger A^\dagger, & (cA)^\dagger &= \bar c A^\dagger,\\ A^{\dagger\dagger} &= A, & \big(\ket u\!\bra v\big)^\dagger &= \ket v\!\bra u .\end{aligned}$$

In Dirac notation: the bra of $A\ket v$ is $\bra v A^\dagger$, and $\bra u A\ket v$ means $\braket{u|Av}$ — read it either way.

orthonormal only

In a basis that is not orthonormal, the matrix of $A^\dagger$ is not the conjugate transpose of the matrix of $A$. Problem P6 computes the correction.

Full proof that $A^\dagger$ is linear. For all $v$: $\braket{A^\dagger(au + bu')|v} = \braket{au + bu'|Av} = \bar a\braket{u|Av} + \bar b\braket{u'|Av} = \bar a\braket{A^\dagger u|v} + \bar b\braket{A^\dagger u'|v} = \braket{aA^\dagger u + bA^\dagger u'|v}$. Two vectors with the same inner product against every $v$ are equal (take $v$ their difference). The conjugate-linearity of the first slot is used twice and cancels.

The matrix. $(A^\dagger)_{ij} = \braket{e_i|A^\dagger e_j} = \overline{\braket{A^\dagger e_j|e_i}} = \overline{\braket{e_j|Ae_i}} = \overline{A_{ji}}$, using conjugate symmetry and the defining identity. The step $A_{ji} = \braket{e_j|Ae_i}$ is the expansion coefficient, which needs the basis orthonormal.

The rules. $\braket{u|ABv} = \braket{A^\dagger u|Bv} = \braket{B^\dagger A^\dagger u|v}$. $\braket{u|cAv} = c\braket{A^\dagger u|v} = \braket{\bar cA^\dagger u|v}$. $\braket{u|A^\dagger v} = \overline{\braket{A^\dagger v|u}} = \overline{\braket{v|Au}} = \braket{Au|v}$, so $(A^\dagger)^\dagger = A$. For the outer product: $\braket{x|\,\ket u\!\bra v\,w} = \braket{v|w}\braket{x|u}$, and $\braket{\,\ket v\!\bra u\,x|w} = \braket{\braket{u|x}v|w} = \overline{\braket{u|x}}\braket{v|w} = \braket{x|u}\braket{v|w}$. Equal.

Notation. Mathematicians write $A^*$; physicists and this course write $A^\dagger$, "A dagger".

Polarisation: where the field matters

the complex polarisation identity

For any operator $A$ on a complex inner-product space,

$$\braket{u|Av} = \frac14\sum_{k=0}^{3} i^{-k}\,\big\langle u + i^kv\,\big|\,A(u + i^kv)\big\rangle .$$

The quadratic form $w\mapsto\braket{w|Aw}$ determines every $\braket{u|Av}$.

consequence

Over $\mathbb C$: if $\braket{w|Aw} = 0$ for all $w$, then $A = 0$. (Take $u = Av$: $\lVert Av\rVert^2 = 0$.)

full proof in the notes (N)

The quarter-turn $J$ on $\mathbb R^2$: $\braket{v|Jv} = 0$ for every real $v$, yet $J \ne 0$.

false over R

Real polarisation recovers only the symmetric part of $\braket{u|Av}$. Over $\mathbb C$ the same $J$ is visible: $w = (1, i)$ gives $\braket{w|Jw} = -2i \ne 0$.

Full proof of the identity. Expand, using linearity in the second slot and $\bra{i^kv} = \overline{i^k}\bra v = i^{-k}\bra v$: $\braket{u + i^kv|A(u + i^kv)} = \braket{u|Au} + i^k\braket{u|Av} + i^{-k}\braket{v|Au} + \braket{v|Av}$. Multiply by $i^{-k}$ and sum over $k = 0, 1, 2, 3$. The terms $\braket{u|Au}$ and $\braket{v|Av}$ carry $\sum_ki^{-k} = 1 - i - 1 + i = 0$; the term $\braket{v|Au}$ carries $\sum_ki^{-2k} = 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 = 0$; the term $\braket{u|Av}$ carries $\sum_k 1 = 4$. Divide by 4. (Checked numerically on a random $3\times3$ complex matrix, to rounding.)

The consequence. If the form vanishes, the right side vanishes for all $u, v$, so $\braket{u|Av} = 0$; with $u = Av$ this is $\lVert Av\rVert^2 = 0$ for every $v$.

Why it fails over $\mathbb R$. Over $\mathbb R$ only $k = 0, 2$ are available, and $\braket{u + v|A(u + v)} - \braket{u - v|A(u - v)} = 2(\braket{u|Av} + \braket{v|Au})$: the form sees only the symmetric part $\tfrac12(A + A^{\mathsf T})$. The quarter-turn is antisymmetric, so its form is identically zero: $Jv$ is $v$ turned by $90^\circ$, always perpendicular to $v$. Over $\mathbb C$, $w = (1, i)$ has $Jw = (-i, 1)$ and $\braket{w|Jw} = \bar1(-i) + \bar i\cdot1 = -i - i = -2i$.

What it is for. Three uses in this unit and the next: unitary means norm-preserving (section 4); $\braket{v|Av}$ real for all $v$ forces $A$ Hermitian (problem P5); and in unit 01, a measurement's expectation values $\braket{\psi|A\psi}$ over all states determine the operator $A$.

Unitary means norm-preserving

three equivalent conditions

For $U$ on a complex inner-product space, these are equivalent:

  1. $U^\dagger U = I$;
  2. $\braket{Uu|Uv} = \braket{u|v}$ for all $u, v$;
  3. $\lVert Uv\rVert = \lVert v\rVert$ for all $v$.
proof

(1)⇒(2): $\braket{Uu|Uv} = \braket{u|U^\dagger Uv}$. (2)⇒(3): take $u = v$. (3)⇒(1): $\braket{w|(U^\dagger U - I)w} = \lVert Uw\rVert^2 - \lVert w\rVert^2 = 0$ for all $w$; by polarisation, $U^\dagger U - I = 0$.

So a unitary maps orthonormal bases to orthonormal bases, and preserves every overlap — in particular unbiasedness: $|\braket{Ue_i|Uf_j}|^2 = |\braket{e_i|f_j}|^2$.

a unitary that turns $x$ into $y$

$U = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}$: $\ U\ket0 = \ket0$, $U\ket1 = i\ket1$, so

$$U\ket\pm = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 \pm i\ket1) = \ket{\pm i}.$$

It fixes $z$ up to phases and carries $x$ onto $y$: the pair $(z, x)$ becomes $(z, y)$, unbiased as before ✓.

$U$ adds $\tfrac\pi2$ to every relative phase, on the circle of Global phase and relative phase.

Where polarisation was needed. Step (3)⇒(1) is the complex polarisation consequence of the previous section applied to $A = U^\dagger U - I$. Schur: every operator is triangular in some orthonormal basis

eigenvalues exist over C

Every operator $A$ on a complex space of dimension $n \ge 1$ has an eigenvalue: $\det(A - \lambda I)$ is a polynomial of degree $n$, and has a complex root by the fundamental theorem of algebra (cited: proved in any complex analysis course, e.g. by Liouville's theorem).

Over $\mathbb R$ it fails: the quarter-turn has $\det(J - \lambda I) = \lambda^2 + 1$, with no real root. No line in the plane is turned into itself.

Schur triangularisation

For every operator $A$ on a finite-dimensional complex inner-product space there is an orthonormal basis in which its matrix is upper triangular: $A = UTU^\dagger$, $U$ unitary, $T$ upper triangular.

proof, by induction on $n$

$n = 1$: nothing to do. Take a unit eigenvector $e_1$, $Ae_1 = \lambda e_1$, and $W = e_1^\perp$. Let $A' = P_WA|_W$, with $P_W$ the orthogonal projector onto $W$. By induction $W$ has an orthonormal basis $e_2, \dots, e_n$ making $A'$ triangular. Then $Ae_j \in \mathrm{span}(e_1, \dots, e_j)$ for every $j$.

The induction, for $n = 4$: each step settles one column, and the block still to be done shrinks by one.

full proof in the notes (N)

Full proof. Induction on $n = \dim V$. For $n = 1$ every matrix is triangular. For $n \gt 1$: $A$ has an eigenvalue $\lambda$ and an eigenvector, which we normalise to $e_1$. Let $W = e_1^\perp$, of dimension $n - 1$, and $P_W = I - \ket{e_1}\!\bra{e_1}$ the orthogonal projector onto it. The map $A' = P_WA|_W : W \to W$ is an operator on an $(n-1)$-dimensional complex inner-product space, so by induction $W$ has an orthonormal basis $e_2, \dots, e_n$ with $A'e_j \in \mathrm{span}(e_2, \dots, e_j)$ for each $j \ge 2$. Now for $j \ge 2$, $Ae_j = \ket{e_1}\!\braket{e_1|Ae_j} + P_WAe_j = \braket{e_1|Ae_j}\,e_1 + A'e_j \in \mathrm{span}(e_1, \dots, e_j)$, and $Ae_1 = \lambda e_1$. So the matrix of $A$ in $e_1, \dots, e_n$ — an orthonormal basis, since $e_1 \perp W$ — has zeros below the diagonal. Let $U$ be the unitary whose columns are the $e_j$; then $U^\dagger AU = T$.

The figure. Column 1 of the matrix of $A$ in $e_1, \dots, e_n$ is $Ae_1 = \lambda_1e_1$: $\lambda_1$ over zeros. Row 1 holds the numbers $\braket{e_1|Ae_j}$, which the proof never needs to control. The block below and right of $\lambda_1$ is the matrix of $A' = P_WA|_W$, and the induction applies the same step to it: an eigenvector $e_2$ of $A'$ settles column 2 (its top entry is again uncontrolled), and so on. Each $e_j$ found is an eigenvector of the current block, not necessarily of $A$ — only $e_1$ is sure to be — but every $\lambda_j$ is an eigenvalue of $A$, as the note on the diagonal of $T$ below shows.

Check on a $2\times2$. $B = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ is lower triangular. Its only eigenvalue is $0$, with unit eigenvector $e_1 = \ket1$; then $W = \mathrm{span}(\ket0)$ and $e_2 = \ket0$. In the basis $e_1, e_2$: $Be_1 = 0$ and $Be_2 = \ket1 = e_1$, so $T = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, upper triangular with the eigenvalue $0$ twice on its diagonal ✓.

The diagonal of $T$ is the list of eigenvalues of $A$ with algebraic multiplicity, since $\det(A - \lambda I) = \det(T - \lambda I) = \prod_j(T_{jj} - \lambda)$.

Where the field is used. Exactly once: the existence of an eigenvalue. Everything else — orthogonal complements, projecting, induction — works over $\mathbb R$. And over $\mathbb R$ the theorem is false: a triangular real $2\times2$ matrix has its eigenvalues on the diagonal, and $J$ has none.

Fundamental theorem of algebra, cited. Every non-constant complex polynomial has a complex root. Proofs: Liouville's theorem in complex analysis; a winding-number argument; or the elementary route that $|p|$ attains a minimum on $\mathbb C$ and, by d'Alembert's lemma, a minimum value other than zero could be decreased. It is the only analysis this unit imports.

Triangular and normal means diagonal

lemma

An upper-triangular matrix $T$ with $T^\dagger T = TT^\dagger$ is diagonal.

proof

Compare the $(1,1)$ entries. Since column 1 of $T$ is $(t_{11}, 0, \dots, 0)$,

$$(T^\dagger T)_{11} = \sum_i|t_{i1}|^2 = |t_{11}|^2,$$ $$(TT^\dagger)_{11} = \sum_j|t_{1j}|^2 = |t_{11}|^2 + \sum_{j\gt1}|t_{1j}|^2 .$$

Equal, so $t_{1j} = 0$ for $j \gt 1$. Then $T = (t_{11}) \oplus T'$ with $T'$ upper triangular and normal: induct.

Normality says column 1 and row 1 have the same length. Column 1 is just $t_{11}$, so the rest of row 1 must vanish.

Full proof. Induction on the size $n$. For $n = 1$ there is nothing to prove. For $n \gt 1$, the $(1,1)$ entry of $T^\dagger T$ is $\sum_i\overline{t_{i1}}t_{i1}$, the squared length of column 1; that of $TT^\dagger$ is $\sum_jt_{1j}\overline{t_{1j}}$, the squared length of row 1. Column 1 of an upper-triangular matrix is zero below the diagonal, so the first is $|t_{11}|^2$. Equality forces every other entry of row 1 to vanish. Now $T$ is block diagonal, $T = \begin{pmatrix}t_{11} & 0\\ 0 & T'\end{pmatrix}$, and $T^\dagger T = TT^\dagger$ holds block by block, so $T'$ is upper triangular and normal of size $n - 1$, hence diagonal by induction.

Check on a non-normal matrix. $N = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$ is upper triangular, not diagonal: its column 1 has squared length $1$ and its row 1 has $1 + 1 = 2$. That mismatch is precisely the $(1,1)$ entries of $N^\dagger N$ and $NN^\dagger$ on the slide Hermitian, unitary, normal, $1$ and $2$.

The idea in a sentence. In an upper-triangular matrix the first column is "short" and the first row is "long"; normality says they have equal length; so the first row is short too, and the argument peels off one row and column at a time.

The spectral theorem for normal operators

the spectral theorem

An operator $A$ on a finite-dimensional complex inner-product space is normal if and only if $V$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $A$: $\ A = UDU^\dagger$ with $U$ unitary and $D$ diagonal.

proof

⇒ Schur gives $T = U^\dagger AU$ upper triangular. $T$ is normal: $T^\dagger T = U^\dagger A^\dagger AU = U^\dagger AA^\dagger U = TT^\dagger$. By the lemma $T$ is diagonal.
⇐ $A^\dagger A = U\bar DDU^\dagger = UD\bar DU^\dagger = AA^\dagger$: diagonal matrices commute.

spectral decomposition

Let $\sigma(A)$, the spectrum, be the set of eigenvalues, and $P_\lambda$ the orthogonal projector onto the eigenspace $\ker(A - \lambda I)$. Then

$$A = \sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\lambda\,P_\lambda,$$

with $\sum_\lambda P_\lambda = I$ and $P_\lambda P_\mu = \delta_{\lambda\mu}P_\lambda$.

The $P_\lambda$ are determined by $A$, so the decomposition is unique.

From the diagonal form to the decomposition. Let $e_1, \dots, e_n$ be the orthonormal eigenbasis, $Ae_i = \lambda_ie_i$. Then $A = \sum_i\lambda_i\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}$ (apply both sides to each $e_j$). Group the terms by eigenvalue: $P_\lambda := \sum_{i:\lambda_i = \lambda}\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}$. Each $P_\lambda$ is the orthogonal projector onto $\mathrm{span}\{e_i : \lambda_i = \lambda\}$, which is exactly $\ker(A - \lambda I)$: a vector $v = \sum c_ie_i$ has $(A - \lambda)v = \sum c_i(\lambda_i - \lambda)e_i$, zero iff $c_i = 0$ whenever $\lambda_i \ne \lambda$. The relations $\sum P_\lambda = I$ (resolution of the identity) and $P_\lambda P_\mu = \delta_{\lambda\mu}P_\lambda$ (orthonormality) follow.

Uniqueness. The set $\sigma(A)$ and the subspaces $\ker(A - \lambda I)$ are defined from $A$ alone, and an orthogonal projector is determined by its image. So any two spectral decompositions of $A$ agree, even though the eigenbasis is not unique when an eigenvalue repeats (any orthonormal basis of the eigenspace will do) — which is why the decomposition is stated with $P_\lambda$, not with basis vectors.

Compare the real theorem. The learner knows the real spectral theorem: a real symmetric matrix has an orthonormal eigenbasis. The complex theorem is wider in two ways: the field is $\mathbb C$, and the hypothesis is normality, not symmetry. Over $\mathbb R$, normality is not enough: $J$ is normal and has no real eigenvector at all.

What the spectrum says about the operator

corollaries, for $A$ normal
  1. $A$ is Hermitian iff $\sigma(A) \subset \mathbb R$.
  2. $A$ is unitary iff $\sigma(A) \subset \{|\lambda| = 1\}$.
  3. Eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.
  4. $Av = \lambda v$ iff $A^\dagger v = \bar\lambda v$.
proof

$A^\dagger = \sum\bar\lambda P_\lambda$ and $A^\dagger A = \sum|\lambda|^2P_\lambda$, so (1): $A^\dagger = A$ iff every $\bar\lambda = \lambda$; (2): $A^\dagger A = I$ iff every $|\lambda|^2 = 1$; (4): both sides say $v \in \mathrm{im}\,P_\lambda$. (3): distinct eigenspaces are images of $P_\lambda, P_\mu$ with $P_\lambda P_\mu = 0$.

Without the spectral theorem, half of (1) and (2) is one line, and worth knowing: if $Av = \lambda v$ with $\lVert v\rVert = 1$,

$$\lambda = \braket{v|Av} = \braket{Av|v} = \bar\lambda\quad (A \text{ Hermitian}),$$ $$|\lambda| = \lVert Av\rVert = \lVert v\rVert = 1\quad (A \text{ unitary}).$$

Full proofs. From $A = \sum\lambda P_\lambda$ with each $P_\lambda$ Hermitian: $A^\dagger = \sum\bar\lambda P_\lambda$ by the adjoint rules. Products: $A^\dagger A = \sum_{\lambda,\mu}\bar\lambda\mu P_\lambda P_\mu = \sum_\lambda|\lambda|^2P_\lambda$. Two operators $\sum a_\lambda P_\lambda$ and $\sum b_\lambda P_\lambda$ are equal iff $a_\lambda = b_\lambda$ for every $\lambda$ (apply both to a vector in the image of $P_\lambda$). That gives (1) and (2), using $I = \sum P_\lambda$. For (4), $\ker(A - \lambda)$ is the image of $P_\lambda$, and $A^\dagger - \bar\lambda = \sum_\mu(\bar\mu - \bar\lambda)P_\mu$ has the same kernel. For (3): if $u \in \mathrm{im}P_\lambda$ and $v \in \mathrm{im}P_\mu$ then $\braket{u|v} = \braket{P_\lambda u|P_\mu v} = \braket{u|P_\lambda P_\mu v} = 0$.

Where this is going. Unit 01 takes "Hermitian" as the mathematical form of a measurable quantity: the spectrum, being real, is the list of possible values, and the eigenprojectors are the measurement. "Unitary" becomes the form of a reversible evolution. The corollaries above are why those choices are coherent.

Commuting normal operators diagonalise together

simultaneous diagonalisation

If $A$ and $B$ are normal and $AB = BA$, there is one orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of both. Conversely, operators diagonal in a common basis commute.

proof

$B$ maps each eigenspace $E_\lambda$ of $A$ into itself: if $Av = \lambda v$ then $A(Bv) = BAv = \lambda Bv$. The $E_\lambda$ are orthogonal and fill $V$, so in a basis adapted to them $B$ is block diagonal. Each block is normal; diagonalise each.

Rechoosing the basis inside $E_\lambda$ cannot spoil $A$, which is $\lambda I$ there.

full proof in the notes (N)

The commutator $[A, B] := AB - BA$ measures the failure. On the running example:

$$[X, Y] = iZ - (-iZ) = 2iZ \ne 0 .$$
unbiased bases share nothing

$X$ and $Y$ have no common eigenvector at all: every eigenvector of $X$ is a multiple of $\ket\pm$, every one of $Y$ a multiple of $\ket{\pm i}$, and these have overlap $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ in modulus — never parallel. Mutually unbiased bases are as far from a common eigenbasis as two bases can be.

Full proof. By the spectral theorem for $A$, $V = \bigoplus_\lambda E_\lambda$ with the eigenspaces $E_\lambda = \ker(A - \lambda)$ mutually orthogonal. $B$ maps each $E_\lambda$ into itself (slide). Choose an orthonormal basis of each $E_\lambda$ and put them together: in that basis the matrix of $B$ is block diagonal, one block per $\lambda$, because $B$ sends basis vectors of $E_\lambda$ to combinations of basis vectors of $E_\lambda$. The matrix of $B^\dagger$, being its conjugate transpose (the basis is orthonormal), is block diagonal with the conjugate-transposed blocks, and $B^\dagger B = BB^\dagger$ holds block by block. So each block $B_\lambda$ is a normal operator on $E_\lambda$; by the spectral theorem $E_\lambda$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of $B_\lambda$. Every vector of $E_\lambda$ is an eigenvector of $A$, so these are common eigenvectors. Their union over $\lambda$ is the basis.

The figure is the case $\dim V = 3$ with one eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A$ repeated twice and one $\mu$ once. The zeros outside the blocks are $\braket{e_i|Be_j}$ with $e_i, e_j$ in different eigenspaces: $Be_j$ stays in $e_j$'s eigenspace, which is orthogonal to $e_i$'s. When every eigenvalue of $A$ is simple, every block is $1\times1$ and $A$'s own eigenbasis already diagonalises $B$.

Converse. If $A = UD_1U^\dagger$ and $B = UD_2U^\dagger$ then $AB = UD_1D_2U^\dagger = UD_2D_1U^\dagger = BA$.

Normality is needed. Problem P9 shows two commuting matrices that are both diagonalisable, share an eigenbasis, and still have no common orthonormal eigenbasis.

The no-common-eigenvector claim. $X$'s eigenspaces are the lines through $\ket+$ and $\ket-$, $Y$'s the lines through $\ket{\pm i}$. A common eigenvector would lie on one line of each, so two of these unit vectors would be parallel, with overlap of modulus 1. Every overlap is $1/\sqrt2$. In unit 01 this becomes: no state gives a sure outcome for both the $x$ and $y$ magnets — and Robertson's uncertainty relation quantifies it via the commutator.

00 · functional calculus

Functional calculus

the functional calculus

For normal $A = \sum_\lambda\lambda P_\lambda$ and any function $f: \sigma(A) \to \mathbb C$,

$$f(A) := \sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)} f(\lambda)\,P_\lambda .$$

Well defined: the decomposition is unique.

properties

$(f + g)(A) = f(A) + g(A)$, $\ (fg)(A) = f(A)\,g(A)$, $\ f(A)^\dagger = \bar f(A)$; $f(A)$ is normal, with spectrum $f(\sigma(A))$.

Proof: each is one line from $P_\lambda P_\mu = \delta_{\lambda\mu}P_\lambda$ and $P_\lambda^\dagger = P_\lambda$.

it agrees with polynomials

With $f(t) = t^n$: $A^n = \sum\lambda^nP_\lambda$. So for every polynomial $p$, "$p(A)$ by powers" and "$p(A)$ by the calculus" are the same operator.

checks on $X = P_+ - P_-$

$X^2 = (1)^2P_+ + (-1)^2P_- = I$ ✓ (and directly, $X$ swaps twice). $\ |X| := \sum|\lambda|P_\lambda = I$. $\ \tfrac12(I + X) = P_+$: the projector is itself a function of $X$.

Move the points of $\sigma(X)$ by $f$ and keep the projectors. Two points landing together add their projectors.

Full proofs of the properties. Sum: $\sum(f + g)(\lambda)P_\lambda = \sum f(\lambda)P_\lambda + \sum g(\lambda)P_\lambda$. Product: $f(A)g(A) = \sum_{\lambda,\mu}f(\lambda)g(\mu)P_\lambda P_\mu = \sum_\lambda f(\lambda)g(\lambda)P_\lambda$. Adjoint: $(\sum f(\lambda)P_\lambda)^\dagger = \sum\overline{f(\lambda)}P_\lambda$. Normal: $f(A)$ and $f(A)^\dagger$ are both functions of $A$, and the product rule shows any two functions of $A$ commute. Spectrum: $f(A)$ acts as $f(\lambda)$ on the image of $P_\lambda$, so its eigenvalues are the values $f(\lambda)$ (several $\lambda$ may share a value, in which case their projectors add).

Powers by induction. $A^0 = I = \sum P_\lambda$, and if $A^n = \sum\lambda^nP_\lambda$ then $A^{n+1} = A^nA = \sum\lambda^{n+1}P_\lambda$ by the product rule. Linear combinations then give every polynomial.

Projectors are polynomials in $A$. For each $\lambda \in \sigma(A)$, the Lagrange polynomial $\ell_\lambda(t) = \prod_{\mu\ne\lambda}\frac{t - \mu}{\lambda - \mu}$ is 1 at $\lambda$ and 0 at the other eigenvalues, so $\ell_\lambda(A) = P_\lambda$. Hence every $f(A)$ is a polynomial in $A$ — the polynomial interpolating $f$ on the spectrum. That is the third route in problem P10, via Cayley–Hamilton. The check $\tfrac12(I + X) = P_+$ is the case $\ell_{+1}(t) = \tfrac{t + 1}{2}$.

Why "normal" is required. The definition needs orthogonal spectral projectors that sum to $I$. Functions of non-normal matrices exist too (via power series, or via Jordan form), but are not needed in this course, except the series exponential on the next slides.

The exponential, and why exp(iH) is unitary

the matrix exponential $$e^A := \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{A^n}{n!},$$

convergent for every operator $A$. For normal $A$, the partial sums are $\sum_\lambda\big(\sum_{n\le N}\lambda^n/n!\big)P_\lambda$, so

$$e^A = \sum_\lambda e^\lambda\,P_\lambda :$$

series and functional calculus agree.

Dimension one: $e^{i\varphi} = \sum(i\varphi)^n/n!$ splits into even terms, $\cos\varphi$, and odd terms, $i\sin\varphi$ — the definition of section 2 ✓.

exponentials of Hermitian operators

If $H$ is Hermitian then $e^{iH}$ is unitary, with

$$\big(e^{iH}\big)^\dagger = e^{-iH}, \qquad e^{isH}e^{itH} = e^{i(s + t)H}.$$
proof

$e^{iH} = \sum e^{i\lambda}P_\lambda$ with every $\lambda$ real, so every $|e^{i\lambda}| = 1$: unitary by the corollary. Adjoint: conjugate each coefficient. Product: $e^{is\lambda}e^{it\lambda} = e^{i(s + t)\lambda}$ and the product rule.

Check: $t = 0$ gives $I$. Unit 01 proves the converse — every continuous family of this kind is $e^{-itH}$ — and calls $H$ the Hamiltonian.

Convergence. Use the operator norm $\lVert A\rVert = \max_{\lVert v\rVert = 1}\lVert Av\rVert$, which satisfies $\lVert AB\rVert \le \lVert A\rVert\lVert B\rVert$, so $\lVert A^n/n!\rVert \le \lVert A\rVert^n/n!$. The real series $\sum\lVert A\rVert^n/n!$ converges (to $e^{\lVert A\rVert}$), so the partial sums are Cauchy in the operator norm, and the finite-dimensional space of operators is complete. Each matrix entry converges absolutely.

Agreement. By the previous slide, $\sum_{n\le N}A^n/n! = \sum_\lambda s_N(\lambda)P_\lambda$ with $s_N(\lambda) = \sum_{n\le N}\lambda^n/n!$. As $N \to \infty$, $s_N(\lambda) \to e^\lambda$ for each of the finitely many $\lambda$, so the sum converges to $\sum e^\lambda P_\lambda$.

The scalar series. $(i\varphi)^{2m} = (-1)^m\varphi^{2m}$ and $(i\varphi)^{2m+1} = i(-1)^m\varphi^{2m+1}$, so the series is $\sum(-1)^m\varphi^{2m}/(2m)! + i\sum(-1)^m\varphi^{2m+1}/(2m+1)! = \cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi$, the Taylor series of cosine and sine. (Rearranging into even and odd parts is legitimate: the series converges absolutely.) So the notation $e^{i\varphi}$ introduced on section 2's first slide is the exponential of $i\varphi$.

Group property. $s \mapsto e^{isH}$ is a homomorphism from $(\mathbb R, +)$ to the unitary group, continuous in $s$. Unit 01 proves Stone's theorem in finite dimension: every continuous one-parameter group of unitaries has this form, for exactly one Hermitian $H$. That is why the Schrödinger equation will not need to be a separate postulate.

00 · functional calculus · example

Worked: exp(iθX) by the spectral calculus

$e^{i\theta X}$ for real $\theta$, with $X = P_+ - P_-$

1. Spectral form. $\sigma(X) = \{+1, -1\}$, so

$$e^{i\theta X} = e^{i\theta}P_+ + e^{-i\theta}P_- .$$

2. Expand the phases. $e^{\pm i\theta} = \cos\theta \pm i\sin\theta$:

$$e^{i\theta X} = \cos\theta\,(P_+ + P_-) + i\sin\theta\,(P_+ - P_-).$$

3. Recognise $P_+ + P_- = I$ and $P_+ - P_- = X$:

$$\boxed{\,e^{i\theta X} = \cos\theta\,I + i\sin\theta\,X\,}$$ $$= \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & i\sin\theta\\ i\sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.$$

Unitary, checked. Each column has squared norm $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$; their inner product is $\cos\theta\cdot i\sin\theta + \overline{i\sin\theta}\cos\theta = 0$ ✓.

Known cases. $\theta = 0$: $I$ ✓. $\ \theta = \tfrac\pi2$: $iX$, which is $X$ up to global phase ✓. $\ \theta = \pi$: $-I$.

By the series, as a cross-check. $X^2 = I$, so even powers are $I$ and odd powers are $X$:

$$\sum\frac{(i\theta)^n}{n!}X^n = \Big(\sum_{\text{even}}\tfrac{(i\theta)^n}{n!}\Big)I + \Big(\sum_{\text{odd}}\tfrac{(i\theta)^n}{n!}\Big)X,$$

which is $\cos\theta\,I + i\sin\theta\,X$ again ✓.

The general pattern. Any Hermitian $H$ with $H^2 = I$ — eigenvalues $\pm1$ — has $e^{i\theta H} = \cos\theta\,I + i\sin\theta\,H$ by the same three lines. $Y$ and $Z$ qualify: $e^{i\theta Z} = \mathrm{diag}(e^{i\theta}, e^{-i\theta})$, which is the formula with $Z$ diagonal.

The column inner product, in full. With the course convention, $\braket{c_1|c_2} = \overline{\cos\theta}\cdot i\sin\theta + \overline{i\sin\theta}\cdot\cos\theta = i\sin\theta\cos\theta - i\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0$.

The case $\theta = \pi$. $e^{i\pi X} = -I$: going "once round" in the exponent of a matrix with eigenvalues $\pm1$ returns to the identity only up to the global phase $-1$. Unit 02 makes this its central surprise — a rotation by $2\pi$ multiplies a state by $-1$ — and the sign here is the same sign.

Three ways. Problem P10 does this a third way, by Cayley–Hamilton, and asks why all three must agree.

Exponentials do not add

when they do

If $AB = BA$ then $e^Ae^B = e^{A + B}$.

Proof: multiply the series; the binomial theorem $(A + B)^n = \sum\binom nkA^kB^{n-k}$ holds because $A$ and $B$ commute.

when they do not

$A = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\ B = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$: $\ A^2 = B^2 = 0$, so the series stop and

$$e^Ae^B = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

But $A + B = X$, and by the calculus

$$e^{X} = eP_+ + e^{-1}P_- = \cosh1\,I + \sinh1\,X$$ $$= \begin{pmatrix}1.5431&1.1752\\1.1752&1.5431\end{pmatrix}.$$

And $e^Be^A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$: three different matrices. The traces are $3$, $3$ and $2\cosh1 = 3.0862$.

check

All three have determinant $1$, as they must: $\det e^M = e^{\operatorname{tr}M}$ (diagonalise, or use Schur) and every exponent here has trace $0$. $\cosh^21 - \sinh^21 = 1$ ✓.

Full proof of the commuting case. $e^Ae^B = \sum_{j,k}\frac{A^jB^k}{j!\,k!}$, a product of absolutely convergent series, which may be rearranged by total degree $n = j + k$: $\sum_n\frac1{n!}\sum_{k}\binom nkA^{n-k}B^k = \sum_n\frac{(A + B)^n}{n!}$. The binomial expansion of $(A + B)^n$ needs $AB = BA$: in general $(A + B)^2 = A^2 + AB + BA + B^2$. For normal commuting $A, B$ there is a second proof: diagonalise both in one basis (previous section), where the statement is $e^ae^b = e^{a + b}$ for numbers.

Check in a known case. Every $M$ commutes with itself, and the theorem says $e^Me^M = e^{2M}$; for $M = i\theta X$ this is $(\cos\theta\,I + i\sin\theta\,X)^2 = \cos2\theta\,I + i\sin2\theta\,X$, the double-angle formulae with $X^2 = I$ ✓.

The counterexample, every step. $e^A = I + A$ since $A^2 = 0$. The product $(I + A)(I + B) = I + A + B + AB$, and $AB = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$, giving $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Likewise $BA = \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$. $A + B = X$, with $e^X = e^{+1}P_+ + e^{-1}P_- = \tfrac{e + e^{-1}}2I + \tfrac{e - e^{-1}}2X$, and $\cosh1 = 1.54308$, $\sinh1 = 1.17520$.

$\det e^M = e^{\operatorname{tr}M}$. By Schur, $M = UTU^\dagger$ with $T$ triangular; then $e^M = Ue^TU^\dagger$ (each power conjugates), $e^T$ is triangular with diagonal entries $e^{t_{jj}}$, so $\det e^M = \prod e^{t_{jj}} = e^{\sum t_{jj}} = e^{\operatorname{tr}M}$.

Why this matters. Quantum circuits multiply exponentials of non-commuting Hermitian operators all the time; that $e^{iA}e^{iB} \ne e^{i(A + B)}$ is the reason simulating a sum of interactions takes many small steps (unit 19, Trotter). Problem P11 measures the gap for $e^{i\theta X}e^{i\theta Z}$, Hermitian exponents this time.

The turn walks round a circle

$R(\theta)$ is a function of $X$, so it keeps each eigenvector of $X$ and multiplies it by a phase: $R(\theta)\ket\pm = \big(\cos\tfrac\theta2 \mp i\sin\tfrac\theta2\big)\ket\pm = e^{\mp i\theta/2}\ket\pm$. With $\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket+ + \ket-)$,

$$R(\theta)\ket0 = e^{-i\theta/2}\,\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket+ + e^{i\theta}\ket-\big),$$

normalised: $\tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1$; at $\theta = 0$ it is $\ket0$ ✓. Up to global phase, a state with relative phase $\theta$ between $\ket+$ and $\ket-$: its $x$ probabilities stay $\tfrac12, \tfrac12$, and only the phase moves.

at θ = π/2

$R\ket0 = \ket{-i}$ and $R\ket1 = -i\ket{+i}$: the $z$ basis carried onto the $y$ basis, up to global phase in each vector.

The states unbiased to $x$, $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket+ + e^{i\varphi}\ket-)$ up to global phase: the circle of Global phase and relative phase, with $x$ in the place of $z$. $R(\theta)$ turns it by $\theta$, and at $\theta = \pi/2$ the $z$ basis reaches the $y$ basis.

The points of the circle. Write $\chi_\varphi = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket+ + e^{i\varphi}\ket-)$. Then $\chi_0 = \ket0$ and $\chi_\pi = \ket1$, by adding and subtracting $\ket\pm = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 \pm \ket1)$. At $\varphi = \pi/2$: $\chi = \tfrac12\big((1 + i)\ket0 + (1 - i)\ket1\big) = \tfrac{1 + i}{2}(\ket0 - i\ket1) = e^{i\pi/4}\ket{-i}$, using $\tfrac{1 - i}{1 + i} = -i$ and $\tfrac{1 + i}{\sqrt2} = e^{i\pi/4}$. Likewise $\chi_{3\pi/2} = e^{-i\pi/4}\ket{+i}$. So the $z$ basis sits at $0$ and $\pi$, the $y$ basis at $\pi/2$ and $3\pi/2$, each up to global phase.

Every point turns by $\theta$. $R(\theta)\chi_\varphi = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(e^{-i\theta/2}\ket+ + e^{i\varphi}e^{i\theta/2}\ket-\big) = e^{-i\theta/2}\chi_{\varphi + \theta}$. With $\varphi = 0$ this is the display on the slide; with $\varphi = \pi$ it carries $\ket1$ to $\chi_{\pi + \theta}$, which at $\theta = \pi/2$ is $\ket{+i}$ up to phase — matching $R\ket1 = -i\ket{+i}$ ✓.

Why it stays unbiased to $x$. $R(\theta)$ is a function of $X$, so it commutes with $X$ and preserves its eigenvectors: $R\ket\pm = e^{\mp i\theta/2}\ket\pm$, a phase on each. Overlaps with $\ket\pm$ therefore never change, and $\ket0$ starts unbiased to $x$. The puzzle, answered

the question, from the overview

Three magnets, each pair 50/50. In the minimal model: three orthonormal bases of a two-dimensional space, pairwise mutually unbiased.

the answer

Over $\mathbb R$: impossible. Worked: the real plane holds at most two.

Over $\mathbb C$: $z$, $x$, $y$. Worked: three unbiased bases in C² — all twelve overlaps $\tfrac12$.

Where the third basis came from, four times over:

  • the relative phase $\pm\pi/2$ on the circle of states unbiased to $z$;
  • the eigenbasis of the Hermitian $Y$, which the test state nearly is;
  • the complex eigenvectors of the real quarter-turn;
  • the end of a continuous unitary turn from $z$.
read precisely

Complex amplitudes are the price of a third unbiased direction — in the minimal model. That real quantum theory fails, on any space, is the 2021–22 network result, cited. That $\mathbb C^2$ has no fourth is unit 02's.

The running example, closed. Every claim in its hover card has now been derived: the three bases and their twelve overlaps (section 2), the projectors summing to the identity (section 3), the bases as eigenbases of $Z$, $X$, $Y$ and of the quarter-turn (section 4), and the unitary $e^{-i\theta X/2}$ joining $z$ to $y$ (section 5). The test state's numbers $\tfrac9{25}, \tfrac{16}{25}$; $\tfrac12, \tfrac12$; $\tfrac{49}{50}, \tfrac{1}{50}$ were each summed to one on its slide.

What "price" means. A real two-dimensional state has one free parameter (an angle); a complex one, up to global phase, has two (the $z$ probabilities fix one, the relative phase is the other). Three unbiased directions need three independent "fair coin" relations, and the extra parameter is what makes room for the third. Unit 02 makes this count exact: the states of $\mathbb C^2$ up to phase form a sphere, a two-dimensional surface, and the real states are a great circle on it.

The tools, for the rest of the course. More important than the puzzle is what was built to answer it: the inner product with its convention, bras as dual vectors, outer products, the adjoint, polarisation, the spectral theorem for normal operators, simultaneous diagonalisation, and functions of operators. Every later unit uses all of them.
