The postulates, as mathematics: states, measurement, and evolution

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. On paper and without notes: for the Elitzur–Vaidman setup — a Mach–Zehnder interferometer with balanced arms and a live bomb in one arm that explodes if the photon reaches it — you derive the three outcome probabilities, \(\tfrac12\) explodes, \(\tfrac14\) certified untouched (\(D_0\) clicks), \(\tfrac14\) inconclusive (\(D_1\) clicks); and you prove finite-dimensional Stone's theorem: every continuous one-parameter group of unitaries is \(e^{-iHt}\) for exactly one Hermitian \(H\).

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Eleven problems, about four and a half hours. Work them on paper first. P6 and the Stone proof of the deck together are the checkpoint.

Conventions, as in the deck: \(\braket{u|v}\) is conjugate-linear in the first slot; \(\braket{\psi|A|\psi} := \braket{\psi|A\psi}\). The paths of the interferometer are \(\ket0\) (path 0) and \(\ket1\) (path 1); the photon enters in path 0; and

\[ B = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\ i&1\end{pmatrix},\quad P(\varphi) = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&e^{i\varphi}\end{pmatrix},\quad X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}. \]

The detectors measure \(Z\) after the second splitter, \(D_0 \leftrightarrow \ket0\), \(D_1 \leftrightarrow \ket1\); with balanced arms \(B^2 = iX\) and \(D_0\) is dark. \(\hbar = 1\).

Some solutions end with an optional check by the course's referee, qclib, run from the course directory with uv run python. Every solution is complete without it.

States and measurement

P1 Phases you can and cannot see medium

exercises: the Born rule on rays, constructing a distinguishing observable, a computed overlap

Let \(e_1, e_2\) be orthonormal vectors in \(V\), and \(a, b\) nonzero complex numbers with \(|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1\).

  1. Prove that for every observable \(A\) and every real \(\alpha\), the states \(\psi\) and \(e^{i\alpha}\psi\) give the same probability for every result of \(A\), and post-measurement states that differ by the same factor \(e^{i\alpha}\).
  2. Let \(\psi = ae_1 + be_2\) and \(\chi = ae_1 + e^{i\gamma}be_2\), with \(e^{i\gamma} \ne 1\). Construct an observable whose distribution on \(\chi\) differs from its distribution on \(\psi\), and compute the difference.
  3. Show that no observable having both \(e_1\) and \(e_2\) as eigenvectors can tell \(\psi\) from \(\chi\).
  4. In the interferometer, \(\psi = B\ket0\) and \(\chi = P(\varphi)B\ket0\). Evaluate your answer to part 2 at \(\varphi = \pi/3\), and say which physical measurement realises your observable.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is about where the factor \(e^{i\alpha}\) goes in \(\braket{\cdot|\cdot}\). Part 2 asks for a yes/no question that \(\psi\) always passes. Part 3 is about what the probabilities of such an observable can depend on.

Rung 2

The projector \(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\) is an observable with results \(1\) and \(0\), and on a state \(\phi\) it gives \(1\) with probability \(|\braket{\psi|\phi}|^2\). For part 3, an observable with \(e_1, e_2\) as eigenvectors has each spectral projector \(P_\lambda\) satisfying \(P_\lambda e_j \in \{0, e_j\}\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "\(\braket{\psi|\chi} = |a|^2 + e^{i\gamma}|b|^2\), so \(|\braket{\psi|\chi}|^2 = |a|^4 + |b|^4 + 2|a|^2|b|^2\cos\gamma\)."

Worked solution

Idea. A global phase cancels against its own conjugate in every probability. A relative phase changes the overlap with the original state, and the projector onto that state measures the overlap.

1. With \(A = \sum\lambda P_\lambda\), the probability of \(\lambda\) on \(e^{i\alpha}\psi\) is \(\braket{e^{i\alpha}\psi|P_\lambda e^{i\alpha}\psi} = \overline{e^{i\alpha}}e^{i\alpha}\braket{\psi|P_\lambda\psi} = \braket{\psi|P_\lambda\psi}\), since the first slot is conjugate-linear and \(|e^{i\alpha}| = 1\). The post-measurement state is \(P_\lambda(e^{i\alpha}\psi)/\lVert P_\lambda(e^{i\alpha}\psi)\rVert = e^{i\alpha}P_\lambda\psi/\lVert P_\lambda\psi\rVert\). \(\blacksquare\)

2. Take \(Q = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi\), the orthogonal projector onto \(\psi\): Hermitian, \(Q^2 = Q\), results \(1\) and \(0\). On \(\psi\): result 1 with probability \(\braket{\psi|\psi}^2 = 1\). On \(\chi\): with probability \[ |\braket{\psi|\chi}|^2 = \big||a|^2 + e^{i\gamma}|b|^2\big|^2 = |a|^4 + |b|^4 + 2|a|^2|b|^2\cos\gamma = 1 - 2|a|^2|b|^2(1 - \cos\gamma), \] using \((|a|^2 + |b|^2)^2 = 1\). Since \(a, b \ne 0\) and \(\cos\gamma \lt 1\) (as \(e^{i\gamma} \ne 1\)), this is strictly less than one: the distributions differ by \(2|a|^2|b|^2(1 - \cos\gamma) = 4|a|^2|b|^2\sin^2\tfrac\gamma2\) in the probability of result 1.

3. If \(e_1, e_2\) are eigenvectors of \(A\), each spectral projector \(P_\lambda\) sends \(e_j\) to \(e_j\) (if \(e_j\)'s eigenvalue is \(\lambda\)) or to \(0\). Write \(c_j(\lambda) \in \{0, 1\}\) accordingly. Then \(P_\lambda\psi = c_1(\lambda)\,a\,e_1 + c_2(\lambda)\,b\,e_2\), and \(\lVert P_\lambda\psi\rVert^2 = c_1(\lambda)|a|^2 + c_2(\lambda)|b|^2\) by Pythagoras. The same computation for \(\chi\) gives \(c_1(\lambda)|a|^2 + c_2(\lambda)|e^{i\gamma}b|^2\), the same number. Every probability depends only on \(|a|\) and \(|b|\). \(\blacksquare\)

4. \(B\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + i\ket1)\), so \(|a|^2 = |b|^2 = \tfrac12\) and \(\gamma = \varphi\): \(|\braket{\psi|\chi}|^2 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac12\cos\varphi = \cos^2\tfrac\varphi2\). At \(\varphi = \pi/3\): \(\cos^2\tfrac\pi6 = \tfrac34\), so the probability of result 1 drops from \(1\) to \(\tfrac34\), a difference of \(\tfrac14 = 4\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\sin^2\tfrac\pi6\) ✓. The projector onto \(\psi = B\ket0\) is realised by the second splitter followed by \(D_1\): \(B\psi = B^2\ket0 = i\ket1\), so "\(D_1\) clicks" happens with probability \(|\braket{1|B\chi}|^2 = |\braket{B\psi|B\chi}|^2 = |\braket{\psi|\chi}|^2\) (unitaries preserve inner products). That is the deck's \(P(D_1) = \cos^2\tfrac\varphi2\) — the fringes are this overlap.

Check. \(\gamma = 0\) gives probability \(1\): no difference, as it must. \(\gamma = \pi\) with \(|a|^2 = |b|^2 = \tfrac12\) gives \(0\): \(ae_1 + be_2\) and \(ae_1 - be_2\) are then orthogonal, and \(Q\) separates them perfectly. And part 3 is consistent with part 2: \(Q = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi\) does not have \(e_1\) as an eigenvector, since \(Qe_1 = \bar a\psi\) is not a multiple of \(e_1\) when \(b \ne 0\).

P2 Expectation from the measure routine

exercises: the Born rule as a probability measure, the functional calculus, moments of a distribution

Let \(A = \sum_\lambda\lambda P_\lambda\) be an observable and \(\psi\) a state, and let \(p(\lambda) = \braket{\psi|P_\lambda|\psi}\).

  1. Prove that for every function \(f: \sigma(A) \to \mathbb R\), the mean of \(f(\text{result})\) under \(p\) is \(\braket{\psi|f(A)|\psi}\). Deduce \(\langle A\rangle = \braket{\psi|A|\psi}\) and \((\Delta A)^2 = \braket{\psi|A^2|\psi} - \braket{\psi|A|\psi}^2\) without postulating either.
  2. Show that \((\Delta A)^2 = \lVert(A - \langle A\rangle)\psi\rVert^2\), and hence that \(\Delta A = 0\) exactly when \(\psi\) is an eigenvector of \(A\).
  3. For the deck's example \(A = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1)\), \(\psi = \tfrac13(1, 2i, 2)\), compute the third moment \(\sum\lambda^3p(\lambda)\) both ways.
  4. For the photon-number observable \(N_0 = \ket0\!\bra0\) on the interferometer's output, compute \(\langle N_0\rangle\) and \(\Delta N_0\) as functions of \(\varphi\), and find where \(\Delta N_0\) is largest.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything is linearity of \(\braket{\psi|\cdot|\psi}\) plus the definition \(f(A) = \sum f(\lambda)P_\lambda\) from unit 00.

Rung 2

For the variance, use \(f(\lambda) = (\lambda - m)^2\) with \(m = \langle A\rangle\), which is real. For part 4, \(N_0\) is a projector, so its results are \(0\) and \(1\) and its mean is the probability of \(1\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: "\(\sum_\lambda f(\lambda)p(\lambda) = \sum_\lambda f(\lambda)\braket{\psi|P_\lambda|\psi} = \braket{\psi|\textstyle\sum_\lambda f(\lambda)P_\lambda|\psi} = \braket{\psi|f(A)|\psi}\)."

Worked solution

Idea. The map \(f \mapsto f(A)\) turns a function on the spectrum into an operator, and \(\braket{\psi|\cdot|\psi}\) turns it back into a number; the Born rule is what makes the round trip a mean.

1. The rung's line is the whole proof: the inner product is linear in its second slot, and unit 00 defined \(f(A) = \sum f(\lambda)P_\lambda\). With \(f(\lambda) = \lambda\): \(\langle A\rangle = \braket{\psi|A|\psi}\). With \(f(\lambda) = (\lambda - m)^2\), \(m = \langle A\rangle\) (real, since it is a mean of real numbers): \((\Delta A)^2 = \braket{\psi|(A - m)^2|\psi}\), where \(f(A) = (A - m)^2\) because the functional calculus agrees with polynomials. Expanding, \(\braket{\psi|A^2|\psi} - 2m\braket{\psi|A|\psi} + m^2 = \braket{\psi|A^2|\psi} - m^2\).

2. \(A - m\) is Hermitian, so \(\braket{\psi|(A - m)^2\psi} = \braket{(A - m)\psi|(A - m)\psi} = \lVert(A - m)\psi\rVert^2\). This is zero iff \(A\psi = m\psi\). Conversely if \(A\psi = \mu\psi\) then \(m = \braket{\psi|A\psi} = \mu\) and the norm vanishes. \(\blacksquare\)

3. From the measure: \(1^3\cdot\tfrac59 + (-1)^3\cdot\tfrac49 = \tfrac19\). From the operator: \(A^3 = A\) (diagonal entries \(\pm1\)), so \(\braket{\psi|A^3|\psi} = \braket{\psi|A|\psi} = \tfrac19(1 + 4 - 4) = \tfrac19\) ✓.

4. The output state is \(\tfrac12[(1 - e^{i\varphi})\ket0 + i(1 + e^{i\varphi})\ket1]\), so \(\langle N_0\rangle = P(D_0) = \sin^2\tfrac\varphi2\). Since \(N_0^2 = N_0\), \((\Delta N_0)^2 = \langle N_0\rangle - \langle N_0\rangle^2 = \sin^2\tfrac\varphi2\cos^2\tfrac\varphi2 = \tfrac14\sin^2\varphi\), so \(\Delta N_0 = \tfrac12|\sin\varphi|\). It is largest, \(\tfrac12\), at \(\varphi = \pi/2\) and \(3\pi/2\), where the detectors are a fair coin; it is zero at \(\varphi = 0\) and \(\pi\), where one detector is dark and the result is certain.

Check. A two-valued variable with values \(0, 1\) and \(P(1) = p\) has variance \(p(1 - p)\), maximal \(\tfrac14\) at \(p = \tfrac12\) ✓. And \(\Delta N_0 = 0\) at \(\varphi = 0\) agrees with part 2: the output state is then \(i\ket1\), an eigenvector of \(N_0\) with eigenvalue \(0\).

P3 Repeatability routine

exercises: Lüders' rule, idempotence of projectors, what a commuting intermediate measurement preserves

  1. Prove that if measuring \(A\) on \(\psi\) gives \(\lambda\), measuring \(A\) again at once gives \(\lambda\) with probability one.
  2. Let \(B\) commute with \(A\). Prove that "measure \(A\), then \(B\), then \(A\)" gives the same result in the first and third measurements, with probability one.
  3. Show by example that part 2 fails without commutation: on \(\ket0\), measure \(Z\), then \(X\), then \(Z\). Find the probability that the two \(Z\) results differ.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each part is a statement about the post-measurement state: is it still in the image of \(P_\lambda\)?

Rung 2

A vector \(\chi\) lies in the image of \(P_\lambda\) iff \(P_\lambda\chi = \chi\). If \(B\) commutes with \(A\), then \(B\)'s spectral projectors \(Q_b\) commute with \(P_\lambda\) (the deck's Compatible observables).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "After the first two measurements the state is \(\chi = Q_bP_\lambda\psi/\lVert Q_bP_\lambda\psi\rVert\), and \(P_\lambda Q_bP_\lambda = Q_bP_\lambda^2 = Q_bP_\lambda\)."

Worked solution

Idea. A result \(\lambda\) is certain exactly on the image of \(P_\lambda\). Lüders' rule puts the state there; a measurement that commutes with \(A\) keeps it there; one that does not, need not.

1. After the result \(\lambda\), the state is \(\chi = P_\lambda\psi/\lVert P_\lambda\psi\rVert\). Since \(P_\lambda^2 = P_\lambda\), \(P_\lambda\chi = \chi\), so the probability of \(\lambda\) is \(\lVert P_\lambda\chi\rVert^2 = \lVert\chi\rVert^2 = 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

2. After \(A\) gives \(\lambda\) and \(B\) gives \(b\) (with nonzero probability), the state is \(\chi = Q_bP_\lambda\psi/\lVert Q_bP_\lambda\psi\rVert\). The \(Q_b\) commute with \(P_\lambda\) (both are diagonal in a common orthonormal eigenbasis, unit 00), so \(P_\lambda\chi = Q_bP_\lambda^2\psi/\lVert\cdot\rVert = \chi\), and the third measurement gives \(\lambda\) with probability \(\lVert P_\lambda\chi\rVert^2 = 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

3. \(Z\) on \(\ket0\) gives \(+1\) with certainty, leaving \(\ket0\). \(X\) on \(\ket0\): results \(\pm1\) with probability \(|\braket{\pm|0}|^2 = \tfrac12\) each, leaving \(\ket+\) or \(\ket-\). \(Z\) on \(\ket\pm\): \(+1\) or \(-1\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each. So the two \(Z\) results differ with probability \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12\).

Check. \(ZX = -XZ \ne XZ\) (unit 00), so part 2 does not apply, and the answer \(\tfrac12\) is the largest possible for a two-outcome measurement in between: \(\ket\pm\) are unbiased to the \(z\) basis (unit 00). Had the middle measurement been of \(Z\) itself, part 1 gives probability \(0\) of a change.

P4 Commuting observables medium

exercises: simultaneous diagonalisation, a joint distribution and its marginals, why non-commuting observables have none

On \(\mathbb C^3\) let

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad B = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix},\qquad \psi = \tfrac13(2, i, 2). \]
  1. Check that \(A\) and \(B\) are Hermitian, commute, and that \(\psi\) is a state.
  2. Find an orthonormal basis of common eigenvectors, and the projectors \(R_{ab} = P_aQ_b\) for \(a, b \in \{1, -1\}\).
  3. Compute the joint distribution \(p(a, b) = \braket{\psi|R_{ab}|\psi}\), check it sums to one, and check both marginals against \(\braket{\psi|A|\psi}\) and \(\braket{\psi|B|\psi}\).
  4. On \(\mathbb C^2\), try the same construction with \(X\) and \(Z\): show that \(P_+Q_0\) is not a projector, and that "\(Z\) then \(X\)" and "\(X\) then \(Z\)" on \(\ket0\) give different distributions of the pair of results.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 3 is four numbers summing to one, one of which turns out to be zero. Everything else is checking.

Rung 2

\(A\) swaps the first two coordinates and fixes the third; \(B\) is \(+1\) on the first two and \(-1\) on the third. Look for eigenvectors of \(A\) inside the eigenspaces of \(B\). Since \(A^2 = B^2 = I\), the projectors are \(P_a = \tfrac12(I + aA)\) and \(Q_b = \tfrac12(I + bB)\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "Common eigenvectors: \(u = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1, 0)\) with \((a, b) = (1, 1)\); \(v = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, -1, 0)\) with \((-1, 1)\); \(w = (0, 0, 1)\) with \((1, -1)\)."

Worked solution

Idea. Commuting observables share an orthonormal eigenbasis; sorting its vectors by their pair of eigenvalues gives the cells of a joint measurement, and the Born rule on the cells is the joint distribution.

1. Both are real symmetric, so Hermitian. \(AB\) is \(A\) with its third column negated; \(BA\) is \(A\) with its third row negated; both equal \(\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&-1\end{pmatrix}\), so \(AB = BA\). \(\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = \tfrac19(4 + 1 + 4) = 1\) ✓.

2. The rung's vectors are orthonormal, and \(Au = u\), \(Bu = u\); \(Av = -v\), \(Bv = v\); \(Aw = w\), \(Bw = -w\). No vector has \((a, b) = (-1, -1)\). So \(R_{11} = \ket u\!\bra u\), \(R_{-1,1} = \ket v\!\bra v\), \(R_{1,-1} = \ket w\!\bra w\), \(R_{-1,-1} = 0\); they are orthogonal projectors summing to \(I\) (resolution of the identity in the basis \(u, v, w\)). Check one product directly: \(P_1Q_1 = \tfrac14(I + A)(I + B)\) has first two rows \(\tfrac12(1, 1, 0)\) and third row \(0\), which is \(\ket u\!\bra u\) ✓.

3. \(\braket{u|\psi} = \tfrac1{3\sqrt2}(2 + i)\), \(\braket{v|\psi} = \tfrac1{3\sqrt2}(2 - i)\), \(\braket{w|\psi} = \tfrac23\). So

\[ p(1, 1) = \tfrac{5}{18},\quad p(-1, 1) = \tfrac{5}{18},\quad p(1, -1) = \tfrac{8}{18},\quad p(-1, -1) = 0, \]

summing to \(\tfrac{18}{18} = 1\) ✓. Marginals: \(P(A = 1) = \tfrac{13}{18}\), \(P(A = -1) = \tfrac5{18}\), so \(\langle A\rangle = \tfrac8{18} = \tfrac49\); directly, \(A\psi = \tfrac13(i, 2, 2)\) and \(\braket{\psi|A\psi} = \tfrac19(2i - 2i + 4) = \tfrac49\) ✓. \(P(B = 1) = \tfrac{10}{18}\), \(P(B = -1) = \tfrac8{18}\), so \(\langle B\rangle = \tfrac2{18} = \tfrac19\); directly \(\tfrac19(4 + 1 - 4) = \tfrac19\) ✓.

4. \(P_+ = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}\), \(Q_0 = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\), and \(P_+Q_0 = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), which is not Hermitian (its transpose differs) and so not an orthogonal projector; \((P_+Q_0)^2 = \tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix} \ne P_+Q_0\) either. Sequentially on \(\ket0\): "\(Z\) then \(X\)" gives \((+1, +1)\) and \((+1, -1)\) with \(\tfrac12\) each and the other two pairs never; "\(X\) then \(Z\)" gives all four pairs \((x, z)\) with \(\tfrac14\) each. The two orders disagree, so there is no single "joint distribution of \(X\) and \(Z\)" for them to be computing.

Check. The joint measurement leaves, after \((a, b) = (1, 1)\), the state \(u\), which is also what "\(A\) then \(B\)" leaves: \(P_1\psi\) is the component of \(\psi\) in \(\mathrm{span}(u, w)\), and \(Q_1\) then removes the \(w\) part. And \(p(-1, -1) = 0\) is forced, whatever \(\psi\) is: that cell is empty.

P5 A detector that never clicks medium

exercises: amplitudes through an unbalanced splitter, when perfect cancellation is possible, fringe visibility as a number

Replace the first splitter by an unbalanced one, \(B_1 = \begin{pmatrix}t&ir\\ir&t\end{pmatrix}\) with \(t, r \ge 0\) real and \(t^2 + r^2 = 1\); keep the second splitter \(B\) and the phase shifter \(P(\varphi)\).

  1. Check that \(B_1\) is unitary.
  2. Show that \(P(D_0) = \tfrac12(1 - 2tr\cos\varphi)\) and \(P(D_1) = \tfrac12(1 + 2tr\cos\varphi)\).
  3. Prove that some \(\varphi\) makes \(D_0\) perfectly dark if and only if \(t = r\).
  4. The visibility of the fringes is \(V = (P_{\max} - P_{\min})/(P_{\max} + P_{\min})\) for \(P(D_0)\) as \(\varphi\) varies. Show \(V = 2tr\), and compute it for \(t^2 = 0.7\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 2 is a squared modulus of a sum of two route amplitudes, one through each arm. The rest follows from the inequality \(2tr \le t^2 + r^2\).

Rung 2

After \(B_1\) and the shifter the state is \(t\ket0 + ire^{i\varphi}\ket1\). Apply \(B\) using \(B\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + i\ket1)\) and \(B\ket1 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(i\ket0 + \ket1)\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "The \(\ket0\) amplitude at the output is \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(t + ire^{i\varphi}\cdot i) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(t - re^{i\varphi})\)."

Worked solution

Idea. \(D_0\) is reached by two routes whose amplitudes have sizes \(t/\sqrt2\) and \(r/\sqrt2\). They can cancel completely only if they are the same size — a balanced splitter. Otherwise the fringes never reach zero, and the visibility measures how far they fall.

1. Columns \((t, ir)\) and \((ir, t)\): squared norms \(t^2 + r^2 = 1\); inner product \(\bar t\cdot ir + \overline{ir}\cdot t = itr - itr = 0\) ✓.

2. \(B_1\ket0 = t\ket0 + ir\ket1\); the shifter gives \(t\ket0 + ire^{i\varphi}\ket1\); then \(B\) gives \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}[t(\ket0 + i\ket1) + ire^{i\varphi}(i\ket0 + \ket1)] = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}[(t - re^{i\varphi})\ket0 + i(t + re^{i\varphi})\ket1]\). So \[ P(D_0) = \tfrac12|t - re^{i\varphi}|^2 = \tfrac12(t^2 + r^2 - 2tr\cos\varphi) = \tfrac12(1 - 2tr\cos\varphi), \] and likewise \(P(D_1) = \tfrac12(1 + 2tr\cos\varphi)\). Sum \(1\) ✓.

3. \(P(D_0) = 0\) needs \(2tr\cos\varphi = 1\). Since \(0 \le 2tr \le t^2 + r^2 = 1\) (from \((t - r)^2 \ge 0\)), this needs \(2tr = 1\) and \(\cos\varphi = 1\), and \(2tr = 1 = t^2 + r^2\) iff \((t - r)^2 = 0\) iff \(t = r\). Conversely \(t = r = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\) and \(\varphi = 0\) give \(0\). \(\blacksquare\)

4. As \(\varphi\) varies, \(P(D_0)\) ranges over \([\tfrac12(1 - 2tr), \tfrac12(1 + 2tr)]\), so \(P_{\max} - P_{\min} = 2tr\) and \(P_{\max} + P_{\min} = 1\): \(V = 2tr\). For \(t^2 = 0.7\), \(r^2 = 0.3\), \(V = 2\sqrt{0.21} = 0.9165\). The darkest \(D_0\) gets is \(\tfrac12(1 - 0.9165) = 0.0417\) of the photons.

Check. Balanced, \(t = r\): \(V = 1\) and the deck's \(\sin^2\tfrac\varphi2\) returns, since \(\tfrac12(1 - \cos\varphi) = \sin^2\tfrac\varphi2\) ✓. No splitting, \(r = 0\): \(V = 0\), as it must be — only one arm carries light, so nothing interferes, and \(P(D_0) = \tfrac12\) for every \(\varphi\). A 70/30 splitter still gives 92% visibility: the depth of the fringes forgives an unequal splitter, while where they fall — set by \(\varphi\), so by the arms to a fraction of a wavelength — forgives nothing.

import numpy as np
from qclib import gates, linalg
B = np.array([[1, 1j], [1j, 1]]) / np.sqrt(2)
t, r = np.sqrt(0.7), np.sqrt(0.3)
B1 = np.array([[t, 1j * r], [1j * r, t]])
print(linalg.is_unitary(B1))                          # True

P6 The bomb tester routine

exercises: a measurement that absorbs, Lüders' rule mid-circuit, total probability; the checkpoint's first half

Elitzur and Vaidman (Found. Phys. 23, 987 (1993)) imagined a bomb so sensitive that a single photon reaching it sets it off. Some bombs are duds, transparent to light. The interferometer has balanced arms (\(\varphi = 0\)), and the bomb sits in path 1. A live bomb acts as a which-path detector on path 1 that absorbs the photon (and explodes) when it finds it there; a dud does nothing.

  1. With a dud, find \(P(D_0)\) and \(P(D_1)\).
  2. With a live bomb, derive the probabilities of the three outcomes: explosion, \(D_0\), \(D_1\).
  3. Explain why a \(D_0\) click certifies a live bomb, and why the photon that produced it never reached the bomb.
  4. Bombs giving \(D_1\) are tested again, as often as needed. What fraction of live bombs is eventually certified without exploding?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 2's answer is three probabilities summing to one. The live bomb is a measurement of the path between the splitters, with a post-measurement state only on the branch where it does not fire.

Rung 2

Between the splitters the state is \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + i\ket1)\). Use the Born rule for the bomb's measurement, then Lüders' rule for the survivor, then the second splitter. For part 4, each test is independent and ends "certified", "exploded" or "repeat".

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "The bomb finds the photon in path 1 with probability \(|\braket{1|B|0}|^2 = |\tfrac i{\sqrt2}|^2 = \tfrac12\); otherwise the state becomes \(P_0B\ket0/\lVert P_0B\ket0\rVert = \ket0\)."

Worked solution

Idea. A dud leaves the interferometer balanced, so \(D_0\) is dark. A live bomb is a which-path measurement: it destroys the cancellation at \(D_0\). So any click at \(D_0\) means the bomb measured the path — and since it did not explode, it found the photon in path 0.

1. Nothing happens in the arms, so the output is \(B^2\ket0 = iX\ket0 = i\ket1\): \(P(D_0) = 0\), \(P(D_1) = |i|^2 = 1\).

2. Between the splitters, \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + i\ket1)\). The bomb measures the path:

  • path 1, probability \(|\tfrac i{\sqrt2}|^2 = \tfrac12\): the photon is absorbed and the bomb explodes;
  • path 0, probability \(\tfrac12\): by Lüders' rule the state becomes \(\ket0\), which the second splitter sends to \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + i\ket1)\): \(D_0\) with conditional probability \(\tfrac12\), \(D_1\) with \(\tfrac12\).
\[ P(\text{explode}) = \tfrac12,\qquad P(D_0) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14,\qquad P(D_1) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14. \]

Sum \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = 1\) ✓.

3. By part 1 a dud never gives \(D_0\); so a \(D_0\) click means the bomb is live. On that run the bomb did not explode, so its measurement had the result "path 0": the post-measurement state was \(\ket0\), with no amplitude in path 1. In the postulates' terms, the photon that reached \(D_0\) was in path 0 when the bomb looked. (What it "did" before that is not something the postulates assign.)

4. Each test of a live bomb ends in certification (\(\tfrac14\)), explosion (\(\tfrac12\)) or repetition (\(\tfrac14\)). Conditioned on the test being conclusive, certification has probability \(\tfrac{1/4}{1/4 + 1/2} = \tfrac13\). Equivalently, summing the geometric series, \(\tfrac14\sum_{k\ge0}(\tfrac14)^k = \tfrac14\cdot\tfrac43 = \tfrac13\). So a third of live bombs are certified untouched; two thirds explode.

Check. The live-bomb numbers are the overview's blocked-arm numbers — a live bomb is a block that explodes — and the dud numbers are the dark detector. A third is a long way from all: P7 shows how to push it toward one. The test was first carried out with single photons by Kwiat et al. (1995), cited in P7.

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 2's answer is a product of \(N\) identical survival probabilities, because after each survival the state is reset by Lüders' rule. Part 3 is a logarithm and two Taylor expansions.

Rung 2

\(-iY = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), and unit 00's formula for \(e^{i\theta H}\) with \(H^2 = I\) applies to \(Y\). Rotations compose: \(R(\theta)^N = R(N\theta)\). For part 3 use \(\ln\cos x = -\tfrac{x^2}2 + O(x^4)\) and \(e^y = 1 + y + O(y^2)\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "At the first stage \(R(\theta)\ket0 = \cos\theta\ket0 + \sin\theta\ket1\); the live bomb finds the photon with probability \(\sin^2\theta\), and otherwise leaves \(\ket0\) — the stage's starting state."

Worked solution

Idea. The turns never accumulate, and the total risk is \(N\sin^2\theta \approx N\theta^2 \to 0\). Repeated measurement freezes the evolution: the quantum Zeno effect (Misra and Sudarshan, J. Math. 1. \(Y\) is Hermitian with \(Y^2 = I\), so by unit 00, \(e^{-i\theta Y} = \cos\theta\,I - i\sin\theta\,Y\), and \(-iY = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\): the matrix shown, the rotation of the plane by \(\theta\). It is unitary (an exponential of \(i\) times a Hermitian). With a dud nothing interrupts, and \(R(\theta)^N = e^{-iN\theta Y} = R(\tfrac\pi2) = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\), so \(\ket0 \mapsto \ket1\): the final measurement gives \(1\) with certainty.

2. Stage one: \(R(\theta)\ket0 = \cos\theta\ket0 + \sin\theta\ket1\). The bomb measures the path: it explodes with probability \(\sin^2\theta\); with probability \(\cos^2\theta\) it does not, and Lüders' rule leaves \(\ket0\). So every stage starts in \(\ket0\) and the stages are independent: the photon survives all \(N\) with probability \((\cos^2\theta)^N = \cos^{2N}\tfrac\pi{2N}\), ending in \(\ket0\). Then the final measurement gives \(0\) — which a dud never gives. So outcome \(0\) certifies a live bomb, untouched.

3. \(\ln\cos^{2N}x = 2N\ln\cos x\) with \(x = \tfrac\pi{2N}\). Since \(\cos x = 1 - \tfrac{x^2}2 + O(x^4)\) and \(\ln(1 - u) = -u + O(u^2)\), \(\ln\cos x = -\tfrac{x^2}2 + O(x^4)\). So \(2N\ln\cos x = -Nx^2 + O(Nx^4) = -\tfrac{\pi^2}{4N} + O(N^{-3})\), and exponentiating, \(\cos^{2N}\tfrac\pi{2N} = 1 - \tfrac{\pi^2}{4N} + O(N^{-2})\). \(\blacksquare\)

P8 Stone's theorem needs continuity hard

exercises: the role of each hypothesis in Stone's theorem, additive functions, an argument by divisibility

Stone's theorem assumes \(t \mapsto U(t)\) continuous. This problem shows the assumption cannot be dropped.

  1. Show that if \(f: \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\) is additive, \(f(s + t) = f(s) + f(t)\), then \(U(t) = e^{if(t)}\) is a homomorphism from \((\mathbb R, +)\) to the unitary group \(U(1)\) of \(\mathbb C\).
  2. Cited: \(\mathbb R\), as a vector space over \(\mathbb Q\), has a basis (a Hamel basis; its existence needs the axiom of choice), and any \(\mathbb Q\)-linearly independent set extends to one. Use this to build an additive \(f\) with \(f(1) = 0\) and \(f(\sqrt2) = 1\).
  3. Prove that the resulting \(U\) is not of the form \(e^{-iht}\) for any real \(h\).
  4. Where exactly does the deck's proof of Stone's theorem fail for this \(U\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 3 is a proof by contradiction, and the contradiction comes from comparing \(f\) with a linear function on \(1\) and \(\sqrt2\).

Rung 2

For part 2, check \(\{1, \sqrt2\}\) is \(\mathbb Q\)-linearly independent, and define \(f\) on the basis then extend \(\mathbb Q\)-linearly. For part 3, suppose \(e^{if(t)} = e^{-iht}\) for all \(t\), and look at \(g(t) = f(t) + ht\): it is additive, and its values lie in \(2\pi\mathbb Z\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: "\(g\) is \(\mathbb Q\)-linear, so for every integer \(n \ge 1\), \(g(t) = n\,g(t/n) \in 2\pi n\mathbb Z\)."

Worked solution

Idea. A homomorphism into the circle is a "phase that adds", and without continuity a phase can add over \(\mathbb Q\) without being proportional to \(t\). Divisibility of \(\mathbb R\) then rules out its agreeing with any \(e^{-iht}\), even up to multiples of \(2\pi\).

1. \(|e^{if(t)}| = 1\), so each \(U(t)\) is unitary on \(\mathbb C\). \(U(s + t) = e^{if(s) + if(t)} = U(s)U(t)\) and \(U(0) = e^{if(0)} = 1\), since \(f(0) = f(0 + 0) = 2f(0)\) forces \(f(0) = 0\).

2. If \(q_1 + q_2\sqrt2 = 0\) with \(q_1, q_2 \in \mathbb Q\) not both zero, then \(q_2 \ne 0\) and \(\sqrt2 = -q_1/q_2\) is rational, false. So \(\{1, \sqrt2\}\) extends to a Hamel basis \(\mathcal B\). Define \(f(1) = 0\), \(f(\sqrt2) = 1\), \(f(\beta) = 0\) for the other \(\beta \in \mathcal B\), and extend \(\mathbb Q\)-linearly: each real \(x\) is uniquely a finite sum \(\sum q_\beta\beta\), and \(f(x) := \sum q_\beta f(\beta)\). This \(f\) is \(\mathbb Q\)-linear, in particular additive.

3. Suppose \(e^{if(t)} = e^{-iht}\) for all real \(t\). Then \(g(t) := f(t) + ht \in 2\pi\mathbb Z\) for all \(t\). Both \(f\) and \(t \mapsto ht\) are \(\mathbb Q\)-linear, so \(g\) is, and for every integer \(n \ge 1\), \(g(t) = n\,g(t/n) \in 2\pi n\mathbb Z\). The only number in \(2\pi n\mathbb Z\) for every \(n\) is \(0\), so \(g \equiv 0\) and \(f(t) = -ht\) for all \(t\). At \(t = 1\): \(0 = -h\), so \(h = 0\); at \(t = \sqrt2\): \(1 = -h\sqrt2 = 0\). Contradiction. \(\blacksquare\)

4. Step 1. Its averages \(A_\varepsilon = \tfrac1\varepsilon\int_0^\varepsilon U\) need not even make sense, and in no sense is \(U(s)\) close to \(1\) for small \(s\). Indeed \(f(\sqrt2 - q) = f(\sqrt2) - q\,f(1) = 1\) for every rational \(q\), and choosing rationals \(q\) close to \(\sqrt2\) puts points \(s = \sqrt2 - q\) arbitrarily close to \(0\) with \(U(s) = e^{i}\), far from \(U(0) = 1\). So \(U\) is not continuous at \(0\), which is all step 1 needed. Steps 2–6 never get started.

Check. The conclusion is consistent with Stone's theorem rather than against it: Stone says a continuous homomorphism is \(e^{-iht}\), so this \(U\) must be discontinuous, and part 4 found the discontinuity directly. Cited: a Lebesgue-measurable additive \(f: \mathbb R \to \mathbb R\) is linear (S. Banach and W. Sierpiński, both in Fund. Math. 1 (1920)), and von Neumann showed that weak measurability suffices in Stone's theorem (Ann. Math. 33, 567 (1932)). So no evolution that can be written down without the axiom of choice is like this one — continuity is a harmless assumption physically, and an indispensable one logically.

Idea. Robertson's bound is \(\lVert a\rVert\lVert b\rVert \ge |\braket{a|b}| \ge |\mathrm{Im}\braket{a|b}|\). On a qubit the first step is always tight, because there is only one direction orthogonal to \(\psi\); so all that can be lost is the real part of \(\braket{a|b}\), a covariance.

Equality throughout iff equality in Cauchy–Schwarz — \(a, b\) linearly dependent (unit 00) — and \(|\braket{a|b}| = |\mathrm{Im}\braket{a|b}|\), i.e. \(\mathrm{Re}\braket{a|b} = 0\). \(\blacksquare\)

3. \(\braket{a|b} = \braket{\psi|(A - \langle A\rangle)(B - \langle B\rangle)\psi} = \langle AB\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle\), and \(\braket{b|a}\), its conjugate, is \(\langle BA\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle\). P11 Uncertainty is not disturbance medium

exercises: reading what a theorem quantifies over, Lüders' rule on eigenstates, an argument in prose

A common reading of Robertson's relation is: "measuring \(A\) disturbs \(B\), and \(\tfrac12|\langle[A, B]\rangle|\) is the least disturbance". This problem refutes that reading with two examples and asks for the argument in words.

  1. At \(\ket0\), compute both sides of \(\Delta X\,\Delta Y \ge |\langle Z\rangle|\). Then measure \(Z\) on \(\ket0\) and compute both sides again for the state afterwards. What did the \(Z\) measurement disturb?
  2. Measure \(X\) and then \(Y\), on many copies of \(\ket0\) and on many copies of \(\ket{+i} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + i\ket1)\). In each case compare the distribution of the \(Y\) results with what \(Y\) gives when measured alone.
  3. Compare: where is the Robertson bound for \(X, Y\) largest, and where does measuring \(X\) change the \(Y\) statistics? Write a short paragraph explaining why Robertson's relation cannot be a statement about disturbance.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each side of Robertson's relation is a function of one state. Disturbance is a comparison between two experiments. The examples are chosen so that the two notions point in opposite directions.

Rung 2

\(\ket0\) is an eigenvector of \(Z\), so Lüders' rule leaves it alone. \(\ket{+i}\) is an eigenvector of \(Y\). After an \(X\) measurement the state is \(\ket+\) or \(\ket-\), each unbiased to the \(y\) basis (unit 00).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "Alone, \(Y\) on \(\ket0\) gives \(\pm1\) with \(|\braket{\pm i|0}|^2 = \tfrac12\) each; after \(X\), the state is \(\ket\pm\), and \(Y\) gives \(\pm1\) with \(|\braket{\pm i|\pm}|^2 = \tfrac12\) each."

Worked solution

Idea. Find a state where the bound is as large as it gets but a measurement disturbs nothing, and a state where the bound is zero but a measurement disturbs everything. Then no reading that ties the bound to disturbance can survive.

1. The deck computed \(\Delta X = \Delta Y = 1\) and \(\langle Z\rangle = 1\) at \(\ket0\): \(1 \ge 1\), equality. \(Z\) on \(\ket0\) gives \(+1\) with certainty and, by Lüders' rule, leaves \(P_0\ket0/\lVert P_0\ket0\rVert = \ket0\): the same state. Both sides are again \(1\). The \(Z\) measurement disturbed nothing, and the bound did not move; it was \(1\) before any measurement was made.

2. On \(\ket0\): \(Y\) alone gives \(\pm1\) with \(\tfrac12\) each. \(X\) first leaves \(\ket\pm\), and \(Y\) then gives \(\pm1\) with \(|\braket{\pm i|\pm}|^2 = \tfrac12\) each. Same distribution: measuring \(X\) had no effect on the \(Y\) statistics. On \(\ket{+i}\): \(Y\) alone gives \(+1\) with certainty. \(X\) first gives \(\pm1\) with \(\tfrac12\) each and leaves \(\ket\pm\); \(Y\) then gives \(\pm1\) with \(\tfrac12\) each. A certain result has become a fair coin: maximal disturbance.

3. At \(\ket{+i}\): \(\langle Z\rangle = \tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0\), so the Robertson bound for \(X, Y\) is \(0\) — trivial — exactly where measuring \(X\) disturbs \(Y\) completely. At \(\ket0\) the bound is \(1\), its largest value for these observables, exactly where measuring \(X\) leaves the \(Y\) statistics unchanged. Paragraph: Robertson's \(\Delta A\) and \(\Delta B\) are the standard deviations of two Born distributions of the same state, each estimated on its own copies; no copy is measured twice, so no measurement acts on another's system. The relation therefore constrains preparations: it says no state makes both distributions narrow beyond \(\tfrac12|\langle[A, B]\rangle|\). How much one measurement changes the statistics of a later one is a different quantity, defined by comparing two experiments, and the examples show it can be large where the bound is zero and zero where the bound is largest. A relation for disturbance needs its own definitions (Ozawa 2003, cited in the deck).

Check. \(\ket{+i}\) is an eigenvector of \(Y\), so \(\Delta Y = 0\) there and \(\Delta X\,\Delta Y = 0 \ge 0\) ✓, consistent with the bound being \(0\). And the two examples are the extreme cases: for these observables, \(|\langle Z\rangle| \le 1\), with \(1\) reached only at \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\).
