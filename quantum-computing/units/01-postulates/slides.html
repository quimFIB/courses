01

The postulates, as mathematics: states, measurement, and evolution

An interferometer in which opening a second path makes a detector go dark. Three postulates describe it — a state is a ray, a measurement is a projection-valued measure, an evolution is a unitary group — and Stone's theorem turns the third into the Schrödinger equation.

needs 00~2½ h slides + 4½ h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 01

Unit 00 worked with a minimal model — a measurement is an orthonormal basis, a probability is a squared overlap — and promised that this unit would make it precise. Here it is made precise in three postulates, each stated as mathematics and each forced by an experiment: states (a unit vector up to phase), measurement (a Hermitian operator, the Born rule, and the state afterwards), and evolution (a continuous one-parameter group of unitaries). A fourth postulate, for composite systems, is named at the end of section 4 and left to unit 03.

Two theorems carry the weight. Stone's theorem, in finite dimension and proved in full, says every such group is $e^{-iHt}$ for exactly one Hermitian $H$ — so the Schrödinger equation is a consequence of the evolution postulate, not an extra one. Robertson's uncertainty relation follows from Cauchy–Schwarz in three lines, and the unit is careful about what it does and does not say.

How to read this deck

One apparatus runs through it: the Mach–Zehnder interferometer, introduced on the next slide as a puzzle and described from nothing in section 1. Proofs are on the slides when they are one line and in these notes under Full proof when they are not; anything imported is labelled cited, with where its proof is. The tools used are all unit 00's: complex inner products and Cauchy–Schwarz, Dirac notation, orthogonal projectors, Hermitian and unitary operators, the spectral theorem with its decomposition $A = \sum\lambda P_\lambda$, simultaneous diagonalisation, and the functional calculus with $e^{iH}$. The one new piece of analysis — differentiating and integrating a matrix-valued function — is set out on its own slide in section 4. No physics is assumed: the interferometer is described from nothing. Every number on the slides was computed.

01 · overview

One photon, two paths, a detector that never clicks

the running example: the Mach–Zehnder interferometer

Photons are sent in one at a time. Each meets a half-silvered mirror, travels one of two arms, meets a second half-silvered mirror, and is caught by detector $D_0$ or $D_1$.

Block either arm: the block absorbs half the photons, and each detector fires on a quarter.
Open both: $D_0$ never fires.

Opening a second route made an outcome impossible. Probabilities of routes add, and are never negative; they cannot do this. Amplitudes add, and can cancel.

section 1

Two paths, and what a state is

The interferometer from nothing; the evidence that one photon goes through it at a time; and why a model that sends the photon one way or the other cannot be right.

01 · states

The Mach–Zehnder interferometer, from nothing

The photon enters along path 0. A beam splitter transmits along path 0 or reflects into path 1; mirrors bring the arms together at a second splitter, whose two outputs reach $D_0$ (up) and $D_1$ (right). The slab in path 1 is the phase shifter.

A beam splitter is a glass plate with a coating so thin that half the light falling on it passes through and half is reflected — a half-silvered mirror.

Two ordinary mirrors turn the two beams toward a second splitter. Two detectors watch its outputs; each gives a click when light arrives.

A phase shifter is a thin slab of glass in one arm. Light is slower in glass, so the slab delays the light in that arm by a fraction of a wavelength.

what ordinary light does

With arms of equal length and no slab, all the light leaves toward $D_1$; $D_0$ is dark. Thicken the slab and the light moves smoothly to $D_0$ and back: fringes. Waves from the two arms cancel at $D_0$ — so far, classical optics.

The instrument. Ludwig Zehnder (1891) and Ludwig Mach (1892) designed it to measure small changes in the refractive index of a gas placed in one arm: the fringes move as the gas changes. Its virtue is that the two arms are widely separated, so anything can be put in one arm without touching the other — which is exactly why this course uses it.

Why ordinary light makes fringes. Classically, light is a wave: at each point an oscillating field. A splitter divides the wave into two weaker waves, the arms carry them, and the second splitter adds them again. Where the two arrive in step they reinforce; where they arrive half a wavelength out of step they cancel. "Balanced arms" means the two routes to $D_0$ arrive exactly out of step (the reason is the phase a reflection adds, worked out on A beam splitter is a 2×2 unitary). The slab shifts one arm's wave, moving the cancellation from $D_0$ toward $D_1$.

Detectors. A photodetector — a photomultiplier tube or an avalanche photodiode — turns arriving light into electrical pulses. At high intensity the pulses merge into a current proportional to the intensity. At very low intensity they come one at a time, as separate clicks. The puzzle lives in that regime: next slide.

Alignment, honestly. In a real instrument the arms are equal to a small fraction of a wavelength only after adjustment, and the dark output is dark only to the extent the splitters are exactly 50/50. Problem P5 computes what an unequal first splitter does to the fringes.

01 · states

One photon at a time

Faint light is not enough. G. I. Taylor (1909) photographed interference fringes in light so faint that an exposure took months. But light from a lamp, however faint, sometimes carries two or more photons at once — the clicks arrive at random, and some arrive together. Faint light did not establish single-photon behaviour.

Grangier, Roger and Aspect, 1986 (cited)

A source that emits photons one at a time: an atom gives off two in quick succession, and the first opens a short window in which the second is watched.

One splitter, two detectors: the detectors click together far less often than any classical wave allows. The photon is not shared between the outputs.

The full interferometer: the fringes are still there, built up click by click.

The anticorrelation test: one photon, one splitter, two detectors. Coincident clicks are far rarer than a classical wave allows.

the facts to be modelled

Each photon gives one click at one detector. Over many photons the clicks follow the fringes, and with balanced arms none reach $D_0$.

The references. G. I. Taylor, "Interference fringes with feeble light", Proc. Camb. Phil. Soc. 15, 114 (1909). P. Grangier, G. Roger and A. Aspect, "Experimental evidence for a photon anticorrelation effect on a beam splitter: a new light on single-photon interferences", Europhys. Lett. 1, 173 (1986).

Why Taylor's experiment does not settle it. In light from a thermal source or a laser the number of photons in any short window is random; turning the intensity down makes the average small but leaves a chance of two at once. Worse, every result of such experiments — fringes in the average, clicks one at a time — is also predicted by a classical wave whose intensity sets the click rate of each detector independently. What a classical wave cannot do is the anticorrelation: a wave split in two would make both detectors fire independently, and so sometimes together.

How the 1986 source works. Calcium atoms are excited and decay in two steps, emitting two photons of different colours within a few nanoseconds. Detecting the first photon "heralds" the second, and the detectors behind the splitter are read only in a short window after the herald. In that window the coincidence rate fell far below what any classical wave allows — not to zero, since no detector or source is perfect, but far enough to rule the classical picture out. With the same source in a Mach–Zehnder interferometer, the fringes were observed with high contrast. Single photons interfere.

What a "photon" is here. Nothing more than: the thing that makes one click. The course never needs the quantum theory of light; it needs only that the photon's path is a two-level system, which is what the next slides build.

Probabilities cannot add

The obvious model. Each photon takes one arm, each with probability $\tfrac12$. At the second splitter a photon from either arm goes to each detector with probability $\tfrac12$.

Sum $1$ ✓. So a photon in arm 0 reaches $D_0$ half the time — and arm 1 likewise, blocking arm 0.

Routes, if the photon takes one: each leaf has probability $\tfrac14$, two of them end at $D_0$.

the contradiction

Both arms open: $P(D_0) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$. Observed: $0$. Two non-negative contributions cannot sum to zero.

What exactly is refuted. The calculation uses two assumptions: (a) each photon travels one arm, with some probability $p_0, p_1$; (b) a photon travelling arm 0 reaches $D_0$ with the same probability whether or not arm 1 is open — after all, it is not in arm 1. Then $P(D_0) = p_0\cdot\tfrac12 + p_1\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$, whatever $p_0, p_1$ are. The experiment gives $0$. So (a) and (b) cannot both hold. Nothing here says which fails, and the course does not need to decide: the postulates below simply do not assign the photon a route, and predict the $0$.

The blocked-arm numbers. These are measured, and the postulates will reproduce them: the block is a detector that absorbs, and problem P6 — the bomb tester — computes $\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac14$ from the postulates. Note how strange the comparison is: blocking a route makes $D_0$ fire on a quarter of the photons; opening it makes $D_0$ fire on none.

Why amplitudes. A set of numbers that can cancel must be able to be negative, or complex. The next two slides assign each route a complex amplitude, add the amplitudes of routes that end at the same detector, and take the squared modulus of the sum — unit 00's rule $p = |\braket{e|\psi}|^2$, applied to the state that comes out of the second splitter.

01 · states

A beam splitter is a 2×2 unitary

the model, on $\mathbb C^2$

$\ket0$ = the photon is in path 0, $\ket1$ = in path 1. A beam splitter acts as

$$B = \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & i\\ i & 1\end{pmatrix},$$ $$B\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket0 + i\ket1\big).$$

Transmission keeps the path, amplitude $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$; reflection switches it, amplitude $\tfrac i{\sqrt2}$. The phase shifter is $P(\varphi) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\varphi})$.

unitary, checked

Columns $c_1 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, i)$, $c_2 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(i, 1)$: $\ \lVert c_j\rVert^2 = \tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1$, $\ \braket{c_1|c_2} = \tfrac12(\bar1\cdot i + \bar i\cdot1) = \tfrac12(i - i) = 0$ ✓. $P(\varphi)$: diagonal, entries of modulus one ✓.

One splitter, photon entering in path 0. The output amplitudes are the first column of $B$; each has squared modulus $\tfrac12$ — half the light each way.

Mirrors are left out: each arm has exactly one, so they multiply both components by the same factor — a global phase, dropped up to global phase.

Why a unitary. The splitter loses no photons: whatever goes in comes out in one of the two outputs. In the model that says $\lVert B\psi\rVert = \lVert\psi\rVert$ for every $\psi$, and unit 00 (Unitary means norm-preserving) proved that a linear norm-preserving map is unitary. Linearity is the content of the amplitude rule on the next slide: the splitter acts on each path's amplitude separately, and the results add.

Full proof that a symmetric splitter has a reflected phase of $\pm\pi/2$. Suppose a 50/50 splitter treats its two inputs alike: $B = \begin{pmatrix}t & r\\ r & t\end{pmatrix}$ with $|t| = |r| = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$. Unitarity requires the columns to be orthogonal: $\bar tr + \bar rt = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\bar tr) = 0$. Writing $r = te^{i\delta}$, $\bar tr = |t|^2e^{i\delta}$, so $\cos\delta = 0$ and $\delta = \pm\pi/2$: the reflected amplitude is $\pm i$ times the transmitted one. This is not a convention but a consequence of losing no light. The choice $+i$, with $t$ real, is the convention, fixed for the whole unit.

Other conventions. Many texts use the real matrix $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$ for a splitter. It differs from $B$ by phases on the input and output ports, which amounts to relabelling which detector is which or shifting $\varphi$ by a constant. Every statement about probabilities survives the change; the formulae for amplitudes do not, so a solution copied from another text may need its phases translated.

The phase shifter. Light of wavelength $\lambda$ in glass of refractive index $n$ travels as if the slab of thickness $d$ were $nd$ long, so its wave falls behind by $(n - 1)d$, a phase of $\varphi = 2\pi(n - 1)d/\lambda$. In the model the slab multiplies the amplitude of path 1 by $e^{i\varphi}$ and leaves path 0 alone. (The sign of the phase is again a convention; the fringes depend only on $\cos\varphi$.)

01 · states

Amplitudes add: the dark detector explained

the rule the matrices encode

A route's amplitude is the product of the amplitudes along it. Routes ending at the same detector add. The probability is the squared modulus of the sum.

With balanced arms, two splitters in a row:

$$B^2 = \frac12\begin{pmatrix}1 - 1 & 2i\\ 2i & -1 + 1\end{pmatrix} = iX,$$ $$B^2\ket0 = i\ket1 .$$

$D_1$ with probability $|i|^2 = 1$; $D_0$ with probability $0$. Up to global phase, two splitters swap the paths.

Amplitudes as arrows in $\mathbb C$. To $D_0$: transmit–transmit gives $\tfrac12$, reflect–reflect gives $i\cdot i/2 = -\tfrac12$; they cancel. To $D_1$: $\tfrac i2 + \tfrac i2 = i$.

Normalised: after one splitter, $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + i\ket1)$ has $\lVert\cdot\rVert^2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ ✓.

The rule is matrix multiplication. The entry $(B^2)_{00} = \sum_kB_{0k}B_{k0}$ is a sum over the intermediate path $k$: route "stay in 0, then stay in 0" contributes $B_{00}B_{00} = \tfrac12$, route "go to 1, then come back to 0" contributes $B_{01}B_{10} = \tfrac i{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac i{\sqrt2} = -\tfrac12$. Product along a route, sum over routes, is exactly how a product of matrices is computed. Nothing beyond linear algebra has been added — but the claim that this is how photons behave is physics, and the next slides make it a postulate.

Every entry of $B^2$. $(B^2)_{00} = \tfrac12(1\cdot1 + i\cdot i) = 0$; $(B^2)_{01} = \tfrac12(1\cdot i + i\cdot1) = i$; $(B^2)_{10} = \tfrac12(i\cdot1 + 1\cdot i) = i$; $(B^2)_{11} = \tfrac12(i\cdot i + 1\cdot1) = 0$. So $B^2 = iX$, with $X$ unit 00's swap matrix. It is unitary, as a product of unitaries must be: $(iX)^\dagger(iX) = \bar ii\,X^2 = I$ ✓.

Which detector is which. The photon leaves the second splitter in path 1, which in the figure is the beam continuing to the right: $D_1$. The dark output, $D_0$, is the one that a photon from path 0 reaches by transmission and a photon from path 1 reaches by reflection. With the opposite sign convention for reflection the roles are the same; with the real matrix of the previous notes, they are swapped.

Reading. Feynman's QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter (1985), chapters 1–2, draws amplitudes as arrows exactly like this figure, for partial reflection of light from glass.

01 · states

Postulate 1: a state is a ray

postulate 1 — states

A closed system — one interacting with nothing outside the description — has a finite-dimensional complex inner-product space $V$, its state space. A state is a unit vector $\psi \in V$, where $\psi$ and $e^{i\alpha}\psi$ are the same state: the state is the ray

$$[\psi] = \{e^{i\alpha}\psi : \alpha \in \mathbb R\}.$$
a qubit

A system with $V = \mathbb C^2$ and a named orthonormal basis $\ket0, \ket1$. The photon's path is one.

The unit vectors $e^{i\alpha}\psi$ form a circle in $V$; postulate 1 says the whole circle is one state.

Why unit: probabilities must sum to one (Parseval, unit 00). Why modulo phase: the next slide, and for every observable in section 2.

narrower than it will end up

A system prepared at random, or part of a larger one, needs a density operator — unit 04.

"Hilbert space". The textbooks say a state lies in a complex Hilbert space: an inner-product space that is complete. Every finite-dimensional inner-product space is complete (it is $\mathbb C^d$ with the standard inner product, after choosing an orthonormal basis), so in this course "Hilbert space" and "finite-dimensional complex inner-product space" mean the same thing. Infinite dimensions — a particle on a line — need completeness and much more care, and this course never goes there.

Why closed. The postulates describe a system that is not interacting with anything left out of the description. A photon in an arm with nothing else there is closed; a photon in an arm with a detector is not, and section 3 handles that case by treating the detector as a measurement. Unit 21 treats open systems in general.

The set of rays. The rays of $V$ form a space of their own, the projective space of $V$. For a qubit it is a sphere — the Bloch sphere of unit 02. Here nothing about its shape is used.

Why "complex". Unit 00 proved that the least model of three magnets, each pair unbiased, cannot be real in dimension two. The interferometer is a second witness: the splitter's amplitude $i$ is forced by unitarity (notes to A beam splitter is a 2×2 unitary), and the phase shifter's $e^{i\varphi}$ sweeps a whole circle of states that differ only in the phase between paths.

Global phase and relative phase, in the arms

Left: a slab in one arm changes the phase of path 1 relative to path 0, and the fringes move. Right: identical slabs in both arms multiply the whole state by $e^{i\varphi}$, and nothing changes.

Relative. Between the splitters, with the slab in path 1:

$\lVert\psi_\varphi\rVert^2 = \tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1$ ✓. Section 2 computes $P(D_0) = \sin^2\tfrac\varphi2$: the fringes.

for every $\varphi$ ✓: still dark.

Unit 00 did this algebra. Its slide Global phase and relative phase showed that the states $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\ket1)$ have $z$-probabilities $\tfrac12, \tfrac12$ for every $\varphi$ but $x$-probabilities that depend on $\varphi$. The interferometer is the same statement made physical: the path probabilities between the splitters are $\tfrac12, \tfrac12$ whatever the slab, and the second splitter turns the relative phase into detector probabilities. A slab sweeps the relative phase continuously; unit 00's circle of states is walked by thickening the glass.

Full computation of the global case. $e^{i\varphi}I$ commutes with everything, so $B\,(e^{i\varphi}I)\,B\ket0 = e^{i\varphi}B^2\ket0 = e^{i\varphi}\cdot i\ket1$. A probability $|\braket{e|e^{i\varphi}\chi}|^2$ never sees the factor, as unit 00 proved for a basis measurement and section 2 proves for every observable.

Why both slabs must be identical. Slabs of thicknesses $d_0$ and $d_1$ give $\mathrm{diag}(e^{i\varphi_0}, e^{i\varphi_1}) = e^{i\varphi_0}\,\mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i(\varphi_1 - \varphi_0)})$: a global phase times a relative one. Only the difference $\varphi_1 - \varphi_0$ is visible. This is why an interferometer measures differences of optical path between its arms, and is insensitive to anything that affects both arms equally.

section 2

Measurement: the Born rule as a probability measure

An observable is a Hermitian operator; its spectral projectors are the outcomes; a state turns them into a probability distribution, and the mean and spread of that distribution are computed by the operator itself.

01 · measurement

Postulate 2, part one: observables

an observable

A Hermitian operator $A$ on $V$. By unit 00's spectral theorem,

$$A = \sum_{\lambda\in\sigma(A)}\lambda\,P_\lambda,$$ $$\textstyle\sum_\lambda P_\lambda = I,\quad P_\lambda P_\mu = \delta_{\lambda\mu}P_\lambda ,$$

with $\sigma(A) \subset \mathbb R$ and $P_\lambda$ the orthogonal projector onto the eigenspace $E_\lambda$. The eigenvalues are the possible results.

a projection-valued measure

On a finite set $\Lambda \subset \mathbb R$: orthogonal projectors $P_\lambda$, $\lambda \in \Lambda$, with $P_\lambda P_\mu = \delta_{\lambda\mu}P_\lambda$ and $\sum P_\lambda = I$. For $S \subseteq \Lambda$, $P_S := \sum_{\lambda\in S}P_\lambda$.

$A = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1)$ on $\mathbb C^3$, drawn as if real: the eigenspace $E_{+1}$ is a plane, $E_{-1}$ the line perpendicular to it. Together they fill the space.

The detectors. "$D_0$ or $D_1$" after the second splitter is $Z = P_0 - P_1$ on the output: result $+1$ for $D_0$, $-1$ for $D_1$.

The same data. Observables and projection-valued measures (with every $P_\lambda \ne 0$) correspond: $A \mapsto \{P_\lambda\}$, and $\{P_\lambda\} \mapsto \sum\lambda P_\lambda$.

Why these axioms, from experiment. A measuring device ends in one of finitely many readings — a click of $D_0$ or of $D_1$, a spot up or down on unit 00's glass plate — and the readings are mutually exclusive and together exhaustive. Postulate 2 assigns each reading a subspace, the readings' subspaces are orthogonal (exclusive) and fill $V$ (exhaustive): that is a projection-valued measure. Labelling each reading with a real number $\lambda$ packs the whole measure into one operator $A = \sum\lambda P_\lambda$, which is normal with real spectrum and so, by unit 00's corollary, Hermitian.

Full proof that the two descriptions are the same. Given Hermitian $A$, the spectral theorem gives the projectors, and they form a projection-valued measure on $\sigma(A)$. Given a projection-valued measure $\{P_\lambda\}$ with every $P_\lambda \ne 0$, put $A = \sum\lambda P_\lambda$. It is Hermitian (real coefficients, Hermitian projectors). On the image of $P_\mu$ it acts as $\mu$, since $AP_\mu = \sum\lambda P_\lambda P_\mu = \mu P_\mu$; the images fill $V$, so the eigenvalues of $A$ are exactly the $\mu$ and its eigenspaces are the images of the $P_\mu$. By uniqueness of the spectral decomposition (unit 00), these are $A$'s spectral projectors. Going round either way returns what you started with.

The labels are bookkeeping. Relabelling the readings by a one-to-one real function $f$ gives the observable $f(A) = \sum f(\lambda)P_\lambda$ — unit 00's functional calculus — with the same projectors: the same measurement with different numbers on the dial. The detectors could equally be read by the observable $P_0$ (result 1 for $D_0$, 0 for $D_1$), which is $\tfrac12(I + Z)$.

The word "measure". $S \mapsto P_S$ is additive on disjoint sets, $P_\varnothing = 0$, $P_\Lambda = I$: a measure whose values are projectors. In infinite dimensions the spectrum can be a continuum and this becomes a genuine measure-theoretic object; in this course it is a finite sum.

01 · measurement

Postulate 2, part two: the Born rule, and the state afterwards

postulate 2 — Born rule and Lüders rule

Measuring $A$ on the state $\psi$ gives the result $\lambda \in \sigma(A)$ with probability

$$p_\psi(\lambda) = \braket{\psi|P_\lambda|\psi} = \lVert P_\lambda\psi\rVert^2,$$

and leaves the system in the state

$$\psi \;\mapsto\; \frac{P_\lambda\psi}{\lVert P_\lambda\psi\rVert}.$$

Notation: $\braket{\psi|A|\psi} := \braket{\psi|A\psi}$. The two forms agree since $P = P^\dagger = P^2$: $\ \braket{\psi|P\psi} = \braket{P\psi|P\psi}$.

$\psi$ splits orthogonally into its pieces in the eigenspaces. The squared lengths of the pieces are the probabilities, and they add to $\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = 1$.

a probability measure on $\sigma(A)$

$p_\psi(\lambda) \ge 0$, and $\sum_\lambda p_\psi(\lambda) = \braket{\psi|\textstyle\sum_\lambda P_\lambda|\psi} = \braket{\psi|\psi} = 1$ ✓. For $S \subseteq \sigma(A)$, $P(\text{result} \in S) = \braket{\psi|P_S|\psi}$.

History. Max Born proposed the probability interpretation in 1926 (Z. Phys. 37, 863), in a paper on scattering; the square of the amplitude appears in a footnote added in proof. John von Neumann's Mathematische Grundlagen der Quantenmechanik (1932) gave the postulates their Hilbert-space form and the post-measurement rule for non-degenerate results. For a degenerate result — an eigenspace of dimension two or more — von Neumann's rule was not this one; Gerhart Lüders (Ann. Phys. 8, 322 (1951)) proposed the projection $P_\lambda\psi/\lVert P_\lambda\psi\rVert$, which is the one in use. The next slide shows what it does that a basis cannot.

Well defined on rays. The probability depends on $\psi$ only through its ray, and the post-measurement state of $e^{i\alpha}\psi$ is $e^{i\alpha}$ times that of $\psi$ — the same ray. Proved on Global phase is invisible to every observable. The post-measurement state is defined only when $p_\psi(\lambda) > 0$; a result of probability zero does not occur.

Additivity. For disjoint $S, T$: $P_{S\cup T} = P_S + P_T$, so the probabilities add. Unit 00's Parseval is the special case in which every $P_\lambda$ has rank one.

Narrower than it will end up. Not every measurement in the laboratory is of this kind: a detector that absorbs the photon leaves no post-measurement state at all, and some measurements have more outcomes than the dimension. Unit 21's POVMs cover them, and are derived from this postulate by measuring a larger system.

01 · measurement · example

Worked: a degenerate result in C³

$A = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -1)$ on $\mathbb C^3$, $\ \psi = \tfrac13\big(\ket0 + 2i\ket1 + 2\ket2\big)$

1. Normalised. $\lVert\psi\rVert^2 = \tfrac19(1 + 4 + 4) = 1$ ✓.

2. Spectral projectors. $\sigma(A) = \{1, -1\}$:

$$P_{+1} = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 0),\qquad P_{-1} = \mathrm{diag}(0, 0, 1).$$

Diagonal with entries $0, 1$: $P^2 = P = P^\dagger$ ✓; $P_{+1} + P_{-1} = I$ ✓; $P_{+1} - P_{-1} = A$ ✓.

3. Probabilities. $P_{+1}\psi = \tfrac13(1, 2i, 0)$ and $P_{-1}\psi = \tfrac13(0, 0, 2)$:

$$p(+1) = \tfrac{1 + 4}9 = \tfrac59, \qquad p(-1) = \tfrac49 ;$$

sum $\tfrac99 = 1$ ✓.

4. After the result $+1$. $\lVert P_{+1}\psi\rVert = \tfrac{\sqrt5}3$, so the state becomes

$$\psi_+ = \tfrac1{\sqrt5}\big(\ket0 + 2i\ket1\big),$$

$\lVert\psi_+\rVert^2 = \tfrac15(1 + 4) = 1$ ✓. The superposition of $\ket0$ and $\ket1$, with its relative phase $i$, survives.

5. What a basis would do. Measuring in the standard basis instead gives $\tfrac19, \tfrac49, \tfrac49$ and leaves $\ket0$, $\ket1$ or $\ket2$. Its results $0, 1$ together have probability $\tfrac59$ — the same — but the state afterwards is different.

6. Repeat: $A$ on $\psi_+$ gives $+1$ with probability $\lVert P_{+1}\psi_+\rVert^2 = 1$.

Why step 5 matters. Lüders' rule leaves $\psi_+ = \tfrac1{\sqrt5}(\ket0 + 2i\ket1)$; the refined basis measurement leaves $\ket0$ (probability $\tfrac15$, given $+1$) or $\ket1$ ($\tfrac45$). These are different, and a later measurement tells them apart: the observable $Q = \ket{\psi_+}\!\bra{\psi_+}$ gives result 1 with certainty on $\psi_+$, but with probability $\tfrac15\cdot|\braket{\psi_+|0}|^2 + \tfrac45\cdot|\braket{\psi_+|1}|^2 = \tfrac15\cdot\tfrac15 + \tfrac45\cdot\tfrac45 = \tfrac{17}{25}$ after the basis measurement. So "measure $A$" and "measure in a basis that refines $A$" are different operations: the second finds out more than $A$ asks, and destroys more. In the interferometer this is the difference between asking "did $D_0$ fire?" and asking "which arm, and which detector?".

Checks on the numbers. The probability $\tfrac59$ is the same either way, as it must be: it is $\braket{\psi|P_{+1}|\psi}$ and $P_{+1} = \ket0\!\bra0 + \ket1\!\bra1$. And $\tfrac{17}{25} \le 1$, with the gap $\tfrac8{25}$ the cost of the extra information.

Repeatability, and the least model recovered

repeatability

If measuring $A$ gives $\lambda$, measuring $A$ again at once gives $\lambda$ with probability one.

proof

The state is $\chi = P_\lambda\psi/\lVert P_\lambda\psi\rVert$, and $P_\lambda\chi = \chi$ because $P_\lambda^2 = P_\lambda$. So $p_\chi(\lambda) = \lVert P_\lambda\chi\rVert^2 = \lVert\chi\rVert^2 = 1$. $\ \blacksquare$

Two measurements of the same observable in a row: the second repeats the first.

unit 00's minimal model, as a special case

Let $A$ be non-degenerate: every eigenspace a line, $P_\lambda = \ket{e_\lambda}\!\bra{e_\lambda}$ with $\{e_\lambda\}$ an orthonormal basis. Then

$$p_\psi(\lambda) = \braket{\psi|e_\lambda}\braket{e_\lambda|\psi} = |\braket{e_\lambda|\psi}|^2,$$ $$\frac{P_\lambda\psi}{\lVert P_\lambda\psi\rVert} = \frac{\braket{e_\lambda|\psi}}{|\braket{e_\lambda|\psi}|}\,e_\lambda,$$

which is $e_\lambda$ up to global phase. A measurement in an orthonormal basis is exactly a non-degenerate observable, its results labelled by real numbers.

Repeatability in the experiments. Unit 00's first row of magnets was this theorem: atoms sorted "up" by one $z$ magnet all go up at a second. In the interferometer the analogue is a detector that registers the photon's arm without absorbing it (section 3): two such detectors in a row along one arm agree.

What is new compared with unit 00. Two things. First, an outcome may be a subspace of dimension more than one, and the state afterwards keeps whatever superposition it had inside that subspace (the previous slide). Second, the labels: unit 00's outcomes were the indices of a basis; here they are eigenvalues, which makes expectations and variances meaningful — the next slide.

The phase in the post-measurement state. $\braket{e_\lambda|\psi}/|\braket{e_\lambda|\psi}|$ is a complex number of modulus one, so the state is $e_\lambda$ times a phase: the same ray as $e_\lambda$. Unit 00 wrote "afterwards the state is $e_i$"; postulate 1 makes the phase irrelevant, so the two statements agree.

Not every real device is repeatable. A detector that absorbs the photon cannot be read twice. Such measurements are described by unit 21; the postulate here is the idealisation that the others are derived from.

Expectation and variance, from the measure

the expectation and variance: defined by the measure, computed by the operator $$\langle A\rangle := \sum_\lambda\lambda\,p_\psi(\lambda) = \braket{\psi|A|\psi},$$ $$(\Delta A)^2 := \sum_\lambda\big(\lambda - \langle A\rangle\big)^2p_\psi(\lambda)$$ $$= \braket{\psi|A^2|\psi} - \braket{\psi|A|\psi}^2 = \lVert(A - \langle A\rangle)\psi\rVert^2 .$$
proof

$\sum\lambda\braket{\psi|P_\lambda|\psi} = \braket{\psi|\sum\lambda P_\lambda|\psi}$. With $m = \langle A\rangle$ and $f(\lambda) = (\lambda - m)^2$, the functional calculus gives $\sum f(\lambda)P_\lambda = (A - m)^2$; expand, or write it as $\braket{(A - m)\psi|(A - m)\psi}$. $\ \blacksquare$

The degenerate example's distribution: $\tfrac49$ at $-1$, $\tfrac59$ at $+1$. Mean $\tfrac19$, spread $\Delta A = \tfrac{\sqrt{80}}9 = 0.9938$.

Sharp results. $\Delta A = 0$ iff $A\psi = \langle A\rangle\psi$: iff $\psi$ is an eigenvector.

Full proof of the variance formula. Let $m = \langle A\rangle$, real since $A$ is Hermitian. By the functional calculus (unit 00) with $f(\lambda) = (\lambda - m)^2$, $\sum_\lambda(\lambda - m)^2P_\lambda = f(A) = (A - mI)^2$, because $f$ is a polynomial and the calculus agrees with polynomials. So $(\Delta A)^2 = \braket{\psi|(A - m)^2|\psi}$. Since $A - m$ is Hermitian this is $\braket{(A - m)\psi|(A - m)\psi} = \lVert(A - m)\psi\rVert^2$. Expanding instead, $\braket{\psi|A^2 - 2mA + m^2|\psi} = \langle A^2\rangle - 2m^2 + m^2 = \langle A^2\rangle - m^2$, using $\braket{\psi|\psi} = 1$.

Sharp results, in full. $\Delta A = 0$ iff $(A - m)\psi = 0$, iff $\psi$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $m$. Conversely an eigenvector has $p_\psi(\lambda) = 1$ for its eigenvalue, so all the measure sits on one point. That is the precise form of "the result is certain".

Not a postulate. Many texts state "$\langle A\rangle = \braket{\psi|A|\psi}$" as if it were an extra rule. It is the mean of the Born distribution, nothing more; problem P2 asks for the derivation from scratch, including the third moment.

Global phase is invisible to every observable

global phase

For every observable $A$, every $\lambda$ and every real $\alpha$: $\psi$ and $e^{i\alpha}\psi$ give the same probabilities, and post-measurement states in the same ray.

proof

$\braket{e^{i\alpha}\psi|P_\lambda|e^{i\alpha}\psi} = \overline{e^{i\alpha}}e^{i\alpha}\braket{\psi|P_\lambda|\psi} = \braket{\psi|P_\lambda|\psi}$ — the first slot is conjugate-linear. And $P_\lambda(e^{i\alpha}\psi)/\lVert\cdot\rVert = e^{i\alpha}\,P_\lambda\psi/\lVert P_\lambda\psi\rVert$. $\ \blacksquare$

and nothing else is invisible

If unit vectors $\psi, \chi$ give the same statistics for every observable, then $\chi = e^{i\alpha}\psi$.

proof

Take $A = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$, an orthogonal projector (unit 00). On $\psi$ it gives $1$ with probability $\braket{\psi|\psi}^2 = 1$; on $\chi$, with probability $|\braket{\psi|\chi}|^2$. So $|\braket{\psi|\chi}| = 1 = \lVert\psi\rVert\lVert\chi\rVert$: equality in Cauchy–Schwarz, so $\chi = c\psi$, and $|c| = 1$. $\ \blacksquare$

So postulate 1 is exactly right: the statistics determine the ray, and nothing finer.

A relative phase is seen. In the arms, $\psi_0$ and $\psi_\varphi$ differ only by the phase on $\ket1$, and

$$|\braket{\psi_0|\psi_\varphi}|^2 = \tfrac14|1 + e^{i\varphi}|^2 = \cos^2\tfrac\varphi2 ,$$

below one unless $e^{i\varphi} = 1$: the projector onto $\psi_0$ tells them apart. The second splitter followed by $D_1$ is that measurement.

Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz. Unit 00 proved $|\braket{u|v}| \le \lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert$ with equality iff $u$ and $v$ are linearly dependent. Here $\psi \ne 0$, so $\chi = c\psi$ for some $c \in \mathbb C$, and $1 = \lVert\chi\rVert = |c|$.

The projector onto a state is an observable. $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi$ is Hermitian with spectrum $\{0, 1\}$ (in dimension two or more): its spectral projectors are itself and $I - \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$. Measuring it asks the yes/no question "is the system in the state $\psi$?". Its probability of "yes" on $\chi$, $|\braket{\psi|\chi}|^2$, is the transition probability between the two states, which reappears in Wigner's theorem in section 4.

Compatible observables

compatible observables have a joint measurement

If $AB = BA$, with spectral projectors $P_a$ and $Q_b$, then the $R_{ab} := P_aQ_b$ form a projection-valued measure on $\sigma(A)\times\sigma(B)$. Its distribution $p(a, b) = \braket{\psi|P_aQ_b|\psi}$ has the Born distributions of $A$ and of $B$ as its marginals.

proof

Unit 00: commuting normal operators diagonalise together, so $P_a$ and $Q_b$ are diagonal in one orthonormal basis and commute. Then $R_{ab}^2 = P_a^2Q_b^2 = R_{ab}$, $R_{ab}^\dagger = Q_bP_a = R_{ab}$, $R_{ab}R_{a'b'} = P_aP_{a'}Q_bQ_{b'} = 0$ unless $(a, b) = (a', b')$, and $\sum_{a,b}R_{ab} = (\sum P_a)(\sum Q_b) = I$; $\sum_bR_{ab} = P_a$. $\ \blacksquare$

Commuting: the common eigenbasis is sorted into cells, one per pair $(a, b)$; each cell is a projector.

non-commuting: no joint distribution

$X$ and $Z$: $P_+Q_0 = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$ is not Hermitian, and on $\psi = (\tfrac12, -\tfrac{\sqrt3}2)$ it gives $\braket{\psi|P_+Q_0|\psi} = \tfrac{1 - \sqrt3}8 = -0.0915$.

Why $P_a$ and $Q_b$ commute, in full. In a common orthonormal eigenbasis (unit 00's simultaneous diagonalisation), each $P_a$ is the sum of $\ket e\!\bra e$ over the basis vectors with $A$-eigenvalue $a$, and each $Q_b$ the sum over those with $B$-eigenvalue $b$. Both are diagonal in that basis, so they commute, and $P_aQ_b$ is the sum over the basis vectors with both eigenvalues: one cell of the figure. A second route: each $P_a$ is a polynomial in $A$ (the Lagrange polynomials of unit 00's functional calculus) and each $Q_b$ a polynomial in $B$.

Sequential measurement agrees. Measure $A$, then $B$. The first gives $a$ with probability $\lVert P_a\psi\rVert^2$; given that, the second gives $b$ with probability $\lVert Q_bP_a\psi\rVert^2/\lVert P_a\psi\rVert^2$. The product is $\lVert Q_bP_a\psi\rVert^2 = \braket{\psi|P_aQ_bQ_bP_a|\psi} = \braket{\psi|P_aQ_b|\psi}$, using commutation and idempotence. The same in the other order. For commuting observables "measure both" is unambiguous.

The non-commuting case. $P_+ = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, $Q_0 = \ket0\!\bra0$, so $P_+Q_0 = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. With $\psi = (\cos a, \sin a)$, $\braket{\psi|P_+Q_0|\psi} = \tfrac12\cos a(\cos a + \sin a)$; at $a = -\pi/3$ this is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12(\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}2) = -0.0915$. A candidate "joint probability" that can be negative is not one. And the sequential versions disagree: on $\ket0$, "$Z$ then $X$" gives $(0, +)$ and $(0, -)$ with $\tfrac12$ each; "$X$ then $Z$" gives all four pairs with $\tfrac14$. Problem P4 works a joint distribution in $\mathbb C^3$.

section 4

Evolution: a unitary group, and its generator

Between measurements a closed system evolves by unitaries that compose in time. Stone's theorem says such a family is $e^{-iHt}$ for exactly one Hermitian $H$ — and the Schrödinger equation follows.

01 · evolution

Postulate 3: evolution is a unitary group

postulate 3 — evolution

Between measurements, a closed system's state evolves as $\psi(t) = U(t)\psi(0)$, where each $U(t)$ is unitary and

$$U(t + s) = U(t)U(s),\qquad U(0) = I,$$

with $t \mapsto U(t)$ continuous: a continuous one-parameter unitary group.

Unitary, because nothing is lost: the elements of the interferometer act linearly on amplitudes and keep $\lVert\psi\rVert = 1$, and a linear map that keeps every norm is unitary (unit 00).

A group, because the rules do not change with time: evolving for $s$ and then for $t$ is evolving for $t + s$.

Glass as a group: a slab of thickness $\ell$ then one of $\ell'$ is a slab of $\ell + \ell'$, and the phase on path 1 turns continuously round the circle.

$U(\ell) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\kappa\ell})$, with $\kappa = 2\pi(n - 1)/\lambda$: $\ U(\ell)U(\ell') = U(\ell + \ell')$ ✓, $U(0) = I$ ✓, continuous ✓. Thickness plays the part of time.

What "closed" buys. The postulate is about a system left alone. A photon crossing glass, a coupler or empty space is such a system; a photon meeting a detector is not, and postulate 2 takes over. The two postulates alternate: unitary evolution between measurements, Lüders' rule at each one.

Time-independent rules. The group law says the change from time $s$ to time $t + s$ depends only on the elapsed $t$. That holds when the system's surroundings are constant in time. A system driven from outside — a qubit under a laser pulse whose strength varies — has an evolution $U(t, s)$ that depends on both times, and is built from short pieces of this kind; unit 25 does that.

Why linear. Section 1's rule — the apparatus acts on each path's amplitude, and the results add — is linearity. A nonlinear evolution would let the two arms' amplitudes influence each other's propagation, and no such effect is seen. Linear nonetheless is part of the postulate, motivated by experiment, not derived; Wigner's theorem on the next slide says how much of it is forced.

The slab family. With $\kappa = 4.96\times10^6$ rad per metre (633 nm light, $n = 1.5$), a full turn of the phase takes $2\pi/\kappa = 1.27\ \mu$m of glass. Why unitary, and not antiunitary

Wigner's theorem (1931, cited)

A bijection of the rays of $V$ that preserves every transition probability $|\braket{\chi|\psi}|^2$ is induced by an operator that is either unitary or antiunitary.

antiunitary

$K$ is conjugate-linear, $K(c\psi) = \bar cK\psi$, and $\braket{K\chi|K\psi} = \overline{\braket{\chi|\psi}}$. Example: complex conjugation of coordinates in a basis.

The motivation. $|\braket{\chi|\psi}|^2$ is the probability that a state $\psi$ passes the test "is it $\chi$?". If preparation and test are both carried forward in time, that probability should not change.

the group law forces the unitary case

If each $U(t)$ is unitary or antiunitary and $U(t + s) = U(t)U(s)$, then every $U(t)$ is unitary.

proof

$U(t) = U(\tfrac t2)U(\tfrac t2)$ is a square. A square of a unitary is unitary; a square of an antiunitary is linear (conjugate twice) and preserves inner products (conjugate twice), so unitary. $\ \blacksquare$

status

"Evolution is unitary" is motivated, not proved: Wigner's theorem is cited, and its hypothesis is a physical assumption.

References. E. P. Wigner, Gruppentheorie und ihre Anwendung auf die Quantenmechanik der Atomspektren (Vieweg, 1931), with the proof completed by V. Bargmann, "Note on Wigner's theorem on symmetry operations", J. Math. Phys. 5, 862 (1964). The proof is a few pages of careful linear algebra and is not given here.

Where antiunitaries occur. Time reversal is antiunitary — it is a symmetry that is not reached continuously from the identity, which is exactly the loophole the group law closes. No evolution in this course is antiunitary.

The square of an antiunitary, in full. If $K$ is antiunitary then $K^2(c\psi) = K(\bar cK\psi) = cK^2\psi$, so $K^2$ is linear, and $\braket{K^2\chi|K^2\psi} = \overline{\braket{K\chi|K\psi}} = \overline{\overline{\braket{\chi|\psi}}} = \braket{\chi|\psi}$, so $K^2$ preserves inner products; a linear map that preserves inner products on a finite-dimensional space is injective, hence invertible, hence unitary. The argument uses only the group law, not continuity — the group law alone makes each $U(t)$ a square. Continuity is still needed, by Stone's theorem, to make the family an exponential.

Why "every transition probability". Preserving all of them is the same as preserving the statistics of every yes/no test of the form "is it $\chi$?", and those tests generate all projective measurements. This is the statement that time evolution is a symmetry of the theory's predictions.

The calculus this needs

matrix-valued functions, entrywise

Fix an orthonormal basis. A function $F: \mathbb R \to \mathrm{End}(V)$ is continuous, differentiable or integrable when each entry's real and imaginary parts are; $F'$ and $\int_a^bF$ are taken entry by entry.

What follows from one-variable calculus, each entrywise:

  • linearity; $\int CF = C\int F$ and $\int FC = (\int F)C$ for constant $C$;
  • $(FG)' = F'G + FG'$, and $(F^\dagger)' = (F')^\dagger$;
  • $\tfrac{d}{dt}\int_0^tF(s)\,ds = F(t)$ for continuous $F$, and $\int_0^\varepsilon F(t + s)\,ds = \int_t^{t+\varepsilon}F(s)\,ds$;
  • $F' \equiv 0$ on $\mathbb R$ implies $F$ constant;
  • $\tfrac1\varepsilon\int_0^\varepsilon F(s)\,ds \to F(0)$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$, for continuous $F$.
the derivative of $e^{-iHt}$

For Hermitian $H = \sum\lambda P_\lambda$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}e^{-iHt} = \frac{d}{dt}\sum_\lambda e^{-i\lambda t}P_\lambda$$ $$= \sum_\lambda(-i\lambda)e^{-i\lambda t}P_\lambda = -iHe^{-iHt}.$$

And $H$ commutes with $e^{-iHt}$: both are functions of $H$.

The determinant is a polynomial in the entries, so $\det F(t)$ is continuous when $F$ is.

that is all

These six facts are the only analysis in this unit. No norms on operators, no series beyond unit 00's exponential.

Why entrywise is enough. Each entry of $F(t)$ is a complex-valued function of one real variable, and a complex-valued function is differentiable or integrable exactly when its real and imaginary parts are. So every rule above is a rule of first-year calculus applied to finitely many functions at once.

The rules, in full. Product rule: $(FG)_{jk} = \sum_mF_{jm}G_{mk}$, a finite sum of products, so $(FG)'_{jk} = \sum_m(F'_{jm}G_{mk} + F_{jm}G'_{mk})$. Adjoint: $(F^\dagger)_{jk} = \overline{F_{kj}}$, and conjugation commutes with differentiation. Constants out of integrals: $(CF)_{jk} = \sum_mC_{jm}F_{mk}$ is a fixed linear combination of entries. Fundamental theorem, and shifting the variable: entrywise, by the one-variable substitution $s \mapsto s - t$. Zero derivative: the mean value theorem on each real and imaginary part of each entry. Averages: $|\tfrac1\varepsilon\int_0^\varepsilon f - f(0)| \le \max_{[0,\varepsilon]}|f - f(0)| \to 0$ for each continuous entry $f$.

Independence of the basis. Changing orthonormal basis replaces $F(t)$ by $WF(t)W^\dagger$ with $W$ a constant unitary; each entry of the new matrix is a fixed linear combination of the old entries, so continuity, derivatives and integrals do not depend on the basis chosen, and they transform the same way.

The exponential's derivative. Unit 00 defined $e^{-iHt} = \sum_\lambda e^{-i\lambda t}P_\lambda$ by the functional calculus. The $P_\lambda$ are constant matrices and $\tfrac{d}{dt}e^{-i\lambda t} = -i\lambda e^{-i\lambda t}$ (the derivative of $\cos\lambda t - i\sin\lambda t$), so differentiating term by term — a finite sum — gives $\sum(-i\lambda)e^{-i\lambda t}P_\lambda$, which is $-iH\cdot e^{-iHt}$ by the product rule of the functional calculus.

Stone's theorem

Stone's theorem, finite-dimensional

Let $t \mapsto U(t)$ be a continuous one-parameter unitary group on a finite-dimensional $V$. There is exactly one Hermitian $H$ with

$$U(t) = e^{-iHt}\quad\text{for all } t \in \mathbb R .$$

Conversely each Hermitian $H$ gives such a group (unit 00). $H$ is the generator of the group — its Hamiltonian.

The idea, in three steps. 1 Averaging $U$ over a short time gives a differentiable function — so $U$ itself is differentiable. 2 Differentiating the group law gives $U' = -iHU$ with $H$ Hermitian. 3 $e^{iHt}U(t)$ has zero derivative, so it is $I$.

In dimension one, $U(s) = e^{-ihs}$ runs round the unit circle. Its average over a short arc lies close to $1$ and is not zero; over a full turn the average is $0$. Step 1 needs the short arc.

History. Marshall Stone announced the theorem in 1930 and published the proof in "On one-parameter unitary groups in Hilbert space", Ann. Math. 33, 643 (1932). In infinite dimensions $H$ may be unbounded, defined only on a dense subspace, and the theorem is a statement about self-adjoint operators with domains; John von Neumann showed in the same volume (Ann. Math. 33, 567 (1932)) that weak measurability can replace continuity. The finite-dimensional case proved here keeps the structure of the proof and drops the domains.

The figure's numbers. With $h\varepsilon = 1.2$, the average $\tfrac1\varepsilon\int_0^\varepsilon e^{-ihs}\,ds = \dfrac{1 - e^{-ih\varepsilon}}{ih\varepsilon} = 0.777 - 0.531i$, of modulus $0.941$: inside the circle, near the arc, far from zero. With $h\varepsilon = 2\pi$ the average is exactly $0$ — the full turn cancels. In dimension one "invertible" means "nonzero", and the figure is step 1 of the proof: for short averaging windows the average is close to $U(0) = 1$, hence invertible.

Why this matters for the course. Textbooks usually state the Schrödinger equation $i\,d\psi/dt = H\psi$ as a separate postulate. Stone's theorem shows that, once evolution is a continuous unitary group, the equation and the Hermitian $H$ come for free: the group has a generator. What physics adds is which $H$ — and finding the $H$ of a given system is most of physics, and none of this course until unit 19.

Stone, the proof: U is differentiable, and H is Hermitian

Step 1: an invertible average. Let $A_\varepsilon := \tfrac1\varepsilon\int_0^\varepsilon U(s)\,ds$. By continuity $A_\varepsilon \to U(0) = I$, so $\det A_\varepsilon \to 1$: fix an $\varepsilon > 0$ with $A_\varepsilon$ invertible.

Step 2: $U$ is differentiable. With $F(t) := \int_0^tU(s)\,ds$, which has $F' = U$,

$$U(t)A_\varepsilon = \frac1\varepsilon\int_0^\varepsilon U(t + s)\,ds$$ $$= \frac1\varepsilon\big[F(t + \varepsilon) - F(t)\big].$$

The right side is differentiable in $t$; so is $U(t) = \big(U(t)A_\varepsilon\big)A_\varepsilon^{-1}$.

Step 3: the generator. Put $H := iU'(0)$. Differentiate $U(t + s) = U(t)U(s)$:

$$\text{in } s \text{ at } 0:\ \ U'(t) = U(t)U'(0) = -iU(t)H,$$ $$\text{in } t \text{ at } 0:\ \ U'(s) = U'(0)U(s) = -iHU(s).$$

Step 4: $H$ is Hermitian. Differentiate $U(t)^\dagger U(t) = I$ at $t = 0$, where $U(0) = I$:

$$U'(0)^\dagger + U'(0) = 0,$$ $$\text{i.e. }\ (-iH)^\dagger - iH = i(H^\dagger - H) = 0 .$$

Full proof, steps 1–4. Work in a fixed orthonormal basis, with the rules of The calculus this needs.

Step 1. $s \mapsto U(s)$ is continuous, so $A_\varepsilon = \tfrac1\varepsilon\int_0^\varepsilon U(s)\,ds \to U(0) = I$ entrywise as $\varepsilon \to 0$. The determinant is continuous in the entries, so $\det A_\varepsilon \to \det I = 1$, and there is $\varepsilon > 0$ with $\det A_\varepsilon \ne 0$. Fix it.

Step 2. Multiplying by the constant $U(t)$ passes inside the integral, and the group law gives $U(t)U(s) = U(t + s)$, so $U(t)A_\varepsilon = \tfrac1\varepsilon\int_0^\varepsilon U(t + s)\,ds = \tfrac1\varepsilon\int_t^{t+\varepsilon}U(s)\,ds$ (substitute $s \mapsto s - t$ in each entry), which is $\tfrac1\varepsilon[F(t + \varepsilon) - F(t)]$ with $F(t) = \int_0^tU$. By the fundamental theorem $F$ is differentiable with $F' = U$, so $t \mapsto U(t)A_\varepsilon$ is differentiable, with derivative $\tfrac1\varepsilon[U(t + \varepsilon) - U(t)]$. Multiplying on the right by the constant $A_\varepsilon^{-1}$ preserves differentiability. So $U$ is differentiable everywhere. (Its derivative, $\tfrac1\varepsilon[U(t + \varepsilon) - U(t)]A_\varepsilon^{-1}$, is continuous; repeating the argument shows $U$ is infinitely differentiable.)

Step 3. For fixed $t$, $s \mapsto U(t + s)$ and $s \mapsto U(t)U(s)$ are the same function; differentiate both at $s = 0$, the second with $U(t)$ constant: $U'(t) = U(t)U'(0)$. Likewise in the first variable: $U'(s) = U'(0)U(s)$. With $U'(0) = -iH$ (that is what $H := iU'(0)$ means), these are $U' = -iUH = -iHU$; in particular $H$ commutes with every $U(t)$.

Step 4. $t \mapsto U(t)^\dagger U(t)$ is the constant $I$. Its derivative, by the product and adjoint rules, is $U'(t)^\dagger U(t) + U(t)^\dagger U'(t)$; at $t = 0$ this is $U'(0)^\dagger + U'(0) = 0$. Now $(-iH)^\dagger = iH^\dagger$, so $iH^\dagger - iH = 0$ and $H^\dagger = H$.

Where continuity was used. Only in step 1. Problem P8 shows it cannot be dropped: there are group homomorphisms $\mathbb R \to U(1)$ that are not of the form $e^{-iht}$.

The crystal. Certain crystals, pumped with a laser, occasionally convert one photon into two of lower energy, emitted in correlated directions — spontaneous parametric down-conversion. Such pairs are the standard source for the experiments of units 03, 04 and 06. Nothing about them is used here.

How the generalisations are derived. A measurement on a system $S$ carried out by coupling it to an auxiliary system $E$, evolving the pair unitarily (postulate 3) and measuring $E$ projectively (postulate 2) is described, on $S$ alone, by a POVM; the same picture with nothing measured gives a channel. The density operator is what remains of a pure state of $S \otimes E$ when $E$ is not looked at. That is the thesis of Part A.

Uncertainty: a statement about preparations

Two observables that do not commute cannot both be sharp on one state. Cauchy–Schwarz says by how much; the interferometer meets the bound exactly.

01 · uncertainty

The Robertson uncertainty relation

the Robertson uncertainty relation (1929)

For Hermitian $A$, $B$ and every state $\psi$,

$$\Delta A\,\Delta B \;\ge\; \tfrac12\big|\langle[A, B]\rangle\big| .$$
proof

Let $a = (A - \langle A\rangle)\psi$, $b = (B - \langle B\rangle)\psi$, so $\lVert a\rVert = \Delta A$ and $\lVert b\rVert = \Delta B$. Expanding, $\braket{a|b} = \langle AB\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$, and $\braket{b|a} = \overline{\braket{a|b}} = \langle BA\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$. So

$$\mathrm{Im}\braket{a|b} = \frac{\braket{a|b} - \braket{b|a}}{2i} = \frac{\langle[A, B]\rangle}{2i}.$$

Cauchy–Schwarz: $\Delta A\,\Delta B \ge |\braket{a|b}| \ge |\mathrm{Im}\braket{a|b}|$. $\ \blacksquare$

$\braket{a|b}$ lies in the disc of radius $\Delta A\,\Delta B$ (Cauchy–Schwarz). Its imaginary part is fixed by the commutator, so the disc must reach at least that high.

$\langle[A, B]\rangle$ is purely imaginary: $[A, B]^\dagger = [B, A] = -[A, B]$.

Full proof. $\langle A\rangle$ and $\langle B\rangle$ are real, so $A - \langle A\rangle$ and $B - \langle B\rangle$ are Hermitian, and $\lVert a\rVert^2 = (\Delta A)^2$ by Expectation and variance, from the measure. Then $\braket{a|b} = \braket{\psi|(A - \langle A\rangle)(B - \langle B\rangle)|\psi} = \langle AB\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle + \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle = \langle AB\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$. Exchanging the roles gives $\braket{b|a} = \langle BA\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$, and conjugate symmetry says it is $\overline{\braket{a|b}}$. Subtract: $\braket{a|b} - \braket{b|a} = \langle[A, B]\rangle$, and for any complex $z$, $z - \bar z = 2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,z$. Finally $|\braket{a|b}| \le \lVert a\rVert\lVert b\rVert$ (unit 00) and $|\mathrm{Im}\,z| \le |z|$.

A stronger form, by the same lines. $|\braket{a|b}|^2 = (\mathrm{Re}\braket{a|b})^2 + (\mathrm{Im}\braket{a|b})^2$, and $\mathrm{Re}\braket{a|b} = \tfrac12\langle AB + BA\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$. So $(\Delta A)^2(\Delta B)^2 \ge \big(\tfrac12\langle AB + BA\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle\big)^2 + \tfrac14|\langle[A, B]\rangle|^2$ — Schrödinger's form (1930). The extra term is a covariance; Robertson drops it.

History. H. P. Robertson, "The uncertainty principle", Phys. Rev. 34, 163 (1929). E. Schrödinger, Sitzungsber. Preuss. Akad. Wiss. 14, 296 (1930). Heisenberg's 1927 paper, which started the subject, argued something different — see Uncertainty is not disturbance.

When the bound is empty. If $[A, B] = 0$ the right side is zero and the relation says nothing — consistent with Compatible observables, where commuting observables have joint distributions and can both be sharp on a common eigenvector.

When it is an equality, and X against Y

the equality case

$\Delta A\,\Delta B = \tfrac12|\langle[A, B]\rangle|$ if and only if $a = 0$, or $b = i\kappa a$ for some real $\kappa$.

proof

Both inequalities must be equalities. Cauchy–Schwarz: $a$, $b$ dependent. $|\mathrm{Im}\,z| = |z|$: $\braket{a|b}$ purely imaginary. If $a \ne 0$, $b = ca$ and $\braket{a|b} = c\lVert a\rVert^2$, so $c \in i\mathbb R$. $\ \blacksquare$

Unit 00 computed $[X, Y] = 2iZ$. So for every qubit state

$$\Delta X\,\Delta Y \ge |\langle Z\rangle| .$$
at $\ket0$

$\langle X\rangle = \braket{0|1} = 0$ and $X^2 = I$, so $\Delta X = 1$. Likewise $Y\ket0 = i\ket1$, so $\langle Y\rangle = i\braket{0|1} = 0$ and $\Delta Y = 1$. And $\langle Z\rangle = 1$:

$$\Delta X\,\Delta Y = 1 = |\langle Z\rangle| .$$

Equality, as the criterion predicts: $a = X\ket0 = \ket1$ and $b = Y\ket0 = i\ket1 = i\,a$ ✓.

Reading. $\ket0$ is sharp for $Z$; for $X$ and for $Y$ it is a fair coin (unit 00: the three bases are mutually unbiased), and the bound says no state does better on both at once.

The case $a = 0$. Then $\Delta A = 0$: $\psi$ is an eigenvector of $A$, and $\langle[A, B]\rangle = 0$ — indeed $\braket{\psi|AB - BA|\psi} = \lambda\braket{\psi|B\psi} - \lambda\braket{\psi|B\psi} = 0$ for $A\psi = \lambda\psi$. Both sides vanish. The interesting equality cases are those with both spreads nonzero.

The numbers at $\ket0$ in full. $X\ket0 = \ket1$, so $\langle X\rangle = \braket{0|1} = 0$; $Y\ket0 = i\ket1$, so $\langle Y\rangle = i\braket{0|1} = 0$; $X^2 = Y^2 = I$, so $\langle X^2\rangle = \langle Y^2\rangle = 1$ and both variances are $1 - 0 = 1$. $\langle Z\rangle = \braket{0|0} = 1$. Here $\langle X\rangle = \langle Y\rangle = 0$, so $a = X\ket0$ and $b = Y\ket0$ exactly.

Problem P10 uses the criterion — not the formula of the next slide — to find every qubit state on which $\Delta X\,\Delta Y = |\langle Z\rangle|$, and asks which of them make the bound non-trivial.

Worked: X and Y on every qubit state

Robertson for $X$, $Y$ on $\psi = \cos\tfrac\theta2\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket1$, $\ 0 \le \theta \le \pi$

1. Every state has this form up to global phase: make the $\ket0$ amplitude real and $\ge 0$; then $|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$ fixes $\theta$. Normalised: $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$ ✓.

2. Expectations, with $\langle X\rangle = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\bar ab)$, $\langle Y\rangle = 2\,\mathrm{Im}(\bar ab)$ for $\psi = (a, b)$:

$$\langle X\rangle = \sin\theta\cos\varphi,\quad \langle Y\rangle = \sin\theta\sin\varphi,$$ $$\langle Z\rangle = \cos\theta .$$

3. Spreads. $X^2 = Y^2 = I$, so $(\Delta X)^2 = 1 - \sin^2\theta\cos^2\varphi$, $(\Delta Y)^2 = 1 - \sin^2\theta\sin^2\varphi$.

4. Multiply and use $\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1$:

$$(\Delta X\,\Delta Y)^2 = \cos^2\theta + \sin^4\theta\,\sin^2\varphi\cos^2\varphi$$ $$\ge \cos^2\theta = \langle Z\rangle^2 \ ✓.$$

$\Delta X\,\Delta Y$ (solid) against the bound $|\cos\theta|$ (dashed), at $\varphi = \pi/4$. They touch only at $\theta = 0, \pi$.

5. Equality iff $\sin\theta = 0$ or $\sin2\varphi = 0$: $\ket0$, $\ket1$, or a relative phase in $\{0, \tfrac\pi2, \pi, \tfrac{3\pi}2\}$.

6. A number. $\theta = \tfrac\pi3$, $\varphi = 0.4$: $\Delta X\,\Delta Y = 0.5678 \ge |\langle Z\rangle| = 0.5$ ✓.

Step 1 in full. A unit vector $(a, b)$: multiply by the phase $\bar a/|a|$ (if $a \ne 0$) to make $a = |a| \ge 0$. Then $|a| \le 1$, so $|a| = \cos\tfrac\theta2$ for exactly one $\theta \in [0, \pi]$, and $|b| = \sqrt{1 - |a|^2} = \sin\tfrac\theta2$, so $b = e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2$. If $a = 0$, the state is $\ket1$ up to phase: $\theta = \pi$. That these two angles are coordinates on a sphere is unit 02's business; here they are just parameters.

Step 2 in full. $X\psi = (b, a)$, so $\langle X\rangle = \bar ab + \bar ba = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\bar ab)$. $Y\psi = (-ib, ia)$, so $\langle Y\rangle = -i\bar ab + i\bar ba = 2\,\mathrm{Im}(\bar ab)$. With $a = \cos\tfrac\theta2$ and $b = e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2$, $\bar ab = \tfrac12\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi}$ (double-angle formula). $\langle Z\rangle = |a|^2 - |b|^2 = \cos\theta$.

Step 4 in full. $(1 - s^2c_\varphi^2)(1 - s^2s_\varphi^2) = 1 - s^2(c_\varphi^2 + s_\varphi^2) + s^4c_\varphi^2s_\varphi^2 = 1 - s^2 + s^4c_\varphi^2s_\varphi^2$ with $s = \sin\theta$, and $1 - \sin^2\theta = \cos^2\theta$. The gap $\sin^4\theta\sin^2\varphi\cos^2\varphi = \tfrac14\sin^4\theta\sin^22\varphi$ is zero exactly when $\sin\theta = 0$ or $\sin2\varphi = 0$.

The numbers. At $\theta = \pi/3$, $\varphi = 0.4$: $(\Delta X\Delta Y)^2 = 0.25 + 0.5625\cdot\sin^2(0.8)/4 = 0.32237$, so $\Delta X\Delta Y = 0.56777$. The figure is computed at $\varphi = \pi/4$, where the gap is largest; at $\theta = \pi/2$ the product is $0.5$ and the bound is $0$.

Path against output, in the interferometer

Two questions about the same state between the splitters: which path (a meter in the arms, $Z$), or which output (the second splitter and the detectors, $W$).

The output observable. Splitter then $Z$ is $W := B^\dagger ZB$ on the state before the splitter. With $B = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(I + iX)$ and $XZX = -Z$:

$$W = \tfrac12(I - iX)Z(I + iX) = \tfrac i2[Z, X] = -Y .$$

On $\psi_\varphi = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + ie^{i\varphi}\ket1)$, by the worked example's formulae with $\theta = \tfrac\pi2$ and phase $\varphi + \tfrac\pi2$:

path$\langle Z\rangle = 0$$\Delta Z = 1$
output$\langle W\rangle = -\cos\varphi$$\Delta W = |\sin\varphi|$
bound$\tfrac12|\langle[Z, W]\rangle| = |\langle X\rangle| = |\sin\varphi|$
equality, for every $\varphi$

$\Delta Z\,\Delta W = |\sin\varphi|$, exactly the bound. Check: $\langle W\rangle = P(D_0) - P(D_1) = \sin^2\tfrac\varphi2 - \cos^2\tfrac\varphi2$ ✓. At $\varphi = 0$ the output is certain and the path is a fair coin.

Why $B^\dagger ZB$. Applying $B$ and then measuring $Z$ gives result $\lambda$ with probability $\braket{B\psi|P_\lambda|B\psi} = \braket{\psi|B^\dagger P_\lambda B|\psi}$, and the $B^\dagger P_\lambda B$ are the spectral projectors of $B^\dagger ZB$ (conjugating by a unitary preserves products, sums, adjoints and the identity). So "splitter, then detectors" is a measurement of $B^\dagger ZB$ on the state before the splitter — Hermitian, as a conjugate of a Hermitian operator by a unitary.

Computing $W$. $(I - iX)Z(I + iX) = Z + iZX - iXZ + XZX = Z + i[Z, X] - Z$, since $XZX = -XXZ = -Z$. And multiplying out, $ZX = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix} = iY$ and $XZ = -iY$, so $[Z, X] = 2iY$ and $W = \tfrac i2\cdot2iY = -Y$.

The table in full. $\psi_\varphi$ has $a = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, $b = \tfrac{ie^{i\varphi}}{\sqrt2}$, so $\bar ab = \tfrac i2e^{i\varphi}$, with real part $-\tfrac12\sin\varphi$ and imaginary part $\tfrac12\cos\varphi$. Hence $\langle X\rangle = -\sin\varphi$, $\langle Y\rangle = \cos\varphi$, $\langle Z\rangle = 0$, so $\langle W\rangle = -\cos\varphi$, and since $Z^2 = W^2 = I$, $\Delta Z = 1$, $\Delta W = \sqrt{1 - \cos^2\varphi} = |\sin\varphi|$. The commutator: $[Z, W] = -[Z, Y] = [Y, Z] = 2iX$, since multiplying out gives $YZ = iX$ and $ZY = -iX$; so the bound is $\tfrac12|2i\langle X\rangle| = |\sin\varphi|$.

Equality by the criterion. $a = Z\psi_\varphi = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - ie^{i\varphi}\ket1)$, and $b = (W + \cos\varphi)\psi_\varphi$ works out to $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-i\sin\varphi\ket0 - \sin\varphi\,e^{i\varphi}\ket1) = -i\sin\varphi\cdot a$: an imaginary multiple, as the criterion demands. In the worked example's terms, $\psi_\varphi$ has $\theta = \pi/2$, so $\langle Z\rangle = 0$, which is one of the equality conditions for the pair $Z, Y$.

01 · uncertainty

Uncertainty is not disturbance

One preparation, many copies. Half are measured for $A$, the other half for $B$; no copy is measured twice. $\Delta A$ and $\Delta B$ are the spreads of the two histograms.

what Robertson says

$\Delta A$ and $\Delta B$ are computed from $\psi$ alone, each from its own Born distribution. The relation limits preparations: no state makes both distributions narrow beyond the bound.

what it does not say

Nothing about how much measuring $A$ disturbs a later measurement of $B$. Heisenberg's 1927 argument — a microscope whose light kicks the electron it sees — was about disturbance, a different claim. A correct disturbance relation needs further definitions (Ozawa 2003, cited).

Example. At $\ket0$, $\Delta X\,\Delta Y \ge 1$ before anything has been measured at all.

The distinction, precisely. Robertson's left side is a function of the state: prepare $\psi$ many times, measure $A$ on some copies and $B$ on others, and compute the two standard deviations. Its right side is also a function of the state. The inequality therefore cannot be about any interaction between the two measurements — there is none; they are made on different copies. Problem P11 makes this an argument.

Disturbance. W. Heisenberg, "Über den anschaulichen Inhalt der quantentheoretischen Kinematik und Mechanik", Z. Phys. 43, 172 (1927), argued that observing an electron's position with light of short wavelength gives it a momentum kick, so that position accuracy and momentum disturbance trade off. That is a statement about a sequence of operations on one system, and making it precise needs a definition of the error of a measurement and of its disturbance. M. Ozawa, "Universally valid reformulation of the Heisenberg uncertainty principle on noise and disturbance in measurement", Phys. Rev. A 67, 042105 (2003), gave one, and showed that the naive product of error and disturbance can fall below $\tfrac12|\langle[A, B]\rangle|$; his corrected relation has extra terms. Neutron experiments confirmed it (J. Erhart et al., Nature Phys. 8, 185 (2012), cited). None of this is developed in the course.

Where disturbance does appear. In section 3: measuring the path destroyed the fringes. That is a fact about Lüders' rule, not about Robertson's relation, and unit 09 turns it into a security guarantee — an eavesdropper who measures leaves a trace.

The dark detector, answered

the question, from the overview

Why does opening a second path make $D_0$ go dark, when either path alone lights it?

the answer

The photon's path is a qubit, and each route to $D_0$ carries a complex amplitude: $\tfrac12$ by path 0, $-\tfrac12$ by path 1. Amplitudes add; their sum is $0$; the Born rule squares it. A blocked path deletes one amplitude, and $|\tfrac12|^2 = \tfrac14$.

  • Section 1: a slab changes a relative phase; slabs in both arms, a global one.
  • Section 2: $\sin^2\tfrac\varphi2$, $\cos^2\tfrac\varphi2$ from the Born rule.
  • Section 3: recording the path turns amplitudes back into probabilities.
  • Section 4: one unitary, each splitter $e^{-iHt}$.
  • Section 5: path and output meet Robertson's bound exactly.

The next step, on the sheet: an absorber that may or may not be in path 1. If $D_0$ ever fires, it is there — though the photon that fired $D_0$ never reached it.

The running example, closed. Every claim in its hover cards has been derived: the splitter as a unitary and $B^2 = iX$ (section 1), the fringes with their sum checked and the known cases $\varphi = 0, \pi$ (section 2), the which-path detector's $\tfrac12, \tfrac12$ for every $\varphi$ (section 3), $M(\varphi)$ and $B = e^{i\pi X/4}$ (section 4), and the equality $\Delta Z\,\Delta W = |\sin\varphi| = |\langle X\rangle|$ (section 5).

What "amplitudes, not probabilities" means here. Not that probabilities are wrong — the postulates produce them — but that the quantity which composes along a route and adds over routes is a complex number, and only at the end of an unrecorded set of routes is its modulus squared. Recording a route (section 3) moves the squaring earlier, and the fringes go.

The bomb. Elitzur and Vaidman (Found. Phys. 23, 987 (1993)) asked: if a bomb in path 1 explodes when a photon reaches it, can the interferometer tell a live bomb from a dud without exploding it? Problem P6 answers it with this unit's postulates — the bomb is a which-path detector that absorbs — and problem P7 pushes the success rate toward one. That computation is half the checkpoint.
