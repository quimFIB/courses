One qubit: the Bloch sphere, and the group that rotates it twice

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. On paper and without notes, given any \(2\times2\) unitary you produce its rotation axis and angle on the Bloch sphere and its ZYZ decomposition \(e^{i\alpha}R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta)\); and you prove that \(\Phi: SU(2)\to SO(3)\) is surjective with kernel \(\{\pm I\}\).

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Eleven problems, about five hours. Work them on paper first.

Conventions, as in the deck: \(\braket{u|v}\) is conjugate-linear in the first slot; \(\ket0 = (1, 0)\), \(\ket1 = (0, 1)\); \(X, Y, Z\) the Pauli matrices, \(\boldsymbol\sigma = (X, Y, Z)\); the Bloch vector \(\mathbf r = \braket{\psi|\boldsymbol\sigma|\psi}\), so \(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\); and

\[ R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) = e^{-i\theta\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2} = \cos\tfrac\theta2\,I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma, \]

which turns the sphere by \(\theta\) about \(\mathbf n\), anticlockwise seen from the tip of \(\mathbf n\). \(H = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(X + Z)\), \(S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i)\), \(T = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4})\). Where a proton appears, \(\hbar\) is restored and the signs are the deck's: \(\gamma \gt 0\), \(H = -\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), and in the rotating frame \(\tilde H = -\tfrac\hbar2(\Delta Z + \Omega X)\) with \(\Delta = \omega_0 - \omega\). Proton: \(\gamma/2\pi = 42.577\) MHz/T.

Some solutions end with an optional check by the course's referee, qclib, run from the course directory with uv run python. Every solution is complete without it.

The sphere

P1 Unit 00's debt, paid in full medium

exercises: the transition-probability formula, antipodes as bases, surjectivity of \(\Phi\) used as a tool

Unit 00 showed that once the \(z\) and \(x\) bases are fixed, the only basis unbiased to both is \(y\), and left open whether every pair of mutually unbiased bases looks like \(z, x\).

  1. Prove that two orthonormal bases of \(\mathbb C^2\) are mutually unbiased if and only if their Bloch axes are perpendicular, and conclude that \(\mathbb C^2\) holds at most three pairwise mutually unbiased bases.
  2. Let \(\mathcal B\), \(\mathcal B'\) be any two mutually unbiased bases. Prove that there is a unitary \(U\) carrying \(\mathcal B\) onto the \(z\) basis and \(\mathcal B'\) onto the \(x\) basis, each vector up to a phase.
  3. Deduce that, given \(\mathcal B\) and \(\mathcal B'\), there is exactly one basis unbiased to both, up to the order and phases of its vectors.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything is a statement about lines through the origin of \(\mathbb R^3\). Translate "basis", "unbiased" and "a unitary" into the sphere's language, then argue about lines.

Rung 2

An orthonormal basis is a pair of orthogonal states, which are antipodes \(\pm\mathbf u\): an axis. The four overlaps between bases at \(\pm\mathbf u\) and \(\pm\mathbf v\) are \(\tfrac12(1 \pm \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\). For part 2, a unitary moves points of the sphere by a rotation, and every rotation is \(\Phi\) of something.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "Let the bases have axes \(\mathbf u \perp \mathbf v\). The frame \((\mathbf u, \mathbf v, \mathbf u\times\mathbf v)\) is orthonormal and right-handed, so the matrix \(R\) with \(R\mathbf u = \hat{\mathbf z}\), \(R\mathbf v = \hat{\mathbf x}\), \(R(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v) = \hat{\mathbf y}\) is in \(SO(3)\)."

Worked solution

Idea. On the Bloch sphere a basis is an axis and unbiasedness is perpendicularity; unitaries are all the rotations. So questions about unbiased bases become questions about orthonormal frames in \(\mathbb R^3\), where the answers are obvious.

1. If \(\{e_1, e_2\}\) is orthonormal, \(|\braket{e_2|e_1}|^2 = 0 = \tfrac12(1 + \mathbf r_1\cdot\mathbf r_2)\) forces \(\mathbf r_2 = -\mathbf r_1\): the basis is the axis \(\pm\mathbf u\). With \(\{f_1, f_2\}\) at \(\pm\mathbf v\), the four squared overlaps are \(\tfrac12(1 + (\pm\mathbf u)\cdot(\pm\mathbf v))\), i.e. \(\tfrac12(1 + \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\) twice and \(\tfrac12(1 - \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\) twice. All equal \(\tfrac12\) iff \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = 0\). Pairwise unbiased bases therefore give pairwise perpendicular unit vectors, which are linearly independent, so there are at most three. \(\blacksquare\)

2. Let \(\mathcal B\) have axis \(\pm\mathbf u\) and \(\mathcal B'\) axis \(\pm\mathbf v\), with \(\mathbf u\perp\mathbf v\) by part 1. Take \(\mathbf w = \mathbf u\times\mathbf v\); then \((\mathbf u, \mathbf v, \mathbf w)\) is a right-handed orthonormal frame, as is \((\hat{\mathbf z}, \hat{\mathbf x}, \hat{\mathbf y})\) since \(\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf x} = \hat{\mathbf y}\). The linear map \(R\) sending the first frame to the second is orthogonal (it maps an orthonormal basis to one), and has determinant \(+1\) because both frames are right-handed. By the surjectivity of \(\Phi\) there is \(U\in SU(2)\) with \(\Phi(U) = R\). By the deck's corollary, \(U\) sends the state with Bloch vector \(\mathbf u\) to the state with Bloch vector \(R\mathbf u = \hat{\mathbf z}\), which is \(\ket0\) up to phase; likewise \(-\mathbf u\mapsto-\hat{\mathbf z}\) (\(\ket1\)) and \(\pm\mathbf v\mapsto\pm\hat{\mathbf x}\) (\(\ket\pm\)). \(\blacksquare\)

3. A basis unbiased to both has an axis perpendicular to \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\): the line \(\mathbb R\mathbf w\), and no other. Its two points \(\pm\mathbf w\) are two states, determined up to phase by the bijection, and they form a basis. So there is exactly one third basis, up to order and phases. Through the unitary of part 2 it is unit 00's \(y\) basis, recovering unit 00's computation. \(\blacksquare\)

Check. For \(\mathcal B = z\), \(\mathcal B' = x\): \(\mathbf u = \hat{\mathbf z}\), \(\mathbf v = \hat{\mathbf x}\), \(R = I\), \(U = I\), and \(\mathbf w = \hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf x} = \hat{\mathbf y}\) — the \(y\) basis, as unit 00 found. A second check: \(\mathcal B = x\), \(\mathcal B' = z\) gives \(R\) the half-turn swapping \(\hat{\mathbf x}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf z}\), and \(U\) may be taken as the Hadamard gate up to phase, which does swap the two bases.

P2 Unbiased to all three routine

exercises: measuring along an axis as \(\tfrac12(1 \pm \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)\), the inverse of the Bloch map, a computed state

  1. Show that no pure state of a qubit is unbiased to all three of the \(z\), \(x\), \(y\) bases at once (every outcome probability \(\tfrac12\)).
  2. Find every pure state that is unbiased to both \(z\) and \(x\).
  3. Find the states whose "up" probabilities along \(z\), \(x\) and \(y\) are all equal, give that common probability as a number, and write one such state as \(\cos\tfrac\theta2\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket1\) with \(\theta\) and \(\varphi\) explicit.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each question is about the Bloch vector \(\mathbf r\) of the state, a unit vector, and its three components.

Rung 2

The probability of "up" along \(\mathbf n\) is \(\tfrac12(1 + \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)\). Unbiased along \(\mathbf n\) means \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r = 0\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: "Unbiased to \(z\), \(x\) and \(y\) means \(r_3 = r_1 = r_2 = 0\), but \(|\mathbf r| = 1\)."

Worked solution

Idea. Unbiased to the basis along \(\mathbf n\) is "perpendicular to \(\mathbf n\)". A unit vector cannot be perpendicular to three perpendicular axes.

1. Along \(\mathbf n\) the outcome probabilities are \(\tfrac12(1 \pm \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)\), both \(\tfrac12\) iff \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r = 0\). Unbiased to \(z\), \(x\), \(y\) would need \(r_3 = r_1 = r_2 = 0\), contradicting \(|\mathbf r| = 1\). \(\blacksquare\)

2. \(r_3 = r_1 = 0\) and \(|\mathbf r| = 1\) give \(\mathbf r = \pm\hat{\mathbf y}\): the states \(\ket{+i}\) and \(\ket{-i}\), up to phase. This is unit 00's "the third basis is forced", now as a one-liner.

3. Equal "up" probabilities mean \(r_1 = r_2 = r_3\), so \(\mathbf r = \pm\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1)\). For the \(+\) sign the common probability is

\[ \tfrac12\Big(1 + \tfrac1{\sqrt3}\Big) = 0.7887, \]

and for the \(-\) sign \(\tfrac12(1 - \tfrac1{\sqrt3}) = 0.2113\). For \(\mathbf r = \tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1)\): \(\cos\theta = \tfrac1{\sqrt3}\), so \(\theta = 54.74^\circ\), and \(\varphi = 45^\circ\) since \(r_1 = r_2 \gt 0\). With \(\cos^2\tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac12(1 + \cos\theta) = 0.7887\):

\[ \ket\psi = 0.8881\,\ket0 + e^{i\pi/4}\,0.4597\,\ket1 . \]

Check. Normalised: \(0.8881^2 + 0.4597^2 = 0.7887 + 0.2113 = 1.0000\) ✓. The probability along \(z\) is \(0.8881^2 = 0.7887\) ✓. Along \(x\): \(r_1 = 2\,\mathrm{Re}(\bar ab) = 2\cdot0.8881\cdot0.4597\cos45^\circ = 0.5774 = \tfrac1{\sqrt3}\) ✓, so the \(x\) "up" probability is \(\tfrac12(1 + 0.5774) = 0.7887\) ✓. Each pair of outcome probabilities sums to one.

P3 Axis and angle medium

exercises: splitting off a phase, reading \(R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)\) from a matrix by traces, the checkpoint's first half

  1. Prove the recipe: for \(U\in U(2)\), choose \(\alpha\) with \(e^{2i\alpha} = \det U\) and set \(V = e^{-i\alpha}U\); then \(V = R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)\) with \(\cos\tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}V\) and \(n_k\sin\tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac i2\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_kV)\). Replacing \(\alpha\) by \(\alpha + \pi\) flips the sign of \(V\); show you can always choose \(\theta\in[0, \pi]\).
  2. Find the global phase \(e^{i\alpha}\), the axis \(\mathbf n\) and the angle \(\theta\in[0, \pi]\) of \(S\), of \(T\), and of \(\sqrt X := \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1 + i&1 - i\\1 - i&1 + i\end{pmatrix}\) (check that it squares to \(X\)).
  3. The same for \(SH = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\i&-i\end{pmatrix}\), and say where it sends each of the axes \(\hat{\mathbf x}, \hat{\mathbf y}, \hat{\mathbf z}\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer for each matrix is three things: a phase, a unit vector, an angle. The deck proved every element of \(SU(2)\) has the form \(\cos\tfrac\theta2 I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\); read the coefficients off with traces.

Rung 2

Trace orthogonality: \(\operatorname{tr}I = 2\), \(\operatorname{tr}\sigma_k = 0\), \(\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_k\sigma_j) = 2\delta_{jk}\). So \(\operatorname{tr}V = 2\cos\tfrac\theta2\) and \(\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_kV) = -2i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,n_k\). For diagonal matrices like \(S\) and \(T\), compare with \(R_z(\theta) = \mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\theta/2}, e^{i\theta/2})\) directly.

Rung 3

First line for \(SH\): "\(\det(SH) = \tfrac12(-i - i) = -i\), so \(e^{2i\alpha} = -i\) and \(\alpha = -\tfrac\pi4\); then \(V = e^{i\pi/4}SH\) has \(\operatorname{tr}V = e^{i\pi/4}\tfrac{1 - i}{\sqrt2} = 1\)."

Worked solution

Idea. Split off the determinant's square root to land in \(SU(2)\); there every element is \(\cos\tfrac\theta2I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), and the Pauli basis's trace orthogonality reads off the four coefficients.

1. \(\det V = e^{-2i\alpha}\det U = 1\), so \(V\in SU(2)\) and \(V = cI - is\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) with \(c = \cos\tfrac\theta2\), \(s = \sin\tfrac\theta2\). Then \(\operatorname{tr}V = 2c\) and \(\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_kV) = c\operatorname{tr}\sigma_k - is\sum_jn_j\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_k\sigma_j) = -2is\,n_k\), so \(n_ks = \tfrac i2\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_kV)\). Replacing \(V\) by \(-V\) replaces \((c, s\mathbf n)\) by \((-c, -s\mathbf n)\), i.e. \(\theta\) by \(2\pi - \theta\) and \(\mathbf n\) by \(-\mathbf n\) (as \(\cos(\pi - \tfrac\theta2) = -c\), \(\sin(\pi - \tfrac\theta2) = s\)); so choose the sign with \(c \ge 0\), giving \(\theta\in[0, \pi]\). When \(s = 0\) (\(V = \pm I\)) every axis works. \(\blacksquare\)

2. \(S\): \(\det S = i\), \(\alpha = \tfrac\pi4\), \(V = e^{-i\pi/4}\mathrm{diag}(1, i) = \mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\pi/4}, e^{i\pi/4}) = R_z(\tfrac\pi2)\). Phase \(e^{i\pi/4}\), axis \(\hat{\mathbf z}\), angle \(\tfrac\pi2\).
\(T\): \(\det T = e^{i\pi/4}\), \(\alpha = \tfrac\pi8\), \(V = \mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\pi/8}, e^{i\pi/8}) = R_z(\tfrac\pi4)\). Phase \(e^{i\pi/8}\), axis \(\hat{\mathbf z}\), angle \(\tfrac\pi4\).
\(\sqrt X\): \((\sqrt X)^2 = \tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}(1 + i)^2 + (1 - i)^2 & 2(1 + i)(1 - i)\\ \cdot & \cdot\end{pmatrix} = \tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}0&4\\4&0\end{pmatrix} = X\) ✓ (the second row is the first reversed, by symmetry). \(\det\sqrt X = \tfrac14\big((1 + i)^2 - (1 - i)^2\big) = \tfrac14(2i + 2i) = i\), so \(\alpha = \tfrac\pi4\) and \(V = e^{-i\pi/4}\sqrt X\). \(\operatorname{tr}V = e^{-i\pi/4}(1 + i) = \sqrt2\), so \(\cos\tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\), \(\theta = \tfrac\pi2\). \(\operatorname{tr}(XV) = e^{-i\pi/4}(1 - i) = -i\sqrt2\), so \(n_1s = \tfrac i2(-i\sqrt2) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\); \(\operatorname{tr}(YV)\) and \(\operatorname{tr}(ZV)\) vanish (the matrix is symmetric with equal diagonal). So \(\mathbf n = \hat{\mathbf x}\): \(\sqrt X = e^{i\pi/4}R_x(\tfrac\pi2)\), a quarter-turn about \(x\) — half of \(X = iR_x(\pi)\), as its name promises.

3. \(\det(SH) = -i\), \(\alpha = -\tfrac\pi4\), \(V = e^{i\pi/4}SH = \tfrac{e^{i\pi/4}}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\i&-i\end{pmatrix}\). \(\operatorname{tr}V = \tfrac{e^{i\pi/4}(1 - i)}{\sqrt2} = e^{i\pi/4}e^{-i\pi/4} = 1\), so \(\cos\tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac12\), \(\theta = \tfrac{2\pi}3\), \(s = \tfrac{\sqrt3}2\). Next, with \(\kappa = e^{i\pi/4}/\sqrt2\): entrywise, \(\operatorname{tr}(\sigma V) = \sum_{jk}\sigma_{jk}V_{kj}\) gives \(\operatorname{tr}(XV) = V_{10} + V_{01} = \kappa(i + 1) = i\), \(\operatorname{tr}(YV) = -iV_{10} + iV_{01} = \kappa(1 + i) = i\), \(\operatorname{tr}(ZV) = V_{00} - V_{11} = \kappa(1 + i) = i\), using \(\kappa(1 + i) = \tfrac{e^{i\pi/4}\sqrt2e^{i\pi/4}}{\sqrt2} = i\). So \(n_ks = \tfrac i2\cdot i = -\tfrac12\) for every \(k\), and \(\mathbf n = -\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1)\).

\[ SH = e^{-i\pi/4}\,R_{\mathbf n}\big(\tfrac{2\pi}3\big),\qquad \mathbf n = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1). \]

A third of a turn about \(-(1,1,1)\) permutes the axes cyclically: \(\hat{\mathbf z}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf y}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf x}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf z}\). Directly: \(H\) swaps \(\hat{\mathbf z}, \hat{\mathbf x}\) and reverses \(\hat{\mathbf y}\); \(S\) turns \(\hat{\mathbf x}\to\hat{\mathbf y}\to-\hat{\mathbf x}\); so \(SH\) takes \(\hat{\mathbf z}\to\hat{\mathbf x}\to\hat{\mathbf y}\), \(\hat{\mathbf x}\to\hat{\mathbf z}\to\hat{\mathbf z}\), \(\hat{\mathbf y}\to-\hat{\mathbf y}\to\hat{\mathbf x}\) ✓.

Check. \(|\mathbf n|^2 = 3\cdot\tfrac13 = 1\) ✓. \(c^2 + s^2 = \tfrac14 + \tfrac34 = 1\) ✓. Rotating three times by \(\tfrac{2\pi}3\) is a full turn, so \((SH)^3 = e^{-3i\pi/4}R_{\mathbf n}(2\pi) = -e^{-3i\pi/4}I = e^{i\pi/4}I\); multiplying out \((SH)^3\) gives exactly that scalar.

import numpy as np
from qclib import gates, linalg
def axis_angle(U):
    alpha = np.angle(np.linalg.det(U)) / 2
    V = np.exp(-1j * alpha) * U
    if np.trace(V).real < 0:                       # choose the root with theta in [0, pi]
        V, alpha = -V, alpha + np.pi
    theta = 2 * np.arccos(min(1.0, np.trace(V).real / 2))
    s_n = np.array([(1j * np.trace(P @ V) / 2).real for P in (gates.X, gates.Y, gates.Z)])
    return alpha, theta, s_n / np.sin(theta / 2)
sqrtX = np.array([[1 + 1j, 1 - 1j], [1 - 1j, 1 + 1j]]) / 2
for name, U in [("S", gates.S), ("T", gates.T), ("sqrtX", sqrtX), ("SH", gates.S @ gates.H)]:
    a, th, n = axis_angle(U)
    ok = np.allclose(U, np.exp(1j * a) * gates.rotation(n, th))
    print(name, round(a / np.pi, 4), round(th / np.pi, 4), np.round(n, 4), ok)

P4 Hamiltonians are precessions routine

exercises: the Pauli decomposition of a Hermitian matrix, commuting exponentials, a time that ends in a number

  1. Prove that for \(H = aI + \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) with \(a\in\mathbb R\), \(0 \ne \mathbf b\in\mathbb R^3\), \(e^{-iHt} = e^{-iat}R_{\hat{\mathbf b}}(2|\mathbf b|t)\), and that the energies are \(a \pm |\mathbf b|\).
  2. For \(H = X + Z\), give the axis and angular frequency of precession, and the first time \(t \gt 0\) at which \(e^{-iHt}\) equals the Hadamard gate up to global phase. Give that time as a number, and the phase.
  3. For \(H = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}3&1 - i\\1 + i&1\end{pmatrix}\), find \(a\), \(\mathbf b\), the energies and the axis.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a short proof using two facts from unit 00 and one from section 1; parts 2 and 3 are matching coefficients against \(aI + \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\).

Rung 2

\(aI\) commutes with everything, so the exponential splits. Write \(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = |\mathbf b|\,\hat{\mathbf b}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) and compare with the definition of \(R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)\). For part 2, the Hadamard gate is \(iR_{\mathbf n}(\pi)\) with \(\mathbf n = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 0, 1)\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "\(X + Z = \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) with \(\mathbf b = (1, 0, 1)\), \(|\mathbf b| = \sqrt2\), so \(e^{-iHt} = R_{\mathbf n}(2\sqrt2\,t)\) with \(\mathbf n = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 0, 1)\)."

Worked solution

Idea. The identity part of a qubit Hamiltonian only makes a global phase; the traceless part is an axis and a speed.

1. \(aI\) and \(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) commute, so \(e^{-iHt} = e^{-iatI}e^{-it\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma}\) (unit 00, commuting exponentials), and \(e^{-iatI} = e^{-iat}I\). Then \(-it\,\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = -i(2|\mathbf b|t)\,\hat{\mathbf b}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2\), so \(e^{-it\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma} = R_{\hat{\mathbf b}}(2|\mathbf b|t)\) by definition. The eigenvalues of \(\hat{\mathbf b}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) are \(\pm1\), so those of \(H\) are \(a \pm |\mathbf b|\). \(\blacksquare\)

2. \(a = 0\), \(\mathbf b = (1, 0, 1)\), \(|\mathbf b| = \sqrt2\): precession about \(\mathbf n = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 0, 1)\) at angular frequency \(2\sqrt2\). The Hadamard gate is \(iR_{\mathbf n}(\pi)\), and \(R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)\) equals \(R_{\mathbf n}(\pi)\) up to phase only when the rotations agree, \(\theta \equiv \pi \pmod{2\pi}\). The first time is \(2\sqrt2\,t = \pi\):

\[ t = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt2} = 1.1107,\qquad e^{-i(X + Z)t} = R_{\mathbf n}(\pi) = -iH . \]

So the phase is \(-i\). (In units: if \(H\) is in units of some angular frequency \(\omega\), the time is \(1.1107/\omega\).)

3. \(a = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}H = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{3 + 1}{2} = 1\). From the matrix of \(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), \(\begin{pmatrix}b_3&b_1 - ib_2\\b_1 + ib_2&-b_3\end{pmatrix}\), and \(H - I = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1 - i\\1 + i&-1\end{pmatrix}\): \(b_3 = \tfrac12\), \(b_1 = \tfrac12\), \(b_2 = \tfrac12\). So \(\mathbf b = \tfrac12(1, 1, 1)\), \(|\mathbf b| = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\), energies \(1 \pm \tfrac{\sqrt3}2 = 1.8660\) and \(0.1340\), axis \(\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1)\), angular frequency \(\sqrt3\).

Check. Part 3: the energies sum to \(2 = \operatorname{tr}H\) ✓ and multiply to \(1 - \tfrac34 = \tfrac14\); \(\det H = \tfrac14(3 - |1 - i|^2) = \tfrac14(3 - 2) = \tfrac14\) ✓. Part 2 at \(t = 0\): \(I\) ✓; the axis \(\mathbf n\) is fixed by the half-turn and \(\hat{\mathbf z}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf x}\), as the Hadamard gate does ✓.

P5 Two quarter-turns make a third of a turn medium

exercises: the product rule for Pauli vectors, composing rotations about different axes, unit 00's cycling unitary explained

  1. Using \((\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = (\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)I + i(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), prove the composition law: with \(c = \cos\tfrac\alpha2\), \(s = \sin\tfrac\alpha2\), \(c' = \cos\tfrac\beta2\), \(s' = \sin\tfrac\beta2\), \[ R_{\mathbf m}(\alpha)R_{\mathbf n}(\beta) = (cc' - ss'\,\mathbf m\cdot\mathbf n)\,I - i\big(sc'\,\mathbf m + cs'\,\mathbf n + ss'\,\mathbf m\times\mathbf n\big)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma . \]
  2. Find the axis and angle of \(R_x(\tfrac\pi2)R_y(\tfrac\pi2)\).
  3. Unit 00's problem P3 found that \(U = \tfrac12(I - iX - iY - iZ)\) conjugates \(Z\to X\to Y\to Z\) and satisfies \(U^3 = -I\). Explain both facts from part 2.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a product of two expressions \(cI - is\,\mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\); part 2 reads an axis and angle from the result; part 3 is about what rotation \(U\) is.

Rung 2

The only non-obvious term in the product is \((-is\,\mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(-is'\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = -ss'(\mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\). In part 2 \(\mathbf m = \hat{\mathbf x}\), \(\mathbf n = \hat{\mathbf y}\), and \(\hat{\mathbf x}\times\hat{\mathbf y} = \hat{\mathbf z}\).

Rung 3

\[ R_x(\tfrac\pi2)R_y(\tfrac\pi2) = \tfrac12I - \tfrac i2(X + Y + Z) = \cos\tfrac\gamma2\,I - i\sin\tfrac\gamma2\,\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma \]

exercises: transforming a time-dependent Hamiltonian, the frame term \(-i\hbar V^\dagger\dot V\), identifying the counter-rotating term and its size

  3. State when the counter-rotating term may be dropped. For the qubit of P10 at \(\omega_0/2\pi = 5\) GHz, compute \(\Omega/\omega_0\) and the Bloch–Siegert shift \(\Omega^2/4\omega_0\) (as a frequency, cited from the deck).

Worked solution

Idea. In a frame turning with the drive the drive stands still, at the price of a frame term \(\tfrac{\hbar\omega}2Z\) that subtracts \(\omega\) from the precession. A field turning the other way does not stand still; it turns at \(2\omega\).

1. \(V = R_z(-\omega t) = e^{i\omega tZ/2}\) implements the rotation by \(-\omega t\) about \(z\), so \(VXV^\dagger = \cos\omega t\,X - \sin\omega t\,Y\), \(VZV^\dagger = Z\), and \(H(t) = V\tilde H_0V^\dagger\) with \(\tilde H_0 = -\tfrac\hbar2(\omega_0Z + \Omega X)\). From the spectral form, \(\dot V = \tfrac{i\omega}{2}ZV\). Substituting \(\psi = V\tilde\psi\) into \(i\hbar\dot\psi = H\psi\): \(i\hbar\dot V\tilde\psi + i\hbar V\dot{\tilde\psi} = V\tilde H_0\tilde\psi\). Multiply by \(V^\dagger\): \(i\hbar\dot{\tilde\psi} = \tilde H_0\tilde\psi - i\hbar V^\dagger\dot V\tilde\psi = \big(\tilde H_0 + \tfrac{\hbar\omega}2Z\big)\tilde\psi = -\tfrac\hbar2\big((\omega_0 - \omega)Z + \Omega X\big)\tilde\psi\). So

\[ \psi(t) = R_z(-\omega t)\,\exp\!\Big(\tfrac{it}{2}(\Delta Z + \Omega X)\Big)\,\psi(0). \]

(Uniqueness: any solution, transformed, solves the constant equation, whose solutions are \(e^{-i\tilde Ht/\hbar}\tilde\psi(0)\) since \(\tfrac{d}{dt}(e^{i\tilde Ht/\hbar}\tilde\psi) = 0\).)

2. \(-\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}2\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = H(t) + H_{\text{cr}}(t)\) with \(H(t)\) as in part 1 and \(H_{\text{cr}} = -\tfrac{\hbar\Omega}2(\cos\omega t\,X + \sin\omega t\,Y)\), since \(2\cos\omega t = \cos\omega t + \cos\omega t\) and the \(\mp\sin\omega t\,Y\) cancel. In the frame, \(H_{\text{cr}}\) becomes \(V^\dagger H_{\text{cr}}V\), and \(V^\dagger = R_z(\omega t)\) turns \((\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t, 0)\) by a further \(+\omega t\):

\[ \tilde H(t) = -\frac\hbar2\Big[\Delta Z + \Omega X + \Omega\big(\cos2\omega t\,X + \sin2\omega t\,Y\big)\Big]. \]

The last term is the counter-rotating term. Over one period \(\pi/\omega\) of \(2\omega t\), \(\int\cos2\omega t\,dt = \int\sin2\omega t\,dt = 0\), so its average vanishes: to first order it does nothing.

3. It may be dropped — the rotating-wave approximation — when it turns much faster than everything else moves: \(\Omega, |\Delta| \ll \omega_0\) (so that \(2\omega \approx 2\omega_0\) is the fastest rate). Its first effect is second order, the Bloch–Siegert shift \(\Omega^2/4\omega_0\) (cited: Bloch and Siegert 1940). For P10's qubit: \(\Omega/\omega_0 = 20\ \text{MHz}/5\ \text{GHz} = 4\times10^{-3}\), and

\[ \frac{\delta\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{(\Omega/2\pi)^2}{4\,\omega_0/2\pi} = \frac{(20\ \text{MHz})^2}{4\times5\ \text{GHz}} = \frac{4\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}^2}{2\times10^{10}\ \text{Hz}} = 20\ \text{kHz}. \]

Check. Part 1 at \(\Omega = 0\) gives \(\psi(t) = R_z(-\omega t)R_z(-\Delta t)\psi(0) = R_z(-\omega_0t)\psi(0)\), free Larmor precession ✓. Units in part 3: \(\text{Hz}^2/\text{Hz} = \text{Hz}\) ✓. Against the proton: \(\Omega/\omega_0\) there is \(7.8\times10^{-6}\), \(511\) times smaller.
