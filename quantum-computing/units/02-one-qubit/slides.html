One qubit: the Bloch sphere, and the group that rotates it twice

The states of a qubit are the points of a sphere, its unitaries are the rotations of that sphere, and every Hamiltonian is an axis and a rate of turning — with one twist: the group that does the rotating goes round twice before it comes home.

Unit 00 found three mutually unbiased bases in $\mathbb C^2$ and left a debt: that there is no fourth. Unit 01 said what a state, a measurement and an evolution are. 02 · Pauli matrices · physics

A proton is a small magnet

Left, the classical picture: a spinning magnet in a field does not swing into line — it sweeps a cone, as a tilted top does under gravity (Larmor, 1897). Right, the quantum one: two levels.

A compass needle in a field has least energy pointing along the field and most pointing against it: $E = -\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$, with $\boldsymbol\mu$ its magnetic moment (strength and direction).

A proton spins, and its magnetism is proportional to its spin. Measured along any axis $\mathbf n$ by a Stern–Gerlach magnet, its moment takes two values, $\pm\gamma\hbar/2$. The constant $\gamma$ is the gyromagnetic ratio; for the proton (CODATA 2018)

$$\frac{\gamma}{2\pi} = 42.577\ \text{MHz/T}.$$
the model, forced by those two outcomes

The moment along $\mathbf n$ is the observable $\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ (next slides), and the energy becomes the Hamiltonian $H = -\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\,\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$.

The physics, from nothing. A magnetic field $\mathbf B$ is measured in tesla (T); the Earth's is about $5\times10^{-5}$ T, a fridge magnet's about $10^{-2}$ T, a hospital scanner's $1.5$ or $3$ T. A small magnet — a compass needle, or the "internal direction" of unit 00's silver atom — has a magnetic moment $\boldsymbol\mu$, a vector whose energy in the field is $E = -\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$: lowest when aligned. That is all the electromagnetism this unit uses.

Why precession, classically. A magnet that also spins, like a top, carries angular momentum $\mathbf L$, and for a spinning charge $\boldsymbol\mu = \gamma\mathbf L$. The field twists it with torque $\boldsymbol\mu\times\mathbf B$, and torque is the rate of change of angular momentum, so $\dot{\boldsymbol\mu} = \gamma\,\boldsymbol\mu\times\mathbf B$: the moment turns sideways, round the field, at angular frequency $\gamma B$, instead of falling into line. Joseph Larmor worked this out in 1897 for the orbiting electrons of an atom. This unit derives the quantum statement as a theorem (section 3), and its notes show that the Bloch vector obeys exactly this classical equation.

Where the model comes from. Like unit 00's silver atoms, a proton shows exactly two outcomes along any axis: it is what physicists call a spin-½ particle, established for the proton by the end of the 1920s (Dennison's 1927 analysis of hydrogen gas), and its moment was first measured by Stern's molecular-beam group in 1933. Unit 00 showed that such a system is $\mathbb C^2$ with the measurement along $z$, $x$, $y$ given by $Z$, $X$, $Y$; the observable for the moment along a general axis, $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, is justified on the slide The Pauli vector, and measuring along any axis. Replacing the classical $\boldsymbol\mu$ by $\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\boldsymbol\sigma$ in $E = -\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$ is the modelling step; that it is right is an experimental fact, borne out by everything that follows, MRI included. That "spin" is an angular momentum is not used anywhere in this unit.

Dimensions. $\gamma$ is in $\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}\,T^{-1}}$ (the slide quotes $\gamma/2\pi$ in hertz per tesla), so $\gamma\hbar B$ is in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}\cdot\mathrm{J\,s} = $ J: an energy, as a Hamiltonian must be with $\hbar$ restored. CODATA 2018 gives $\gamma_p/2\pi = 42.577\,478\,518$ MHz/T.

02 · Pauli matrices

The Pauli algebra

the Pauli matrices, unit 00's X, Y, Z $$X = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad Y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},$$ $$Z = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},$$

written also $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$. Each is Hermitian and unitary, with eigenvalues $\pm1$ (unit 00).

Multiply them out:

$$XY = iZ,\quad YZ = iX,\quad ZX = iY,$$ $$YX = -iZ,\quad ZY = -iX,\quad XZ = -iY.$$
one formula for all nine products $$\sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}\,I + i\sum_l\varepsilon_{jkl}\,\sigma_l ,$$

with the Levi-Civita symbol $\varepsilon_{jkl}$: $+1$ for $(1,2,3)$ and its cyclic shifts, $-1$ for the others, $0$ if an index repeats.

So: distinct Paulis anticommute, $\sigma_j\sigma_k = -\sigma_k\sigma_j$; the commutator is $[\sigma_j, \sigma_k] = 2i\sum_l\varepsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$; and

$$\operatorname{tr}\sigma_j = 0, \qquad \operatorname{tr}(\sigma_j\sigma_k) = 2\delta_{jk}.$$

Check: $XYZ = (iZ)Z = iI$, and by the formula $XY\cdot Z = i\varepsilon_{123}Z^2 = iI$ ✓.

Full proof. The diagonal cases are $X^2 = Y^2 = Z^2 = I$, unit 00. For the off-diagonal ones, multiply: $XY = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix} = iZ$; $YZ = \begin{pmatrix}0&i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = iX$; $ZX = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix} = iY$. The reversed products follow by taking adjoints: the Paulis are Hermitian, so $YX = Y^\dagger X^\dagger = (XY)^\dagger = (iZ)^\dagger = -iZ$, and likewise for the other two. These nine cases are exactly the formula, read entry by entry. Anticommutation is the formula for $j \ne k$ added to itself with $j, k$ swapped ($\varepsilon$ is antisymmetric); the commutator is the difference. The traces: $\operatorname{tr}\sigma_j = 0$ by inspection, and $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_j\sigma_k) = 2\delta_{jk} + i\sum\varepsilon_{jkl}\operatorname{tr}\sigma_l = 2\delta_{jk}$.

The cross product inside. The structure constants $\varepsilon_{jkl}$ are those of the cross product: $\mathbf e_j\times\mathbf e_k = \sum_l\varepsilon_{jkl}\mathbf e_l$. Dividing the commutator by $(2i)^2 = -4$ gives $[\sigma_j/2i, \sigma_k/2i] = \sum_l\varepsilon_{jkl}\,\sigma_l/2i$: the matrices $\sigma_j/2i$ commute exactly as the vectors $\mathbf e_j$ cross. That coincidence is the reason the next sections find rotations of three-space inside $2\times2$ matrices.

History. Wolfgang Pauli introduced the three matrices in 1927 to describe the electron's two-valued spin (Z. Phys. 43, 601). The letters $X, Y, Z$ are quantum computing's; physics writes $\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$.

The commutator, for unit 01. $[X, Y] = 2iZ$ is the instance unit 01 used in Robertson's uncertainty relation.

02 · Pauli matrices

A basis of all 2×2 matrices

the Pauli basis

$\{I, X, Y, Z\}$ is a basis of $M_2(\mathbb C)$, orthogonal for the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product $\braket{A, B} = \operatorname{tr}(A^\dagger B)$, each of squared norm $2$. So every $A$ is

$$A = \tfrac12\sum_{\mu = 0}^{3}\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_\mu A)\,\sigma_\mu\qquad(\sigma_0 = I),$$

and $A$ is Hermitian iff the four coefficients are real.

proof

$\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_\mu^\dagger\sigma_\nu) = \operatorname{tr}(\sigma_\mu\sigma_\nu) = 2\delta_{\mu\nu}$ (previous slide, and $\operatorname{tr}I = 2$): four orthogonal nonzero vectors in a space of dimension $4$. Hermitian $A$: $\overline{\operatorname{tr}(\sigma A)} = \operatorname{tr}\big((\sigma A)^\dagger\big) = \operatorname{tr}(A\sigma) = \operatorname{tr}(\sigma A)$.

A traceless Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix is therefore exactly $\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma := b_1X + b_2Y + b_3Z$ for one real $\mathbf b\in\mathbb R^3$:

$$\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = \begin{pmatrix}b_3 & b_1 - ib_2\\ b_1 + ib_2 & -b_3\end{pmatrix}.$$
the proton's Hamiltonian, in Paulis

$H = -\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\,\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$: zero $I$-part, Pauli coefficients $-\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}{2}(B_x, B_y, B_z)$, all real — Hermitian ✓. For $\mathbf B = B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\omega_0 := \gamma B_0$:

$$H = -\frac{\hbar\omega_0}{2}Z = \begin{pmatrix}-\hbar\omega_0/2 & 0\\ 0 & +\hbar\omega_0/2\end{pmatrix}.$$

$\ket0$, along the field, is the lower level; the gap is $\hbar\omega_0$.

The Hilbert–Schmidt inner product. $\operatorname{tr}(A^\dagger B) = \sum_{i,j}\overline{A_{ij}}B_{ij}$: it is the standard inner product of $\mathbb C^4$ applied to the four entries, so it is an inner product in unit 00's sense (conjugate-linear in the first slot, as the course requires). Orthogonal nonzero vectors are linearly independent — take the inner product of a vanishing combination with each — so four of them in a four-dimensional space are a basis. The coefficient of $\sigma_\mu$ in $A$ is $\braket{\sigma_\mu, A}/\braket{\sigma_\mu, \sigma_\mu} = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_\mu A)$, since $\sigma_\mu^\dagger = \sigma_\mu$.

Hermitian iff real, both ways. If the coefficients $c_\mu$ are real then $A^\dagger = \sum\bar c_\mu\sigma_\mu^\dagger = A$. The converse is on the slide, using $\operatorname{tr}(M^\dagger) = \overline{\operatorname{tr}M}$ and the cyclicity of the trace.

A check. The projector $\ket+\!\bra+ = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ has coefficients $\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\ket+\!\bra+) = \tfrac12$ on $I$, $\tfrac12\braket{+|X|+} = \tfrac12$ on $X$, and $\tfrac12\braket{+|Y|+} = \tfrac12\braket{+|Z|+} = 0$: so $\ket+\!\bra+ = \tfrac12(I + X)$ ✓ — the first instance of the formula $\tfrac12(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ of section 2.

The proton's levels. With $\hbar$ restored, $H$ is an energy and its eigenvalues are $\mp\hbar\omega_0/2$. At $1.5$ T the gap is $\hbar\omega_0 = 1.0546\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}\times 4.013\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}} = 4.23\times10^{-26}$ J, or $2.64\times10^{-7}$ eV — about $10^{-5}$ of the thermal energy $k_BT$ at body temperature ($4.28\times10^{-21}$ J), which is why, at body temperature, thermal agitation leaves only about five protons in a million more in the lower level than in the upper (Boltzmann's distribution, quoted; such populations are unit 04's subject), and why MRI needs so many of them.

Sign. For the proton $\gamma \gt 0$, so the minus sign puts the state along the field lowest. Some particles (the neutron, the electron) have $\gamma \lt 0$, and their levels are the other way round.

02 · Pauli matrices

The Pauli vector, and measuring along any axis

the Pauli vector

$\boldsymbol\sigma = (X, Y, Z)$, a triple of matrices; for $\mathbf a\in\mathbb R^3$, $\ \mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = a_1X + a_2Y + a_3Z$.

the product rule $$(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = (\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b)\,I + i\,(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma .$$

Proof: expand $\sum_{j,k}a_jb_k\sigma_j\sigma_k$ by the Pauli algebra; $\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{jkl}a_jb_k = (\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)_l$.

for a unit vector n

$(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2 = |\mathbf n|^2I = I$, and $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ is Hermitian and traceless. So its eigenvalues are $+1$ and $-1$, once each, and

$$\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = P_+ - P_-,\qquad P_\pm = \tfrac12(I \pm \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma).$$

Stern–Gerlach along $\mathbf n$ is the measurement of the observable $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ (unit 01's Born rule, with outcomes labelled $\pm1$). It is $Z$, $X$, $Y$ for $\mathbf n = \hat{\mathbf z}, \hat{\mathbf x}, \hat{\mathbf y}$, as unit 00 found.

Check: $\mathbf n = (0.6, 0, 0.8)$: $\ (0.6X + 0.8Z)^2 = (0.36 + 0.64)I = I$; the cross term $0.48(XZ + ZX) = 0$.

Full proof of the product rule. $(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = \sum_{j,k}a_jb_k\sigma_j\sigma_k = \sum_{j,k}a_jb_k\big(\delta_{jk}I + i\sum_l\varepsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l\big) = (\sum_ja_jb_j)I + i\sum_l\big(\sum_{j,k}\varepsilon_{jkl}a_jb_k\big)\sigma_l$, and the inner sum is the $l$-th component of $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$. The $a_j, b_k$ are numbers and commute with everything; only the order of $\sigma_j$ and $\sigma_k$ matters, and the formula keeps it.

Eigenvalues. If $(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)v = \lambda v$ then $v = (\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2v = \lambda^2v$, so $\lambda = \pm1$; the two eigenvalues of a Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix sum to its trace, $0$, so they are $+1$ and $-1$, once each. By the spectral theorem (unit 00), $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = P_+ - P_-$ with $P_+ + P_- = I$; solving, $P_\pm = \tfrac12(I \pm \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$. Check that these are projectors: $P_+^2 = \tfrac14(I + 2\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + I) = P_+$, and $P_+^\dagger = P_+$ since $\mathbf n$ is real.

Why this is the measurement along $\mathbf n$. Turning the magnet must turn the observable. Section 3 proves that the rotation of three-space taking $\hat{\mathbf z}$ to $\mathbf n$ is implemented by a unitary $U$ with $UZU^\dagger = \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$; so measuring "$Z$ after the rotation" is measuring $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. Unit 00's three axes are the special cases; that the two outcomes come out as $\pm1$ for every axis, with nothing in between, is the Stern–Gerlach result itself.

Anticommutation, used in the check. $XZ + ZX = -iY + iY = 0$. More generally, the product rule with $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b = 0$ gives $(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = -(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$: perpendicular directions give anticommuting observables. That is used in section 3.

02 · the Bloch sphere

Every state, in two angles

the projective line CP¹

The pure states of a qubit, as unit 01 defined them: unit vectors of $\mathbb C^2$ modulo a global phase, $\psi \sim e^{i\alpha}\psi$. Equivalently, the one-dimensional subspaces (lines) of $\mathbb C^2$. Write $[\psi]$ for the class.

lemma: two angles

Every unit $\psi\in\mathbb C^2$ is, up to global phase,

$$\ket\psi = \cos\tfrac\theta2\,\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\ket1,$$

with $0\le\theta\le\pi$ unique, and $\varphi$ unique mod $2\pi$ unless $\theta = 0$ or $\pi$, where it is arbitrary.

proof

Write $\psi = (a, b)$, $|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$. There is exactly one $\theta\in[0, \pi]$ with $|a| = \cos\tfrac\theta2$, $|b| = \sin\tfrac\theta2$. Polar forms $a = |a|e^{i\alpha}$, $b = |b|e^{i\beta}$; then

$$\psi = e^{i\alpha}\big(\cos\tfrac\theta2,\ e^{i(\beta - \alpha)}\sin\tfrac\theta2\big),$$

so $\varphi = \beta - \alpha$. If $a = 0$ or $b = 0$, its phase is free, and so is $\varphi$.

Normalised: $\cos^2\tfrac\theta2 + \sin^2\tfrac\theta2 = 1$. Why half-angles? So that $\theta$ runs over $[0, \pi]$ — the range of a polar angle — while $|a|$ runs from $1$ to $0$.

Full proof of uniqueness. Two representatives of the same class differ by a phase, which changes neither $|a|$ nor $|b|$, so $\theta$ is determined by $\cos\tfrac\theta2 = |a|$ on $[0, \pi]$, where $\cos\tfrac\theta2$ is strictly decreasing. When $0 \lt \theta \lt \pi$ both $a$ and $b$ are nonzero, and the phase difference $\beta - \alpha$ is unchanged by a global phase ($\alpha$ and $\beta$ shift together), so $\varphi$ is determined mod $2\pi$. When $\theta = 0$ the state is $\ket0$ up to phase and $\varphi$ multiplies a zero amplitude; when $\theta = \pi$ it is $\ket1$ up to phase, and the factor $e^{i\varphi}$ is itself a global phase.

The count. $\mathbb C^2$ has four real dimensions; normalising removes one and the global phase another, leaving two — the two angles. Unit 00's "price" of the third unbiased direction was exactly this second real parameter, $\varphi$; real amplitudes have $\varphi\in\{0, \pi\}$ only.

Why the name. $\mathbb{CP}^1$, the complex projective line, is the set of lines through the origin in $\mathbb C^2$, just as $\mathbb{RP}^1$ is the set of lines through the origin in $\mathbb R^2$ — a circle. The next three slides prove that $\mathbb{CP}^1$ is a sphere. That is the complex counterpart of a picture you already know.

02 · the Bloch sphere

The Bloch map

The Bloch sphere. $\theta$ is a polar angle from the north pole, $\varphi$ an azimuth from the $x$ axis.

the Bloch vector

For a unit $\psi$, the triple of expectation values (unit 01)

$$\mathbf r(\psi) = \braket{\psi|\boldsymbol\sigma|\psi} = \big(\braket X, \braket Y, \braket Z\big).$$

It depends only on $[\psi]$: a phase $e^{i\alpha}$ on the ket meets $e^{-i\alpha}$ on the bra.

in the two angles

For $\psi = (a, b)$: $\ \mathbf r = \big(2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\bar ab,\ 2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\bar ab,\ |a|^2 - |b|^2\big)$. With the lemma's form, $\bar ab = \tfrac12\sin\theta\,e^{i\varphi}$, so

$$\mathbf r = (\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta).$$

Check: $\ket0 \mapsto (0, 0, 1)$; $\ \ket1 \mapsto (0, 0, -1)$; $\ \ket+ \mapsto (1, 0, 0)$. And $|\mathbf r|^2 = \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$.

Full computation. $\braket{\psi|X|\psi} = (\bar a, \bar b)\cdot(b, a) = \bar ab + \bar ba = 2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\bar ab$. $\braket{\psi|Y|\psi} = (\bar a, \bar b)\cdot(-ib, ia) = -i\bar ab + i\bar ba = -i(\bar ab - \overline{\bar ab}) = -i\cdot2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,\bar ab = 2\,\mathrm{Im}\,\bar ab$. $\braket{\psi|Z|\psi} = |a|^2 - |b|^2$. With $a = \cos\tfrac\theta2$, $b = e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2$: $\bar ab = e^{i\varphi}\cos\tfrac\theta2\sin\tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac12e^{i\varphi}\sin\theta$, and $|a|^2 - |b|^2 = \cos^2\tfrac\theta2 - \sin^2\tfrac\theta2 = \cos\theta$. The double-angle formulae turn half-angles into the sphere's angles: that is why the lemma used half-angles.

The components are measurement statistics. $\braket{\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma} = p_+ - p_-$, the probability of "up along $\mathbf n$" minus that of "down". So $\mathbf r$ is the list of the three Stern–Gerlach differences along $x$, $y$, $z$ — exactly the three numbers unit 00's notes previewed for its test state. The test state is computed in full in Worked: unit 00's test state on the sphere.

History. The sphere is named after Felix Bloch, whose 1946 theory of nuclear induction (Phys. Rev. 70, 460) described a population of precessing protons by a vector — the ancestor of every MRI scanner. The same sphere describes the polarisation of light, where it was introduced by Henri Poincaré in 1892 and is called the Poincaré sphere. A photon's horizontal, diagonal and circular polarisations are its $z$, $x$ and $y$ axes.

02 · the Bloch sphere

A state's projector is a point of the sphere

theorem

For every unit $\psi$, with $\mathbf r = \mathbf r(\psi)$,

$$\ket\psi\!\bra\psi = \tfrac12\big(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\big), \qquad |\mathbf r| = 1 .$$
proof

Expand in the Pauli basis. The $I$-coefficient is $\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\ket\psi\!\bra\psi = \tfrac12\braket{\psi|\psi} = \tfrac12$; the $\sigma_k$-coefficient is $\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_k\ket\psi\!\bra\psi) = \tfrac12\braket{\psi|\sigma_k|\psi} = \tfrac12r_k$.

Then $P = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$ is a projector, and by the product rule

$$P^2 = \tfrac14\big(I + 2\,\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + |\mathbf r|^2I\big),$$

which equals $P$ iff $|\mathbf r|^2 = 1$.

checks

$\ket0\!\bra0 = \tfrac12(I + Z) = \mathrm{diag}(1, 0)$ ✓. $\ \ket{+i}\!\bra{+i} = \tfrac12(I + Y) = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}$ — unit 00's matrix ✓.

Two readings.

  • $|\mathbf r| = 1$ without coordinates: it is exactly the statement $P^2 = P$.
  • $\psi$ is the "up" state along its own Bloch vector: $P = P_+$ for $\mathbf n = \mathbf r$, so $(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\ket\psi = \ket\psi$. Every pure state is the $+1$ outcome of a Stern–Gerlach magnet along some axis.

The matrix $\tfrac12(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ with $|\mathbf r| \lt 1$ is not a projector at all. What it is — a mixed state, inside the sphere — is unit 04's.

Full proof of the trace step. $\operatorname{tr}(A\ket\psi\!\bra\psi) = \braket{\psi|A|\psi}$: by unit 00's outer-product calculus, $A\ket\psi\!\bra\psi = \ket{A\psi}\!\bra\psi$, whose trace is $\braket{\psi|A\psi}$. The expansion coefficients are therefore $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac12r_k$, and the $r_k$ are real because the $\sigma_k$ are Hermitian — as they must be, $P$ being Hermitian.

The square. $(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2 = I + 2\,\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + (\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2$, and the product rule with $\mathbf a = \mathbf b = \mathbf r$ gives $(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2 = |\mathbf r|^2I$ (the cross product of a vector with itself vanishes). So $P^2 - P = \tfrac14(|\mathbf r|^2 - 1)I$, zero iff $|\mathbf r| = 1$. Since $P$ is a rank-one orthogonal projector (unit 00), $|\mathbf r| = 1$.

Check on unit 00's $y$ projector. $\tfrac12(I + Y) = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\i&1\end{pmatrix}$, which unit 00's slide Resolving the identity, three ways computed as column $(1, i)$ times row $(1, -i)$ over two.

A preview, not used here. Unit 04 writes a general state as $\rho = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ with $|\mathbf r| \le 1$: the pure states are the surface, the mixtures fill the ball. This slide's computation is why: positivity of $\rho$ is $|\mathbf r| \le 1$, purity $\rho^2 = \rho$ is $|\mathbf r| = 1$.

02 · the Bloch sphere

The Bloch map is a bijection onto the sphere

theorem: CP¹ is a sphere

The Bloch map $[\psi] \mapsto \mathbf r(\psi)$ is a bijection from $\mathbb{CP}^1$ onto the unit sphere $S^2\subset\mathbb R^3$.

proof

Well defined, into $S^2$: the Bloch map slide, and $|\mathbf r| = 1$ from the previous slide.

Injective. $\mathbf r$ determines $P = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$, and $P$ determines its image, the line $\mathbb C\psi$ — which is $[\psi]$.

Surjective. Given $|\mathbf r| = 1$, $P := \tfrac12(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ is the projector $P_+$ for $\mathbf n = \mathbf r$ (rank one, trace $1$). Pick a unit $\psi$ in its image: $P = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$, whose Bloch vector is $\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol\sigma P) = \mathbf r$.

the poles need no special case

In the angles, the poles are where $\varphi$ is undefined — but the proof never uses angles. At $\mathbf r = (0, 0, \pm1)$ it gives $P = \mathrm{diag}(1, 0)$ or $\mathrm{diag}(0, 1)$, and the states $\ket0$, $\ket1$.

The inverse, explicitly. For $\mathbf r \ne (0, 0, -1)$, the first column of $2P = I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ is $(1 + r_3,\ r_1 + ir_2)$, a vector in the image:

$$\ket\psi = \frac{(1 + r_3)\ket0 + (r_1 + ir_2)\ket1}{\sqrt{2(1 + r_3)}} .$$

Normalised: $(1 + r_3)^2 + r_1^2 + r_2^2 = 2(1 + r_3)$, using $|\mathbf r| = 1$.

Full proof of the trace in the surjectivity step. $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_kP) = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\sigma_k + \tfrac12\sum_jr_j\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_k\sigma_j) = 0 + \tfrac12\cdot2r_k = r_k$, by trace orthogonality. And $\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_k\ket\psi\!\bra\psi) = \braket{\psi|\sigma_k|\psi}$, so the Bloch vector of $\psi$ is $\mathbf r$. The rank of $P$ is one because it is a projector with trace $\tfrac12\cdot2 = 1$ (a projector's trace is its rank: its eigenvalues are $0$s and $1$s).

Injectivity, in more detail. If $\mathbf r(\psi) = \mathbf r(\chi)$ then $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi = \ket\chi\!\bra\chi$ by the previous slide. Apply both to $\psi$: $\psi = \braket{\chi|\psi}\chi$, so $\psi$ is a multiple of $\chi$, and the multiple has modulus one since both are unit vectors. So $[\psi] = [\chi]$.

The explicit inverse, checked. At $\mathbf r = (0, 0, 1)$ it gives $\ket0$ ✓. At $\mathbf r = (1, 0, 0)$: $(\ket0 + \ket1)/\sqrt2 = \ket+$ ✓. At the south pole the first column vanishes; use the second column, $(r_1 - ir_2,\ 1 - r_3)$, which there is $(0, 2)$, giving $\ket1$. That one chart misses one point is not a defect of the map: no single formula can cover a sphere without a singularity, which is a topological fact about $S^2$ (the hairy-ball kind of fact; not needed).

More than a bijection. The map and its inverse are continuous, so $\mathbb{CP}^1$ and $S^2$ are the same topological space. Nothing later uses this beyond the picture.

Overlaps are angles

the transition probability

For unit $\psi$, $\chi$ with Bloch vectors $\mathbf r$, $\mathbf s$ at angle $\alpha$:

$$|\braket{\chi|\psi}|^2 = \tfrac12(1 + \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s) = \cos^2\tfrac\alpha2 .$$
proof

$|\braket{\chi|\psi}|^2 = \operatorname{tr}\big(\ket\chi\!\bra\chi\,\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\big)$ (unit 00), and

$$\tfrac14\operatorname{tr}\big((I + \mathbf s\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\big) = \tfrac14(2 + 2\,\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s)$$

by trace orthogonality.

so

Orthogonal states are antipodes ($\mathbf s = -\mathbf r$). Unbiased states, overlap $\tfrac12$, are at right angles. Measuring along $\mathbf n$: $\ p_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)$.

Angles on the sphere are twice the angles between state vectors: orthogonal kets, at $90^\circ$ in $\mathbb C^2$'s sense, sit $180^\circ$ apart.

The measurement formula. The outcome $+1$ of $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ has projector $P_+ = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$, the projector of the state with Bloch vector $\mathbf n$. So $p_+ = \braket{\psi|P_+|\psi} = \operatorname{tr}(P_+\ket\psi\!\bra\psi) = \tfrac12(1 + \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)$, and $p_- = \tfrac12(1 - \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)$. Sum: $1$ ✓. The expectation is $p_+ - p_- = \mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r$, which for $\mathbf n = \hat{\mathbf x}$ recovers $r_1 = \braket X$ ✓.

The half-angle. $\tfrac12(1 + \cos\alpha) = \cos^2\tfrac\alpha2$. The state vectors themselves make an "angle" whose cosine is $|\braket{\chi|\psi}| = \cos\tfrac\alpha2$: half the angle on the sphere. Two orthogonal kets ($90^\circ$ between them) become antipodes ($180^\circ$); two unbiased ones ($45^\circ$) become perpendicular ($90^\circ$). Unit 00's real plane, where unbiased lines meet at $45^\circ$, is the great circle of real states, drawn at double its angles.

Checks. $\psi = \chi$: $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r = 1$, probability $1$ ✓. $\ket0$ against $\ket1$: $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s = -1$, probability $0$ ✓. $\ket0$ against $\ket+$: $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s = 0$, probability $\tfrac12$ ✓.

Six states, and unit 00's debt paid

Unit 00's three bases are the three axes. The orange great circle is the states with real amplitudes, $\varphi\in\{0, \pi\}$. The arrow is the test state.

For the proton the north pole $\ket0$ is "along the field", the lower level; the south pole $\ket1$ the upper. The equator is where $\braket Z = 0$: equal odds of either level.

theorem (unit 00's debt)

Two orthonormal bases of $\mathbb C^2$ are mutually unbiased iff their Bloch axes are perpendicular. Hence $\mathbb C^2$ holds exactly three pairwise mutually unbiased bases.

proof

A basis $\{e_1, e_2\}$ is an antipodal pair $\pm\mathbf u$: an axis. With $\{f_1, f_2\}$ at $\pm\mathbf v$, the four overlaps are $\tfrac12(1 \pm \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)$, all $\tfrac12$ iff $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = 0$. Pairwise perpendicular lines in $\mathbb R^3$: at most three. $z, x, y$ reach three.

Full proof. For an orthonormal basis $\{e_1, e_2\}$, $e_2$ is orthogonal to $e_1$, so by the previous slide its Bloch vector is the antipode of $e_1$'s: the basis is determined by the line $\mathbb R\mathbf u$, and conversely every line through the origin gives a basis (its two endpoints, by the bijection). For a second basis at $\pm\mathbf v$, the overlaps $|\braket{f_j|e_i}|^2 = \tfrac12(1 + (\pm\mathbf u)\cdot(\pm\mathbf v))$ take the values $\tfrac12(1 + \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)$ and $\tfrac12(1 - \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)$, both $\tfrac12$ iff $\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v = 0$. So $k$ pairwise unbiased bases are $k$ pairwise perpendicular unit vectors $\mathbf u_1, \dots, \mathbf u_k$, which are linearly independent (take the dot product of a vanishing combination with each), so $k \le 3$.

What unit 00 left open, closed. Unit 00 showed that with $z$ and $x$ fixed, the third basis is forced to be $y$. The geometric statement does more: any two unbiased bases are two perpendicular axes, and the third is forced to be the axis perpendicular to both. Problem P1 asks you to prove in addition that a unitary carries any unbiased pair onto $z$ and $x$ — which needs section 4.

The real states. Real amplitudes $(\cos\tfrac\theta2, \pm\sin\tfrac\theta2)$ have $\mathrm{Im}\,\bar ab = 0$, so $r_2 = 0$: the great circle in the $xz$-plane. Unit 00's real plane holds two unbiased bases because a great circle holds two perpendicular axes; the third needs the $y$ direction, off the circle — which needs $\mathrm{Im}\,\bar ab \ne 0$, which needs $\mathbb C$.

In higher dimension there is no such sphere: the pure states of $\mathbb C^d$ form a space of real dimension $2d - 2$, not a sphere for $d \ge 3$, and the bound $d + 1$ on mutually unbiased bases (cited in unit 00) needs a different proof.

The rotation operators

the rotation operator, course convention

For a unit vector $\mathbf n$ and real $\theta$,

$$R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) := e^{-i\theta\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2} = \cos\tfrac\theta2\,I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma .$$
proof of the closed form

Unit 00's functional calculus on $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = P_+ - P_-$: $\ e^{-i\theta/2}P_+ + e^{i\theta/2}P_-$; substitute $P_\pm = \tfrac12(I \pm \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ and collect.

Unitary (the exponential of $-i\times$Hermitian, unit 00), with $\det R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) = e^{-i\theta/2}e^{i\theta/2} = 1$. Check: $R_{\mathbf n}(0) = I$.

The three coordinate axes, $c = \cos\tfrac\theta2$, $s = \sin\tfrac\theta2$:

$$R_x(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}c & -is\\ -is & c\end{pmatrix},\quad R_y(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}c & -s\\ s & c\end{pmatrix},$$ $$R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix}e^{-i\theta/2} & 0\\ 0 & e^{i\theta/2}\end{pmatrix}.$$
the half-angle is deliberate

The $2$ in $\theta/2$ is chosen so that $\theta$ is the angle the sphere turns through — the next slide. Unit 00's $e^{-i\theta X/2}$ was $R_x(\theta)$. $R_y$ is the only one with real entries: a real rotation of the plane by $\theta/2$.

Gates, named: $S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i) = e^{i\pi/4}R_z(\tfrac\pi2)$ and $T = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4}) = e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\tfrac\pi4)$.

Full proof. $e^{-i\theta/2}\cdot\tfrac12(I + N) + e^{i\theta/2}\cdot\tfrac12(I - N) = \tfrac{e^{-i\theta/2} + e^{i\theta/2}}{2}I + \tfrac{e^{-i\theta/2} - e^{i\theta/2}}{2}N = \cos\tfrac\theta2\,I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,N$, with $N = \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. The same answer comes from the power series, using $N^2 = I$: even powers give the cosine series, odd powers the sine series — unit 00's Worked: exp(iθX) by the spectral calculus with $X$ replaced by $N$.

The matrices. $R_x(\theta) = cI - isX$; $R_y(\theta) = cI - isY = cI - is\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{pmatrix}$, since $(-i)(-i) = -1$ and $(-i)(i) = 1$; $R_z(\theta) = cI - isZ = \mathrm{diag}(c - is, c + is)$.

The gates. $e^{i\pi/4}R_z(\pi/2) = e^{i\pi/4}\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\pi/4}, e^{i\pi/4}) = \mathrm{diag}(1, i) = S$ ✓, and the same with $\pi/8$ gives $T$. These are the course's conventions (AUTHORING.md): $S$ and $T$ are quarter- and eighth-turns about $z$, up to global phase. Composition along one axis. $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)R_{\mathbf n}(\theta') = R_{\mathbf n}(\theta + \theta')$, since the exponents commute. Along different axes there is no such rule; problem P5 composes two quarter-turns about $x$ and $y$.

Conjugation rotates the Pauli vector

theorem

Let $R$ be the rotation of $\mathbb R^3$ by $\theta$ about $\mathbf n$ (right-hand rule). For every $\mathbf r\in\mathbb R^3$,

$$R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)\,(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\,R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)^\dagger = (R\mathbf r)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma .$$
proof

Write $U = cI - isN$, $N = \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. Split $\mathbf r = (\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf n)\mathbf n + \mathbf m$ with $\mathbf m\perp\mathbf n$. $N$ commutes with $U$, so the first part is fixed. $M = \mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ anticommutes with $N$, so $MU^\dagger = UM$ and

$$\begin{aligned}UMU^\dagger = U^2M &= (\cos\theta\,I - i\sin\theta\,N)M\\ &= \cos\theta\,M + \sin\theta\,(\mathbf n\times\mathbf m)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma,\end{aligned}$$

using $NM = i(\mathbf n\times\mathbf m)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. That is the rotation of $\mathbf m$ by $\theta$ in the plane $\perp\mathbf n$.

The part of $\mathbf r$ along $\mathbf n$ stays; the perpendicular part turns by $\theta$, anticlockwise seen from the tip of $\mathbf n$.

Full proof. (i) $U$ is a combination of $I$ and $N$, so $UN = NU$ and $U(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger = NUU^\dagger = N$: the component along the axis is unchanged. (ii) For $\mathbf m\perp\mathbf n$, the product rule gives $NM = (\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf m)I + i(\mathbf n\times\mathbf m)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = i(\mathbf n\times\mathbf m)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ and $MN = i(\mathbf m\times\mathbf n)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = -NM$. Then $MU^\dagger = M(cI + isN) = (cI - isN)M = UM$. So $UMU^\dagger = UUM = U^2M$, and $U^2 = (c^2 - s^2)I - 2ics\,N = \cos\theta\,I - i\sin\theta\,N$ by the double-angle formulae and $N^2 = I$. Finally $-i\sin\theta\,NM = -i\sin\theta\cdot i(\mathbf n\times\mathbf m)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = \sin\theta(\mathbf n\times\mathbf m)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$.

Why that is the rotation. For $\mathbf m\perp\mathbf n$, the vectors $\mathbf m$ and $\mathbf n\times\mathbf m$ have the same length and are perpendicular, and $(\mathbf m, \mathbf n\times\mathbf m, \mathbf n)$ is right-handed (for $\mathbf n = \hat{\mathbf z}$, $\mathbf m = \hat{\mathbf x}$ it is $(\hat{\mathbf x}, \hat{\mathbf y}, \hat{\mathbf z})$). So $\cos\theta\,\mathbf m + \sin\theta\,\mathbf n\times\mathbf m$ is $\mathbf m$ turned by $\theta$ toward $\mathbf n\times\mathbf m$: anticlockwise about $\mathbf n$. Adding the fixed part gives Rodrigues' formula (1840), $R\mathbf r = \cos\theta\,\mathbf r + \sin\theta\,\mathbf n\times\mathbf r + (1 - \cos\theta)(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)\mathbf n$, and linearity in $\mathbf r$ finishes it.

Check, $\mathbf n = \hat{\mathbf z}$, $\mathbf r = \hat{\mathbf x}$. $R_z(\theta)XR_z(\theta)^\dagger$: the $(0,1)$ entry of $X$ picks up $e^{-i\theta/2}\cdot e^{-i\theta/2} = e^{-i\theta}$, so the result is $\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-i\theta}\\e^{i\theta}&0\end{pmatrix} = \cos\theta\,X + \sin\theta\,Y$ ✓ — $\hat{\mathbf x}$ turned by $\theta$ toward $\hat{\mathbf y}$.

Why the half-angle again. $U$ appears twice in $UMU^\dagger$, so the angle $\theta/2$ in $U$ is doubled. That doubling is the whole of section 4: $U$ and $-U$ conjugate identically.

corollary

proof

$U\ket\psi\!\bra\psi U^\dagger = \tfrac12\big(I + U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger\big) = \tfrac12\big(I + (R\mathbf r)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\big)$, the projector of the point $R\mathbf r$.

the sphere does not see phases

check against unit 00

That is $\hat{\mathbf z}$ turned by $\theta$ about $\hat{\mathbf x}$ — toward $-\hat{\mathbf y}$, since $\hat{\mathbf x}\times\hat{\mathbf z} = -\hat{\mathbf y}$. Worked: the Hadamard gate is a half-turn

$R_{\mathbf n}(\pi)$ for $\mathbf n = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 0, 1)$

1. The closed form at $\theta = \pi$: $\cos\tfrac\pi2 = 0$, $\sin\tfrac\pi2 = 1$, and $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(X + Z)$:

$$R_{\mathbf n}(\pi) = -\frac{i}{\sqrt2}(X + Z) = -\frac{i}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix} = -iH .$$

2. Name it. The Hadamard gate $H := \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(X + Z)$, the course's convention, is the half-turn about $\mathbf n$ up to the global phase $i$: $H = iR_{\mathbf n}(\pi)$. Hermitian, and unitary: $H^2 = \tfrac12(X^2 + XZ + ZX + Z^2) = I$.

3. On the sphere. A half-turn sends $\mathbf v \mapsto 2(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v)\mathbf n - \mathbf v$:

$$\hat{\mathbf z} \mapsto (1, 0, 1) - \hat{\mathbf z} = \hat{\mathbf x},\quad \hat{\mathbf x}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf z},\quad \hat{\mathbf y}\mapsto-\hat{\mathbf y}.$$

4. Check by matrices: $HZH = X$, $\ HXH = Z$, $\ HYH = -Y$; and on states $H\ket0 = \ket+$, $H\ket+ = \ket0$, $H\ket{+i} = e^{i\pi/4}\ket{-i}$.

The Hadamard gate swaps the $z$ and $x$ axes and reverses $y$: a half-turn about the axis midway between $z$ and $x$.

Step 3, the half-turn formula. Rodrigues at $\theta = \pi$: $\cos\pi\,\mathbf v + 0 + (1 - \cos\pi)(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v)\mathbf n = 2(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf v)\mathbf n - \mathbf v$. For $\mathbf v = \hat{\mathbf z}$, $\mathbf n\cdot\hat{\mathbf z} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, so $2\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 0, 1) = (1, 0, 1)$, minus $\hat{\mathbf z}$ leaves $\hat{\mathbf x}$. For $\mathbf v = \hat{\mathbf y}$, $\mathbf n\cdot\hat{\mathbf y} = 0$, so $\hat{\mathbf y}\mapsto -\hat{\mathbf y}$.

Step 4 in full. $HZ = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(XZ + I)$, so $HZH = \tfrac12(XZ + I)(X + Z) = \tfrac12(XZX + XZZ + X + Z) = \tfrac12(-Z + X + X + Z) = X$, using $XZX = -XXZ = -Z$. $HXH = Z$ follows by conjugating $HZH = X$ with $H$ ($H^2 = I$). $HYH$: $Y$ anticommutes with both $X$ and $Z$, so $YH = -HY$ and $HYH = -H^2Y = -Y$. On states: $H(1, 0) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$; $H\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, i) = \tfrac12(1 + i, 1 - i) = \tfrac{1 + i}{2}(1, -i) = e^{i\pi/4}\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, -i)$, using $\tfrac{1 - i}{1 + i} = -i$ and $\tfrac{1 + i}{\sqrt2} = e^{i\pi/4}$. So $\ket{+i} \mapsto \ket{-i}$ up to global phase, the point $\hat{\mathbf y}\mapsto-\hat{\mathbf y}$ ✓.

Why Hadamard matters. It exchanges the $z$ and $x$ bases, so it turns a measurement in one into a measurement in the other. Unit 13 uses it on many qubits at once as a Fourier transform. The name is Jacques Hadamard's, from the $\pm1$ matrices of his 1893 determinant problem; the $2\times2$ one is the smallest.

Phase bookkeeping. As a matrix, $H \ne R_{\mathbf n}(\pi)$: they differ by $i$, and $H$ has determinant $-1$ while every $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$ has determinant $1$. The sphere cannot tell them apart. Section 4 shows which phases are forced.

Every Hamiltonian is a precession

theorem

Every Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix is $H = a\,I + \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $a\in\mathbb R$, $\mathbf b\in\mathbb R^3$. For $\mathbf b \ne 0$ (with $\hbar = 1$),

$$e^{-iHt} = e^{-iat}\,R_{\hat{\mathbf b}}(2|\mathbf b|t).$$

The Bloch vector precesses about $\hat{\mathbf b}$ at angular frequency $2|\mathbf b|$ — the gap between the energies $a \pm |\mathbf b|$.

proof

The form: the Pauli basis, real coefficients. $aI$ commutes with $\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, so the exponential splits (unit 00), and $e^{-it\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma} = e^{-i(2|\mathbf b|t)\,\hat{\mathbf b}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}$.

Read it. The $I$ part is a global phase: it shifts both energies and changes nothing observable. The traceless part is an axis and a speed. Unit 01's Stone theorem said every evolution is $e^{-iHt}$; for a qubit, every evolution is therefore a steady turning of the sphere about a fixed axis.

checks

Full proof. Every Hermitian $2\times2$ matrix has real Pauli coefficients (section 1), $a = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}H$ and $b_k = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_kH)$. The energies. Full derivation. $H/\hbar = -\tfrac{\gamma B_0}{2}Z$ is $a I + \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $a = 0$ and $\mathbf b = (0, 0, -\omega_0/2)$; $|\mathbf b| = \omega_0/2$ and $\hat{\mathbf b} = -\hat{\mathbf z}$. For a spin in a uniform field the expectation values move exactly as the classical moment does. The sense. For $\gamma \gt 0$ the precession is clockwise seen from the tip of $\mathbf B$; for the electron and neutron, $\gamma \lt 0$, anticlockwise. SU(2) covers the rotations twice

The map from $2\times2$ unitaries to rotations is a group homomorphism. It is onto, and exactly two unitaries, $\pm U$, give each rotation. So a full turn is $-I$.

SU(2), and why every element is a rotation operator

the groups

$U(2)$: the $2\times2$ unitaries, under multiplication. The special unitary group $SU(2) = \{V\in U(2) : \det V = 1\}$, a subgroup.

lemma: phases split off

Every $U\in U(2)$ is $e^{i\alpha}V$ with $V\in SU(2)$; the pair $(e^{i\alpha}, V)$ is unique up to $(-e^{i\alpha}, -V)$.

Proof: $|\det U| = 1$; choose $\alpha$ with $e^{2i\alpha} = \det U$, set $V = e^{-i\alpha}U$, so $\det V = e^{-2i\alpha}\det U = 1$. The other root is $\alpha + \pi$.

theorem

Every $V\in SU(2)$ equals $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$ for some unit $\mathbf n$ and some $\theta\in[0, 2\pi]$.

proof

$V$ is unitary, so normal, with eigenvalues of modulus one whose product is $1$: $e^{-i\theta/2}$ and $e^{i\theta/2}$, $\theta\in[0, 2\pi]$. If they coincide, $V = \pm I = R_{\mathbf n}(0)$ or $R_{\mathbf n}(2\pi)$. Otherwise

$$V = e^{-i\theta/2}P + e^{i\theta/2}(I - P)$$

with $P$ a rank-one projector, $P = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ for a unit $\mathbf n$ (section 2). That is the closed form of $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$.

Check: $-iH$ has eigenvalues $\mp i = e^{\mp i\pi/2}$, so $\theta = \pi$ — the worked half-turn ✓.

Full proof of the theorem. By the spectral theorem (unit 00), $V = \lambda_1P_1 + \lambda_2P_2$ with $|\lambda_j| = 1$ and $\lambda_1\lambda_2 = \det V = 1$, so $\lambda_2 = \bar\lambda_1$. The pair is $e^{\mp i\beta}$ for one $\beta\in[0, \pi]$; label them so that $\lambda_1 = e^{-i\beta}$, and set $\theta = 2\beta\in[0, 2\pi]$. If $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2$ then $\lambda_1 = \pm1$ and $V = \pm I$; and $R_{\mathbf n}(0) = I$, $R_{\mathbf n}(2\pi) = \cos\pi\,I = -I$ for every $\mathbf n$. Otherwise the eigenspaces are lines, $P := P_1$ is a rank-one orthogonal projector, so $P = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$ for a unit $\psi$, and by the theorem A state's projector is a point of the sphere, $P = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ with $\mathbf n$ the Bloch vector of $\psi$. Then $V = e^{-i\theta/2}\tfrac12(I + N) + e^{i\theta/2}\tfrac12(I - N) = \cos\tfrac\theta2\,I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2\,N$.

So $SU(2)$ is the set of rotation operators, and $U(2)$ is the set of rotation operators times phases. The axis is the Bloch vector of the $e^{-i\theta/2}$-eigenvector. Problem P6 gives the other standard description, $V = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha&-\bar\beta\\\beta&\bar\alpha\end{pmatrix}$ with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$, which shows $SU(2)$ is a three-sphere.

The check. $-iH = -\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $(-i)^2\det H = (-1)(-1) = 1$ ✓, and $H$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$, so $-iH$ has $\mp i$. $e^{-i\theta/2} = -i$ gives $\theta = \pi$, and the $-i$-eigenvector of $-iH$ is the $+1$-eigenvector of $H$, whose Bloch vector is $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 0, 1)$ — the axis of the worked example ✓.

The homomorphism from SU(2) to rotations

the rotation group SO(3), and Φ

$SO(3) = \{R\in M_3(\mathbb R) : R^{\mathsf T}R = I,\ \det R = 1\}$. For $U\in SU(2)$ define $\Phi(U)\in M_3(\mathbb R)$ by

$$U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger = \big(\Phi(U)\mathbf r\big)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\quad\text{for all }\mathbf r,$$

so $\Phi(U)_{jk} = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}\big(\sigma_jU\sigma_kU^\dagger\big)$.

check, U = R_z(θ)

$X\mapsto\cos\theta X + \sin\theta Y$, $\ Y\mapsto -\sin\theta X + \cos\theta Y$, $\ Z\mapsto Z$: $\ \Phi(R_z(\theta))$ is the familiar $3\times3$ rotation by $\theta$ about $z$ ✓.

theorem

$\Phi$ is a well-defined group homomorphism $SU(2)\to SO(3)$, and $\Phi(R_{\mathbf n}(\theta))$ is the rotation by $\theta$ about $\mathbf n$.

proof

Well defined: $U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger$ is Hermitian and traceless, so it is $\mathbf s\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ for one real $\mathbf s$, linear in $\mathbf r$. Homomorphism:

$$\begin{aligned}UV(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)V^\dagger U^\dagger &= U\big((\Phi(V)\mathbf r)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\big)U^\dagger\\ &= \big(\Phi(U)\Phi(V)\mathbf r\big)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma.\end{aligned}$$

Into $SO(3)$: every $U$ is some $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$, and section 3's theorem says $\Phi$ of it is the rotation by $\theta$ about $\mathbf n$.

Full proof of well-definedness. $(U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger)^\dagger = U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^\dagger U^\dagger = U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger$, and $\operatorname{tr}(U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger) = \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = 0$. By the Pauli basis a traceless Hermitian matrix is $\mathbf s\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $\mathbf s$ real and unique, and $\mathbf r\mapsto\mathbf s$ is linear because conjugation is. So it is a real $3\times3$ matrix, with entries $s_j = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(\sigma_j\,\mathbf s\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$, giving the trace formula with $\mathbf r = \mathbf e_k$.

Orthogonality without section 3. $\det(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = \det\begin{pmatrix}r_3&r_1 - ir_2\\r_1 + ir_2&-r_3\end{pmatrix} = -r_3^2 - (r_1^2 + r_2^2) = -|\mathbf r|^2$, and conjugation preserves determinants, so $|\Phi(U)\mathbf r| = |\mathbf r|$: $\Phi(U)$ is orthogonal. That $\det\Phi(U) = +1$, not $-1$, is where the slide uses section 3: each $\Phi(U)$ is an actual rotation. (A second argument: $SU(2)$ is connected — problem P6 shows it is a three-sphere — and $\det\Phi$ is a continuous function to $\{\pm1\}$ equal to $1$ at $I$.)

The check in full. $R_z(\theta)XR_z(\theta)^\dagger = \cos\theta X + \sin\theta Y$ was computed in the notes to Conjugation rotates the Pauli vector; $Y$ is the same with $\hat{\mathbf y}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf y} = -\hat{\mathbf x}$ turned in; $Z$ commutes with $R_z(\theta)$. So the columns of $\Phi(R_z(\theta))$ are $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta, 0)$, $(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta, 0)$, $(0, 0, 1)$.

On $U(2)$. Defining $\Phi$ by the same formula on all of $U(2)$ changes nothing, since a phase cancels; its kernel there is all the phases $e^{i\alpha}I$. Restricting to $SU(2)$ leaves only the sign — next slides.

Euler's rotation theorem

Euler's rotation theorem (1775)

Every $R\in SO(3)$ is a rotation by some angle $\theta$ about some unit axis $\mathbf n$.

proof

An axis. $\det(R - I) = \det(R - RR^{\mathsf T}) = \det R\,\det(I - R^{\mathsf T}) = \det(I - R)$, and for $3\times3$ matrices $\det(I - R) = -\det(R - I)$. So $\det(R - I) = 0$: some unit $\mathbf n$ has $R\mathbf n = \mathbf n$.

A turn about it. $R$ is orthogonal, so it maps the plane $\mathbf n^\perp$ to itself, and $\det R = 1\cdot\det(R|_{\mathbf n^\perp})$. A $2\times2$ orthogonal matrix of determinant one is $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\\sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ in a right-handed basis of $\mathbf n^\perp$.

This is the step that uses three: in even dimension $\det(-A) = \det A$ and the argument proves nothing.

A rotation fixes a line and turns the plane perpendicular to it rigidly.

Full proof. The determinant identity: $R^{\mathsf T}R = I$ gives $RR^{\mathsf T} = I$, so $R - I = R - RR^{\mathsf T} = R(I - R^{\mathsf T})$ and $\det(R - I) = \det R\det(I - R^{\mathsf T}) = 1\cdot\det(I - R)^{\mathsf T} = \det(I - R) = (-1)^3\det(R - I)$. Hence $1$ is an eigenvalue, with a real eigenvector since $R - I$ is a real singular matrix. For $\mathbf v\perp\mathbf n$: $R\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n = R\mathbf v\cdot R\mathbf n = \mathbf v\cdot\mathbf n = 0$, so $R$ preserves $\mathbf n^\perp$. In a basis $(\mathbf e_1, \mathbf e_2, \mathbf n)$, orthonormal and right-handed, $R$ is block-diagonal with blocks $Q$ ($2\times2$, orthogonal) and $1$, so $\det Q = \det R = 1$. An orthogonal $Q$ has first column a unit vector $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ and second column a unit vector perpendicular to it, $\pm(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$; determinant one picks $+$. So $R\mathbf e_1 = \cos\theta\,\mathbf e_1 + \sin\theta\,\mathbf e_2$ and $\mathbf e_2 = \mathbf n\times\mathbf e_1$: rotation by $\theta$ about $\mathbf n$ in the right-hand sense.

Uniqueness. For $R \ne I$ the axis is unique up to sign, and $(\mathbf n, \theta)$ and $(-\mathbf n, 2\pi - \theta)$ describe the same rotation. The angle is read off the trace: $\operatorname{tr}R = 1 + 2\cos\theta$.

History and the background. Leonhard Euler proved in 1775 that any motion of a rigid body about a fixed point is a rotation about some axis. The proof above is the linear-algebra one, needing only determinants; it is the standard fact about $SO(3)$ that the background assumes, proved here because the surjectivity of $\Phi$ rests on it.

Φ is onto, and its kernel is ±I

theorem

$\Phi: SU(2)\to SO(3)$ is surjective, with kernel $\{I, -I\}$. Hence

$$SO(3)\cong SU(2)/\{\pm I\},$$

and every rotation has exactly two preimages, $\pm U$: $SU(2)$ is a double cover of $SO(3)$.

proof

Onto: by Euler, $R$ is the rotation by $\theta$ about $\mathbf n$, which is $\Phi(R_{\mathbf n}(\theta))$.

Kernel: $\Phi(U) = I$ iff $U\sigma_kU^\dagger = \sigma_k$ for $k = 1, 2, 3$ iff $U$ commutes with $X$, $Y$, $Z$ — which forces $U = \pm I$ (worked next).

Then: the first isomorphism theorem; preimages of one rotation form a coset $U\{\pm I\}$.

What it says. Rotations of the sphere and unitaries up to phase are the same thing: $U(2)/\{\text{phases}\} \cong SO(3)$. But inside $SU(2)$, where the phase is pinned down to a sign, every rotation has two lifts.

two lifts, not two rotations

$R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$ and $-R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) = R_{\mathbf n}(\theta + 2\pi)$ move every point of the sphere identically. They differ as operators on $\mathbb C^2$ — by the sign every vector picks up.

Check: $-I = R_{\mathbf n}(2\pi)$ is in the kernel, and indeed the rotation by $2\pi$ is the identity of $\mathbb R^3$ ✓.

Full proof that $U(2)$ modulo phases is $SO(3)$. Extend $\Phi$ to $U(2)$ by the same formula. It is still a homomorphism, still onto (already on $SU(2)$), and its kernel is the set of $U$ commuting with all three Paulis, which by the next slide's computation is $\{\lambda I : |\lambda| = 1\}$. So $U(2)/U(1) \cong SO(3)$: a qubit gate up to global phase is exactly a rotation of the Bloch sphere, and any rotation is some gate.

Why $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta + 2\pi) = -R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$. $\cos(\tfrac\theta2 + \pi) = -\cos\tfrac\theta2$ and $\sin(\tfrac\theta2 + \pi) = -\sin\tfrac\theta2$ in the closed form.

Covering, in the topological sense. Near each point, $\Phi$ is a homeomorphism from a neighbourhood of $U$ to a neighbourhood of $\Phi(U)$, and the two preimages $\pm U$ are far apart; so $\Phi$ is a two-sheeted covering map, and since $SU(2)\cong S^3$ is simply connected, it is the universal cover of $SO(3)$. This vocabulary is not used later and is not developed; the algebraic statement — kernel $\{\pm I\}$ — is all the course needs.

Where it returns. Unit 10 builds controlled gates from the $SU(2)$ form of a unitary, where the sign becomes a relative phase between the control's branches and can no longer be dropped. Unit 11's gate synthesis works in $SU(2)$ for the same reason.

A turn by 2π is −I

spinors turn twice $$R_{\mathbf n}(2\pi) = \cos\pi\,I - i\sin\pi\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma = -I,$$ $$R_{\mathbf n}(4\pi) = \cos2\pi\,I = I .$$

A vector of $\mathbb C^2$ acted on this way is a spinor: a full turn of the sphere multiplies it by $-1$, and it takes two full turns to come home.

the proton, after one turn

At $t = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 15.66$ ns, $\ e^{-iHt/\hbar} = R_z(-2\pi) = -I$: the Bloch vector is back where it started and the state is $-\psi$.

Along $\theta\mapsto R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$ the operator returns only at $4\pi$; the rotation it induces returns at $2\pi$.

invisible on its own

$-\psi$ and $\psi$ differ by a global phase, so no measurement on the qubit alone can tell them apart (unit 01). The sign can only show relative to something not rotated.

The general statement. Along any path of evolution $U(t) = e^{-iHt}$ with $H = \mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ traceless, the operator is $R_{\hat{\mathbf b}}(2|\mathbf b|t)$, and $\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}U(t) = \cos(|\mathbf b|t)$, which is $-1$ exactly when the Bloch sphere has made an odd number of full turns. A trace term $aI$ in $H$ would add the phase $e^{-iat}$ and move the sign around — but that phase is shared by every state and is just as invisible.

Why "spinor". The objects were found by Élie Cartan in 1913, and the word is usually credited to Paul Ehrenfest. It names exactly this: objects on which rotations act only up to a sign. Vectors of $\mathbb R^3$ come back after $2\pi$; spinors need $4\pi$. Every particle physicists call "spin-½" — electron, proton, neutron — has a two-dimensional state space carrying this action.

A classical analogy, offered with caution. Hold a cup flat on your palm and turn it a full turn about the vertical: your arm is twisted. A second full turn in the same sense, carried over the head, untwists it. The configuration of cup and arm returns after $4\pi$, not $2\pi$ — a physical model of the fact that $SO(3)$ has a loop that cannot be shrunk but whose double can. It is an illustration, not a proof.

Seeing the sign: neutron interferometry

A beam of neutrons is split by one blade of a silicon crystal, the arms recombined, and the counts at D1 recorded as a magnetic field in arm B turns the spin by $\alpha$. The counts return at $\alpha = 4\pi$, not $2\pi$.

From nothing. A neutron is an uncharged particle of the atomic nucleus. Sent through a perfect silicon crystal, it is reflected with an amplitude, not with certainty, so a blade of crystal splits it as unit 01's half-silvered mirror splits light. It has a magnetic moment, so a field in one arm turns its spin.

The computation. Take the spin along the field, $\ket0$. Then arm B carries $R_z(\alpha)\ket0 = e^{-i\alpha/2}\ket0$: a relative phase $\phi = -\alpha/2$ between the arms. Unit 01's interferometer gives $P(\mathrm{D1}) = \cos^2\tfrac\phi2 = \cos^2\tfrac\alpha4$: period $4\pi$. At $\alpha = 2\pi$ the bright detector goes dark.

Rauch et al., Phys. Lett. A 54, 425 (1975); Werner et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 35, 1053 (1975). Cited: both found the $4\pi$ period.

The apparatus. A neutron interferometer is cut from a single, flawless silicon crystal into three parallel blades on a common base (Rauch, Treimer and Bonse, 1974). Neutrons from a research reactor travel as waves whose wavelength is comparable to the spacing of silicon's atomic planes, so each blade reflects part of the beam and transmits part, with amplitudes fixed by the crystal: blade one splits the beam, blade two turns each arm back toward the other, blade three recombines them in front of two detectors. Because the blades are one crystal, the arms stay aligned to a fraction of an atomic spacing — the reason the experiment works at all. Abstractly it is unit 01's Mach–Zehnder interferometer, and unit 01's analysis applies with "photon" replaced by "neutron".

The computation in full. The field in arm B, along the spin axis, applies $R_z(\alpha)$ for some angle $\alpha$ proportional to field strength times time spent in it. On $\ket0$ this is the phase $e^{-i\alpha/2}$, so the path state after the field has a relative phase $\phi = -\alpha/2$ on arm B. Unit 01: with relative phase $\phi$, $P(\mathrm{D0}) = \sin^2\tfrac\phi2$ and $P(\mathrm{D1}) = \cos^2\tfrac\phi2$, summing to one. So $P(\mathrm{D1}) = \cos^2\tfrac\alpha4 = \tfrac12(1 + \cos\tfrac\alpha2)$: $1$ at $\alpha = 0$, $0$ at $2\pi$, $1$ at $4\pi$. If a $2\pi$ rotation were $+I$, the period would be $2\pi$ (dashed curve).

Other spin states, and what the experiments did. For a spin $\chi$ not along the field, arm B carries $R(\alpha)\chi$, a different spin state, and the interference term involves $\braket{\chi|R(\alpha)\chi} = \cos\tfrac\alpha2 - i\sin\tfrac\alpha2\,(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_\chi)$ — whose real part is $\cos\tfrac\alpha2$ for every $\chi$. The experiments used unpolarised beams; describing a particle with both a path and a spin needs unit 03's tensor product, and an unpolarised beam needs unit 04, so the general case is not derived here. The $4\pi$ period they measured is the one computed above.

a time-dependent Hamiltonian

Unit 01's Stone theorem covers a fixed $H$. With $H(t)$, the law is $i\hbar\,\dot\psi = H(t)\psi$ — the generator taken instant by instant; unit 25 proves products of short constant steps converge to it. It keeps $\lVert\psi\rVert$ fixed, since $H(t)$ is Hermitian.

Left: in the lab the drive turns. Right: in a frame turning with it, both fields stand still, and add to one fixed field — the next slide.

Why "same sense". For $\gamma \gt 0$ the free precession is $R_z(-\omega_0t)$, clockwise from $+z$, and the field $(\cos\omega t, -\sin\omega t, 0)$ also turns clockwise. A field turning the other way is the counter-rotating one of the slide A linear drive is two rotating ones; it is almost invisible to the spin, as that slide explains.

The Hamiltonian. $-\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $\mathbf B(t)$ as on the slide gives $-\tfrac{\hbar}{2}\big[\gamma B_1\cos\omega t\,X - \gamma B_1\sin\omega t\,Y + \gamma B_0Z\big]$. It is Hermitian at every $t$ (real Pauli coefficients).

The evolution law, and norm conservation. For a time-dependent field the evolution is no longer a one-parameter group, so Stone's theorem does not apply directly. The law $i\hbar\dot\psi = H(t)\psi$ is the natural extension — over a short time in which $H$ barely changes, it reproduces $e^{-iH\Delta t/\hbar}$ — and unit 25 justifies it by bounding the error of piecewise-constant approximations. Here it is taken as the law. It preserves the norm: $\tfrac{d}{dt}\braket{\psi|\psi} = \braket{\dot\psi|\psi} + \braket{\psi|\dot\psi} = \tfrac{i}{\hbar}\braket{H\psi|\psi} - \tfrac{i}{\hbar}\braket{\psi|H\psi} = 0$, by Hermiticity. This deck never needs a general existence theorem: the next slide writes the solution down and checks it.

How a scanner makes $B_1$. A coil around the patient carries an alternating current at $\omega$, near $\omega_0$; it makes a field oscillating along one direction, which the slide just named decomposes into a co-rotating and a counter-rotating part. The rotating frame, derived

theorem: the rotating frame

Let $V(t) = R_z(-\omega t)$ and $\tilde\psi := V(t)^\dagger\psi$. Then $\tilde\psi$ obeys $i\hbar\dot{\tilde\psi} = \tilde H\tilde\psi$ with the constant

$$\tilde H = -\frac\hbar2\big(\Delta\,Z + \Omega\,X\big),\qquad \Delta := \omega_0 - \omega ,$$

the detuning. So $\psi(t) = R_z(-\omega t)\,e^{-i\tilde Ht/\hbar}\psi(0)$.

proof

By section 3, $VXV^\dagger = \cos\omega t\,X - \sin\omega t\,Y$, so $H(t) = V\big[-\tfrac\hbar2(\omega_0Z + \Omega X)\big]V^\dagger$. With $\dot V = \tfrac{i\omega}{2}ZV$ and $\psi = V\tilde\psi$:

$$\begin{aligned}i\hbar\dot{\tilde\psi} &= \big(V^\dagger HV - i\hbar V^\dagger\dot V\big)\tilde\psi\\ &= \Big(-\tfrac\hbar2(\omega_0Z + \Omega X) + \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z\Big)\tilde\psi .\end{aligned}$$
read it: an effective field

$\tilde H = -\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\mathbf B_{\text{eff}}\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $\mathbf B_{\text{eff}} = \big(B_1,\ 0,\ B_0 - \omega/\gamma\big)$: in the turning frame the drive stands still along $x$, and turning removes $\omega/\gamma$ of the static field. On resonance, $\omega = \omega_0$, only $B_1$ is left.

signs, against the convention box

With the proton's signs, $\tilde H$ carries an overall minus, and $\Delta \gt 0$ means driving below $\omega_0$. Texts that write $\tilde H = +\tfrac\hbar2(\Delta Z + \Omega X)$ turn the other way; every probability depends only on $\Delta^2$ and $\Omega^2$.

Check: $\Omega = 0$ gives $\psi(t) = R_z(-\omega t)R_z(-(\omega_0 - \omega)t)\psi(0) = R_z(-\omega_0t)\psi(0)$ — free precession ✓.

Full proof. (i) $V = R_z(-\omega t)$ implements the rotation by $-\omega t$ about $z$, which sends $\hat{\mathbf x}$ to $(\cos\omega t, -\sin\omega t, 0)$; so $VXV^\dagger = \cos\omega t\,X - \sin\omega t\,Y$, and $VZV^\dagger = Z$. Hence $V\big[-\tfrac\hbar2(\omega_0Z + \Omega X)\big]V^\dagger = H(t)$. (ii) $V(t) = e^{i\omega tZ/2}$, whose derivative is $\tfrac{i\omega}{2}ZV$ (unit 01's derivative of $e^{-iHt}$, from the spectral form). (iii) If $\psi = V\tilde\psi$, the product rule gives $\dot\psi = \dot V\tilde\psi + V\dot{\tilde\psi}$, so $i\hbar V\dot{\tilde\psi} = H V\tilde\psi - i\hbar\dot V\tilde\psi$; multiply by $V^\dagger$. $-i\hbar V^\dagger\dot V = -i\hbar\cdot\tfrac{i\omega}{2}Z = \tfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}Z$. Adding: $-\tfrac\hbar2\big((\omega_0 - \omega)Z + \Omega X\big)$.

The solution exists, and is the only one. $\tilde\psi(t) = e^{-i\tilde Ht/\hbar}\tilde\psi(0)$ solves the constant equation, so $\psi(t) = V(t)e^{-i\tilde Ht/\hbar}\psi(0)$ solves the lab one — run step (iii) backwards. If $\chi$ were another solution with $\chi(0) = \psi(0)$, then $\tilde\chi = V^\dagger\chi$ solves the constant equation, and $\tfrac{d}{dt}\big(e^{i\tilde Ht/\hbar}\tilde\chi\big) = 0$ forces $\tilde\chi(t) = e^{-i\tilde Ht/\hbar}\tilde\chi(0)$: the same.

Why the frame is not a trick. $\tilde\psi$ and $\psi$ differ by the $z$-rotation $V(t)$, which changes neither $|\braket{0|\cdot}|$ nor $|\braket{1|\cdot}|$ (it is diagonal). So level populations are the same in both frames, and the rotating frame may be used to compute them. Only the azimuth on the sphere differs, by $-\omega t$.

Checked numerically against a direct step-by-step integration of the lab equation, for $\omega_0 = 10$, $\Omega = 0.7$, $\omega = 9.6$: the two unitaries agree to the integration error.

Rabi oscillation

Rabi oscillation (Rabi, 1937)

Starting in $\ket0$, the probability of the upper level is

$$P_1(t) = \frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega'^2}\,\sin^2\frac{\Omega't}{2},$$

with the generalised Rabi frequency $\Omega' = \sqrt{\Omega^2 + \Delta^2}$.

proof

$\tilde H = -\tfrac{\hbar\Omega'}{2}\,\mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, $\ \mathbf m = (\Omega, 0, \Delta)/\Omega'$: a precession about $\mathbf m$ (section 3),

$$e^{-i\tilde Ht/\hbar} = \cos\tfrac{\Omega't}{2}\,I + i\sin\tfrac{\Omega't}2\,\mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma .$$

Its $(1, 0)$ entry is $i\sin\tfrac{\Omega't}{2}(m_1 + im_2) = i\tfrac{\Omega}{\Omega'}\sin\tfrac{\Omega't}{2}$; $V(t)$ is diagonal and leaves $|\cdot|$ alone.

Sum: $P_0 = \cos^2\tfrac{\Omega't}2 + \tfrac{\Delta^2}{\Omega'^2}\sin^2\tfrac{\Omega't}2$, and $P_0 + P_1 = 1$ ✓.

On resonance the population swings fully between the levels. Detuned, it swings faster and never completes: the peak is $\Omega^2/\Omega'^2$, which is $\tfrac12$ at $\Delta = \Omega$ and $\tfrac15$ at $\Delta = 2\Omega$.

Full proof. $\Delta Z + \Omega X = \Omega'\,\mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $|\mathbf m| = 1$. The theorem Every Hamiltonian is a precession, with $\hbar$ restored and $\mathbf b = -\tfrac{\Omega'}2\mathbf m$, gives $e^{-i\tilde Ht/\hbar} = R_{\mathbf m}(-\Omega't) = \cos\tfrac{\Omega't}2I + i\sin\tfrac{\Omega't}2\,\mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. The $(1, 0)$ entry of $\mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ is $m_1 + im_2$ (from the matrix of $\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ in section 1), here $\Omega/\Omega'$, while $I$ has none. So $\braket{1|\tilde\psi(t)} = i\tfrac{\Omega}{\Omega'}\sin\tfrac{\Omega't}{2}$, and $\braket{1|\psi(t)} = \braket{1|V(t)\tilde\psi(t)} = e^{-i\omega t/2}\braket{1|\tilde\psi(t)}$, of the same modulus. The $(0, 0)$ entry is $\cos\tfrac{\Omega't}2 + i\sin\tfrac{\Omega't}2\,m_3$, of squared modulus $\cos^2 + \tfrac{\Delta^2}{\Omega'^2}\sin^2$; adding $\tfrac{\Omega^2}{\Omega'^2}\sin^2$ gives $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$.

On the sphere. In the rotating frame the Bloch vector circles the fixed axis $\mathbf m$, the direction of $\mathbf B_{\text{eff}}$, starting at the north pole. On resonance the axis lies in the equator and the circle passes through the south pole; detuned, the axis tilts up toward $z$ and the circle no longer reaches it. The lowest point reached has $\braket Z = 1 - 2\Omega^2/\Omega'^2$.

Checks. $\Delta = 0$: $P_1 = \sin^2\tfrac{\Omega t}2$, reaching $1$ at $t = \pi/\Omega$. $\Omega = 0$: $P_1 = 0$ for ever — no drive, no transitions. $|\Delta|\gg\Omega$: the peak $\Omega^2/\Delta^2$ is small, which is why driving far from $\omega_0$ does nothing.

History. Isidor Rabi, "Space quantization in a gyrating magnetic field", Phys. Rev. 51, 652 (1937), solved exactly this problem; in 1938 his group used it to measure nuclear moments in molecular beams by sweeping $\omega$ through resonance — the method behind atomic clocks and, through Bloch and Purcell's 1946 experiments on bulk matter, MRI.

Normalised: $\tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1$. An equal superposition: $P_0 = P_1 = \tfrac12$.

the π-pulse, t = π/Ω $$R_x(-\pi) = iX:\quad \ket0\mapsto i\ket1,\ \ \ket1\mapsto i\ket0 .$$

A flip, up to global phase. Two $\pi$-pulses give $(iX)^2 = -I$: a $2\pi$ turn.

In the rotating frame: the $\pi/2$-pulse lays $\ket0$ on the equator at $+y$; the $\pi$-pulse carries it to the south pole. In the lab both states also precess at $\omega_0$.

Every step. $R_x(-\theta) = \cos\tfrac\theta2I + i\sin\tfrac\theta2X$. A linear drive is two rotating ones

A single coil makes a field that oscillates along one line, $2B_1\cos\omega t\,\hat{\mathbf x}$. As vectors in the plane,

$$2\cos\omega t\,\hat{\mathbf x} = \underbrace{(\cos\omega t, -\sin\omega t)}_{\text{co-rotating}} + \underbrace{(\cos\omega t, \sin\omega t)}_{\text{counter}} .$$

In the rotating frame the co-rotating half stands still, as before; the counter-rotating term turns at $2\omega$:

$$\begin{aligned}\tilde H(t) = -\tfrac\hbar2\big[&\Delta Z + \Omega X\\ &+ \Omega(\cos2\omega t\,X + \sin2\omega t\,Y)\big].\end{aligned}$$

An oscillating vector is the sum of two equal vectors turning in opposite senses. Only the co-rotating one keeps pace with the precession.

Full derivation. The lab Hamiltonian with $\mathbf B = (2B_1\cos\omega t, 0, B_0)$ is the slide Driving the proton's one plus $-\tfrac{\hbar\Omega}{2}(\cos\omega t\,X + \sin\omega t\,Y)$, the counter-rotating part: it is $-\tfrac{\hbar\Omega}{2}WXW^\dagger$ with $W(t) = R_z(\omega t)$, since $WXW^\dagger = \cos\omega t\,X + \sin\omega t\,Y$. The rotating-wave approximation, and its price

the rotating-wave approximation

Drop the counter-rotating term. It is justified when

$$\Omega,\ |\Delta| \ll \omega_0 .$$
its first correction (cited)

The counter-rotating term shifts the resonance up by the Bloch–Siegert shift

$$\delta\omega \approx \frac{\Omega^2}{4\omega_0}$$

(Bloch and Siegert, Phys. Rev. 57, 522, 1940).

the two pictures

Larmor precession and Rabi oscillation are how every qubit platform is controlled: nuclei, ions, superconducting circuits.

For the proton: $\Omega/\omega_0 = 7.8\times10^{-6}$. The shift is

$$\frac{\delta\omega}{2\pi} = \frac{(499.9\ \text{Hz})^2}{4\times63.87\ \text{MHz}} = 0.98\ \text{mHz},$$

a fraction $\tfrac14(\Omega/\omega_0)^2 = 1.5\times10^{-11}$ of the Larmor frequency. A $\pi$-pulse misses full inversion by at most about $(3\Omega/4\omega_0)^2 = 3\times10^{-11}$, depending on the drive's phase at switch-on. Negligible.

where it stops being negligible

A superconducting qubit at $5$ GHz driven at $\Omega/2\pi = 20$ MHz: $\Omega/\omega_0 = 4\times10^{-3}$, five hundred times larger, and a $20$ kHz shift. Unit 25 drives such a qubit.

What is cited and what was computed. The shift $\Omega^2/4\omega_0$ is Bloch and Siegert's result, obtained by perturbation theory in $\Omega/\omega_0$; a modern derivation averages the fast term over its period (Floquet theory; J. H. Shirley, Phys. Rev. 138, B979, 1965). It is not proved here. Its sign — upward — and size were checked numerically for this deck by integrating the full linear-drive equation with $\Omega/\omega_0 = 0.1$: the effective Rabi frequency is smallest at $\omega = \omega_0 + 0.0025\,\omega_0 = \omega_0 + \Omega^2/4\omega_0$. The $\pi$-pulse's shortfall is also numerical, and it depends on where in its cycle the drive $2B_1\cos(\omega t + \chi)$ is switched on. Integrating a resonant $\pi$-pulse from $\ket0$ at $\Omega/\omega_0 = 0.04$, $0.02$, $0.01$ with $\chi = 0$ (switched on at a crest) gives shortfalls $1.0\times10^{-4}$, $2.5\times10^{-5}$, $6.25\times10^{-6}$, each $(\Omega/4\omega_0)^2$; scanning $\chi$ moves the factor between nearly $0$ and $9$, the largest at $\chi = \pi/2$ (switched on at a zero of the field). So the shortfall is at most about $(3\Omega/4\omega_0)^2$: of order $(\Omega/\omega_0)^2$, with no fixed coefficient.

The numbers. $499.86^2/(4\times63.866\times10^6) = 9.78\times10^{-4}$ Hz. $\tfrac14(7.83\times10^{-6})^2 = 1.53\times10^{-11}$. $(7.83\times10^{-6}/4)^2 = 3.8\times10^{-12}$, and nine times that is $3.4\times10^{-11}$. For the superconducting qubit: $20\times10^6/5\times10^9 = 4\times10^{-3}$; $(20\ \text{MHz})^2/(4\times5\ \text{GHz}) = 20$ kHz; and $4\times10^{-3}/7.83\times10^{-6} = 511$.

The Z-Y-Z decomposition

theorem: ZYZ decomposition

Every $U\in U(2)$ is, for some real $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$,

$$U = e^{i\alpha}\,R_z(\beta)\,R_y(\gamma)\,R_z(\delta).$$

Multiply out, with $c = \cos\tfrac\gamma2$, $s = \sin\tfrac\gamma2$, $p = \tfrac{\beta + \delta}2$, $q = \tfrac{\beta - \delta}2$:

$$R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta) = \begin{pmatrix}e^{-ip}c & -e^{-iq}s\\ e^{iq}s & e^{ip}c\end{pmatrix}.$$

The angles $\beta, \gamma, \delta$ are Euler angles.

proof

1. $U = e^{i\alpha}V$, $V\in SU(2)$ (lemma of section 4). An $SU(2)$ matrix is determined by its first column $(a, b)$: $V = \begin{pmatrix}a&-\bar b\\b&\bar a\end{pmatrix}$, $|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$ (problem P6).

2. Choose $\gamma\in[0, \pi]$ with $c = |a|$, $s = |b|$. Write $a = |a|e^{i\mu}$, $b = |b|e^{i\nu}$ and solve

$$p = -\mu,\qquad q = \nu .$$

3. The product's first column is then $(a, b)$, and it is in $SU(2)$, so it equals $V$.

Check $U = R_x(\theta)$: $\ R_x(\theta) = R_z(-\tfrac\pi2)R_y(\theta)R_z(\tfrac\pi2)$ — turn $x$ to $y$, rotate about $y$, turn back.

The product, every entry. $R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta) = \begin{pmatrix}c&-s\\s&c\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\delta/2}, e^{i\delta/2}) = \begin{pmatrix}ce^{-i\delta/2}&-se^{i\delta/2}\\se^{-i\delta/2}&ce^{i\delta/2}\end{pmatrix}$; multiplying on the left by $\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\beta/2}, e^{i\beta/2})$ multiplies the first row by $e^{-i\beta/2}$ and the second by $e^{i\beta/2}$, giving the slide's matrix. (Checked numerically at random angles.)

Step 1 in full. If $V\in SU(2)$ has first column $(a, b)$, the second column is a unit vector orthogonal to it, so it is $\lambda(-\bar b, \bar a)$ with $|\lambda| = 1$ (the orthogonal complement of $(a, b)$ in $\mathbb C^2$ is a line, spanned by $(-\bar b, \bar a)$: $\bar a(-\bar b) + \bar b\bar a = 0$). Then $\det V = \lambda(|a|^2 + |b|^2) = \lambda$, so $\lambda = 1$. Problem P6 does this as the Cayley–Klein form.

Step 2 when an entry vanishes. If $b = 0$ ($\gamma = 0$), $\nu$ is undefined and only $\beta + \delta = -2\mu$ is fixed: choose any split. If $a = 0$ ($\gamma = \pi$), only $\beta - \delta = 2\nu$ is fixed. Otherwise $\beta = \nu - \mu$ and $\delta = -\mu - \nu$, determined mod $2\pi$ — up to the sign ambiguity of step 1, which shifts $\alpha$ by $\pi$ and both $\mu$ and $\nu$ by $\pi$.

The check. $R_z(-\tfrac\pi2)$ implements the rotation by $-\tfrac\pi2$ about $z$, sending $\hat{\mathbf y}$ to $\hat{\mathbf x}$. Conjugating, $R_z(-\tfrac\pi2)R_y(\theta)R_z(-\tfrac\pi2)^\dagger$ is the rotation by $\theta$ about the image of the $y$ axis, the $x$ axis. In matrices: $R_z(-\tfrac\pi2)YR_z(\tfrac\pi2) = X$, and exponentials conjugate term by term. Problem P8 proves, independently of Euler's theorem, that $R_z$ and $R_y$ generate $SO(3)$.

With $\beta = 0$, $\gamma = \tfrac\pi2$, $\delta = \pi$: the first column is $(e^{-i\pi/2}c,\ e^{-i\pi/2}s) = -\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$, and the second $(-e^{i\pi/2}s,\ e^{i\pi/2}c) = \tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}(-1, 1)$, which is $V$'s second column $(-\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}, \tfrac{i}{\sqrt2})$ ✓. Times $e^{i\pi/2} = i$: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$ and $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, -1)$ ✓.

The other root. $\alpha = -\tfrac\pi2$ gives $V' = -V$, first column $\tfrac{i}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)$, $\mu = \nu = \tfrac\pi2$, so $\beta = 0$, $\delta = -\pi$: $H = -iR_y(\tfrac\pi2)R_z(-\pi)$. Since $R_z(-\pi) = -R_z(\pi)$ this is the same product — the decomposition is unique only up to such sign trades.

On the sphere, step 4. $R_z(\pi)$ (applied first) sends $\hat{\mathbf x}\mapsto-\hat{\mathbf x}$ and fixes $\hat{\mathbf z}$; $R_y(\tfrac\pi2)$ sends $\hat{\mathbf z}\mapsto\hat{\mathbf z}\cos\tfrac\pi2 + \sin\tfrac\pi2\,\hat{\mathbf y}\times\hat{\mathbf z} = \hat{\mathbf x}$ and $-\hat{\mathbf x}\mapsto-(\hat{\mathbf y}\times\hat{\mathbf x}) = \hat{\mathbf z}$. So $z\leftrightarrow x$, as the half-turn of the earlier worked example did.

02 · Euler angles

Euler angles, drawn — and when they are not unique

The geometric reading. $R_z(\delta)$ fixes $\hat{\mathbf z}$; $R_y(\gamma)$ tilts it by $\gamma$ toward $\hat{\mathbf x}$; $R_z(\beta)$ swings it round by $\beta$. So $R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta)$ sends

$$\hat{\mathbf z}\ \mapsto\ (\sin\gamma\cos\beta,\ \sin\gamma\sin\beta,\ \cos\gamma):$$

$\gamma$ and $\beta$ are the polar angle and longitude of where the north pole goes; $\delta$ is the twist about the pole before the move.

degenerate cases

$\gamma = 0$: $R_z(\beta)R_z(\delta) = R_z(\beta + \delta)$ — only the sum is determined.

$\gamma = \pi$: $R_y(\pi)R_z(\delta) = R_z(-\delta)R_y(\pi)$, so only $\beta - \delta$ is.

At the poles the longitude is undefined: section 2's lemma again. Mechanics calls it gimbal lock.

Tilt by $\gamma$ about $y$, then turn by $\beta$ about $z$: the north pole lands at polar angle $\gamma$, azimuth $\beta$.

Full proof of the reading. $R_z(\delta)$ implements a rotation about $\hat{\mathbf z}$, which fixes $\hat{\mathbf z}$. $R_y(\gamma)$ turns $\hat{\mathbf z}$ by $\gamma$ about $\hat{\mathbf y}$ to $\cos\gamma\,\hat{\mathbf z} + \sin\gamma\,\hat{\mathbf y}\times\hat{\mathbf z} = (\sin\gamma, 0, \cos\gamma)$. $R_z(\beta)$ turns that by $\beta$ about $\hat{\mathbf z}$: $(\sin\gamma\cos\beta, \sin\gamma\sin\beta, \cos\gamma)$. On states: $R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta)\ket0$ is, up to global phase, $\cos\tfrac\gamma2\ket0 + e^{i\beta}\sin\tfrac\gamma2\ket1$ — exactly section 2's two-angle form with $(\theta, \varphi) = (\gamma, \beta)$.

The $\gamma = \pi$ identity. $R_y(\pi) = -iY$ anticommutes with $Z$, so $R_y(\pi)ZR_y(\pi)^\dagger = -Z$, and exponentiating, $R_y(\pi)R_z(\delta)R_y(\pi)^\dagger = R_z(-\delta)$. Then $R_z(\beta)R_y(\pi)R_z(\delta) = R_z(\beta)R_z(-\delta)R_y(\pi) = R_z(\beta - \delta)R_y(\pi)$.

Gimbal lock. A camera or a gyroscope mounted in three nested rings, each turning about one of the Euler axes, loses a degree of freedom when the middle ring is at $0$ or $\pi$: the outer and inner rings then turn about the same line. The Apollo guidance platform had to avoid exactly this configuration. The same degeneracy is in problem P9, parts 3 and 4.

02 · the close

The close: a spin echo

Seen from above in the rotating frame. Protons in slightly different fields precess at slightly different rates and fan out; a $\pi$-pulse reflects the fan; the same rates then bring every one back to $-y$ at once.

The problem. No magnet is perfectly uniform: a proton where the field is off by $\delta/\gamma$ has detuning $\delta$, and in the rotating frame drifts as $R_z(-\delta t)$. After a $\pi/2$-pulse the population fans out and the signal fades.

The cure (Hahn, 1950): $\pi/2$-pulse, wait $\tau$, $\pi$-pulse, wait $\tau$. Pulses short enough that $|\delta|\ll\Omega$ act as exact $R_x$ turns. Then the spin echo brings every proton to the same point, whatever its $\delta$.

Why the fan reverses. After the $\pi/2$-pulse each proton starts at $+\hat{\mathbf y}$ and turns by $-\delta\tau$ about $z$, so its azimuth is $\tfrac\pi2 - \delta\tau$. The $\pi$-pulse is a half-turn about $x$, $(x, y, z)\mapsto(x, -y, -z)$: azimuth $\phi\mapsto-\phi$, so the protons sit at $-\tfrac\pi2 + \delta\tau$ — the ones that were ahead are now behind by the same amount. Another $\tau$ at the same rates subtracts $\delta\tau$ from each, and all reach $-\tfrac\pi2$, the $-y$ direction, together. The next slide does this as a product of matrices.

Each proton is a pure state. Every proton here follows its own unitary; nothing about the population as a whole is used. Describing the population — and the part of the fading that an echo cannot undo, from fields that fluctuate in time — is unit 21's.

History. Erwin Hahn, "Spin echoes", Phys. Rev. 80, 580 (1950). He discovered the echo by accident, as an unexplained second signal after two pulses. The idea — undo an unknown but static error by a well-timed flip — is the ancestor of dynamical decoupling in quantum computers, and of the refocusing pulses in every MRI sequence.

02 · the close

The echo as one rotation, in Euler angles

The product for a proton of detuning $\delta$, read right to left:

$$U_\delta = R_z(-\delta\tau)\,R_x(-\pi)\,R_z(-\delta\tau)\,R_x(-\tfrac\pi2).$$

The key identity. A half-turn about $x$ reverses $z$, so $R_x(-\pi)R_z(\phi) = R_z(-\phi)R_x(-\pi)$. Hence

$$\begin{aligned}U_\delta &= R_z(-\delta\tau)R_z(+\delta\tau)R_x(-\pi)R_x(-\tfrac\pi2)\\ &= R_x(-\tfrac{3\pi}{2}).\end{aligned}$$
δ has gone

Every proton gets the same unitary. On $\ket0$: $\ R_x(-\tfrac{3\pi}2)\ket0 = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - i\ket1) = -\ket{-i}$, Bloch vector $-\hat{\mathbf y}$ ✓, normalised ✓.

In Euler angles. $R_x(-\tfrac{3\pi}2) = R_x(\tfrac\pi2)R_x(-2\pi) = -R_x(\tfrac\pi2)$, and $R_x(\theta) = R_z(-\tfrac\pi2)R_y(\theta)R_z(\tfrac\pi2)$:

$$U_\delta = e^{i\pi}\,R_z(-\tfrac\pi2)\,R_y(\tfrac\pi2)\,R_z(\tfrac\pi2).$$

$\alpha = \pi$, $\beta = -\tfrac\pi2$, $\gamma = \tfrac\pi2$, $\delta_{\text{Euler}} = \tfrac\pi2$. The global phase $e^{i\pi}$ is the $-1$ of section 4: the sequence turns the sphere through $\tfrac{3\pi}{2}$ about $-x$, and the operator remembers a full $2\pi$ of it.

the question, answered

The proton precesses about the field at $\gamma B_0$; a resonant field $B_1$ turns it about a horizontal axis at $\gamma B_1$; and any pulse sequence is one rotation, three Euler angles and a phase.

The key identity in full. $R_x(-\pi) = iX$, and $XZX^\dagger = -Z$, so $R_x(-\pi)ZR_x(-\pi)^\dagger = -Z$; exponentiating, $R_x(-\pi)R_z(\phi)R_x(-\pi)^\dagger = R_z(-\phi)$, i.e. $R_x(-\pi)R_z(\phi) = R_z(-\phi)R_x(-\pi)$. With $\phi = -\delta\tau$: $R_x(-\pi)R_z(-\delta\tau) = R_z(\delta\tau)R_x(-\pi)$, and $R_z(-\delta\tau)R_z(\delta\tau) = I$. Along one axis the angles add: $R_x(-\pi)R_x(-\tfrac\pi2) = R_x(-\tfrac{3\pi}2)$. (Checked numerically for several $\delta$.)

The state. $R_x(\tfrac\pi2)\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - i\ket1) = \ket{-i}$, so $R_x(-\tfrac{3\pi}{2})\ket0 = -\ket{-i}$: Bloch vector $-\hat{\mathbf y}$, the echo's direction in the figure ✓. Normalised: $\tfrac12(1 + 1) = 1$.

The Euler angles by the general recipe. $-R_x(\tfrac\pi2) = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&-i\\-i&1\end{pmatrix}$ is in $SU(2)$. Taking $\alpha = \pi$, $V = R_x(\tfrac\pi2)$ with first column $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1, -i)$: $|a| = |b|$, $\gamma = \tfrac\pi2$; $\mu = 0$, $\nu = -\tfrac\pi2$; $\beta = \nu - \mu = -\tfrac\pi2$, $\delta = -\mu - \nu = \tfrac\pi2$ ✓.

The running example, closed. The question on the overview — what does one proton do in the field, and how do you flip it — is answered: it precesses at $\omega_0/2\pi = 63.87$ MHz (section 3); a full turn is $-I$, visible only against a reference (section 4); a resonant $11.74\ \mu$T field flips it in $1.0$ ms (section 5), with the rotating-wave approximation good to a few parts in $10^{11}$; and a pulse sequence with an unknown detuning collapses to a single known rotation, which section 6 writes in the form every later unit uses.

What this unit bought, and where it stops

the toolkit
  • Pauli algebra: $\sigma_j\sigma_k = \delta_{jk}I + i\varepsilon_{jkl}\sigma_l$; $(\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(\mathbf b\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b\,I + i(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$; the Pauli basis.
  • The Bloch sphere: $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$; $\mathbb{CP}^1\to S^2$ a bijection; $|\braket{\chi|\psi}|^2 = \tfrac12(1 + \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s)$; three MUBs exactly.

  • The double cover: $\Phi: SU(2)\to SO(3)$ onto, kernel $\{\pm I\}$; $R_{\mathbf n}(2\pi) = -I$.
  • Driving: the rotating frame, Rabi's formula, $\pi$- and $\pi/2$-pulses, the RWA and its Bloch–Siegert price.
  • ZYZ: $U = e^{i\alpha}R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta)$.
