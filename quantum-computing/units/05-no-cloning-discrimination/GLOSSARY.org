The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: Bob's photon, polarised \(\ket0\) (horizontal) or
\(\ket+ = \frac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)\) (diagonal), each with probability
\(\frac12\), with overlap \(\braket{0|+} = \frac1{\sqrt2}\). He cannot copy it: a
unitary would have to turn the overlap \(0.7071\) into \(0.5\). The best single
measurement, an analyser at \(-22.5^\circ\), identifies it with probability
\(\cos^2\frac\pi8 = \frac12(1 + \frac1{\sqrt2}) \approx 0.8536\); the \(z\) basis
manages \(0.75\). The photon comes from Herbert's 1982 FLASH proposal, which a
copier would have turned into a faster-than-light telegraph.

* Herbert's telegraph

- no-signalling :: For any projective measurement on Alice's side and any on
  Bob's, Bob's outcome probabilities are the same whatever Alice measures, or
  whether she measures or applies a unitary at all:
  \(\sum_\lambda\braket{\psi|(P_\lambda\otimes Q_\mu)|\psi} =
  \braket{\psi|(I\otimes Q_\mu)|\psi}\). Unit 03's problem P10; unit 04 extends
  it to every local operation. /Slide: Bob alone sees a fair coin./
- stimulated emission :: A photon passing an atom that holds a releasable
  amount of energy triggers it to emit a second photon identical to the first:
  same direction, frequency and polarisation (Einstein 1917, cited). The
  mechanism of the laser. /Slide: The copier Herbert had in mind: a laser
  amplifier./
- laser amplifier :: A medium of energy-loaded ("pumped") atoms in which
  stimulated emission multiplies an incoming photon into many. Herbert's
  proposed copier for FLASH.
- spontaneous emission :: Emission by a loaded atom with no photon to trigger
  it, at a random time and with random polarisation. It adds noise to every
  laser amplifier's copies — exactly enough, Milonni–Hardies and Mandel showed
  (cited), to hide Alice's choice in FLASH.

* No-cloning, proved twice

- cloner :: For a set \(S\) of states of \(H\): a unitary \(U\) on
  \(H\otimes H\) and a fixed blank \(s\) with \(U(\psi\otimes s) =
  e^{i\gamma_\psi}\psi\otimes\psi\) for every \(\psi\in S\); the phase may depend
  on \(\psi\). /Slide: What a copier would have to be./
- blank state :: The fixed state \(s\) of the second system that a cloner
  overwrites with the copy. Fixed because the machine is not told \(\psi\).
- universal cloner :: A cloner for every state of \(H\). None exists.
- no-cloning theorem :: No universal cloner exists (Wootters–Zurek; Dieks;
  1982), by linearity: copying \(\ket0, \ket1\) forces the entangled
  \(\frac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})\) on \(\ket+\). Sharper, by inner
  products: a cloner for \(\psi, \varphi\) needs \(|\braket{\psi|\varphi}| =
  |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|^2\), so the two are orthogonal or the same state.
  /Slide: Proof one: linearity./
- ancilla :: An extra system in a fixed state, added to the one being worked
  on — a machine's own parts, or a measuring apparatus. It helps neither a
  cloner (\(c = c^2\braket{e_\psi|e_\varphi}\) still forces \(|c|\in\{0, 1\}\))
  nor a discriminator (the overlap is unchanged). /Slide: A machine with parts
  of its own./
- probabilistic cloning :: Copying that sometimes fails and reports the
  failure; possible for any two distinct states, indeed any linearly
  independent set, with probability below one (Duan–Guo 1998, cited). Not a cloner in this unit's sense.

* What does copy

- fan-out :: Splitting a classical wire in two, copying a bit. In quantum
  terms, \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\) with target \(\ket0\): \(\ket x\ket0 \mapsto
  \ket x\ket x\). Possible because classical bits are orthogonal states.
  /Slide: CNOT copies the z basis./

* Telling two states apart

- state discrimination :: Minimum-error version: a fair coin prepares
  \(\psi\) or \(\varphi\); a projective measurement and a guessing rule name it.
  Only the projector \(P\) onto the outcomes read as "\(\psi\)" matters. /Slide:
  The task: one copy, one guess./
- success probability :: \(p_{\rm succ} = \frac12\braket{\psi|P|\psi} +
  \frac12\braket{\varphi|(I - P)|\varphi} = \frac12 + \frac12\operatorname{tr}(P\Delta)\),
  with \(\Delta = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi - \ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi\). It equals
  \(1\) for some measurement iff \(\psi\perp\varphi\).
- Helstrom bound :: For two equiprobable pure states, the best success
  probability over all projective measurements, with or without an ancilla, is
  \(\frac12(1 + \sqrt{1 - |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|^2})\) (over all measurements
  once unit 21's Naimark theorem is in hand): \(0.8536\) for \(\ket0\) against \(\ket+\).
  Proved from the eigenvalues \(\pm\sqrt{1 - |c|^2}\) of \(\Delta\) on the plane
  of the two states (Helstrom 1976, cited, for the general theory). /Slide: The
  Helstrom bound./
- Helstrom measurement :: The measurement that attains the bound: the
  eigenbasis \(e_\pm\) of \(\Delta\), lying \(45^\circ\) on either side of the
  bisector of \(\psi\) and \(\varphi\), guessing \(\psi\) on \(e_+\). For
  \(\ket0, \ket+\): the basis at \(-\pi/8\) and \(3\pi/8\), an analyser at
  \(-22.5^\circ\). /Slide: Proof, step three: no projector beats s./
- unambiguous discrimination :: Never wrong, sometimes "don't know". For two
  equiprobable pure states the best conclusive probability is \(1 -
  |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|\) (Ivanovic, Dieks, Peres, 1987–88), \(0.2929\) for
  \(\ket0, \ket+\); stated here, proved in unit 21. Projectively on a qubit,
  only \(\frac14\). /Slide: Never wrong, sometimes silent./
- fidelity of a copy :: The probability that the copy passes the test "is it
  \(\psi\)?", a measurement in \(\{\psi, \psi^\perp\}\):
  \(\braket{\Omega|(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\otimes I)|\Omega}\) for copy 1 of the
  output \(\Omega\). \(1\) for a perfect copy. /Slide: Approximate copies,
  stated./
- Bužek–Hillery cloner :: A universal \(1\to2\) cloner whose copies each have
  fidelity \(\frac56\) for every input (1996, cited); optimal among symmetric
  universal cloners (Gisin–Massar 1997, cited). Measure-and-prepare reaches
  only \(\frac23\) on average.
