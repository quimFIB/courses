05

No-cloning, and how well two states can be told apart

An unknown quantum state cannot be copied, and two states that are not orthogonal cannot be told apart for certain — two theorems with one root. This unit proves both, and finds exactly how well the best measurement can do.

needs 03~2 h slides + 4 h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 05

Unit 03 showed what a pair of systems can hold that no pair of separate states can. This unit turns to the other side: what a single unknown state withholds. Two theorems say it, and they have one root, non-orthogonality. An unknown state cannot be copied; and two states that are not orthogonal cannot be told apart with certainty, with the exact best success probability — the Helstrom bound — proved here for two pure states.

Both feed what comes later: unit 07's teleportation moves a state without copying it, unit 09's eavesdropper cannot copy what she intercepts and so must disturb it, and unit 12's account of what a quantum algorithm can read out of its final state starts here.

05 · overview

Bob holds one of two photons

the running example: Bob's photon

Bob holds one photon. Its polarisation is $\ket0$ (horizontal) or $\ket+ = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)$ (diagonal, at $45^\circ$), each with probability $\tfrac12$, and nobody tells him which. Both are normalised: $1$ and $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12$. Their overlap is $\braket{0|+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$.

Two questions. Can he copy it? How well can he tell which it is?

Section 1 says why anyone wanted to know: a proposal, in 1982, for a telegraph faster than light, which needed exactly this copier.

Bob alone sees a fair coin

Bob measures his photon in $z$ or in $x$. The probability that his result is the first exit ($0$, or $+$), by total probability over Alice's results:

Bob in $z$Bob in $x$
Alice in $z$$\tfrac12\cdot0 + \tfrac12\cdot1 = \tfrac12$$\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$
Alice in $x$$\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$$\tfrac12\cdot0 + \tfrac12\cdot1 = \tfrac12$

Every entry is $\tfrac12$. Whatever Alice chose, Bob's records look like a fair coin.

no-signalling, for projective measurements (unit 03, problem P10)

For any projective measurement $\{P_\lambda\}$ on Alice's side, any $\{Q_\mu\}$ on Bob's, and any state $\psi$, Bob's outcome probabilities are the same whatever Alice measures, or whether she measures at all, or applies a unitary first:

$$\textstyle\sum_\lambda\braket{\psi|(P_\lambda\otimes Q_\mu)|\psi} = \braket{\psi|(I\otimes Q_\mu)|\psi} .$$
what Herbert needed

A way round this, for one photon: some device that makes Bob's statistics depend on Alice's choice. The theorem says no measurement on Bob's side alone will. Herbert's idea was to copy the photon first.

The table, entry by entry. Alice in $z$, Bob in $z$: Alice's $0$ (probability $\tfrac12$) leaves $\ket1$, which gives Bob's $0$ with probability $|\braket{0|1}|^2 = 0$; Alice's $1$ leaves $\ket0$, giving $0$ with probability $1$. Alice in $z$, Bob in $x$: $|\braket{+|1}|^2 = |\braket{+|0}|^2 = \tfrac12$. Alice in $x$, Bob in $z$: $|\braket{0|-}|^2 = |\braket{0|+}|^2 = \tfrac12$. Alice in $x$, Bob in $x$: $|\braket{+|-}|^2 = 0$ and $|\braket{+|+}|^2 = 1$. Each entry is a probability of Bob's first exit; the second exit gets $\tfrac12$ in every case, and each column's two exits sum to $1$ ✓.

The theorem's proof is unit 03's problem P10, in two lines: the probability that Alice gets $\lambda$ and Bob $\mu$ is $\lVert(P_\lambda\otimes Q_\mu)\psi\rVert^2 = \braket{\psi|(P_\lambda\otimes Q_\mu)|\psi}$, and summing over $\lambda$ with $\sum_\lambda P_\lambda = I$ gives Bob's probability with no measurement by Alice. A unitary $V\otimes I$ first changes nothing, since $(V\otimes I)^\dagger(I\otimes Q_\mu)(V\otimes I) = V^\dagger V\otimes Q_\mu = I\otimes Q_\mu$. This is the only form of no-signalling this unit uses. Unit 04 proves it for every local operation, but unit 04 is not needed here.

Why this matters physically. If Bob's statistics did depend on Alice's choice, Alice could send a message by choosing, and Bob could read it at once, however far apart they are.

05 · Herbert's telegraph

FLASH: copy Bob's photon, then look

Herbert's proposal (Found. Phys. 12, 1171, 1982), with a copier:

  1. Bob turns his one photon into $20$ identical copies.
  2. He sends $10$ through a $z$ analyser and $10$ through an $x$ analyser.
  3. If Alice chose $z$, his photon was $\ket0$ or $\ket1$: all ten $z$ results agree, and the $x$ results are fair coins. If she chose $x$, the other way round.

The numbers. Ten fair coins all agree with probability $2\cdot2^{-10} = \tfrac1{512}$. Bob's rule: "Alice chose the basis whose ten results agree; if both do, toss a coin". He is wrong with probability $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac1{512} = \tfrac1{1024} \approx 0.001$.

why that would be extraordinary

Alice's choice would reach Bob instantly, at any distance. Relativity (Einstein 1905, cited, and tested ever since) forbids any signal faster than light.

What Bob would see if Alice chose $z$ and his photon were $\ket0$. Had she chosen $x$, the lower row would agree and the upper one scatter.

The name. FLASH stood for "First Laser-Amplified Superluminal Hookup". Herbert's version had Alice choose between linear polarisation (horizontal/vertical) and circular polarisation, unit 00's $z$ and $y$ bases. The deck uses $z$ and $x$, the two linear analysers of unit 03; the argument is identical, since any two of the three bases are mutually unbiased.

The error probability, in full. Suppose Alice chose $z$. Then Bob's photon is $\ket0$ or $\ket1$ and so are all twenty copies, so the ten $z$ results agree with probability $1$. Each $x$ result on $\ket0$ or $\ket1$ is $+$ or $-$ with probability $\tfrac12$, independently; all ten agree with probability $2^{-10}$ (all $+$) $+\ 2^{-10}$ (all $-$) $= 2^{-9} = \tfrac1{512}$. Bob errs only if both rows agree and his coin then picks $x$: $\tfrac1{512}\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac1{1024} \approx 0.000977$. The case "Alice chose $x$" is the same with the rows exchanged.

Relativity, in one paragraph. No physics is assumed in this course, so this is stated, not derived: special relativity (Einstein 1905) holds that no signal, of any kind, travels faster than light, and every experiment since has agreed. Faster-than-light signals would, for some observers, arrive before they were sent. A proposal to violate it is worth reading exactly to find the error.

05 · Herbert's telegraph

The copier Herbert had in mind: a laser amplifier

An atom stores energy in discrete amounts, and can release one amount as one photon of a definite frequency. A pumped medium is a crystal or gas whose atoms have been loaded with that energy.

stimulated emission (Einstein 1917, cited): a photon passing a loaded atom can trigger it to release a second photon identical to the first — same direction, frequency and polarisation. One photon becomes two, then four. That is a laser amplifier.

spontaneous emission: loaded atoms also release photons on their own, at random times and with random polarisation, and some join the beam.

the catch

The copies come with noise. Milonni and Hardies, and Mandel (1982–83, cited), showed that spontaneous emission is exactly enough to hide Alice's choice. Section 2 shows why no device could do better.

A short bar across a beam marks its polarisation. Stimulated photons copy the input's; spontaneous ones do not, and cannot be told from the copies.

The physics, from nothing, and only as much as is needed. An atom's energy takes discrete values (unit 01's "stationary states" with their energies; why the values are discrete is atomic physics, not this course). Dropping from a higher to a lower value releases the difference as one photon. Einstein showed in 1917 (cited) that thermal radiation is consistent only if there are two ways to do this: stimulated emission, triggered by a photon already present and producing a twin of it, and spontaneous emission, which happens anyway. A laser, and a laser amplifier, exploit the first. Neither can switch off the second.

The refutation of FLASH's device, cited. P. W. Milonni and M. L. Hardies, "Photons cannot always be replicated", Phys. Lett. A 92, 321 (1982), and L. Mandel, Nature 304, 188 (1983): for an ideal amplifier the spontaneously emitted photons are just numerous enough, and just random enough, that Bob's records have the same statistics whatever Alice chose. Section 5 explains why that is not luck: any device that is a unitary on the photon and its own parts must behave this way.

Refereed, published, refuted

1981–82. Nick Herbert's FLASH goes to referees in 1981 and appears in Foundations of Physics in 1982. One referee, Asher Peres, recommended publication knowing it was wrong: the error was instructive, and finding it would move the subject on (Peres 2003, cited).

The same year, two short papers found it:

  • W. K. Wootters and W. H. Zurek, "A single quantum cannot be cloned", Nature 299, 802;
  • D. Dieks, "Communication by EPR devices", Phys. Lett. A 92, 271.

Both: linearity alone forbids a copier. Section 2 gives their argument and a sharper one.

the lesson of the episode

FLASH did not misuse quantum mechanics anywhere except in one assumption: that a copier exists. Every other step is correct. So either quantum mechanics permits faster-than-light signalling, or the copier is impossible. It is the copier.

a wrong paper that was worth printing

The no-cloning theorem is now a cornerstone of quantum cryptography (unit 09). It was found because a wrong idea was published and people went looking for the mistake.

No-cloning, proved twice

Linearity forbids a machine that copies every state. Preservation of inner products forbids one that copies any two states that are neither orthogonal nor the same.

05 · no-cloning

What a copier would have to be

a cloner

A cloner for a set $S$ of states of $H$ is a unitary $U$ on $H\otimes H$ and a fixed unit vector $s\in H$, the blank state, with

$$U(\psi\otimes s) = e^{i\gamma_\psi}\,\psi\otimes\psi\quad(\psi\in S).$$

It is a universal cloner if $S$ is every state of $H$.

  • Unitary: the photon and the blank, isolated, evolve by a unitary (unit 01, postulate 3).
  • Fixed blank: the machine is not told $\psi$; that is what "unknown" means.
  • A phase $e^{i\gamma_\psi}$, which may depend on $\psi$, is allowed: states are rays (unit 01).

Wires carry systems, time runs left to right, and a box is a unitary acting on the wires it covers. Unit 10 makes this picture a formal model.

machines with parts of their own

A real copier has internal parts that may change. They are added on A machine with parts of its own, and change nothing.

Why this is the right definition. "Copying" means: whatever the input state $\psi$ (among those the machine is built for), the output is two systems each in state $\psi$. The machine's design, $U$ and $s$, cannot depend on $\psi$: a machine that is told $\psi$ can simply prepare a second copy from the description, and that is not copying an unknown state. The output $\psi\otimes\psi$ is a product state, as two independent copies must be (unit 03's composite-system postulate: systems prepared independently are in a product state).

Why the phase. $\psi$ and $e^{i\alpha}\psi$ are the same state. A machine that outputs $e^{i\gamma}\psi\otimes\psi$ has produced two copies of the state $\psi$, and must count as a success. The theorems below allow $\gamma_\psi$ to be any function of $\psi$.

Why both factors are $H$. The blank must be able to hold a copy, so it is another system of the same kind. A machine whose blank lives in a bigger space, or which uses internal parts, is covered on A machine with parts of its own.

Proof one: linearity

the no-cloning theorem, first form (Wootters–Zurek; Dieks; 1982)

There is no universal cloner for a qubit.

proof

Suppose $U(\ket0\ket s) = e^{ia}\ket{00}$ and $U(\ket1\ket s) = e^{ib}\ket{11}$. Since $\ket{+}\ket s = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket s + \ket1\ket s)$, linearity forces

$$U(\ket+\ket s) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(e^{ia}\ket{00} + e^{ib}\ket{11}\big),$$

whose coefficient matrix $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\mathrm{diag}(e^{ia}, e^{ib})$ has rank $2$: an entangled state (unit 03). A copy $e^{i\gamma}\ket{{+}{+}}$ is a product, rank $1$. They differ. $\blacksquare$

Norms: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ and $4\cdot\tfrac14 = 1$ ✓.

Coefficient matrices, rows indexed by qubit 0 (phases $a = b = 0$ shown). No phase turns the left one into the right one.

Full proof. A universal cloner copies in particular $\ket0$ and $\ket1$, so there are phases $a, b$ with $U(\ket0\otimes s) = e^{ia}\ket0\ket0$ and $U(\ket1\otimes s) = e^{ib}\ket1\ket1$. The map $v \mapsto v\otimes s$ is linear, and so is $U$, hence

$U(\ket+\otimes s) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}U(\ket0\otimes s) + \tfrac1{\sqrt2}U(\ket1\otimes s) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(e^{ia}\ket{00} + e^{ib}\ket{11})$.

Its coefficient matrix is $C = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}e^{ia}&0\\0&e^{ib}\end{pmatrix}$, with $\det C = \tfrac12e^{i(a+b)} \ne 0$, so rank $2$ and the state is entangled (unit 03: a two-qubit pure state is a product iff its coefficient matrix has rank $1$, iff $\det C = 0$). A universal cloner must also copy $\ket+$: $U(\ket+\otimes s) = e^{ic}\ket+\ket+$ for some $c$, a product state with coefficient matrix $\tfrac{e^{ic}}2\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, determinant $0$. The same vector cannot have both matrices. $\blacksquare$

Any dimension. If $\dim H \ge 2$, take orthonormal $e_1, e_2$ and run the same argument with $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(e_1 + e_2)$: the forced output has coefficient matrix of rank $2$ in the basis $\{e_i\otimes e_j\}$, a copy has rank $1$.

What it uses. Only linearity — not unitarity. Any linear machine fails. That is the form in which Wootters–Zurek and Dieks stated it. Its weakness: it needs three states, two orthogonal and their superposition. It says nothing about a machine built for just $\ket0$ and $\ket+$. Proof two: inner products does.

Proof two: inner products

no-cloning, second form

If a cloner copies $\psi$ and $\varphi$, then $|\braket{\psi|\varphi}| = 0$ or $1$: the two are orthogonal or the same state.

proof

A unitary keeps inner products, so take the inner product of $U(\psi\otimes s)$ with $U(\varphi\otimes s)$ both ways:

$$\braket{\psi|\varphi}\underbrace{\braket{s|s}}_{1} = e^{i(\gamma_\varphi - \gamma_\psi)}\braket{\psi|\varphi}^2 .$$

Take moduli: $|c| = |c|^2$ with $c = \braket{\psi|\varphi}$, so $|c|\in\{0, 1\}$. $\blacksquare$

$|c| = 1$ means $\varphi = e^{i\alpha}\psi$: the equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz (unit 00).

$|c|$ and $|c|^2$ agree only at $0$ and $1$. The marker is the running example.

sharper than proof one

It forbids a copier for any pair that is neither orthogonal nor equal — two states suffice. The theorem is exactly about non-orthogonality.

Full proof. Write $c = \braket{\psi|\varphi}$. Since $U$ is unitary, $\braket{Uu|Uv} = \braket{u|U^\dagger Uv} = \braket{u|v}$ for all $u, v$. With $u = \psi\otimes s$ and $v = \varphi\otimes s$:

Left side. $\braket{\psi\otimes s|\varphi\otimes s} = \braket{\psi|\varphi}\braket{s|s} = c$, by unit 03's inner product on a tensor product and $\lVert s\rVert = 1$.

Right side. $\braket{e^{i\gamma_\psi}\psi\otimes\psi|e^{i\gamma_\varphi}\varphi\otimes\varphi} = e^{-i\gamma_\psi}e^{i\gamma_\varphi}\braket{\psi|\varphi}\braket{\psi|\varphi} = e^{i(\gamma_\varphi - \gamma_\psi)}c^2$, the first phase conjugated because the inner product is conjugate-linear in its first slot.

So $c = e^{i(\gamma_\varphi - \gamma_\psi)}c^2$, and taking moduli $|c| = |c|^2$, i.e. $|c|(1 - |c|) = 0$. If $|c| = 1$, Cauchy–Schwarz holds with equality, so $\varphi = \lambda\psi$ for some $\lambda$, and $|\lambda| = 1$ since both are unit vectors: the same ray. $\blacksquare$

Without phases. If the machine must output exactly $\psi\otimes\psi$, the identity is $c = c^2$, so $c\in\{0, 1\}$: even $\psi$ and $-\psi$ cannot both be "copied" exactly, since $U(-\psi\otimes s) = -\psi\otimes\psi \ne (-\psi)\otimes(-\psi)$. That is an artefact of insisting on vectors rather than states, which is why the definition allows the phase.

Why it is sharper. Proof one needs a machine that handles three particular states. Proof two kills a machine designed for just two, so long as they overlap partially. Together with the converse in section 3 it gives an exact criterion: a set of states can be cloned iff any two of them are orthogonal or equal.

Worked: no copier for |0⟩ and |+⟩

worked example: Bob cannot copy his photon

1. Suppose $U(\ket0\ket s) = e^{ia}\ket0\ket0$ and $U(\ket+\ket s) = e^{ib}\ket+\ket+$, with $\lVert s\rVert = 1$.

2. Before. $\braket{0\otimes s|{+}\otimes s} = \braket{0|+}\braket{s|s} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\cdot1 \approx 0.7071$.

3. After. $\braket{e^{ia}00|e^{ib}{+}{+}} = e^{i(b-a)}\braket{0|+}\braket{0|+} = e^{i(b-a)}\cdot\tfrac12$, of modulus $0.5$.

4. $U$ is unitary, so step 2 equals step 3. Moduli: $0.7071 = 0.5$. False.

5. So no unitary and no blank copy both $\ket0$ and $\ket+$. $\blacksquare$

In angles: $\arccos0.7071 = 45^\circ$ and $\arccos0.5 = 60^\circ$. Copying would open the angle between the states; a unitary is a rigid motion and cannot.

The angle between two states, $\arccos|\braket{\psi|\varphi}|$, is what a unitary must keep. Copying would turn $45^\circ$ into $60^\circ$.

Each step's justification. Step 2 uses unit 03's $\braket{a\otimes b|c\otimes d} = \braket{a|c}\braket{b|d}$ and $\braket{0|+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\braket{0|0} + \braket{0|1}) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$. Step 3 uses the same rule twice and the conjugate-linearity of the first slot, which turns $e^{ia}$ into $e^{-ia}$. Step 4 is the defining property of a unitary, $U^\dagger U = I$. Nothing about $s$ was used except $\lVert s\rVert = 1$, and nothing about $a, b$: no blank and no choice of phases helps.

The angle. For unit vectors define the angle between the states $\psi, \varphi$ as $\arccos|\braket{\psi|\varphi}| \in [0, \pi/2]$. A unitary keeps it. For real vectors in a plane, like these, it is the ordinary angle between the lines they span. Here the two copies $\ket{00}$ and $\ket{{+}{+}}$ live in $\mathbb C^4$, and $\arccos\tfrac12 = 60^\circ$; the picture draws them in the plane they span. (Unit 02 would draw $\ket0$ and $\ket+$ as two points a quarter-turn apart on the Bloch sphere; this deck does not need that picture.)

What it leaves open. A machine that copies approximately, or that copies sometimes and reports failure otherwise. Both exist; Never wrong, sometimes silent and Approximate copies, stated say what is known.

A machine with parts of its own

no-cloning, with an ancilla

Let the machine have its own system $E$, starting in $e$, and let $U$ be any unitary on $H\otimes H\otimes E$ with

$$U(\psi\otimes s\otimes e) = \psi\otimes\psi\otimes e_\psi$$

for two states $\psi$ and $\varphi$. Then $|\braket{\psi|\varphi}|\in\{0, 1\}$.

proof

Inner products: $c = c^2\braket{e_\psi|e_\varphi}$. Cauchy–Schwarz: $|\braket{e_\psi|e_\varphi}| \le 1$, so $|c| \le |c|^2$, so $|c|(1 - |c|) \le 0$; with $|c| \le 1$, $|c| \in \{0, 1\}$. $\blacksquare$

$E$ can be anything: the lab, the laser medium, the light it emits. Every device whose workings are a unitary on something fails.

The machine's final state $e_\psi$ may depend on $\psi$ in any way. It does not help.

what this does not cover

Measure, then prepare: that would need to tell $\ket0$ from $\ket+$ for certain, which section 4 forbids. Copying that sometimes fails and says so exists (probabilistic cloning), but it is not a cloner.

Full proof. Phases are absorbed into $e_\psi$, which is a unit vector because $U$ keeps norms and $\lVert\psi\otimes\psi\rVert = 1$. With $c = \braket{\psi|\varphi}$, unitarity and unit 03's product rule for inner products give

$c\,\braket{s|s}\braket{e|e} = \braket{\psi\otimes s\otimes e|\varphi\otimes s\otimes e} = \braket{U(\psi\otimes s\otimes e)|U(\varphi\otimes s\otimes e)} = c^2\braket{e_\psi|e_\varphi}$.

So $c = c^2\braket{e_\psi|e_\varphi}$. Taking moduli and using $|\braket{e_\psi|e_\varphi}| \le \lVert e_\psi\rVert\lVert e_\varphi\rVert = 1$: $|c| \le |c|^2$. Since also $|c| \le 1$ by Cauchy–Schwarz, $|c|^2 \le |c|$, so $|c| = |c|^2$ and $|c| \in \{0, 1\}$. $\blacksquare$

More copies. A machine making $N \ge 2$ copies, $U(\psi\otimes s^{\otimes(N-1)}\otimes e) = \psi^{\otimes N}\otimes e_\psi$, gives $c = c^N\braket{e_\psi|e_\varphi}$ and so $|c| \le |c|^N$: again $|c|\in\{0, 1\}$. FLASH's twenty-copy machine is ruled out the same way.

The two cases left out. A machine that measures and then prepares copies from the result is not a unitary as written. But it could only copy $\ket0$ and $\ket+$ perfectly if its measurement told them apart with certainty, and Certainty needs orthogonality proves that impossible. A machine that is allowed to fail, and to know when it has, can copy any two distinct states — indeed any linearly independent set — with some probability less than one (Duan and Guo, Phys. Rev. Lett. 80, 4999, 1998, cited): that is probabilistic cloning, and it never copies with certainty.

What does copy

Orthogonal states copy freely: a classical bit is the orthogonal case. The theorem is exactly about what lies between.

proof

The theorem is about non-orthogonality, not about superposition: $\ket+$ is a superposition and copies fine among its own kind.

Within a kind, the states are at $90^\circ$ and copy. Across kinds they are at $45^\circ$, and nothing copies both.

The $x$-kind copier. Problem P2 finds it explicitly: change basis with the unitary that swaps the $z$ and $x$ bases, copy with CNOT, change back. It copies $\ket\pm$ onto a blank $\ket+$, and what it does to $\ket0$ is the mirror image of Worked: CNOT on |+⟩|0⟩.

A common misreading. "Quantum states cannot be copied" is often heard as "superpositions cannot be copied". Both halves of that are wrong in general. The basis states $\ket0, \ket1$ are copyable only as a set, by a machine built for that set; the superpositions $\ket\pm$ are copyable as a set too. What cannot be done is to copy states from a set containing a non-orthogonal pair — and "an unknown state", with no promise at all, is the extreme case: every pair.

The running example. Bob's photon, $\ket0$ or $\ket+$, sits across the two kinds. That is exactly what makes it uncopyable, and, section 4 will show, imperfectly distinguishable.

section 4

Telling two states apart

One copy, one measurement, one guess. Orthogonal states are told apart for certain; for any others there is a best success probability, and it is Helstrom's.

05 · telling apart

The task: one copy, one guess

state discrimination (minimum error)

A fair coin prepares $\psi$ or $\varphi$. Bob makes a projective measurement $\{P_k\}$ and guesses by a rule $g$: outcome $k \mapsto$ "$\psi$" or "$\varphi$". His success probability is

$$p_{\rm succ} = \tfrac12\!\!\sum_{g(k) = \psi}\!\!\braket{\psi|P_k|\psi} + \tfrac12\!\!\sum_{g(k) = \varphi}\!\!\braket{\varphi|P_k|\varphi} .$$
only two outcomes matter

With $P = \sum_{g(k)=\psi}P_k$, an orthogonal projector,

$$p_{\rm succ} = \tfrac12\braket{\psi|P|\psi} + \tfrac12\braket{\varphi|(I - P)|\varphi} .$$

Every outcome Bob reads as "$\psi$" is lumped into one projector $P$; the rest is $I - P$. Nothing else about the measurement matters.

The game for Bob's photon: $\psi = \ket0$, $\varphi = \ket+$.

Full proof that only $P$ matters. The $P_k$ are orthogonal projectors with $P_kP_l = \delta_{kl}P_k$ and $\sum_kP_k = I$ (unit 01's projection-valued measure). Let $A = \{k : g(k) = \psi\}$ and $P = \sum_{k\in A}P_k$. Then $P^\dagger = P$, and $P^2 = \sum_{k, l\in A}P_kP_l = \sum_{k\in A}P_k = P$: an orthogonal projector. Its complement is $\sum_{k\notin A}P_k = I - P$. By linearity of $\braket{\psi|\cdot|\psi}$, the first sum in $p_{\rm succ}$ is $\braket{\psi|P|\psi}$ and the second is $\braket{\varphi|(I - P)|\varphi}$. $\blacksquare$

The formula, unpacked. By the law of total probability, $p_{\rm succ} = \tfrac12\Pr(\text{guess }\psi\mid\psi) + \tfrac12\Pr(\text{guess }\varphi\mid\varphi)$, and by the Born rule $\Pr(k\mid\psi) = \braket{\psi|P_k|\psi}$.

Scope. Bob's measurement here is projective on the system itself. An ancilla does not help extends the result to a measurement on the system together with an extra apparatus. Unequal probabilities for $\psi$ and $\varphi$ are problem P8. Measurements more general than projective are unit 21's; An ancilla does not help says why they cannot beat the bound proved here.

Certainty needs orthogonality

perfect discrimination

Some projective measurement has $p_{\rm succ} = 1$ if and only if $\braket{\psi|\varphi} = 0$.

proof

$p_{\rm succ} = 1$ forces $\braket{\psi|P|\psi} = 1$ and $\braket{\varphi|P|\varphi} = 0$. Since $\braket{v|P|v} = \lVert Pv\rVert^2$ and $\lVert v - Pv\rVert^2 = \lVert v\rVert^2 - \lVert Pv\rVert^2$, that says $P\psi = \psi$ and $P\varphi = 0$. Then

$$\braket{\psi|\varphi} = \braket{P\psi|\varphi} = \braket{\psi|P\varphi} = 0 .$$

Conversely $P = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$ works when $\psi\perp\varphi$. $\blacksquare$

So $\ket0$ and $\ket+$, with $\braket{0|+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, can never be told apart for certain.

Never wrong on $\psi$ puts $\psi$ in the range of $P$; never wrong on $\varphi$ puts $\varphi$ in its kernel. Range and kernel are orthogonal. A state at $45^\circ$ lies in neither.

Full proof. $p_{\rm succ} = \tfrac12\braket{\psi|P|\psi} + \tfrac12\braket{\varphi|(I - P)|\varphi}$ is an average of two numbers in $[0, 1]$, so it equals $1$ iff both are $1$: $\braket{\psi|P|\psi} = 1$ and $\braket{\varphi|P|\varphi} = 0$. For an orthogonal projector, $\braket{v|P|v} = \braket{v|P^\dagger P|v} = \lVert Pv\rVert^2$, and by Pythagoras ($Pv \perp v - Pv$, since $\braket{Pv|v - Pv} = \braket{v|(P - P^2)|v} = 0$) $\lVert v\rVert^2 = \lVert Pv\rVert^2 + \lVert v - Pv\rVert^2$. With $v = \psi$: $\lVert\psi - P\psi\rVert^2 = 1 - 1 = 0$, so $P\psi = \psi$. With $v = \varphi$: $\lVert P\varphi\rVert = 0$. Then $\braket{\psi|\varphi} = \braket{P\psi|\varphi} = \braket{\psi|P^\dagger\varphi} = \braket{\psi|P\varphi} = 0$. Conversely, if $\psi\perp\varphi$, take $P = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$: $\braket{\psi|P|\psi} = 1$ and $\braket{\varphi|P|\varphi} = |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|^2 = 0$. $\blacksquare$

The one root. Both theorems of this unit come down to the overlap. A cloner needs $|c|\in\{0, 1\}$; a perfect discriminator needs $c = 0$. And they are linked: a perfect discriminator for $\psi, \varphi$ would give a copier (measure, then prepare two copies of the answer), and a copier would beat the best discriminator (copy, then measure both copies) — problem P12 turns the second into a third proof of no-cloning.

Why physics cares. Unit 01 defined a measurement's outcomes as eigenvalues with orthogonal eigenspaces. That orthogonality is not a convention: this theorem says a measurement can only ever separate perfectly what is orthogonal.

Proof, step one: the success is linear in P

Rewrite $\braket{\varphi|(I - P)|\varphi} = 1 - \braket{\varphi|P|\varphi}$:

$$p_{\rm succ} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\big(\braket{\psi|P|\psi} - \braket{\varphi|P|\varphi}\big).$$

Unit 00's $\operatorname{tr}\ket v\!\bra u = \braket{u|v}$, with $v = Au$, gives $\braket{u|A|u} = \operatorname{tr}(A\ket u\!\bra u)$. So

step one $$p_{\rm succ} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(P\Delta),$$ $$\Delta := \ket\psi\!\bra\psi - \ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi .$$

$\Delta$ lives on a plane. It is Hermitian; $\Delta v = \psi\braket{\psi|v} - \varphi\braket{\varphi|v}$ lies in $S = \mathrm{span}(\psi, \varphi)$ for every $v$, and is $0$ for $v \perp S$.

However large $H$ is, $\Delta$ acts only on the plane of the two states. The whole problem is two-dimensional.

Full proof. The rewriting: $\braket{\varphi|(I - P)|\varphi} = \braket{\varphi|\varphi} - \braket{\varphi|P|\varphi} = 1 - \braket{\varphi|P|\varphi}$, so $p_{\rm succ} = \tfrac12\braket{\psi|P|\psi} + \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\braket{\varphi|P|\varphi}$.

The trace identity: for any operator $A$ and vector $u$, $A\ket u\!\bra u = \ket{Au}\!\bra u$, and unit 00's problem P4 proved $\operatorname{tr}\ket v\!\bra u = \braket{u|v}$; with $v = Au$ that is $\operatorname{tr}(A\ket u\!\bra u) = \braket{u|Au}$. By linearity of the trace, $\braket{\psi|P|\psi} - \braket{\varphi|P|\varphi} = \operatorname{tr}(P\ket\psi\!\bra\psi) - \operatorname{tr}(P\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi) = \operatorname{tr}(P\Delta)$.

$\Delta$ on the plane: $\Delta^\dagger = (\ket\psi\!\bra\psi)^\dagger - (\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi)^\dagger = \Delta$. For any $v$, $\Delta v = \braket{\psi|v}\psi - \braket{\varphi|v}\varphi \in S$; if $v \perp S$, both coefficients vanish. So $\Delta$ maps $H$ into $S$ and kills $S^\perp$; being Hermitian, it maps $S$ into $S$, and its spectral decomposition (unit 00) has all its nonzero eigenvalues on $S$.

Why "linear in $P$" is the key. The success is an affine function of the operator $P$. Maximising a linear function over projectors is a spectral problem: put $P$ on the eigenvectors with positive eigenvalues. Step three does exactly that.

A projector's range, $30^\circ$ from $e_+$: the shadows' squared lengths are $0.75$ and $0.25$, difference $0.5$. Turning the range onto $e_+$ makes them $1$ and $0$.

Full proof. By linearity of the trace and $\operatorname{tr}(P\ket e\!\bra e) = \braket{e|P|e}$ (step one's identity), $\operatorname{tr}(P\Delta) = s\braket{e_+|P|e_+} - s\braket{e_-|P|e_-}$. For an orthogonal projector $\braket{v|P|v} = \lVert Pv\rVert^2$, and $\lVert Pv\rVert^2 \le \lVert Pv\rVert^2 + \lVert v - Pv\rVert^2 = \lVert v\rVert^2$ (Pythagoras, as on Certainty needs orthogonality). So $\braket{e_+|P|e_+} \le 1$ and $\braket{e_-|P|e_-} \ge 0$, giving $\operatorname{tr}(P\Delta) \le s$. Equality needs $\lVert Pe_+\rVert = 1$, i.e. $Pe_+ = e_+$ (Pythagoras again), and $Pe_- = 0$. Then $p_{\rm succ} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(P\Delta) \le \tfrac12(1 + s)$.

Attained. Extend $\{e_+, e_-\}$ to an orthonormal basis of $H$ by adding a basis of $S^\perp$, measure in it, guess $\psi$ on $e_+$ and $\varphi$ on everything else. Then $P = \ket{e_+}\!\bra{e_+}$, which has $Pe_+ = e_+$, $Pe_- = 0$. The outcomes in $S^\perp$ never occur, since both states lie in $S$; how they are guessed is irrelevant. $\blacksquare$

The figure's numbers. If the range of $P$ is a line at angle $30^\circ$ from $e_+$, then $\lVert Pe_+\rVert^2 = \cos^230^\circ = 0.75$ and $\lVert Pe_-\rVert^2 = \cos^260^\circ = 0.25$, so $\operatorname{tr}(P\Delta) = 0.5\,s$, half the best.

The shape of the argument. It is the variational characterisation of the largest eigenvalue, applied to a matrix with one positive and one negative eigenvalue. Unit 21 runs the same argument for any two density operators, where the answer becomes the trace norm.

Full proof. From step two, $\Delta|_S = \begin{pmatrix}s^2 & -|c|s\\ -|c|s & -s^2\end{pmatrix} = s\begin{pmatrix}\sin\alpha & -\cos\alpha\\ -\cos\alpha & -\sin\alpha\end{pmatrix}$ with $\alpha\in[0, \pi/2]$ defined by $\cos\alpha = |c|$, $\sin\alpha = s$. Check: $R(\cos\beta, \sin\beta)^{\mathsf T} = (\cos2\beta\cos\beta + \sin2\beta\sin\beta,\ \sin2\beta\cos\beta - \cos2\beta\sin\beta)^{\mathsf T} = (\cos\beta, \sin\beta)^{\mathsf T}$ by the subtraction formulas, and likewise $R(-\sin\beta, \cos\beta)^{\mathsf T} = -(-\sin\beta, \cos\beta)^{\mathsf T}$. So $e_+ = \cos\beta\,u_1 + \sin\beta\,u_2$ with $\beta = \tfrac\alpha2 - \tfrac\pi4$, and $e_-$ at $\beta + \tfrac\pi2 = \tfrac\alpha2 + \tfrac\pi4$.

Their average is $\tfrac12(1 + s)$, matching step three. $\blacksquare$

An ancilla does not help

Helstrom survives extra apparatus

Append an ancilla in a fixed state $a$, apply any unitary $V$ on $H\otimes A$, then measure projectively. The success is still at most $\tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{1 - |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|^2})$.

proof

The device discriminates $\psi' = V(\psi\otimes a)$ from $\varphi' = V(\varphi\otimes a)$, and

$$\braket{\psi'|\varphi'} = \braket{\psi|\varphi}\braket{a|a} = \braket{\psi|\varphi}.$$

The bound depends only on that overlap. $\blacksquare$

Unit 21 shows every general measurement can be built this way (Naimark's theorem, cited), so $0.8536$ is final for Bob's photon.

A meter, drawn as a dial, is a projective measurement. However large the ancilla and however clever $V$, the overlap the meters face is the original one.

and a copier would have beaten it

Full proof. The device's statistics on input $\psi$ are those of a projective measurement on the unit vector $\psi' = V(\psi\otimes a)$ of $H\otimes A$; likewise for $\varphi$. So its success is the success of some projective measurement discriminating $\psi'$ from $\varphi'$, which Proof, step three bounds by $\tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{1 - |\braket{\psi'|\varphi'}|^2})$ — that proof worked in any Hilbert space. What "every general measurement" means, cited. Unit 21 introduces positive operator-valued measures, the most general measurements, and Naimark's dilation theorem: every one of them is an ancilla, a unitary, and a projective measurement, exactly as drawn. Given that theorem, this slide's proof shows the Helstrom bound holds for all measurements. Unit 21 also proves the bound directly for mixed states.

Why a copier would break it. If $U$ cloned $\psi$ and $\varphi$, then "clone, then do Helstrom on the pair" would be an ancilla-and-unitary device, bounded by $\tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{1 - |c|^2})$; but it discriminates states of overlap $|c|^2$, achieving $\tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{1 - |c|^4})$, strictly larger when $0 \lt |c| \lt 1$. Never wrong, sometimes silent

unambiguous discrimination

Three answers: "$\psi$", "$\varphi$", "don't know". The first two must never be wrong. Maximise the probability of a conclusive answer.

the optimum, stated (Ivanovic 1987; Dieks 1988; Peres 1988)

For equal priors the best conclusive probability is $1 - |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|$. Proved in unit 21, where general measurements exist.

Bob's photon: $1 - \tfrac1{\sqrt2} = 0.2929$, against Helstrom's $0.8536$. Never being wrong costs most of the answers.

On the qubit alone, projectively, only $\tfrac14$: measure in $z$; outcome $1$ proves "$\ket+$", outcome $0$ says nothing. Success $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14$.

A conclusive outcome must be impossible for one of the states: $\ket1$ never comes from $\ket0$, and $\ket-$ never from $\ket+$. They are not orthogonal, so no single basis contains both.

Sources, cited. I. D. Ivanovic, Phys. Lett. A 123, 257 (1987); D. Dieks, Phys. Lett. A 126, 303 (1988); A. Peres, Phys. Lett. A 128, 19 (1988). The optimum $1 - |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|$ needs a measurement with three outcomes on a qubit, which no projective measurement on $\mathbb C^2$ has; unit 21's positive operator-valued measures provide it, and that unit proves optimality.

The projective quarter, in full. A projective measurement on $\mathbb C^2$ has either one outcome (useless) or a basis $\{f, f^\perp\}$. An outcome may be read as "$\ket+$" only if $\ket0$ can never produce it, so $f\perp\ket0$, i.e. $f = \ket1$ up to phase; it may be read as "$\ket0$" only if it is $\perp\ket+$, i.e. $\ket-$. The basis $\{\ket0, \ket1\}$ has a conclusive outcome $\ket1$, reached only from $\ket+$, with probability $\tfrac12\cdot|\braket{1|+}|^2 = \tfrac14$; its other outcome $\ket0$ is inconclusive. The basis $\{\ket+, \ket-\}$ likewise gives $\tfrac12\cdot|\braket{-|0}|^2 = \tfrac14$. No basis contains both $\ket1$ and $\ket-$, since $\braket{1|-} = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2} \ne 0$. So $\tfrac14$ is the best on the qubit alone, and $0.2929 \gt 0.25$ is a case where general measurements strictly beat projective ones (with an ancilla, by Naimark, they become projective again).

Why "never wrong" costs. The Helstrom measurement accepts a $14.6\%$ error to answer always. Unambiguous discrimination refuses all error, and must then say "don't know" whenever the outcome could have come from either state — which, for states this close, is most of the time. Problem P10 puts the two side by side.

the fidelity of a copy

Why nothing on Bob's side can signal

unit 03, applied to Bob's whole laboratory

Let Bob's side be his photon, any blanks and any machine, starting in a fixed state. Whatever unitary $V$ he applies there and whatever projective measurement he then makes, his outcome probabilities are the same whichever measurement Alice makes.

So the copier $C$ cannot be a unitary — one more proof of no-cloning. And any real device is a unitary on photon and parts: the laser amplifier's copies must be noisy enough to hide Alice's choice. Spontaneous emission is that noise.

FLASH's error, located

Every step was quantum mechanics except one: a device that is not. The telegraph fails exactly because the copier cannot exist.

Bob's side, drawn as one box: a unitary and a projective measurement on his photon and everything he adds to it. Unit 03's theorem covers the box whole.

Full proof. The joint state is $\Psi^-\otimes\beta$ in $H_A\otimes(H_B\otimes K)$, where $\beta\in K$ is the fixed starting state of Bob's blanks and machine. Regroup the factors as $H_A\otimes H_{B'}$ with $H_{B'} = H_B\otimes K$: Bob's procedure, a unitary $V$ on $H_{B'}$ followed by a projective measurement $\{Q_\mu\}$, has outcome probabilities $\braket{\Psi^-\otimes\beta|(I\otimes V^\dagger Q_\mu V)|\Psi^-\otimes\beta}$ if Alice does nothing, and $\{V^\dagger Q_\mu V\}$ is a projective measurement on Bob's side. Unit 03's problem P10 shows that Alice's projective measurement, whichever it is, leaves these probabilities unchanged. $\blacksquare$

The logic. (1) Unit 03: nothing of this form on Bob's side can depend on Alice's choice. (2) A copier would have been a telegraph: a unitary copier would make it depend. Hence (3) no unitary copier of $\ket0, \ket1, \ket+, \ket-$ exists. The same reasoning covers every real attempt: the laser amplifier, modelled with the medium and the light it emits as Bob's "machine", is such a box, so its copies cannot carry Alice's choice. The cited calculations of Milonni–Hardies and Mandel find the mechanism; this argument shows there had to be one.

What it does not need. Neither relativity nor density operators. Unit 04 proves a stronger no-signalling theorem, for every local operation; this unit needed only unit 03's projective version.

  • No-cloning by linearity (no universal cloner) and by inner products ($|c| = |c|^2$), with or without a machine of its own.
  • The exact criterion: a set can be cloned iff its members are pairwise orthogonal or equal; $\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}$ and its shifts do it.
  • Certainty needs orthogonality.
  • Helstrom: $\tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{1 - |c|^2})$, via $p_{\rm succ} = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(P\Delta)$ on the plane of the two states; the basis straddling the bisector.
  • Extras: an ancilla does not help; $N$ copies give overlap $c^N$.
  • Stated: unambiguous discrimination $1 - |c|$; the $\tfrac56$ cloner.

05 · checkpoint

Done when

checkpoint

On paper and without notes, you can:

  • prove the no-cloning theorem both ways — by linearity, and by inner products, $\braket{\psi|\varphi} = \braket{\psi|\varphi}^2$ up to phase forcing $|\braket{\psi|\varphi}| \in \{0, 1\}$;
  • prove the Helstrom bound $\tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{1 - |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|^2})$ for two equiprobable pure states, and produce for $\ket0$ against $\ket+$ the value $\tfrac12(1 + \tfrac1{\sqrt2}) \approx 0.8536$ and the optimal measurement: the basis at angles $-\pi/8$ and $3\pi/8$.

Failing it is unambiguous

A no-cloning proof that never uses unitarity or linearity, or that forgets the blank must be fixed; a Helstrom bound asserted rather than derived from the eigenvalues of $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi - \ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi$; $0.75$ given as the best; a measurement basis that is not orthonormal.
