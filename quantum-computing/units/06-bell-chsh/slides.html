Bell, CHSH and Tsirelson: the classical bound, the quantum bound, and the gap between

Two players who cannot talk win a simple game at most three times in four if their coordination is classical, and $\cos^2\tfrac\pi8 \approx 0.8536$ of the time with a shared singlet — both numbers proved optimal, both tested in the laboratory.

needs 04~3 h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 06

Unit 03 proved that the singlet's statistics come from no model in which each photon carries a quantum state of its own, however the source chooses them. It ended on a warning: that is not Bell's theorem. A model in which each photon carries classical instructions — a table of answers to every question it might be asked — was not ruled out, and cannot be by perfect anticorrelation alone. This unit rules it out, by an inequality every such model obeys and the singlet violates. Then it turns round and proves the matching upper bound that quantum mechanics itself obeys, Tsirelson's $2\sqrt2$. The gap between $2$ and $2\sqrt2$ is the unit's subject, and the gap between $2\sqrt2$ and $4$ is its open question.

What the unit does not do: it takes Bell's theorem exactly as far as its hypotheses and stops. Which hypothesis one should give up, and what that says about reality, is a question about interpretation that the curriculum excludes on purpose. The theorem is a theorem whichever way that debate goes.

06 · overview

A game two players cannot talk their way through

the running example: the CHSH game

A referee draws two fair bits $x, y \in \{0, 1\}$, independently, and sends $x$ to Alice and $y$ to Bob. Each answers one bit, $a$ and $b$, without communicating. They win if

$$a \oplus b = x \wedge y .$$

So on inputs $00$, $01$, $10$ they must agree; on $11$ they must differ. They may agree on any strategy beforehand, and share anything they like — random numbers, or half of an entangled pair.

How often can they win? Three times in four, classically, and no more (section 1). With a shared singlet, $0.8536$ (section 3) — and no quantum strategy does better (section 4).

The rules. Solid arrows are the questions, dashed ones the answers; the shared resource is fixed before the questions are asked.

The plan. 1 local hidden variables, as mathematics · 2 the bound $|S| \le 2$ · 3 the singlet reaches $2\sqrt2$ · 4 Tsirelson: nothing quantum reaches further · 5 the experiments, and a box that reaches $4$.

A dotted underline is a glossary term (click, or G); a solid one like the game is the running example (hover); N shows the notes, where the longer proofs live.

Why a game. The game is the CHSH inequality of section 2 rewritten so that the number at stake is a probability of winning, which needs no physics to state. A nonlocal game in general has this shape: a referee with questions drawn from a known distribution, players who agree a strategy in advance and then cannot communicate, and a rule saying which answers win. The CHSH game is the simplest one in which shared entanglement beats every classical strategy. The game form is later than the inequality: Cleve, Høyer, Toner and Watrous (2004) made nonlocal games a subject in complexity theory.

Where the running example is. Section 1 proves $\tfrac34$ is the classical maximum by checking all sixteen deterministic strategies (Worked: sixteen strategies, none wins four). Section 2 rewrites the game as the CHSH quantity, $\Pr(\text{win}) = \tfrac12 + S/8$. Section 3 plays it with a singlet at the four directions of Worked: the four directions, drawn. Section 4 shows no quantum strategy beats $\cos^2\tfrac\pi8$. Section 5 reads Delft's 2015 experiment as a game won $0.8025$ of the time, and meets a box that wins always. The game, closed lines the four numbers up.

Why "cannot communicate" is enforceable. In the experiments of section 5 the players are two laboratories, and the questions are asked so late that no signal, even one travelling at the speed of light, could carry $x$ to Bob before he answers. Section 1 explains why that is the right way to forbid communication.

The experiment, from nothing

One pair per run. Each lab tosses its own coin, sets its analyser to one of two settings, and records which detector fires: $+1$ or $-1$.

The source is unit 03's crystal: it emits pairs of photons whose joint polarisation is the singlet. One photon flies to each lab.

Each lab holds unit 03's analyser — a polarising beam splitter with a detector on each exit — that can be turned to one of two angles. A coin in the lab picks which, once the photon is already on its way.

The record of a run is $(x, y, \alpha, \beta)$: the two coins and the two outcomes, $\alpha, \beta \in \{+1, -1\}$.

this is the game

Coins are the referee's bits, outcomes are the answers ($\alpha = (-1)^a$). The question is what correlations between $\alpha$ and $\beta$ the source can produce.

Each part, from nothing. The source and the analyser are exactly unit 03's: a laser photon splits in a crystal into two photons whose polarisations form a two-qubit state, and an analyser at angle $\varphi$ sends a photon polarised at $\varphi$ out of one exit and a photon polarised at $\varphi + 90^\circ$ out of the other. Section 3 writes the analyser as an observable. Everything else on the slide is bookkeeping: label the two exits $+1$ and $-1$, and give each lab a choice between two angles, made at random.

Why two settings per side. With one setting per side the only statistic is one correlation, and any number between $-1$ and $1$ is produced by some classical source (a coin shared between the labs, biased suitably). Unit 03 showed that two settings on both sides with perfect anticorrelation already rule out random product states; ruling out every classical model needs four combinations of settings, and that is what the CHSH quantity combines.

Electrons instead of photons. The Delft experiment of section 5 used electron spins, measured along a chosen axis. Unit 02's Stern–Gerlach measurement along $\mathbf n$ is the observable $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, and the description of this slide carries over word for word: the setting is an axis, the outcome is $\pm1$.

06 · local hidden variables

Why the timing matters: nothing outruns light

a fact of physics, taken as given

No signal and no influence travels faster than light, $c = 299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. This is a principle of special relativity (Einstein 1905); it is cited, not derived, here.

The consequence. If B's outcome is recorded less than (distance between the labs)$/c$ after A's coin is tossed, nothing about A's coin can have reached B in time; and symmetrically. Events this close in time and this far apart are space-like separated.

Delft's numbers. Labs $1280$ m apart; light needs $1280/c = 4.27\ \mu\mathrm s$. Each lab chose and measured inside that window.

So "Bob's answer cannot depend on Alice's question" is enforced by geometry, not by trusting the players.

Time runs up, light travels at $45^\circ$. Everything A's coin can influence lies in the shaded cone; B's coin and outcome lie outside it, and symmetrically.

The diagram. A spacetime diagram draws one dimension of space across and time up, with units chosen so that light moves one unit of distance per unit of time — so a light signal is a line at $45^\circ$. Anything slower is steeper. An event is a point: something happening at one place at one time. Whatever an event can affect lies in its light cone, the region above it between the two $45^\circ$ lines. Two events neither of which is in the other's light cone are space-like separated: no signal can link them, in either direction.

The principle, cited. That nothing — no signal, no physical influence carrying information — propagates faster than light is part of special relativity (A. Einstein, Annalen der Physik 17, 891 (1905)), and is not proved in this course; the sister course, Mechanics to Relativity, develops special relativity from its postulates. Unit 04's no-signalling theorem is the quantum side of the same coin: quantum mechanics never lets Alice's choice change Bob's statistics, so it does not contradict the principle.

The number. $1280\ \mathrm m / (2.998\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}) = 4.27\times10^{-6}$ s. In Delft, from the start of the random choice of setting to the start of the readout took at most $480$ ns, and the readout itself $3.7\ \mu$s, inside the window (Hensen et al., Nature 526, 682 (2015), cited). Dimensions: m over m s$^{-1}$ is s ✓.

What this buys. In the mathematical model of the next slide, Alice's outcome is not allowed to depend on Bob's setting. In a theorem that is a hypothesis; in an experiment it is made physically compulsory, given relativity, by arranging the events like this.

06 · local hidden variables

A local hidden-variable model

a local hidden-variable model

A finite or countable set $\Lambda$ with a probability distribution $p$, and two response functions

$$A\colon\{0,1\}\times\Lambda \to \{\pm1\},\qquad B\colon\{0,1\}\times\Lambda \to \{\pm1\}.$$

Each run: $\lambda$ is drawn from $p$; on settings $x, y$ the outcomes are $A(x, \lambda)$ and $B(y, \lambda)$. The correlation of the settings is

$$E(x, y) = \sum_{\lambda}p(\lambda)\,A(x, \lambda)\,B(y, \lambda) \in [-1, 1].$$

Three hypotheses are built in. Realism: the outcome is fixed by $\lambda$ and the setting. Locality: $A$ does not see $y$, $B$ does not see $x$. Free choice: $p$ does not depend on $x, y$.

The settings enter only on their own side; the only thing the sides share is $\lambda$, fixed at the source.

generality

$\lambda$ can be anything — a real number, a whole history. A general probability space is the same proof with integrals; the next slide shows countable is no loss anyway.

What the words mean. "Hidden variable": $\lambda$ is whatever the source puts into the pair that the quantum state does not show. It is the classical instruction sheet of unit 03's warning, made general. A model is local if each outcome depends only on what is at that lab — its own setting and its own share of $\lambda$ — and that is exactly what the response functions' arguments say.

Each hypothesis, as a formula. Realism: outcomes are values of functions $A(x, \lambda)$, $B(y, \lambda)$ (section 1's last slide relaxes this to random responses and shows nothing changes). Locality: the first argument of $A$ is $x$, not $(x, y)$. Free choice — also called measurement independence: the joint distribution of $(x, y, \lambda)$ is a product, so $\Pr(\lambda \mid x, y) = p(\lambda)$. All three are used in the bound of section 2, and section 5's slide on loopholes says what an experiment does to make each one hold.

The measure-theoretic version. In general $(\Lambda, \mathcal F, \mu)$ is a probability space and $A(x, \cdot)$, $B(y, \cdot)$ are measurable functions into $\{\pm1\}$; then $E(x, y) = \int_\Lambda A(x, \lambda)B(y, \lambda)\,d\mu(\lambda)$. Every proof in this unit goes through with $\sum_\lambda p(\lambda)$ replaced by $\int d\mu$, using only linearity and monotonicity of the integral. The next slide shows more: only the probabilities of the sixteen possible response tables matter, and the sets of $\lambda$ giving each table are measurable, so the general case reduces to a finite one.

History. J. S. Bell, "On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen paradox", Physics 1, 195 (1964), introduced exactly this framework, to answer Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen's 1935 argument that quantum mechanics must be incomplete. The sheet derives Bell's own inequality (problem P3).

06 · local hidden variables

Every model is a mixture of deterministic strategies

a deterministic strategy

A pair of functions $f, g\colon\{0, 1\} \to \{\pm1\}$: Alice answers $f(x)$, Bob $g(y)$. There are $4\times4 = 16$ of them.

theorem

For every local hidden-variable model there are weights $q(f, g) \ge 0$, summing to one, with

$$E(x, y) = \sum_{f, g}q(f, g)\,f(x)\,g(y)$$

for all four input pairs $x, y$.

proof

Each $\lambda$ has a response table $f_\lambda = A(\cdot, \lambda)$, $g_\lambda = B(\cdot, \lambda)$. Put $q(f, g) = \sum\{p(\lambda) : f_\lambda = f,\ g_\lambda = g\}$ and group the sum defining $E$ by table. $\blacksquare$

Drawn with three corners; there are sixteen. Every model sits inside the hull of the deterministic strategies, and $E$ is linear in the weights.

so

Any quantity linear in the $E(x, y)$ — the CHSH quantity, the game's winning probability — is at most its largest value over the 16 corners.

Full proof

Partition $\Lambda$ into the sixteen sets $\Lambda_{f, g} = \{\lambda : A(\cdot, \lambda) = f,\ B(\cdot, \lambda) = g\}$, some possibly empty. On $\Lambda_{f, g}$ the product $A(x, \lambda)B(y, \lambda)$ equals $f(x)g(y)$, a constant. So $E(x, y) = \sum_{f, g}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda_{f, g}}p(\lambda)f(x)g(y) = \sum_{f, g}q(f, g)f(x)g(y)$, rearranging a sum of non-negative weights times bounded terms (absolutely convergent, so the order does not matter even for countable $\Lambda$). The weights are non-negative and $\sum_{f, g}q(f, g) = \sum_\lambda p(\lambda) = 1$. The same grouping gives the full joint distribution: $\Pr(\alpha, \beta \mid x, y) = \sum_{f, g}q(f, g)\,[f(x) = \alpha]\,[g(y) = \beta]$.

Linear quantities are maximised at corners. If $L = \sum_{x, y}c_{xy}E(x, y)$ then $L = \sum_{f, g}q(f, g)L(f, g)$ with $L(f, g) = \sum c_{xy}f(x)g(y)$, an average of the sixteen values $L(f, g)$, hence at most their maximum. This is the whole reason the classical bounds of this unit are finite checks.

In the game's language. A shared random strategy — the players agree beforehand to toss a common coin and pick a deterministic strategy by it — is exactly a local hidden-variable model, with $\lambda$ the coin. Shared randomness can never beat the best deterministic strategy at a game, by the remark above.

Worked: sixteen strategies, none wins four

worked example: the game's classical value

Write Alice's strategy as bits $(a_0, a_1)$ — her answers to $x = 0, 1$ — and Bob's as $(b_0, b_1)$.

1. The four conditions for winning on each input pair:

$$\begin{aligned}a_0\oplus b_0 &= 0, & a_0\oplus b_1 &= 0,\\ a_1\oplus b_0 &= 0, & a_1\oplus b_1 &= 1.\end{aligned}$$

2. Add them mod 2. Each of $a_0, a_1, b_0, b_1$ occurs twice on the left, so the left sides sum to $0$; the right sides sum to $1$. All four cannot hold.

3. More: the number of failed conditions is odd. So every strategy wins on 3 or 1 of the four input pairs.

4. The table checks all 16: eight win 3, eight win 1. With inputs uniform, a strategy wins with probability $\tfrac34$ or $\tfrac14$.

5. Mixtures average these (previous slide): classical value $= \tfrac34$, reached by "always answer $0$".

Each step. Step 1: on input $(x, y)$ the winning condition is $a_x\oplus b_y = x\wedge y$; the four input pairs give the four equations. Step 2: XOR is addition in $\mathbb F_2$, and $a_0$ appears in the first two equations, $a_1$ in the last two, $b_0$ in the first and third, $b_1$ in the second and fourth: so the sum of the left sides is $2a_0 + 2a_1 + 2b_0 + 2b_1 = 0$ in $\mathbb F_2$, while the right sides sum to $0 + 0 + 0 + 1 = 1$. Step 3: let $F$ be the set of failed equations; adding all four "left side minus right side" gives $|F| \bmod 2$ (each failed equation contributes $1$), and it equals $0 - 1 = 1$, so $|F|$ is odd. Step 4: each input pair has probability $\tfrac14$, so winning on $w$ pairs means winning with probability $w/4$.

The table, read. The eight strategies winning three times are $(a_0a_1, b_0b_1) \in \{(00, 00), (00, 01), (01, 00), (01, 10), (10, 01), (10, 11), (11, 10), (11, 11)\}$ — computed by running through all sixteen. For example $(00, 00)$, "everyone always answers $0$", agrees always, so it wins on $00$, $01$, $10$ and loses on $11$. And $(00, 10)$: Alice answers $0$, Bob answers $1 - y$; on $00$ they differ (lose), on $01$ agree (win), on $10$ differ (lose), on $11$ agree (lose): one win ✓.

Over the sixteen strategies the average number of wins is $(8\cdot3 + 8\cdot1)/16 = 2$, so a uniformly random strategy wins half the time, as answering independently at random would. That is the baseline $\tfrac12$ that the CHSH quantity measures deviations from (next section).

06 · local hidden variables

Random responses add nothing

a stochastic local model

Given $\lambda$, Alice's outcome is random: $\alpha$ with probability $p_A(\alpha\mid x, \lambda)$, Bob's $\beta$ with $p_B(\beta\mid y, \lambda)$, independently given $\lambda$:

$$\begin{aligned}\Pr(\alpha, \beta\mid x, y) = \sum_\lambda p(\lambda)\,&p_A(\alpha\mid x, \lambda)\\ \times\,&p_B(\beta\mid y, \lambda).\end{aligned}$$
theorem

Every stochastic local model has the same statistics as a deterministic one with a larger $\Lambda$.

proof: put the coins into λ

Let $\lambda' = (\lambda, u_0, u_1, v_0, v_1)$, the $u_x$, $v_y \in \{\pm1\}$ drawn independently from $p_A(\cdot\mid x, \lambda)$, $p_B(\cdot\mid y, \lambda)$. Set $A'(x, \lambda') = u_x$, $B'(y, \lambda') = v_y$. Summing out the unused coins gives back the formula above. $\blacksquare$

A coin tossed at the analyser is, statistically, a coin tossed at the source for each setting in advance.

So every bound proved for deterministic models holds for random ones too (problem P2).

Full proof

Put $\Lambda' = \Lambda\times\{\pm1\}^4$ with $p'(\lambda, u_0, u_1, v_0, v_1) = p(\lambda)\,p_A(u_0\mid0, \lambda)\,p_A(u_1\mid1, \lambda)\,p_B(v_0\mid0, \lambda)\,p_B(v_1\mid1, \lambda)$: non-negative, and summing over each $u$, $v$ in turn (each conditional distribution sums to one) gives $\sum_\lambda p(\lambda) = 1$ ✓. It does not depend on $x, y$, so free choice holds. With $A'(x, \lambda') = u_x$, $B'(y, \lambda') = v_y$ — local, deterministic — $\Pr'(\alpha, \beta\mid x, y) = \sum p'(\lambda')[u_x = \alpha][v_y = \beta]$. Sum first over the coin for the other setting on each side, $u_{1-x}$ and $v_{1-y}$, each contributing a factor $1$; what remains is $\sum_\lambda p(\lambda)p_A(\alpha\mid x, \lambda)p_B(\beta\mid y, \lambda)$, the original statistics. $\blacksquare$

Where independence given λ is used. The stochastic model's joint distribution factorises given $\lambda$: Alice's randomness and Bob's are independent once $\lambda$ is fixed. That is the stochastic form of locality — if they were correlated given $\lambda$, the correlation would itself be a shared variable and belong in $\lambda$. Bell called the factorised form "local causality".

Why this matters. It is sometimes said that Bell's theorem refutes "determinism" rather than locality. This slide shows that the deterministic models are no less general than the stochastic ones, so that reading confuses a convenience of the proof with a hypothesis of the theorem. Which hypothesis to give up is beyond this unit.

06 · the bound

The CHSH quantity, and the game in its terms

the CHSH quantity (Clauser, Horne, Shimony, Holt 1969)

Alice's settings $a, a'$ ($x = 0, 1$), Bob's $b, b'$ ($y = 0, 1$). With $E$ the correlation of each pair,

$$\begin{aligned}S = {}&E(a, b) + E(a, b')\\ &+ E(a', b) - E(a', b').\end{aligned}$$
the game, rewritten

With answers $\alpha = (-1)^a$, $\beta = (-1)^b$, every strategy wins with probability

$$\Pr(\text{win}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac18 S .$$
proof

$a\oplus b = 0 \iff \alpha\beta = 1$. So on $xy \ne 11$, $\Pr(\text{win}) = \tfrac12(1 + E)$; on $11$, $\tfrac12(1 - E)$. Average the four with weight $\tfrac14$. $\blacksquare$

The sign of each term in $S$ is the sign the game rewards on that input pair.

Checks. Random answers: $S = 0$, $\Pr = \tfrac12$ ✓. "Always $0$": $S = 2$, $\Pr = \tfrac34$ ✓. Always winning needs $S = 4$.

Full proof

On input $(x, y)$, the outcomes are $\alpha, \beta \in \{\pm1\}$ with $\alpha\beta = (-1)^{a\oplus b}$. So $\Pr(a\oplus b = 0) = \Pr(\alpha\beta = 1) =: P_+$ and $\Pr(\alpha\beta = -1) = P_- = 1 - P_+$; then $E = P_+ - P_- = 2P_+ - 1$, so $P_+ = \tfrac12(1 + E)$ and $P_- = \tfrac12(1 - E)$. The game wants $P_+$ on the three pairs with $x\wedge y = 0$ and $P_-$ on $(1, 1)$. Inputs are uniform, so

$\Pr(\text{win}) = \tfrac14\big[\tfrac12(1 + E_{00}) + \tfrac12(1 + E_{01}) + \tfrac12(1 + E_{10}) + \tfrac12(1 - E_{11})\big] = \tfrac12 + \tfrac18(E_{00} + E_{01} + E_{10} - E_{11})$,

and the bracket is $S$ with $a, a', b, b'$ standing for $x = 0, 1$ and $y = 0, 1$. Nothing here assumed a model: it holds for any strategy whatever, classical, quantum or otherwise, which is what makes the game a fair referee between them.

Which term carries the minus sign is a convention: any one of the four will do, after relabelling which of a player's settings is primed. And $-S$ is the same quantity for a player who flips their answers; what matters is $|S|$. Every result of this unit holds for every placement of the sign.

The CHSH paper. J. F. Clauser, M. A. Horne, A. Shimony and R. A. Holt, "Proposed experiment to test local hidden-variable theories", Phys. Rev. Lett. 23, 880 (1969). Its point was experimental: Bell's 1964 inequality assumed perfect anticorrelation, which no real apparatus delivers; CHSH needs no such assumption.

06 · the bound

The CHSH inequality

the CHSH inequality

In every local hidden-variable model, $\ |S| \le 2$.

proof: bound it pointwise, then average

Fix $\lambda$ and write $A = A(a, \lambda)$, $A' = A(a', \lambda)$, $B = B(b, \lambda)$, $B' = B(b', \lambda)$, all $\pm1$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}s(\lambda) &:= AB + AB' + A'B - A'B'\\ &= A(B + B') + A'(B - B').\end{aligned}$$

As $B, B' = \pm1$, one of $B + B'$, $B - B'$ is $0$ and the other is $\pm2$. So $s(\lambda) = \pm2$. And $S = \sum_\lambda p(\lambda)s(\lambda)$, an average of numbers in $\{-2, 2\}$:

$$|S| \le \sum_\lambda p(\lambda)\,|s(\lambda)| = 2 . \ \blacksquare$$

The game: $\Pr(\text{win}) = \tfrac12 + S/8 \le \tfrac12 + \tfrac28 = \tfrac34$ — the worked example's number, a second way.

In every cell exactly one of the two brackets survives, with size 2; then $|A| = |A'| = 1$ finishes it.

check at two corners

"Always $+1$": $s = 1 + 1 + 1 - 1 = 2$. Flip Bob on $b'$: $1 - 1 + 1 + 1 = 2$.

Full proof, every step. For a fixed $\lambda$ the four outcomes are numbers, so $AB + AB' + A'B - A'B'$ factors as shown. If $B = B'$ then $B - B' = 0$ and $B + B' = 2B = \pm2$, so $s = 2AB = \pm2$. If $B = -B'$ then $B + B' = 0$ and $B - B' = 2B$, so $s = 2A'B = \pm2$. Then $S = \sum_\lambda p(\lambda)\big(A(a,\lambda)B(b,\lambda) + \dots - A(a', \lambda)B(b', \lambda)\big) = \sum_\lambda p(\lambda)s(\lambda)$ by linearity of the sum — this is where free choice enters: the same $p$ weights all four correlations, though in the experiment they are measured on different runs. The triangle inequality and $\sum p = 1$ give $|S| \le 2$.

The second proof, by corners. By Every model is a mixture of deterministic strategies, $S$ is an average of its values at the sixteen deterministic strategies, and the pointwise computation above, read for a deterministic strategy, says each of those is $\pm2$. The two proofs are the same proof; the first groups the sum by $\lambda$, the second by response table.

Equality. $S = 2$ exactly when $s(\lambda) = 2$ for every $\lambda$ of positive weight: every such $\lambda$ behaves as one of the eight deterministic strategies that win on three input pairs.

General Λ. With a probability measure, $S = \int s\,d\mu$ and $|\int s\,d\mu| \le \int|s|\,d\mu = 2$: the same two lines.

06 · the bound

Every hypothesis, used once

hypothesiswhere the proof uses it
Realism: outcomes are functions $A(x, \lambda)$the four outcomes of one $\lambda$ are numbers to add, though only one pair is ever measured
Locality: $A$ does not see $y$$A$ is the same number in $AB$ and $AB'$, so it factors out of $B + B'$
Free choice: $p(\lambda\mid x, y) = p(\lambda)$the four correlations, measured on different runs, are averages over the same $p$
Outcomes $\pm1$$B, B' = \pm1$ makes one bracket vanish

Drop one and the bound fails. Let $A$ see $y$: "answer $x\wedge y$", with Bob answering $0$, wins always. Let $p$ depend on $(x, y)$: $\lambda$ can carry the inputs to both sides — the same thing.

superdeterminism

If the settings and $\lambda$ have a common cause — the coins are correlated with the source because both were fixed long ago — free choice fails, and no experiment can rule that out: any data whatever can be produced. It is a hypothesis of the theorem, not a gap in its proof.

what the theorem says, exactly

If a theory of the source reproduces a measured $|S| \gt 2$, it violates realism with locality, or free choice. Which, is not this unit's question.

The counterexamples, checked. Signalling: with $a = x\wedge y$ (Alice sees both inputs) and $b = 0$, $a\oplus b = x\wedge y$ on every input, so $\Pr(\text{win}) = 1$ and $S = 4$. Free choice violated: let $\lambda = (x, y)$ itself — the source "knew" the coins — and run the same strategy reading the inputs from $\lambda$; again $S = 4$. Outcomes not $\pm1$: if outcomes may be any numbers in $[-k, k]$, the same proof gives $|S| \le k\,(|B + B'| + |B - B'|) \le 2k^2$, so $2$ is the bound for outcomes normalised to $\pm1$. (The quantum side of section 3 also uses $\pm1$ outcomes, so the comparison is fair.)

What "realism" really asks. Section 1's last slide showed random local responses reduce to deterministic ones. The assumption that does the work, beyond locality and free choice, is that the joint distribution factorises given $\lambda$ — Bell's "local causality" (J. S. Bell, "The theory of local beables", 1976). The four outcomes of the deterministic version are a bookkeeping device that factorisation makes available.

Superdeterminism. The term is Bell's. The point is logical: if the settings $x, y$ are themselves functions of $\lambda$, a "model" may read off the inputs and answer accordingly, and $S = 4$ is reachable. Experiments make it implausible — Delft drew its settings from fast random number generators, and later tests chose settings with light from distant quasars — but cannot eliminate it, since any physical randomness source could in principle share a cause with the source of pairs in the distant past. This unit takes free choice as a hypothesis and stops there.

A classical model that comes close

Bell's sign model

Settings are angles $\theta$ in a plane, with direction $\mathbf n_\theta$. $\lambda$ is a uniformly random direction in the same plane, and

$$\begin{aligned}A(\theta, \lambda) &= \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf n_\theta\cdot\lambda),\\ B(\theta, \lambda) &= -\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf n_\theta\cdot\lambda).\end{aligned}$$

The two signs differ exactly when the line perpendicular to $\lambda$ falls between $\mathbf n_{\theta_a}$ and $\mathbf n_{\theta_b}$: probability $\Delta/\pi$, with $\Delta = |\theta_a - \theta_b| \le \pi$. So

$$E(\theta_a, \theta_b) = -\big(1 - \tfrac{2\Delta}\pi\big) = -1 + \tfrac{2\Delta}{\pi}.$$

Checks: perfect anticorrelation at $\Delta = 0$ ✓; $E = 0$ at right angles ✓; at the four directions, $E = -\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, +\tfrac12$ and $S = -2$: on the bound, not past it.

Both have perfect anticorrelation and agree at $0$, $\pi/2$, $\pi$. At $\pi/4$ the singlet's $-0.7071$ beats the model's $-0.5$, and the CHSH quantity adds four such gaps.

Full proof of the correlation

Write $\lambda = (\cos\mu, \sin\mu)$ with $\mu$ uniform on $[0, 2\pi)$ and $\mathbf n_\theta = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$; then $\mathbf n_\theta\cdot\lambda = \cos(\mu - \theta)$, positive on an open half-circle of $\mu$ centred on $\theta$. Two half-circles centred at $\theta_a$ and $\theta_b$, with $\Delta = |\theta_a - \theta_b| \le \pi$, disagree (one contains $\mu$, the other not) on two arcs of length $\Delta$ each: total $2\Delta$ out of $2\pi$. So $\Pr(\text{signs differ}) = \Delta/\pi$ and $\mathbb E[\operatorname{sign}\cdot\operatorname{sign}] = (1 - \Delta/\pi) - \Delta/\pi = 1 - 2\Delta/\pi$; Bob's extra minus sign gives the formula. The probability-zero set where $\mathbf n_\theta\cdot\lambda = 0$ is assigned $+1$; it changes nothing. This $\lambda$ is a continuous variable: the integral version of the model, as section 1 allowed.

The S value. At $a = 0$, $a' = \pi/2$, $b = \pi/4$, $b' = -\pi/4$ the differences are $\pi/4$, $\pi/4$, $\pi/4$, $3\pi/4$, so $E = -\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, -\tfrac12, +\tfrac12$ and $S = -\tfrac12 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 = -2$. A computed check, with $\mu$ on a grid of two million points: $-0.5000$, $+0.5000$, $S = -2.000$. By the CHSH inequality nothing better was possible for it.

Why this model matters. It answers the natural objection "surely a clever enough classical model can mimic the singlet". This one is clever: it reproduces perfect anticorrelation at every angle (unit 03's fact), and the right correlation at $0$, $\pi/2$, $\pi$. What it cannot do is curve: its correlation is an average of $\pm1$-valued products, and the CHSH quantity detects the cosine's extra bend. Bell gave a model of this kind, with $\lambda$ on the sphere, in his 1964 paper, to show that the theorem needs intermediate angles.

Observables with outcomes ±1

a ±1-valued observable

A Hermitian operator $A$ with $A^2 = I$.

what they are

(a) Its eigenvalues are in $\{\pm1\}$, so $A = P_+ - P_-$ with $P_\pm = \tfrac12(I \pm A)$, and $A$ is unitary.

(b) Every two-outcome projective measurement $\{P, I - P\}$, outcomes labelled $\pm1$, is one: $A = 2P - I$.

(c) On a qubit: $A = \pm I$, or $A = \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $|\mathbf n| = 1$.

proof

(a) $Av = \lambda v \Rightarrow v = A^2v = \lambda^2v$; spectral theorem. (b) $(2P - I)^2 = 4P - 4P + I$. (c) If the eigenvalues differ, $\operatorname{tr}A = 0$, so $A = \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ (unit 02's Pauli basis), and $A^2 = |\mathbf n|^2I$. $\blacksquare$

Unit 02's picture: the $+1$ eigenstate of $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ has Bloch vector $\mathbf n$, the $-1$ eigenstate $-\mathbf n$.

This unit's settings lie in the $xz$ plane: $\mathbf n = (\sin\theta, 0, \cos\theta)$ and

$$A(\theta) = \cos\theta\,Z + \sin\theta\,X = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & -\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}.$$

Check: $A(\theta)^2 = (\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta)I = I$ ✓, and $A(0) = Z$, $A(\pi/2) = X$.

Full proof

(a) $A$ is Hermitian, so by unit 00's spectral theorem $A = \sum_\lambda\lambda P_\lambda$ over its eigenvalues. Each eigenvalue satisfies $\lambda^2 = 1$ (shown on the slide), so $\lambda \in \{\pm1\}$ and $A = P_+ - P_-$, with $P_+ + P_- = I$ (either may be zero); solving gives $P_\pm = \tfrac12(I \pm A)$. Unitary: $A^\dagger A = A^2 = I$. (b) $P$ is a projector, $P^2 = P = P^\dagger$, so $2P - I$ is Hermitian and squares to $4P^2 - 4P + I = I$. Its $+1$ eigenspace is the range of $P$, its $-1$ eigenspace the range of $I - P$, so measuring the observable $2P - I$ (unit 01) is the measurement $\{P, I - P\}$ with outcomes labelled $+1$, $-1$. (c) On $\mathbb C^2$: if both eigenvalues are $+1$, $P_+ = I$ and $A = I$; both $-1$, $A = -I$. Otherwise each has multiplicity one and $\operatorname{tr}A = 0$. Unit 02 showed $I, X, Y, Z$ are a basis of all $2\times2$ matrices, Hermitian ones having real coefficients; trace zero kills the $I$ coefficient, so $A = \mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ with $\mathbf n \in \mathbb R^3$, and unit 02's product rule gives $A^2 = |\mathbf n|^2I$, so $|\mathbf n| = 1$.

Why only ±1 outcomes. The game and the CHSH quantity compare a quantum strategy with a classical one that answers $\pm1$; to compare like with like the quantum measurements must have two outcomes too, and (b) says that is exactly a $\pm1$-valued observable. A measurement with more outcomes, grouped into two, is again one of these. Generalised measurements (POVMs) are unit 21's; section 4 says why Tsirelson's bound survives them.

The matrix. $\cos\theta Z + \sin\theta X = \cos\theta\,\mathrm{diag}(1, -1) + \sin\theta\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, as written. Its $+1$ eigenvector is $(\cos\tfrac\theta2, \sin\tfrac\theta2)$, check: first entry of $A$ times it, $\cos\theta\cos\tfrac\theta2 + \sin\theta\sin\tfrac\theta2 = \cos(\theta - \tfrac\theta2) = \cos\tfrac\theta2$ ✓. Half the Bloch angle, as unit 02 said.

The polarisation analyser is A(2φ)

the analyser as an observable

Unit 03's analyser at $\varphi$ measures in the basis $\ket\varphi = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$, $\ket{\varphi^\perp} = (-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)$. Transmitted is $+1$, reflected $-1$:

$$\begin{aligned}\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi - \ket{\varphi^\perp}\!\bra{\varphi^\perp} &= \begin{pmatrix}\cos2\varphi & \sin2\varphi\\ \sin2\varphi & -\cos2\varphi\end{pmatrix}\\ &= A(2\varphi).\end{aligned}$$
proof

With $c = \cos\varphi$, $s = \sin\varphi$: $\begin{pmatrix}c^2 & cs\\ cs & s^2\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}s^2 & -cs\\ -cs & c^2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}c^2 - s^2 & 2cs\\ 2cs & s^2 - c^2\end{pmatrix}$. $\blacksquare$

An analyser at $22.5^\circ$ is $A(45^\circ) = (Z + X)/\sqrt2$. Checks: $\varphi = 0$ gives $Z$ ✓, $45^\circ$ gives $X$ ✓, $90^\circ$ gives $-Z$, the exits swapped ✓.

two angles, one factor of two

$\theta$ is always the Bloch angle. A photon analyser is turned by $\varphi = \theta/2$; an electron spin's axis (Delft) by $\theta$ itself.

Where this comes from. Unit 03 modelled a photon's linear polarisation at angle $\alpha$ as $\ket\alpha = \cos\alpha\ket0 + \sin\alpha\ket1$, and the analyser at $\varphi$ as the measurement in the orthonormal basis $\{\ket\varphi, \ket{\varphi + 90^\circ}\}$, which reproduced Malus's law. By unit 01, a measurement with outcome labels $\pm1$ on the two basis vectors is the observable $(+1)\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi + (-1)\ket{\varphi^\perp}\!\bra{\varphi^\perp}$. The proof is matrix multiplication of $\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi$, whose entries are $\cos^2\varphi$, $\cos\varphi\sin\varphi$, $\sin^2\varphi$, and the double-angle formulas $c^2 - s^2 = \cos2\varphi$, $2cs = \sin2\varphi$.

The factor of two is unit 02's. Orthogonal states are antipodes on the Bloch sphere, so physical angles between polarisations double on it: H and V ($90^\circ$ apart in the lab) are the poles. All formulas of this unit are written in the Bloch angle, and the slide for the four directions gives both.

An honest note on the experiments. Real analysers are not perfect, detectors miss some photons, and the source is not exactly the singlet; section 5 deals with what that costs. Here, as in unit 03, the analyser is ideal.

Correlations of a shared quantum state

two labs, one state

A state $\rho$ of $AB$; Alice measures a $\pm1$-valued observable $A_x = P^x_+ - P^x_-$, Bob $B_y = Q^y_+ - Q^y_-$. Then

$$\Pr(\alpha, \beta\mid x, y) = \operatorname{tr}\big(\rho\,(P^x_\alpha\otimes Q^y_\beta)\big),$$ $$E(x, y) = \operatorname{tr}\big(\rho\,(A_x\otimes B_y)\big).$$
proof

The $P^x_\alpha\otimes Q^y_\beta$ are four projectors summing to $I\otimes I$: unit 04's Born rule gives the first formula. Then $\sum_{\alpha\beta}\alpha\beta\,P^x_\alpha\otimes Q^y_\beta = A_x\otimes B_y$. $\blacksquare$

the CHSH operator $$\begin{aligned}C &= A\otimes B + A\otimes B' + A'\otimes B - A'\otimes B',\\ S &= \operatorname{tr}(\rho\,C).\end{aligned}$$

Qubit 0 (Alice) on top. A meter labelled with an observable measures it.

No order is needed: $P\otimes I$ and $I\otimes Q$ commute (unit 03), so "who measures first" changes nothing.

No signal: $\sum_\beta\Pr(\alpha, \beta\mid x, y) = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_A P^x_\alpha)$, the same for every $y$ (unit 04).

Check: the four probabilities sum to $\operatorname{tr}\rho = 1$ ✓.

Full proof

The products $P^x_\alpha\otimes Q^y_\beta$ are projectors, $(P\otimes Q)^2 = P^2\otimes Q^2$, $(P\otimes Q)^\dagger = P^\dagger\otimes Q^\dagger$ (unit 03); they are orthogonal for different $(\alpha, \beta)$, and $\sum_{\alpha,\beta}P^x_\alpha\otimes Q^y_\beta = (\sum_\alpha P^x_\alpha)\otimes(\sum_\beta Q^y_\beta) = I\otimes I$. Measuring both sides is the projective measurement with these four projectors — unit 03 showed that measuring A then B, or B then A, gives exactly this joint distribution — and unit 04's rule for a density operator gives $\Pr(\alpha, \beta) = \operatorname{tr}(\rho\,P^x_\alpha\otimes Q^y_\beta)$, each $\ge 0$, summing to $\operatorname{tr}\rho = 1$. The correlation is $E = \sum_{\alpha\beta}\alpha\beta\Pr(\alpha, \beta) = \operatorname{tr}\big(\rho\sum\alpha\beta\,P_\alpha\otimes Q_\beta\big)$ and $\sum_{\alpha\beta}\alpha\beta\,P_\alpha\otimes Q_\beta = (\sum_\alpha\alpha P_\alpha)\otimes(\sum_\beta\beta Q_\beta) = A_x\otimes B_y$ by bilinearity.

The marginal. $\sum_\beta\operatorname{tr}(\rho\,P\otimes Q_\beta) = \operatorname{tr}(\rho\,P\otimes I) = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_AP)$ by the defining property of the partial trace (unit 04). It does not mention $y$: quantum correlations never signal, whatever their CHSH value. Section 5 asks whether no-signalling is what holds them to $2\sqrt2$, and finds it is not.

The CHSH operator is Hermitian (a real combination of tensor products of Hermitian operators), and $S = \operatorname{tr}(\rho C)$ by linearity of the trace. For a pure state, $S = \braket{\psi|C|\psi}$. So a quantum strategy's CHSH value is an expectation value of one fixed operator — which is why section 4 can bound it by bounding that operator.

The operator norm

the operator norm $$\lVert M\rVert = \max_{\lVert v\rVert = 1}\lVert Mv\rVert ,$$

the most $M$ stretches a unit vector — the largest singular value of $M$ (unit 03's SVD).

four facts

(a) $\lVert M + N\rVert \le \lVert M\rVert + \lVert N\rVert$ and $\lVert MN\rVert \le \lVert M\rVert\lVert N\rVert$.

(b) $\lVert U\rVert = 1$ for unitary $U$ — so for every $\pm1$-valued observable.

(c) $\lVert M\otimes N\rVert \le \lVert M\rVert\,\lVert N\rVert$.

(d) Hermitian $H$: every eigenvalue has $|\lambda| \le \lVert H\rVert$, and $|\operatorname{tr}(\rho H)| \le \max|\lambda|$ for every density operator $\rho$.

The norm is the longest semi-axis of the image of the unit sphere.

proof of the two that are not one line

(c) $M\otimes N = (M\otimes I)(I\otimes N)$; and for $v = \sum_jv_j\otimes e_j$, $\ \lVert(M\otimes I)v\rVert^2 = \sum_j\lVert Mv_j\rVert^2 \le \lVert M\rVert^2\lVert v\rVert^2$.

(d) $|\lambda| = \lVert Hv\rVert \le \lVert H\rVert$ for a unit eigenvector. With $H = \sum_jh_j\ket{f_j}\!\bra{f_j}$, $\ \operatorname{tr}(\rho H) = \sum_jh_j\braket{f_j|\rho|f_j}$: weights $\ge 0$ summing to $1$. $\blacksquare$

Full proof of all four

The maximum exists: $v \mapsto \lVert Mv\rVert$ is continuous on the unit sphere of a finite-dimensional space, which is compact. Equivalently $\lVert Mv\rVert \le \lVert M\rVert\lVert v\rVert$ for every $v$. (a) $\lVert(M + N)v\rVert \le \lVert Mv\rVert + \lVert Nv\rVert$ (triangle inequality, unit 00) and $\lVert MNv\rVert \le \lVert M\rVert\lVert Nv\rVert \le \lVert M\rVert\lVert N\rVert\lVert v\rVert$. (b) Unitaries preserve norms (unit 00); a $\pm1$-valued observable is unitary (Observables with outcomes ±1). (c) Every $v \in H_A\otimes H_B$ is $\sum_jv_j\otimes e_j$ for an orthonormal basis $e_j$ of $H_B$ (unit 03's basis theorem), and then $\lVert v\rVert^2 = \sum_j\lVert v_j\rVert^2$ because the $v_j\otimes e_j$ are orthogonal for different $j$ (unit 03's inner product, $\braket{v_j\otimes e_j|v_k\otimes e_k} = \braket{v_j|v_k}\delta_{jk}$). $(M\otimes I)v = \sum_jMv_j\otimes e_j$, so its squared norm is $\sum\lVert Mv_j\rVert^2 \le \lVert M\rVert^2\sum\lVert v_j\rVert^2$. The same for $I\otimes N$ (expand in a basis of $H_A$), and (a) for the product. (In fact equality holds; it is not needed.) (d) Unit 00's spectral theorem gives the orthonormal eigenbasis $f_j$; $\operatorname{tr}(\rho H) = \sum_jh_j\operatorname{tr}(\rho\ket{f_j}\!\bra{f_j})$ and $\operatorname{tr}(\rho\ket f\!\bra f) = \braket{f|\rho|f} \ge 0$ since $\rho \ge 0$; the weights sum to $\operatorname{tr}\rho = 1$ (unit 04). An average of the $h_j$ lies between the least and greatest of them.

The SVD remark. With $M = W\Sigma V^\dagger$ (unit 03's complex SVD), $\lVert Mv\rVert = \lVert\Sigma V^\dagger v\rVert$ and $V^\dagger$ permutes unit vectors, so the maximum is the largest singular value. For Hermitian $H$ the singular values are the $|\lambda|$, so (d)'s first half is an equality. The slide uses only the inequality.

Worked: squaring the CHSH operator

worked example: $C^2 = 4I - [A, A']\otimes[B, B']$, every term — only $A^2 = A'^2 = B^2 = B'^2 = I$ is used

1. Group. $C = A\otimes(B + B') + A'\otimes(B - B')$.

2. Square, by $(P\otimes Q)(R\otimes T) = PR\otimes QT$ — four products, order kept:

$$\begin{aligned}C^2 = {}&A^2\otimes(B + B')^2\\ &+ A'^2\otimes(B - B')^2\\ &+ AA'\otimes(B + B')(B - B')\\ &+ A'A\otimes(B - B')(B + B').\end{aligned}$$

3. The brackets, using $B^2 = B'^2 = I$:

$$\begin{aligned}(B \pm B')^2 &= 2I \pm (BB' + B'B),\\ (B + B')(B - B') &= -[B, B'],\\ (B - B')(B + B') &= [B, B'].\end{aligned}$$

4. Collect. With $A^2 = A'^2 = I$ the first two terms add to $I\otimes4I$, the $\pm(BB' + B'B)$ cancelling; the last two give $-AA'\otimes[B, B'] + A'A\otimes[B, B']$:

$$C^2 = 4\,I\otimes I - [A, A']\otimes[B, B'] .$$

Each step. Step 2 is bilinearity of $\otimes$ on operators and unit 03's product rule; the order inside each factor is kept, which matters since $A$ and $A'$ need not commute. Step 3: $(B + B')^2 = B^2 + BB' + B'B + B'^2 = 2I + BB' + B'B$ and $(B - B')^2 = 2I - BB' - B'B$; their sum, each tensored with $I$ (as $A^2 = A'^2 = I$), is $I\otimes4I$ — the cross terms cancel. $(B + B')(B - B') = B^2 - BB' + B'B - B'^2 = -BB' + B'B$, and $(B - B')(B + B') = B^2 + BB' - B'B - B'^2 = BB' - B'B$. Step 4: $-AA'\otimes[B, B'] + A'A\otimes[B, B'] = -(AA' - A'A)\otimes[B, B']$. Nothing was assumed about dimension, or about the observables beyond squaring to $I$.

History. B. S. Cirel'son (Tsirelson), "Quantum generalizations of Bell's inequality", Lett. Math. Phys. 4, 93 (1980). The squaring identity is the short proof; Tsirelson's paper went further, characterising all quantum correlations of this kind by vectors in a real inner-product space.

Tsirelson's bound

Tsirelson's bound

For $\pm1$-valued observables $A, A'$ on $H_A$ and $B, B'$ on $H_B$, of any dimension, and every state $\rho$ of $AB$, pure or mixed:

$$|S| = |\operatorname{tr}(\rho\,C)| \le 2\sqrt2 .$$
proof

1. $\lVert[A, A']\rVert \le \lVert AA'\rVert + \lVert A'A\rVert \le 2$, by (a) and (b); likewise $\lVert[B, B']\rVert \le 2$.

2. By the identity, (a) and (c): $\ \lVert C^2\rVert \le 4 + \lVert[A, A']\rVert\,\lVert[B, B']\rVert \le 8$.

3. $C$ is Hermitian. If $Cv = \lambda v$, $\lVert v\rVert = 1$, then $C^2v = \lambda^2v$, so $\lambda^2 \le \lVert C^2\rVert \le 8$ by (d).

4. By (d) again, $|\operatorname{tr}(\rho C)| \le \max|\lambda| \le 2\sqrt2$. $\blacksquare$

Classical: $[-2, 2]$. Quantum: $[-2\sqrt2, 2\sqrt2]$. The ends $\pm4$ are where a strategy that won always would sit.

Why it can beat 2 at all: if $[A, A'] = 0$ or $[B, B'] = 0$, then $C^2 = 4I$ and $|S| \le 2$. Quantum mechanics gains only through incompatible measurements — on both sides at once.

Full proof, every step

1. $[A, A'] = AA' - A'A$, so by (a) $\lVert[A, A']\rVert \le \lVert AA'\rVert + \lVert A'A\rVert \le 2\lVert A\rVert\lVert A'\rVert = 2$, using (b). 2. The identity $C^2 = 4I - [A, A']\otimes[B, B']$ from Worked: squaring the CHSH operator, then (a) for the difference and (c) for the tensor product: $\lVert C^2\rVert \le \lVert4I\rVert + \lVert[A, A']\rVert\lVert[B, B']\rVert \le 4 + 4$. 3. $C$ is a real combination of tensor products of Hermitian operators, so Hermitian (unit 03: $(P\otimes Q)^\dagger = P^\dagger\otimes Q^\dagger$), and has an orthonormal eigenbasis by unit 00. For an eigenvalue $\lambda$, $\lambda^2$ is an eigenvalue of the Hermitian $C^2$, so $\lambda^2 \le \lVert C^2\rVert$ by (d). 4. Fact (d) for $H = C$.

"Any dimension" is the point. Nothing used $\dim H_A = 2$: Alice and Bob may hold many qubits each, or any finite-dimensional systems, and the bound still holds. A quantum strategy cannot buy its way past $2\sqrt2$ with more entanglement. (Infinite dimensions: the same proof, with bounded operators; not needed in this course.)

General measurements, cited forward. Unit 21 introduces generalised measurements (POVMs) and proves Naimark's dilation theorem: every POVM is a projective measurement on a larger space. A two-outcome POVM on $H_A$ therefore becomes a $\pm1$-valued observable on $H_A\otimes K$ for an auxiliary space $K$ Alice holds, and the theorem above, valid in every dimension, applies unchanged. So Tsirelson's bound holds for every quantum strategy, not only projective ones.

When is 2 the bound? If $[A, A'] = 0$ then $C^2 = 4I$ and all eigenvalues of $C$ are $\pm2$, so $|S| \le 2$. That is the quantum shadow of the classical theorem: compatible observables can be measured together, and have joint values to put into section 2's pointwise argument.

the quantum value of the CHSH game

Over all quantum strategies — any shared state, any dimension, any two-outcome measurements (general ones via unit 21) —

proof

$\le$: $\Pr(\text{win}) = \tfrac12 + S/8$ for every strategy, and $S \le 2\sqrt2$ by Tsirelson. $\ \ge$: The game, played with a singlet reaches it. $\blacksquare$

Against the classical value $\tfrac34$: a gain of $0.1036$, about one game in ten.

In the lab, every winning pair of settings is $22.5^\circ$ apart, and the winning probability is Malus's law at that angle.

no-signalling is not the reason

Quantum correlations never signal (unit 04). Section 5 shows a no-signalling box that wins always — so the $0.8536$ ceiling comes from Hilbert space, not from relativity.

The proof, unpacked. A quantum strategy is: a state $\rho$ on $H_A\otimes H_B$ fixed in advance, and for each input a two-outcome measurement on one's own factor. Projective two-outcome measurements are exactly $\pm1$-valued observables (Observables with outcomes ±1, part (b)), so the strategy has a CHSH operator $C$ and $\Pr(\text{win}) = \tfrac12 + \operatorname{tr}(\rho C)/8$ by section 2's identity, which holds for every strategy. Tsirelson bounds $\operatorname{tr}(\rho C)$ by $2\sqrt2$. General measurements reduce to projective ones by Naimark's dilation (unit 21), as the previous notes said. Players may also share classical randomness on top: that is a mixture of quantum strategies, which averages their winning probabilities and so cannot beat the best one.

Loopholes, and the hypothesis no experiment removes

A loophole is a way an imperfect experiment fails to make the theorem's hypotheses hold, so that a local model could fake $|S| \gt 2$.

  • Locality loophole. If B's measurement lies in the light cone of A's setting choice, B's box may "know" $x$. Closed by space-like separation.
  • Detection loophole. If many runs give no click and only clicks are counted, a local model can decline to fire when asked a question it would answer badly. The fix assumed instead, fair sampling, is not testable. Closed by efficient detection — every run counted.
  • Memory. Boxes may remember earlier runs. Closed by a statistical analysis that allows it (next slides).

2015: Delft, Vienna and NIST closed locality and detection together.

The coins' pasts overlap with each other's and the source's. Free choice is the assumption that nothing there correlates the coins with $\lambda$.

free choice cannot be closed

Superdeterminism lives in the shared past. No experiment excludes it, because it is a hypothesis of the theorem rather than a flaw of an apparatus.

The detection loophole, concretely. Suppose the hidden variable tells each box, for each setting, either an answer or "do not click". If the analysis keeps only runs where both sides clicked, the box can arrange to drop exactly the runs on which its predetermined answers would lose the game, and the kept runs can show $|S|$ up to $4$. Closing it needs the fraction of runs with an outcome to be high: for CHSH with a maximally entangled state, detection efficiency above $2(\sqrt2 - 1) \approx 0.8284$ (A. Garg and N. D. Mermin, Phys. Rev. D 35, 3831 (1987), cited). Delft avoided the problem by counting every heralded trial, each of which gave an outcome on both sides.

Locality, concretely. If the events are not space-like separated, the theorem's locality hypothesis is not enforced by relativity, and a local model in which a slow signal carries $x$ to Bob would be allowed; Why the timing matters drew the arrangement that rules it out.

Memory. Delft's analysis allowed each trial's local model to depend on everything that had happened in earlier trials. That costs nothing, as the next slide and problem P11 show: given the past, each trial is still a local model, so it still wins with probability at most $\tfrac34$.

The figure. Past light cones point downward: everything that could have influenced an event. Any two events' past light cones overlap eventually, so a common cause for the two coins and the source can never be excluded by geometry alone.

Reading Delft's number

Two ways to say "how surprising".

A sigma count: $(2.42 - 2)/0.20 = 2.1$ standard deviations. A Gaussian tail beyond $2.1\sigma$ is $0.018$. But it assumes independent, identically distributed trials and a normal distribution — exactly what a local model with memory need not respect.

The game count: the game was won $k = 196$ times in $n = 245$ (fraction $0.800$). In any local model, even one remembering all past trials, each trial is won with probability $\le\tfrac34$ given the past. So

$$p \le \Pr\big(\mathrm{Bin}(245, \tfrac34) \ge 196\big) = 0.0391 .$$

That is Delft's $0.039$: a bound that assumes nothing about how the trials are distributed — only locality and free choice, trial by trial.

The best any local model can do, trial by trial. The shaded tail — 196 wins or more — has probability $0.039$.

what 245 trials can and cannot say

They reject local models at the $4\%$ level. They cannot pin $S$ down: $2.42 \pm 0.20$ is consistent with the ideal $2.83$ only at about $2\sigma$.

The numbers. $(2.42 - 2)/0.20 = 2.10$. A one-sided Gaussian tail beyond $2.1$ standard deviations is $0.0179$; Delft quotes $0.019$ for its "conventional" analysis, the difference coming from rounding $S$ and its error. The binomial tail $\sum_{j=196}^{245}\binom{245}j(\tfrac34)^j(\tfrac14)^{245-j} = 0.03908$, computed. Under $\mathrm{Bin}(245, \tfrac34)$ the mean is $183.75$ and the standard deviation $\sqrt{245\cdot\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac14} = 6.78$, so $196$ is $1.8$ of those standard deviations above the mean — and that is the honest "sigma count" for this data.

Why the bound is valid with memory. Condition on everything that happened before trial $i$. The boxes' behaviour on trial $i$ is then some local model — its hidden variable may include the whole history — and the inputs of trial $i$ are fresh, uniform and independent of that history (free choice). So the conditional probability of winning trial $i$ is at most $\tfrac34$, whatever happened before. A count of wins built from trials each won with conditional probability at most $q$ is never more likely to reach $k$ than a binomial with parameter $q$: problem P11 proves this by induction on $n$. Delft's published analysis also allowed for slight predictability of its random number generators, which moves the bound by less than the quoted digits.

Why $k/n$ is not exactly $\tfrac12 + S/8$. $S$ is computed from the four correlations separately, each over its own number of trials; the identity $\Pr(\text{win}) = \tfrac12 + S/8$ assumes each input pair occurs a quarter of the time. With random inputs the four counts were only roughly equal, so $196/245 = 0.800$ against $0.8025$.

What it cannot establish. A $p$-value of $0.039$ is the probability of data this extreme if local realism held; it is not the probability that local realism holds. With 245 trials the experiment cannot distinguish $S = 2.42$ from the ideal $2\sqrt2$ sharply, nor show that the source is close to a singlet. Its achievement was to close both loopholes at once; the photon experiments of the same year supplied the statistics.

06 · the PR box · example

Worked: a box that wins every time

worked example: the Popescu–Rohrlich box

A hypothetical device, a PR box, shared by Alice and Bob: inputs $x, y$, outputs $a, b$, with

$$P(a, b\mid x, y) = \begin{cases}\tfrac12 & a\oplus b = xy,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}.\end{cases}$$

1. A distribution. For each $(x, y)$, exactly two of the four $(a, b)$ satisfy $a\oplus b = xy$: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ ✓.

2. No signal. Alice's marginal: $P(a\mid x, y) = \sum_bP(a, b\mid x, y)$. For each $a$, exactly one $b$ fits, so $P(a\mid x, y) = \tfrac12$ — independent of $y$. Bob's likewise.

3. The correlations. On $00, 01, 10$ the outputs always agree: $E = +1$. On $11$ they always differ: $E = -1$.

$$S = 1 + 1 + 1 - (-1) = 4,$$ $$\Pr(\text{win}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac48 = 1 .$$

Popescu and Rohrlich, Found. Phys. 24, 379 (1994). No signal passes; the game is won with certainty.

so

No-signalling allows $S = 4$. What stops quantum mechanics at $2\sqrt2$ is not relativity: it is the structure of Hilbert space — Tsirelson's proof used $\otimes$, operator norms and $A^2 = I$, never no-signalling.

Each step. Step 1: for fixed $(x, y)$ the condition $a\oplus b = xy$ fixes $b$ given $a$, so there are two solutions, $(0, xy)$ and $(1, 1\oplus xy)$, each with probability $\tfrac12$. Step 2: the marginal sums over $b$, and only $b = a\oplus xy$ contributes, with $\tfrac12$. So Alice's output is a fair coin whatever $y$ is, and by the same argument Bob's whatever $x$ is. Bob cannot learn anything about $x$ from his side: the box is no-signalling. Step 3: agreement on $xy = 0$ means $\alpha\beta = 1$ with certainty, so $E = 1$; disagreement on $11$ gives $E = -1$. The CHSH sign pattern then gives $4$, the largest value any four correlations in $[-1, 1]$ could produce.

Not a local model, not quantum. It is not local: $|S| = 4 \gt 2$. It is not quantum: $4 \gt 2\sqrt2$. It is a perfectly consistent probability table that respects the no-signalling principle of Why the timing matters. S. Popescu and D. Rohrlich, "Quantum nonlocality as an axiom", Found. Phys. 24, 379 (1994), asked whether quantum mechanics might be characterised as the most nonlocal theory consistent with relativity; this box says no.

Why no one has built one. Quantum mechanics forbids it, by Tsirelson. That is all. Whether nature might contain PR-box correlations beyond quantum theory is an experimental question, and every experiment so far agrees with quantum mechanics.

Why $2\sqrt2$? Principles proposed to single out the quantum set from the outside: information causality (Pawłowski et al. 2009), macroscopic locality (Navascués and Wunderlich 2010), and that PR boxes would make every distributed computation trivial (van Dam 2005). Each rules out the PR box; none is known to give exactly the quantum set.

The three sets, precisely. A "box" is a table $P(a, b\mid x, y)$ for $a, b, x, y \in \{0, 1\}$: sixteen numbers, constrained to be distributions. No-signalling boxes have marginals independent of the other side's input; they form a polytope (an intersection of finitely many half-spaces, bounded) of dimension 8, whose corners are the sixteen deterministic local strategies and eight PR-type boxes. Local boxes are the mixtures of deterministic strategies: section 1's convex hull. Quantum boxes are those $\operatorname{tr}(\rho\,P_a^x\otimes Q_b^y)$: convex, but not a polytope — its boundary is curved, as the Tsirelson direction shows. Local $\subset$ quantum $\subset$ no-signalling, and both inclusions are strict, by the singlet and by the PR box.

The principles, cited. M. Pawłowski, T. Paterek, D. Kaszlikowski, V. Scarani, A. Winter and M. Żukowski, "Information causality as a physical principle", Nature 461, 1101 (2009): receiving $m$ classical bits should never give access to more than $m$ bits of a distant database; it implies $|S| \le 2\sqrt2$ for CHSH. M. Navascués and H. Wunderlich, "A glance beyond the quantum model", Proc. R. Soc. A 466, 881 (2010). W. van Dam (2005): with PR boxes, two parties could compute any Boolean function of their distributed inputs with one bit of communication. Whether some such principle recovers exactly the quantum set, for every Bell scenario, is open.

06 · the PR box

Real numbers are not enough: a network Bell test

Unit 00's debt. Unit 00 showed the minimal model of one qubit needs $\mathbb C$, but a real theory on a doubled space reproduces every single-system statistic. What rules it out needs several parties.

Renou et al., Nature 600, 625 (2021) — cited, not proved

Three parties in a chain, with two independent sources: one shares a pair between Alice and Bob, the other between Bob and Charlie. Bob makes an entangled measurement on his two qubits. A Bell-type score of the joint outcomes has a quantum value that no real-Hilbert-space quantum theory reaches, if independent sources give product states.

Tested in 2022 (Phys. Rev. Lett. 128, 040402 and 040403), one with photons, one with superconducting qubits: the real bound was exceeded.

The network. Its force comes from the sources' independence, which a single-source Bell test cannot use.

read precisely

The minimal model forces $\mathbb C$ (unit 00); the full theory needs this three-party experiment to force it. The reasoning is this unit's kind — bounds on correlations between distant parties — taken one step further.

What "real quantum theory" means here. The same postulates — states, projective measurements, Born rule — over real Hilbert spaces, with systems combined by the real tensor product. Unit 00's notes explained how such a theory survives every single-system test (Stueckelberg's doubling). Two parties are not enough either: any two-party Bell correlation of complex quantum theory can be reproduced by a real one, by a similar doubling applied to the shared state. Three parties with two independent sources break the symmetry, because the doubling trick would need the two sources to share a hidden correlation that their independence forbids.

Cited. M.-O. Renou, D. Trillo, M. Weilenmann, T. P. Le, A. Tavakoli, N. Gisin, A. Acín and M. Navascués, "Quantum theory based on real numbers can be experimentally falsified", Nature 600, 625 (2021). The two 2022 experiments: Phys. Rev. Lett. 128, 040402 and 040403. The quantum and real bounds for their score, and the proof that real models cannot reach the quantum value, are in the paper; this course states the result and does not prove it.

The hypothesis to watch. Like Bell's theorem, this one has hypotheses: that independent sources are described by product states, and the real tensor product rule. The experiment refutes real quantum theory with those rules, which is the version anyone had proposed.

The game, closed

The four numbers of the game. Delft's bar is its one-standard-deviation range, $0.7775$ to $0.8275$.

  • $\tfrac34$: the classical value. Sixteen strategies, an odd cycle, and $|S| \le 2$ — using realism with locality, and free choice.
  • $\cos^2\tfrac\pi8 \approx 0.8536$: the singlet at the four directions. Optimal: Tsirelson's $C^2 = 4I - [A, A']\otimes[B, B']$.
  • $0.8025$: Delft, loophole-free, rejecting local models with $p = 0.039$.
  • $1$: the PR box, no-signalling but not quantum.
which hypothesis each gap breaks

$\tfrac34 \to 0.8536$: any theory reproducing it gives up local realism or free choice. $0.8536 \to 1$: forbidden by quantum mechanics, allowed by relativity — why nature stops here is open.
