07

Teleportation and dense coding: the exchange rate between qubits, bits and ebits

One shared Bell pair and two classical bits move an unknown qubit; one Bell pair and one qubit move two bits. Neither trade can be improved, nothing is copied, nothing outruns the bits — and a measured fidelity above 2/3 proves the pair was used.

needs 04, 05~2 h slides + 4 h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 07

Unit 03 built the Bell states; unit 04 proved that half of one is the maximally mixed state and that nothing Alice does locally changes it; unit 05 proved that an unknown state can be neither copied nor read. This unit spends a Bell pair. Spent one way, it moves a qubit using only a phone line (teleportation); spent the other way, it doubles what a qubit can carry (dense coding). Both derivations are one identity each. The work is in what they do not do — clone, signal, or beat the exchange rates — and in the number, $\tfrac23$, that tells a real teleporter from a fake.

07 · overview

Moving a qubit that nobody can look at

the running example: teleporting one qubit

Alice is handed a qubit C in the state

$$\ket\psi = \cos\tfrac\theta2\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket1,$$

normalised, $\cos^2\tfrac\theta2 + \sin^2\tfrac\theta2 = 1$, with $\theta, \varphi$ unknown to her. She shares one Bell pair $\ket{\Phi^+}$ with Bob, and they have a phone line that carries bits. Can Bob end up holding $\ket\psi$?

Why it looks hopeless. Measuring C yields one outcome and destroys $\psi$ (unit 01). One copy never determines $\theta, \varphi$, and copies cannot be made (unit 05). Describing $\psi$ in bits would take infinitely many.

Yet two bits will do — with the pair. The unit is the identity behind that, what it forbids, and what it costs.

The problem. Qubit C stays in Alice's lab; only bits cross the phone line.

The plan. 1 the identity: $\ket\psi\ket{\Phi^+}$ rewritten · 2 the protocol: four branches, four corrections · 3 what does not happen: no learning, no cloning, no signal · 4 the reverse trade: dense coding, and why neither can be beaten · 5 benchmarks: $\tfrac23$, and two experiments.

A dotted underline is a glossary term (click, or G); a solid one like the identity is the running example (hover); N shows the notes.

Where the running example is. Section 1 rewrites $\ket\psi_C\ket{\Phi^+}_{AB}$ in Alice's Bell basis (Worked: the identity, term by term). Section 2 turns the rewriting into a protocol and traces all four of Alice's outcomes to Bob's corrected state (All four branches, on the running example). Section 3 shows Alice's outcomes are uniform whatever $\theta, \varphi$ are, that $\psi$ leaves Alice's lab, and that Bob's qubit is $I/2$ until the bits arrive. Section 4 runs the trade backwards and proves neither direction can be improved. Section 5 asks how good a teleporter must be to have used the pair at all — measuring $\psi$ in $z$ and phoning the result gets $\tfrac23$ on average — and reads two experiments against that line. The close settles the exchange rate.

Why "unknown" matters. If Alice knew $\theta$ and $\varphi$ she could phone them, to whatever precision Bob wants, and Bob could prepare the state himself. The problem is about a state handed over by someone else, or produced by a computation, which Alice cannot read without destroying (unit 05: one copy does not determine the state). That is exactly the situation inside a quantum computer moving data between modules, and in a quantum network.

Conventions. As fixed in AUTHORING and units 00–04: $\braket{u|v}$ conjugate-linear in the first slot; $\ket0 = (1, 0)$, $\ket1 = (0, 1)$; in $\ket{cab}$ the leftmost symbol is qubit 0; $X, Y, Z$ the Pauli matrices, $H = (X + Z)/\sqrt2$. The three qubits are C (qubit 0), A (qubit 1), B (qubit 2). Global phases are dropped only with the words "up to global phase".

07 · the identity

Three qubits, three resources

the resources, counted
  • 1 qubit: one two-level system delivered intact, in whatever state it was;
  • 1 cbit: one classical bit delivered — a phone call carrying 0 or 1;
  • 1 ebit: one copy of $\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})$, one half in each lab, prepared in advance.

The cast. C is Alice's input (qubit 0); A and B are the halves of the ebit, Alice's (qubit 1) and Bob's (qubit 2). The start is

$$\ket\psi_C\otimes\ket{\Phi^+}_{AB}, \qquad \text{norm } 1\cdot1 = 1 .$$

Every operation below acts on C and A only, or on B only. The labs never exchange a qubit.

the Bell basis, with two-bit labels

With $\sigma_{jk} := X^kZ^j$, that is $\sigma_{00} = I$, $\sigma_{01} = X$, $\sigma_{10} = Z$, $\sigma_{11} = XZ$,

$$\begin{aligned}\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}} &:= (I\otimes\sigma_{jk})\ket{\Phi^+}\\ &= \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{0k} + (-1)^j\ket{1\bar k}\big).\end{aligned}$$

Unit 03's four Bell states, labelled by the Pauli that makes each from $\ket{\Phi^+}$. $\bar k = 1 - k$.

The labels, checked. Unit 03 (The four Bell states) computed $(I\otimes X)\ket{\Phi^+} = \ket{\Psi^+}$, $(I\otimes Z)\ket{\Phi^+} = \ket{\Phi^-}$, $(I\otimes XZ)\ket{\Phi^+} = \ket{\Psi^-}$. The closed form: $\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket0 + \ket1\ket1)$, and $X^kZ^j$ sends $\ket0 \mapsto X^k\ket0 = \ket k$ and $\ket1 \mapsto (-1)^jX^k\ket1 = (-1)^j\ket{\bar k}$. So $(I\otimes X^kZ^j)\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{0k} + (-1)^j\ket{1\bar k})$. For $jk = 11$: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} - \ket{10}) = \ket{\Psi^-}$ ✓. They are orthonormal (unit 03), so the $\mathcal B_{jk}$ are a basis of $\mathbb C^2\otimes\mathbb C^2$.

The four $\sigma_{jk}$ are unitary. Each is a product of unitaries. In particular $(XZ)^\dagger = Z^\dagger X^\dagger = ZX$, and $XZ\,ZX = XZ^2X = X^2 = I$. Unit 02's algebra gives $XZ = -iY$, so $\sigma_{11}$ is $Y$ up to global phase — the deck keeps $XZ$ because it is what the identity produces.

Why "ebit". An ebit is a unit of entanglement in the way a bit is a unit of information: one maximally entangled pair of qubits. Section 4 measures what one is worth. The convention that labels $\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}$ by sign bit $j$ and parity bit $k$ is the one Nielsen and Chuang use for their $\ket{\beta_{jk}}$; this deck writes $\mathcal B$ because $\beta$ is busy as an amplitude.

Resources as physical things. A qubit "delivered" is, in the experiments of section 5, a photon sent down a fibre or through the air; a cbit is an ordinary electrical or radio signal; an ebit is a photon pair from the crystal of unit 03, one photon sent to each lab and stored or used on arrival. Producing and distributing ebits is the hard part in practice; section 5 returns to it.

07 · the identity

The computational basis, written in the Bell basis

lemma: inverting the Bell basis $$\begin{aligned}\ket{00} &= \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{\Phi^+} + \ket{\Phi^-}\big),\\ \ket{11} &= \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{\Phi^+} - \ket{\Phi^-}\big),\\ \ket{01} &= \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{\Psi^+} + \ket{\Psi^-}\big),\\ \ket{10} &= \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{\Psi^+} - \ket{\Psi^-}\big).\end{aligned}$$
proof

$\ket{\Phi^+} + \ket{\Phi^-} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(2\ket{00}\big) = \sqrt2\,\ket{00}$; divide by $\sqrt2$. The other three are the same, with a difference for $\ket{11}$ and $\ket{10}$. $\blacksquare$

Normalised: each right side has squared norm $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$, since the Bell states are orthonormal ✓.

The Bell basis is the computational basis turned by $45^\circ$ in each of two real planes — so turning back is the same picture read the other way.

The $\Phi$'s lie in $\operatorname{span}\{\ket{00}, \ket{11}\}$ and the $\Psi$'s in $\operatorname{span}\{\ket{01}, \ket{10}\}$ — unit 03's diagonal and off-diagonal coefficient matrices. The lemma inverts that, plane by plane.

Full proof, all four. $\ket{\Phi^+} - \ket{\Phi^-} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11} - \ket{00} + \ket{11}) = \sqrt2\ket{11}$. $\ket{\Psi^+} + \ket{\Psi^-} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(2\ket{01}) = \sqrt2\ket{01}$, and $\ket{\Psi^+} - \ket{\Psi^-} = \sqrt2\ket{10}$.

Another way to see it. The matrix whose columns are the Bell states in the order $\Phi^+, \Psi^+, \Phi^-, \Psi^-$ is real and orthogonal, so its inverse is its transpose: the coefficient of $\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}$ in $\ket{ab}$ is $\braket{\mathcal B_{jk}|ab}$, a real number read off the Bell state's own coefficients. For instance $\braket{\Psi^-|10} = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, the minus sign in the lemma.

Why bother. The identity of the next slide is the state $\ket\psi_C\ket{\Phi^+}_{AB}$, expanded in the computational basis of all three qubits and then rewritten with Alice's pair CA in the Bell basis. This lemma is the only tool the rewriting needs.

Worked: the identity, term by term

rewrite $\ket\psi_C\ket{\Phi^+}_{AB}$ with CA in the Bell basis, $\ \ket\psi = \alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1$

1. Expand (unit 03's bilinearity):

$$\begin{aligned}\ket\psi\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(&\alpha\ket{000} + \alpha\ket{011}\\ +\,&\beta\ket{100} + \beta\ket{111}\big).\end{aligned}$$

2. Split each ket as $\ket{ca}_{CA}\ket b_B$: $\ \ket{000} = \ket{00}\ket0$, $\ket{011} = \ket{01}\ket1$, $\ket{100} = \ket{10}\ket0$, $\ket{111} = \ket{11}\ket1$.

3. Substitute the lemma for each CA ket:

$$\begin{aligned}\alpha\ket{00}\ket0 &= \tfrac{\alpha}{\sqrt2}\big(\Phi^+ + \Phi^-\big)\ket0\\ \alpha\ket{01}\ket1 &= \tfrac{\alpha}{\sqrt2}\big(\Psi^+ + \Psi^-\big)\ket1\\ \beta\ket{10}\ket0 &= \tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt2}\big(\Psi^+ - \Psi^-\big)\ket0\\ \beta\ket{11}\ket1 &= \tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt2}\big(\Phi^+ - \Phi^-\big)\ket1\end{aligned}$$

4. Collect by Bell state; the prefactor is $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2} = \tfrac12$:

$$\begin{aligned}\ket\psi\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac12\Big[&\ket{\Phi^+}\big(\alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1\big)\\ +\,&\ket{\Psi^+}\big(\beta\ket0 + \alpha\ket1\big)\\ +\,&\ket{\Phi^-}\big(\alpha\ket0 - \beta\ket1\big)\\ +\,&\ket{\Psi^-}\big(-\beta\ket0 + \alpha\ket1\big)\Big].\end{aligned}$$

5. Recognise Bob's four vectors: $\ \alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1 = \psi$; $\ \beta\ket0 + \alpha\ket1 = X\psi$; $\ \alpha\ket0 - \beta\ket1 = Z\psi$; $\ -\beta\ket0 + \alpha\ket1 = X(\alpha\ket0 - \beta\ket1) = XZ\psi$.

6. Check at $\psi = \ket0$ ($\alpha = 1$, $\beta = 0$): the bracket is $(\Phi^+ + \Phi^-)\ket0 + (\Psi^+ + \Psi^-)\ket1 = \sqrt2(\ket{00}\ket0 + \ket{01}\ket1)$, so the right side is $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{000} + \ket{011}) = \ket0\ket{\Phi^+}$ ✓.

Step 3, the coefficients. Collecting the $\ket{\Phi^+}$ terms: from line 1, $\tfrac{\alpha}{\sqrt2}\ket0$; from line 4, $\tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt2}\ket1$. The $\ket{\Phi^-}$ terms: $\tfrac{\alpha}{\sqrt2}\ket0$ from line 1, $-\tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt2}\ket1$ from line 4. The $\ket{\Psi^+}$ terms: $\tfrac{\alpha}{\sqrt2}\ket1$ (line 2), $\tfrac{\beta}{\sqrt2}\ket0$ (line 3). The $\ket{\Psi^-}$ terms: $\tfrac\alpha{\sqrt2}\ket1$ (line 2), $-\tfrac\beta{\sqrt2}\ket0$ (line 3). With the overall $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ from step 1 every coefficient carries $\tfrac12$. Every one of the eight terms of step 3 has been used once.

Step 5, the Paulis. $X$ swaps the two amplitudes; $Z$ negates the amplitude of $\ket1$. So $Z\psi = \alpha\ket0 - \beta\ket1$, and applying $X$ to that swaps the amplitudes: $XZ\psi = -\beta\ket0 + \alpha\ket1$. The order matters: $ZX\psi = Z(\beta\ket0 + \alpha\ket1) = \beta\ket0 - \alpha\ket1 = -XZ\psi$. The two differ by a global phase, so as states they agree; as vectors in the identity, $XZ$ is the one that appears.

On the running example. $\alpha = \cos\tfrac\theta2$, $\beta = e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2$. Nothing in the computation used their values, which is the point: the identity holds for every $\psi$, and is linear in it.

History. Bennett, Brassard, Crépeau, Jozsa, Peres and Wootters, Phys. Rev. Lett. 70, 1895 (1993), wrote exactly this rewriting (with the singlet as the shared pair) and named the protocol it suggests "teleportation", with the remark that the name is borrowed from science fiction and that nothing material is transported.

07 · the identity

The teleportation identity

the teleportation identity

For every $\ket\psi\in\mathbb C^2$,

$$\ket\psi_C\ket{\Phi^+}_{AB} = \tfrac12\sum_{jk}\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}_{CA}\otimes\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi_B,$$

with $\sigma_{jk} = X^kZ^j$. Proof: Worked: the identity, term by term, read with the labels $\mathcal B_{00} = \Phi^+$, $\mathcal B_{01} = \Psi^+$, $\mathcal B_{10} = \Phi^-$, $\mathcal B_{11} = \Psi^-$. $\blacksquare$

checks

Norm. The four terms are orthogonal (orthogonal Bell states on CA), each of squared norm $\tfrac14\lVert\sigma_{jk}\psi\rVert^2 = \tfrac14$ since $\sigma_{jk}$ is unitary: total $4\cdot\tfrac14 = 1$, as on the left ✓.

Basis input $\psi = \ket0$: checked in step 6 of the worked example ✓.

One vector, written as four orthogonal pieces. In each, Bob's factor is $\psi$ with a known Pauli applied.

nothing has happened yet

This is a rewriting of the starting state. Bob's qubit is still half of $\ket{\Phi^+}$, in state $I/2$. What makes it useful is that Alice can measure in the basis that appears on the right.

Why the right side is an expansion in a basis. The vectors $\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}\otimes\ket b$, $j, k, b\in\{0,1\}$, are eight orthonormal vectors of $\mathbb C^8$ (tensor products of orthonormal bases, unit 03), so every three-qubit vector has a unique expansion in them. Grouping by $jk$, the coefficient of $\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}$ is a vector of B; the identity says it is $\tfrac12\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$.

The norm check in full. $\lVert\sum_{jk}\tfrac12\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}\otimes v_{jk}\rVert^2 = \sum_{jk,j'k'}\tfrac14\braket{\mathcal B_{jk}|\mathcal B_{j'k'}}\braket{v_{jk}|v_{j'k'}} = \tfrac14\sum_{jk}\lVert v_{jk}\rVert^2$, and $\lVert\sigma_{jk}\psi\rVert = \lVert\psi\rVert = 1$.

Linearity. Both sides are linear in $\ket\psi$. So the identity also holds when C is entangled with a fourth system R: apply it to each term of $\sum_i\ket{r_i}_R\ket{\psi_i}_C$. Problem P4 uses this to show teleportation moves entanglement, and P5 to derive entanglement swapping.

A common misreading. "Bob's qubit is already in one of four states" is false: before Alice measures, the three qubits are in one pure state, $\ket\psi\ket{\Phi^+}$, and Bob's reduced state is $\operatorname{tr}_A\ket{\Phi^+}\!\bra{\Phi^+} = I/2$ (unit 04, Worked: every Bell state, traced). The four pieces become alternatives only once Alice's measurement selects one.

Reading a circuit diagram

the notation (a picture of a product of operators)
  • Each horizontal wire is a qubit; qubit 0 on top; time runs left to right.
  • A boxed letter on a wire is that single-qubit unitary on that qubit, $I$ on the others.
  • ● joined to ⊕ is $\mathrm{CNOT}$, control at ●: $\ \mathrm{CNOT}_{01} = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes X$, so $\ket{c, t}\mapsto\ket{c, t\oplus c}$ (unit 03, problem P2). Unitary: it permutes the basis.
  • A meter is a measurement in the $z$ basis; a double line carries its classical outcome.
  • A gate fed by a double line is applied if the bit is 1.
order

Gates drawn $G_1$ then $G_2$ act as the operator $G_2G_1$: the diagram reads left to right, the product right to left. Unit 10 makes circuits a formal model of computation; here a diagram is only a picture.

Top: $\ket{\Phi^+}$ made from $\ket{00}$ by $H$ then $\mathrm{CNOT}$. Bottom: a meter, and a classically controlled gate.

Check the top line: $(H\otimes I)\ket{00} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{10})$; $\mathrm{CNOT}$ flips the second qubit in the second term: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11}) = \ket{\Phi^+}$ ✓.

CNOT, unitary. $\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}$ sends $\ket{00}\mapsto\ket{00}$, $\ket{01}\mapsto\ket{01}$, $\ket{10}\mapsto\ket{11}$, $\ket{11}\mapsto\ket{10}$: a permutation of the orthonormal basis, so unitary; it is Hermitian too, and squares to $I$. In blocks, $\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&X\end{pmatrix}$, which is how unit 03's problem P2 wrote it. On three qubits, $\mathrm{CNOT}$ between C and A means $\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\otimes I_B$.

Meters and the measurement postulate. A meter on qubit $q$ is the projective measurement $\{\ket0\!\bra0_q, \ket1\!\bra1_q\}$, each tensored with $I$ on the other qubits (unit 03's rule for measuring one subsystem). Outcome $b$ has probability $\lVert(\ket b\!\bra b_q\otimes I)v\rVert^2$, and the state becomes the normalised projection (unit 01). Two meters on two qubits commute, so the order in which they are drawn does not matter.

Classically controlled gates. "Apply $X$ if the bit is 1" is not an operator on a fixed space: it is a different operator in each branch of the measurement. A diagram with double lines therefore denotes a family of products, one per outcome. Unit 10 replaces it by a single unitary (the "deferred measurement principle"); nothing here needs that.

Why bother with pictures. The teleportation protocol is three operators, a measurement and a branch-dependent correction. Written as a formula it hides which lab each operation happens in; drawn, the labs are the wires.

The Bell measurement, rotated to the computational basis

a Bell measurement

The projective measurement $\{\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}\!\bra{\mathcal B_{jk}}\}_{jk}$, outcome the two bits $jk$ — four orthogonal projectors summing to $I$, as the Bell states are an orthonormal basis.

lemma

$(H\otimes I)\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}} = \ket{jk}$. So a CNOT, then $H$ on the first qubit, then a meter on each qubit, is a Bell measurement with outcome $jk$.

proof: run it backwards

$(H\otimes I)\ket{jk} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket0 + (-1)^j\ket1\big)\ket k$; then $\mathrm{CNOT}$ flips $k$ in the second term, giving

$$\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{0k} + (-1)^j\ket{1\bar k}\big) = \ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}.$$

Both factors square to $I$, so $(H\otimes I)\mathrm{CNOT}$ undoes this. $\blacksquare$

A Bell measurement built from one two-qubit gate, one one-qubit gate and two meters.

Why it measures the Bell basis. With $U = (H\otimes I)\mathrm{CNOT}$: $\ |\braket{jk|Uv}|^2 = |\braket{U^\dagger jk|v}|^2 = |\braket{\mathcal B_{jk}|v}|^2$, the Born rule for the Bell basis.

Check $jk = 11$: $\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{\Psi^-} = \ket-\ket1$, then $H\ket- = \ket1$: outcome $11$ ✓.

All four, forwards. $\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{\Phi^\pm} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} \pm \ket{10}) = \ket\pm\ket0$, and $H\ket\pm = \ket0, \ket1$: outcomes $00$ and $10$. $\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{\Psi^\pm} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} \pm \ket{11}) = \ket\pm\ket1$: outcomes $01$ and $11$. So the CNOT reads the parity $k$ into the second qubit and leaves the sign on the first as $\ket\pm$; the Hadamard turns the sign into a bit (unit 02: $H\ket+ = \ket0$, $H\ket- = \ket1$).

The post-measurement state. After the meters, Alice's two qubits are in $\ket{jk}$ — not in $\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}$, because the rotation was applied first. Either way they are in a state fixed by the outcome alone. Section 3 uses this.

Full proof of the Born-rule step. The measurement is the projectors $\ket{jk}\!\bra{jk}$ applied to $Uv$. Then $\Pr(jk) = \lVert\ket{jk}\!\bra{jk}Uv\rVert^2 = |\braket{jk|Uv}|^2$, and $\braket{jk|Uv} = \braket{U^\dagger jk|v}$ by the definition of the adjoint (unit 00). $U^\dagger = \mathrm{CNOT}^\dagger(H\otimes I)^\dagger = \mathrm{CNOT}(H\otimes I)$, which sends $\ket{jk}$ to $\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}$ by the lemma. The same argument with $U$ replaced by $U\otimes I_B$ covers the case where the pair is part of a larger system — the teleportation case.

In the laboratory. For photons there is no deterministic CNOT; section 5's experiments used interference at a beam splitter instead, and could tell apart only some of the four Bell states.

07 · the protocol

The teleportation circuit

Teleportation. Everything above the dashed line happens in Alice's lab, below it in Bob's; the double lines are the phone call.

  1. Start: $\ket\psi_C\ket{\Phi^+}_{AB} = \tfrac12\sum\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$ (the identity).
  2. CNOT from C to A, and
  3. $H$ on C: by the lemma the state is now $\tfrac12\sum_{jk}\ket{jk}_{CA}\otimes\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi_B$.
  4. Alice reads $jk$ — probability $\tfrac14$ each — and phones the two bits.
  5. Bob applies $\sigma_{jk}^{-1} = Z^jX^k$: first $X$ if $k = 1$, then $Z$ if $j = 1$. He holds $Z^jX^kX^kZ^j\ket\psi = \ket\psi$.
the teleportation protocol

Consumes one ebit and two cbits; delivers $\ket\psi$ exactly, in every branch, for every $\psi$.

Step 3 in full. The operator applied in steps 2–3 is $U\otimes I_B$ with $U = (H\otimes I)\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}$. By linearity and the identity, $(U\otimes I)\ket\psi\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac12\sum_{jk}U\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}\otimes\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi = \tfrac12\sum_{jk}\ket{jk}\otimes\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$.

Step 4 in full. Measuring C and A projects with $\ket{jk}\!\bra{jk}\otimes I$: the unnormalised branch is $\tfrac12\ket{jk}\otimes\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$, of squared norm $\tfrac14$, and the normalised post-measurement state is $\ket{jk}\otimes\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$ (unit 01's rule, unit 03's form for a subsystem). Probabilities $4\cdot\tfrac14 = 1$ ✓.

Step 5: the inverse. $(X^kZ^j)^{-1} = Z^{-j}X^{-k} = Z^jX^k$, since $X^2 = Z^2 = I$. As gates in time order: $X^k$ first, then $Z^j$, which is the order drawn. Applying them in the other order, $X^kZ^j$, gives $X^kZ^jX^kZ^j\ket\psi$; for $jk = 11$ that is $XZXZ\ket\psi = -\ket\psi$ — the same state up to global phase. So the order of Bob's two corrections does not matter physically, only for the vector.

Where things happen. Steps 2–4 are in Alice's lab and touch only C and A; step 5 is in Bob's and touches only B; the two bits travel by an ordinary channel. No qubit crosses between the labs after the ebit was shared.

Worked: the branch where Alice reads 11

outcome $jk = 11$, the singlet $\ket{\Psi^-}$, traced to Bob's corrected qubit

1. The branch. From the identity, the $\mathcal B_{11} = \Psi^-$ piece is $\tfrac12\ket{\Psi^-}_{CA}\otimes XZ\ket\psi_B$. After CNOT and $H$ it is $\tfrac12\ket{11}\otimes XZ\ket\psi$.

2. The probability is its squared norm, $\tfrac14\lVert XZ\psi\rVert^2 = \tfrac14$; Alice reads $11$ and phones it.

3. Bob holds, for $\psi = \alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1$,

$$XZ\ket\psi = X(\alpha\ket0 - \beta\ket1) = -\beta\ket0 + \alpha\ket1 .$$

4. Bob corrects with $Z^1X^1$: first $X$, then $Z$.

$$\begin{aligned}X(-\beta\ket0 + \alpha\ket1) &= \alpha\ket0 - \beta\ket1,\\ Z(\alpha\ket0 - \beta\ket1) &= \alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1 = \ket\psi \ \checkmark\end{aligned}$$

5. On the running example, $\alpha = \cos\tfrac\theta2$, $\beta = e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2$: before the correction Bob holds $-e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket0 + \cos\tfrac\theta2\ket1$, normalised ($\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$); after it, $\ket\psi$ itself.

6. Check on $\psi = \ket+$ ($\theta = \tfrac\pi2$, $\varphi = 0$, $\alpha = \beta = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$):

$$XZ\ket+ = X\ket- = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-\ket0 + \ket1) = -\ket- ,$$

which is the state $\ket-$ up to global phase — the wrong state, orthogonal to $\ket+$. The correction: $X(-\ket-) = \ket-$ (since $X\ket- = -\ket-$), then $Z\ket- = \ket+$ ✓.

7. Alice is left with $\ket{11}$, the same for every $\psi$.

Step 1 from the circuit, not the identity. One can also push $\ket\psi\ket{\Phi^+}$ through the gates directly. $\mathrm{CNOT}_{CA}$: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\alpha\ket0(\ket{00} + \ket{11}) + \beta\ket1(\ket{10} + \ket{01})\big)$. Then $H$ on C: $\tfrac12\big(\alpha(\ket0 + \ket1)(\ket{00} + \ket{11}) + \beta(\ket0 - \ket1)(\ket{10} + \ket{01})\big)$. The coefficient of $\ket{11}_{CA}$ collects $\alpha\ket1_B$ from the first product and $-\beta\ket0_B$ from the second: $\tfrac12(-\beta\ket0 + \alpha\ket1)$, as in step 3 ✓.

Why the uncorrected state is useless here. For $\psi = \ket+$, Bob's raw qubit is $\ket-$, which a measurement in the $x$ basis tells apart from $\ket+$ with certainty. 07 · what does not happen

Alice learns nothing about $\psi$

theorem: uniform outcomes

For every input $\ket\psi$, Alice's four outcomes $jk$ each have probability exactly $\tfrac14$, and afterwards her qubits C and A are in $\ket{jk}$.

proof

By the identity, after CNOT and $H$ the state is $\tfrac12\sum\ket{jk}\otimes\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$. Outcome $jk$ has probability $\big\lVert\tfrac12\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi\big\rVert^2 = \tfrac14$, because $\sigma_{jk}$ is unitary. The projected state is $\ket{jk}\otimes\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$. $\blacksquare$

Sum: $4\cdot\tfrac14 = 1$ ✓. Check at $\psi = \ket0$: the right side of the identity has pieces $\tfrac12\Phi^+\ket0$, $\tfrac12\Psi^+\ket1$, $\tfrac12\Phi^-\ket0$, $\tfrac12\Psi^-\ket1$ — each $\tfrac14$ ✓.

The distribution of the two bits does not depend on $\psi$ at all.

so

The bits are independent of $\psi$. Anyone tapping the phone line sees two fair coins. And Alice's own qubits end in $\ket{jk}$, which records nothing about $\theta, \varphi$.

Mixed inputs. If C is in a density operator $\rho = \sum_ip_i\ket{\psi_i}\!\bra{\psi_i}$, the outcome probabilities average those of the $\psi_i$: $\sum_ip_i\cdot\tfrac14 = \tfrac14$. So the bits are uniform for every input state whatsoever.

Why "learns nothing" is exact. The joint distribution of $(\psi, jk)$, for any prior over inputs, factorises: $\Pr(jk \mid \psi) = \tfrac14$ for every $\psi$, so the posterior over $\psi$ given $jk$ equals the prior. No estimate of $\theta$ or $\varphi$ can be formed from $jk$ that could not be formed without it.

Why it must be so. Problem P3 shows the converse: if the outcome distribution depended on $\psi$, Bob's state before the bits would depend on $\psi$ too, and Alice could signal by choosing $\psi$. So uniformity is not luck; the no-signalling theorem demands it.

A consequence for security. The two bits can be sent in the clear. An eavesdropper who copies them learns nothing about $\psi$ and, without Bob's half of the pair, cannot reconstruct it either — which is why teleportation reappears as a primitive in quantum networks.

07 · what does not happen

The original is destroyed, so no-cloning survives

The state has moved; the systems have not. Qubit C is still in Alice's lab, in a state that says nothing about $\psi$.

what is left behind

After the protocol the three qubits are in $\ket{jk}_{CA}\otimes\ket\psi_B$ — one copy of $\psi$, at Bob's, and a product with Alice's record, which is the same for every $\psi$.

why it must be so: unit 05

The no-cloning theorem: no process turns $\ket\psi$ into $\ket\psi\ket\psi$ for two non-orthogonal states. If C still held $\psi$ while B received it, the protocol would be a cloner. It is not, and the theorem says no improvement of it could be.

"Teleportation" is the name for this: a state disappears in one place and appears in another, while the matter stays put and a classical message passes between.

"Destroyed" means "no longer in C". Alice's measurement projects C and A onto $\ket{jk}$. Whatever correlations $\psi$ had with the rest of the world are now carried by B (problem P4), and C carries none. Nothing is annihilated: qubit C is still there, ready to be reused.

A teleporter that kept the original would clone — the argument. Suppose some protocol left C in $\ket\psi$ and B in $\ket\psi$ for every $\psi$. Then for each branch its action on $\psi$ would be a linear map (a product of unitaries and projectors) sending $\ket\psi\otimes(\text{the rest})$ to $\ket\psi\ket\psi\otimes(\text{junk})$. Unit 05's linearity proof applies branch by branch: from $\ket0$ and $\ket1$ it forces the output on $\ket+$ to be a sum $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00}\ket{g_0} + \ket{11}\ket{g_1})$, which is never $\ket{++}\otimes\ket g$, since the coefficient of $\ket{01}$ is zero. Contradiction.

The word. Bennett and co-authors' 1993 abstract describes the original as being destroyed and the state as "disassembled into, then later reconstructed from, purely classical information and purely nonclassical Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen correlations". Here EPR correlations means the ebit.

Before the bits arrive, Bob holds $I/2$

theorem

After Alice's measurement, and before Bob learns $jk$, Bob's qubit is in

$$\rho_B = \tfrac14\sum_{jk}\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\sigma_{jk}^\dagger = \frac I2$$

for every $\psi$.

proof 1: no-signalling (unit 04)

At the start $\rho_B = \operatorname{tr}_{CA}\big(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\otimes\ket{\Phi^+}\!\bra{\Phi^+}\big) = \operatorname{tr}_A\ket{\Phi^+}\!\bra{\Phi^+} = I/2$. Alice then applies unitaries to C, A and measures them, outcome not told to Bob: by the no-signalling theorem, $\rho_B$ does not change. $\blacksquare$

proof 2: the branches, averaged

Not knowing $jk$, Bob holds $\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$ with probability $\tfrac14$ each: the ensemble's operator is the sum shown (unit 04). For any $2\times2$ matrix $M = m_0I + \mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$,

$$\tfrac14\sum_{jk}\sigma_{jk}M\sigma_{jk}^\dagger = m_0 I = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(M)\,I,$$

because each of $\sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$ is kept by two of $I, X, Z, XZ$ and negated by the other two. With $M = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$, $\operatorname{tr}M = 1$. $\blacksquare$

the Pauli twirl

$M \mapsto \tfrac14\sum_{jk}\sigma_{jk}M\sigma_{jk}^\dagger$: averaging over the four Paulis sends every state to $I/2$.

Full proof of the twirl

Unit 02: $I, \sigma_x, \sigma_y, \sigma_z$ are a basis of all $2\times2$ matrices, with $M = m_0I + \mathbf m\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ and $m_0 = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M$ (trace orthogonality). Conjugation is linear, so it suffices to twirl each basis element. $I$ is fixed by all four. For $\sigma_x$: $I\sigma_xI = \sigma_x$, $X\sigma_xX = \sigma_x$, $Z\sigma_xZ = -\sigma_x$, $(XZ)\sigma_x(XZ)^\dagger = -\sigma_x$; sum $0$. For $\sigma_y$: $+, -, -, +$; sum $0$. For $\sigma_z$: $+, -, +, -$; sum $0$. (Each sign is $+$ when the Pauli commutes with $\sigma_a$ and $-$ when it anticommutes, from unit 02's algebra.) So the twirl of $M$ is $m_0I$.

Proof 1, the step "unchanged". Steps 2–4 of the protocol are a unitary $U\otimes I_B$ on CA, then a projective measurement $\{\ket{jk}\!\bra{jk}\otimes I_B\}$ whose outcome Bob does not know. Unit 04's no-signalling theorem, parts (a) and (b), with Alice's system taken to be CA: neither changes $\operatorname{tr}_{CA}$. The first equality uses $\operatorname{tr}_{CA}(\sigma\otimes\tau) = \operatorname{tr}(\sigma)\tau$ for a product across the cut C | AB, then $\operatorname{tr}_A$ of $\ket{\Phi^+}\!\bra{\Phi^+}$, which unit 04 computed.

Two proofs, one fact. Proof 1 says the answer must be $I/2$ without computing anything; proof 2 computes it and gets $I/2$. That they agree is a consistency check on the identity: if the identity had produced, say, only three of the four Paulis, proof 2 would give a $\psi$-dependent answer and contradict proof 1.

Nothing outruns the phone call

The worry. The moment Alice reads $jk$, Bob's qubit is $\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$ — "already, at any distance". Could Bob exploit it?

no: until the bits arrive

Every statistic Bob can measure is $\operatorname{tr}(Q\rho_B)$ with $\rho_B = I/2$ — the same whatever $\psi$ Alice fed in, and the same as before she did anything. Alice's choice of $\psi$ is invisible to Bob until the phone call lands.

guessing does not help

If Bob guesses $jk$ at random and corrects, he applies a random Pauli to a qubit in state $I/2$; it stays $I/2$, and his fidelity with $\psi$ is $\tfrac12$ — the same as doing nothing (problem P9).

Relativity's rule — no signal faster than light — is therefore safe: the bits are an ordinary signal, and only with them does Bob's qubit become $\psi$. Teleportation is no faster than the phone line.

Time runs down. Bob's qubit is $I/2$ until the bits arrive; then one Pauli makes it $\psi$.

Read precisely, as unit 04 asked. "Bob's qubit is $\sigma_{jk}\ket\psi$ the moment Alice reads $jk$" is a statement about a story — the ensemble $\{\tfrac14, \sigma_{jk}\ket\psi\}$, which is how Alice, knowing $jk$, would describe Bob's qubit. The state Bob's qubit is in, for Bob, is the operator $\rho_B = I/2$, and it is the same operator for every $\psi$. Unit 04's HJW theorem (The Schrödinger–HJW theorem: all the stories of one state) says that the ensemble $\{\tfrac14, \sigma_{jk}\psi\}$ and the ensemble $\{\tfrac12, \ket0;\ \tfrac12, \ket1\}$ are two stories of the same operator, and no measurement tells them apart.

The guess, in full. Bob draws $j'k'$ uniformly and applies $Z^{j'}X^{k'}$ to a qubit whose state, to him, is $I/2$. Any unitary fixes $I/2$: $U\tfrac I2U^\dagger = \tfrac I2$. So his output is $I/2$, with fidelity $\braket{\psi|\tfrac I2|\psi} = \tfrac12$ for every $\psi$. He is right a quarter of the time and holds $\psi$ then; the other three-quarters he holds $\psi$ moved by a half-turn — and the average is exactly the same $I/2$.

Relativity. The course uses one fact from special relativity, already met in unit 04: no signal, of any kind, travels faster than light in vacuum. The bits are carried by light or radio at best, so Bob's correction happens at least $d/c$ after Alice's measurement, where $d$ is the distance between the labs.

Dense coding: two bits in one qubit

dense coding (Bennett and Wiesner, 1992)

Alice (qubit 0) and Bob (qubit 1) share $\ket{\Phi^+}$. To send the bits $jk$:

  1. Alice applies $Z^jX^k$ to her qubit — first $X$ if $k = 1$, then $Z$ if $j = 1$;
  2. she sends her qubit to Bob: one qubit;
  3. Bob makes a Bell measurement on both (CNOT, then $H$, then meters) and reads $jk$.
why it works

$(Z^jX^k\otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+} = \ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}$ exactly, so Bob's measurement returns $jk$ with probability $1$.

proof: the transpose trick

For any $M$, $(M\otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_{a,b}M_{ba}\ket b\ket a = (I\otimes M^{\mathsf T})\ket{\Phi^+}$. And $(Z^jX^k)^{\mathsf T} = X^kZ^j = \sigma_{jk}$, as $X, Z$ are symmetric. $\blacksquare$

Alice must apply $\sigma_{jk}^{\mathsf T}$, not $\sigma_{jk}$: for $jk = 11$, $(XZ\otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+} = -\ket{\Psi^-}$, the same state only up to global phase.

Full proof of the transpose trick

$\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_a\ket a\ket a$. Then $(M\otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_aM\ket a\otimes\ket a = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_{a,b}M_{ba}\ket b\ket a$, using $M\ket a = \sum_bM_{ba}\ket b$. Similarly $(I\otimes N)\ket{\Phi^+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_{a,b}N_{ba}\ket a\ket b$; renaming $a \leftrightarrow b$, this is $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_{a,b}N_{ab}\ket b\ket a$, which equals the first when $N_{ab} = M_{ba}$, that is $N = M^{\mathsf T}$. Unit 03's $(U\otimes\bar U)\ket{\Phi^+} = \ket{\Phi^+}$ is the special case $M = U$, $N = \bar U$: $(U\otimes\bar U)\ket{\Phi^+} = (I\otimes\bar UU^{\mathsf T})\ket{\Phi^+} = \ket{\Phi^+}$, as $\bar UU^{\mathsf T} = \overline{UU^\dagger} = I$.

The transposes. $X^{\mathsf T} = X$, $Z^{\mathsf T} = Z$, and $(AB)^{\mathsf T} = B^{\mathsf T}A^{\mathsf T}$, so $(Z^jX^k)^{\mathsf T} = X^kZ^j$.

07 · the reverse trade

Entanglement does not enlarge a classical channel

lemma

Alice and Bob share any state. Alice is given a message $x$, uniform over $2^K$ values, does anything she likes in her lab, and sends Bob $n$ cbits. Bob then does anything in his and outputs $\hat x$. Then

$$\Pr(\hat x = x) \le 2^{\,n - K} .$$
proof

Bob guesses the $n$ bits uniformly instead of waiting, and runs his procedure. (i) This uses no communication, so by no-signalling his output's distribution does not depend on $x$: he is right with probability $2^{-K}$. (ii) His guess equals Alice's real message with probability $2^{-n}$, and then the run is exactly the real one. So $2^{-n}\Pr(\hat x = x) \le 2^{-K}$. $\blacksquare$

Replace the message by a guess: no communication is left, and no-signalling caps the success.

so

With $n$ cbits, $2^{n+1}$ equally likely messages are read correctly at most half the time. In particular one cbit cannot carry two bits, however much entanglement is shared: $\Pr \le 2^{1-2} = \tfrac12$.

Full proof

Model Alice's actions given $x$ as unitaries and measurements on her systems, ending with an $n$-bit string $c$; Bob's, given a string $c'$, as unitaries and a final measurement on his systems with outcome $\hat x$. All of Alice's operators act as $\cdot\otimes I$ and all of Bob's as $I\otimes\cdot$, so they commute, and the joint distribution of all outcomes does not depend on the order in which the two labs act (unit 03: measurements on different subsystems commute).

(i) In the guessing run Bob uses $c'$, drawn uniformly and independently. Let Bob act first. His output distribution is computed from his reduced state, which by unit 04's no-signalling theorem is whatever it was before Alice did anything — independent of $x$. Call it $q(\hat x)$. Then $\Pr(\hat x = x) = \sum_x2^{-K}q(x) = 2^{-K}$.

(ii) Fix any value $c_0$. The event "$c' = c_0$ and $c = c_0$ and $\hat x = x$" has probability $2^{-n}\Pr_{\text{real}}(c = c_0,\ \hat x = x)$: given $c' = c_0$, Bob performs exactly the operations he performs in the real protocol when he receives $c_0$, and $c'$ is independent of everything else. Summing over $c_0$: $\Pr(c' = c \text{ and } \hat x = x) = 2^{-n}\Pr_{\text{real}}(\hat x = x)$. This event is contained in "$\hat x = x$" of the guessing run, so $2^{-n}\Pr_{\text{real}}(\hat x = x) \le 2^{-K}$.

Check. $n = K$: the bound is $1$, met by sending $x$. $\ n = 0$: $2^{-K}$, a blind guess. Shared entanglement can still help in other tasks — unit 06's CHSH game, not needed here — but not to raise this number.

Dense coding cannot be beaten

the Holevo bound (Holevo 1973; cited, proved in unit 08)

A message encoded in the state of $n$ qubits, with no entanglement shared beforehand, gives whoever receives it at most $n$ bits of information about the message, whatever measurement is made.

the zero-error case, proved here

States $\rho_1, \dots, \rho_N$ on $\mathbb C^d$ that some measurement tells apart with certainty number at most $d$.

proof

If outcome $x$ has probability $\operatorname{tr}(P_x\rho_x) = 1$, each eigenvector of $\rho_x$ with nonzero eigenvalue lies in the range of $P_x$; pick one, $v_x$. The ranges of different $P_x$ are orthogonal, so $v_1, \dots, v_N$ are orthonormal in $\mathbb C^d$. $\blacksquare$

Dense coding uses all of Bob's room.

consequences

One qubit alone: at most $2$ perfectly readable messages — $1$ bit. So dense coding's second bit needs the ebit.

One ebit + one qubit: Bob ends with two qubits, $d = 4$, so at most $4$ messages — $2$ bits, which dense coding achieves. Three bits are impossible (problem P7).

The zero-error proof, every step. Let $\{P_y\}$ be the measurement and suppose $\operatorname{tr}(P_x\rho_x) = 1$ for each $x$. Write $\rho_x = \sum_i\lambda_i\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}$ with $\lambda_i \gt 0$, $\sum\lambda_i = 1$. Then $1 = \sum_i\lambda_i\braket{e_i|P_x|e_i}$, and each $\braket{e_i|P_x|e_i} = \lVert P_xe_i\rVert^2 \le 1$, so every one equals $1$: $\lVert P_xe_i\rVert = \lVert e_i\rVert$, which for a projector means $P_xe_i = e_i$. Pick one such $v_x := e_1$ for each $x$. For $x \ne x'$, $\braket{v_x|v_{x'}} = \braket{P_xv_x|P_{x'}v_{x'}} = \braket{v_x|P_xP_{x'}v_{x'}} = 0$. So the $v_x$ are orthonormal, and there are at most $d$ of them.

Bob's own systems. If Bob first adjoins an ancilla in a fixed state $\ket0$ and measures the larger system, apply the argument to $\rho_x\otimes\ket0\!\bra0$: the vectors $v_x\otimes\ket0$ are orthonormal, hence so are the $v_x$. When no entanglement was shared, Bob's other systems are in a state that does not depend on $x$, and the same argument works with it in place of $\ket0\!\bra0$.

What Holevo adds. The zero-error count says nothing about protocols that are allowed to err, or that send many qubits and decode them jointly. Holevo's theorem covers both, using the entropy of unit 08: the mutual information between message and outcome is at most $n$ for $n$ qubits. With unlimited shared entanglement the bound becomes $2n$ (cited; it follows from the same entropy inequalities), so dense coding is optimal even then; branch 08's sheet shows dense coding meets the bound with an ebit. A. S. Holevo, Problems of Information Transmission 9, 177 (1973).

07 · the reverse trade

Teleportation cannot be cheapened

theorem: both bits are needed

No protocol teleports every qubit state exactly using shared entanglement (any amount) and one cbit.

proof: feed it dense coding

Share one more $\ket{\Phi^+}$ on $A'B'$. Alice dense-codes $jk$ into $A'$ and teleports $A'$ with the supposed protocol — one cbit. Bob holds $A'B'$ in $\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}$ and reads $jk$ with certainty: two bits through one cbit, where the lemma allows success $\tfrac12$. $\blacksquare$

Top: a one-cbit teleporter would let one cbit carry two bits. Bottom: a teleporter without an ebit would clone.

theorem: the ebit is needed

With classical messages alone, no protocol teleports every state exactly.

proof: bits can be copied

Copy Alice's message to Bob and to Charlie, whose lab is built like Bob's. Both prepare $\psi$: a cloner, which unit 05 forbids. $\blacksquare$

Full proof that a teleporter preserves entanglement

The first proof teleports $A'$, which is entangled with $B'$; it needs a protocol that is exact on single states to be exact on halves of entangled states too. Purify everything (unit 04) so each branch $b$ of the supposed protocol acts on C and the shared resources as a linear map $L_b$ (unitaries and projectors). Exactness on every pure $\psi$ means Bob's qubit is pure $\psi$ in every branch, so the branch's output is a product $\ket\psi_B\otimes\ket{g_b(\psi)}$. Linearity on $\ket0$, $\ket1$ and $\ket0 + \ket1$ gives $\ket0\ket{g_b(0)} + \ket1\ket{g_b(1)} = (\ket0 + \ket1)\ket g$ for some $\ket g$, so $g_b(0) = g_b(1)$: up to a scalar, $L_b$ acts as $\ket\psi\mapsto\ket\psi\otimes\ket{g_b}$ with $\ket{g_b}$ independent of $\psi$. Applied to the C-part of $\sum_ic_i\ket{r_i}_R\ket{\psi_i}_C$ it gives $\big(\sum_ic_i\ket{r_i}_R\ket{\psi_i}_B\big)\otimes\ket{g_b}$: the entangled state arrives intact.

The ebit proof in full. Without shared entanglement Bob's lab starts in a state uncorrelated with Alice's, and may as well be pure (purify it inside his lab). Charlie builds an identical lab. Alice's message is a bit string; copying bits is a classical operation. In each branch the combined action on $\ket\psi$ is linear and yields $\ket\psi_{\text{Bob}}\ket\psi_{\text{Charlie}}\otimes(\text{junk})$, and unit 05's linearity argument (as in The original is destroyed) rules that out. Shared classical randomness changes nothing: it is copied with the message.

The source. Bennett et al. (1993) already observed that the two bits cannot be dispensed with; the dense-coding composition is the standard way to make that a proof. Problem P8 writes the whole argument out, lemma included.

07 · benchmarks

Average fidelity: one number for a whole teleporter

the fidelity of an output with a pure target

$F(\psi, \rho) := \braket{\psi|\rho|\psi} = \operatorname{tr}(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\rho)$: the probability that $\rho$ passes the test "is it $\psi$?", the measurement $\{\ket\psi\!\bra\psi,\ I - \ket\psi\!\bra\psi\}$. It lies in $[0, 1]$ and equals $1$ exactly when $\rho = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$.

the average fidelity $$\bar F := \int F\big(\psi, \rho_{\text{out}}(\psi)\big)\,\mathrm d\mu(\psi),$$

with $\mu$ uniform on the Bloch sphere: $\mathrm d\mu = \frac{\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm d\varphi}{4\pi}$, area normalised to $1$. Rotations preserve area, so $\mu$ is rotation-invariant: no state is favoured.

Input on the sphere, output inside the ball. Fidelity reads the angle and the length.

in Bloch terms

If $\psi$ has Bloch vector $\mathbf r$ and $\rho = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf s\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$: $\ F = \tfrac12(1 + \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s)$.

Proof: $\tfrac14\operatorname{tr}\big((I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(I + \mathbf s\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\big) = \tfrac14(2 + 2\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s)$ by trace orthogonality, as in unit 02's overlap formula.

Why this test. Unit 05's question was how well a state can be identified. Here the question is how well a device reproduces a state, and the natural yardstick is the probability that a referee who knows $\psi$ accepts the output. Unit 21 defines a fidelity between two mixed states; when one of them is pure it reduces to $\braket{\psi|\rho|\psi}$, so nothing here will need revising.

The range. $\braket{\psi|\rho|\psi} \ge 0$ because $\rho$ is positive (unit 04), and $\le 1$ because $\rho$'s eigenvalues are at most $1$. It equals $1$ only if $\rho\psi = \psi$ with $\rho$ of trace one and positive, which forces $\rho = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$. Checks: $\rho = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$ gives $\mathbf s = \mathbf r$, $F = 1$; $\rho = I/2$ gives $\mathbf s = 0$, $F = \tfrac12$; $\rho$ the orthogonal state gives $\mathbf s = -\mathbf r$, $F = 0$ ✓.

The uniform measure. In the angles of unit 02, the area element of the unit sphere is $\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm d\varphi$, with total $\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm d\varphi = 4\pi$. A rotation of $\mathbb R^3$ is an isometry, so it maps any region to one of the same area: $\mu(R\,S) = \mu(S)$. That rotation invariance is the only property the next slide uses.

Why an average, not the worst case. Both are used. The average answers "how good on a state nobody chose", is what experiments report, and is the quantity the classical bound of the next slides is proved for. The worst case is informative too: for measure-and-prepare in $z$ it is $\tfrac12$, on the equator.

07 · benchmarks

Averages over the sphere, without integrals

lemma

For $(x, y, z)$ uniform on the unit sphere:

$$\langle x\rangle = \langle y\rangle = \langle z\rangle = 0,$$ $$\langle x^2\rangle = \langle y^2\rangle = \langle z^2\rangle = \tfrac13,$$

and $\langle xy\rangle = \langle yz\rangle = \langle zx\rangle = 0$.

proof: rotate, then add

Squares. The turn by $120^\circ$ about $(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt3$ sends $(x, y, z)\mapsto(z, x, y)$. The measure is rotation-invariant, so $\langle x^2\rangle = \langle y^2\rangle = \langle z^2\rangle$; they add to $\langle x^2 + y^2 + z^2\rangle = \langle1\rangle = 1$.

The rest. The half-turn about $z$, $(x, y, z)\mapsto(-x, -y, z)$, gives $\langle x\rangle = -\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle xz\rangle = -\langle xz\rangle$. Half-turns about the other axes do the rest. $\blacksquare$

A turn by $120^\circ$ about the diagonal carries $x$ to $y$, $y$ to $z$, $z$ to $x$.

check by integration

$\langle z^2\rangle = \frac1{4\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi\cos^2\theta\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta\,\mathrm d\varphi = \tfrac12\int_{-1}^1u^2\,\mathrm du = \tfrac13$ ✓.

The rotation, checked. The map $(x, y, z)\mapsto(z, x, y)$ has matrix $\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$, a permutation matrix with determinant $+1$ (a cyclic permutation is even), so it is in $SO(3)$: a rotation. It fixes $(1, 1, 1)$, and its trace is $0 = 1 + 2\cos\alpha$, so its angle is $\alpha = 120^\circ$ (a rotation by $\alpha$ has trace $1 + 2\cos\alpha$, as its matrix in a basis containing the axis shows). Invariance of $\mu$ gives $\langle f\circ R\rangle = \langle f\rangle$ for every integrable $f$; with $f = x^2$, $f\circ R = z^2$.

The integral. Substitute $u = \cos\theta$, $\mathrm du = -\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta$; the $\varphi$ integral gives $2\pi$; $\frac{2\pi}{4\pi}\int_{-1}^1u^2\,\mathrm du = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac23 = \tfrac13$. The same substitution shows $z$ itself is uniform on $[-1, 1]$ — Archimedes' hat-box theorem: the area of a band of the sphere depends only on its height.

What it buys. Every fidelity in this section is $\tfrac12(1 + \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf s(\mathbf r))$ with $\mathbf s$ an affine function of $\mathbf r$ ($\mathbf s = A\mathbf r + \mathbf b$), so $\bar F$ is the average of a polynomial of degree two in $(x, y, z)$. The lemma evaluates every such average.

Six points do as well. The six axis states $\pm\hat x, \pm\hat y, \pm\hat z$ have the same averages: $\langle x\rangle = 0$, $\langle x^2\rangle = \tfrac26 = \tfrac13$, $\langle xy\rangle = 0$. So the average of any degree-two polynomial over these six equals its average over the sphere. Problem P11 proves this, and it is why section 5's satellite experiment could test six inputs and report the sphere average.

07 · benchmarks · example

Worked: measure and prepare scores two-thirds

measure-and-prepare in $z$, on the running example

1. The strategy. No ebit. Alice measures $\psi$ in $z$ and phones the outcome $b$; Bob prepares $\ket b$.

2. Output. $\Pr(0) = \cos^2\tfrac\theta2$, $\Pr(1) = \sin^2\tfrac\theta2$, summing to $1$:

$$\rho_{\text{out}} = \cos^2\tfrac\theta2\,\ket0\!\bra0 + \sin^2\tfrac\theta2\,\ket1\!\bra1 .$$

3. Fidelity. $\braket{\psi|0}\braket{0|\psi} = \cos^2\tfrac\theta2$ and $|\braket{1|\psi}|^2 = \sin^2\tfrac\theta2$, so

$$\begin{aligned}F(\theta) &= \cos^4\tfrac\theta2 + \sin^4\tfrac\theta2 = 1 - 2\cos^2\tfrac\theta2\sin^2\tfrac\theta2\\ &= 1 - \tfrac12\sin^2\theta .\end{aligned}$$

4. Average. $\sin^2\theta = x^2 + y^2$, so by the lemma $\langle\sin^2\theta\rangle = \tfrac13 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac23$, and

$$\bar F = 1 - \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac23 = \tfrac23 .$$

Perfect at the poles, where the $z$ measurement is harmless; one half on the equator, where it is a coin flip.

5. Checks. $\theta = 0$ ($\ket0$): $F = 1$ ✓. $\ \theta = \tfrac\pi2$ ($\ket+$): $\rho_{\text{out}} = I/2$, $F = \tfrac12$ ✓. Bloch form: $\mathbf s = (0, 0, z)$, so $F = \tfrac12(1 + z^2)$ and $\bar F = \tfrac12(1 + \tfrac13) = \tfrac23$ ✓ — the same.

6. Integral check: $\tfrac12\int_0^\pi(1 - \tfrac12\sin^2\theta)\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta = 1 - \tfrac14\cdot\tfrac43 = \tfrac23$ ✓.

Step 3, the algebra. $(\cos^2 + \sin^2)^2 = 1$ gives $\cos^4 + \sin^4 = 1 - 2\cos^2\sin^2$, and $2\cos\tfrac\theta2\sin\tfrac\theta2 = \sin\theta$ gives $2\cos^2\tfrac\theta2\sin^2\tfrac\theta2 = \tfrac12\sin^2\theta$.

Step 4, why $\sin^2\theta = x^2 + y^2$. $x = \sin\theta\cos\varphi$, $y = \sin\theta\sin\varphi$. The answer does not depend on $\varphi$: the $z$ measurement throws the phase away, and the output is diagonal.

Step 6. $\int_0^\pi\sin\theta\,\mathrm d\theta = 2$ and $\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,\mathrm d\theta = \tfrac43$, so $\tfrac12\big(2 - \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac43\big) = 1 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac23$. The $\varphi$ integral and the $4\pi$ contributed the factor $\tfrac12$.

Other bases. Measuring along a fixed axis $\mathbf n$ gives $F = \tfrac12(1 + (\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r)^2)$, average $\tfrac23$ again by rotation invariance; a randomly chosen axis averages $\tfrac23$s and gives $\tfrac23$. Problem P10 works both. Every number on this slide was computed, including a numerical quadrature: $0.666667$.

07 · benchmarks

The classical benchmark: two-thirds

the classical benchmark (Massar and Popescu 1995; cited)

Any strategy with no shared entanglement — Alice does anything to one copy of $\psi$ and sends any classical message, Bob prepares a state from it — has

$$\bar F \le \tfrac23,$$

and measure-and-prepare in $z$ attains it.

so, a test

A measured average fidelity above $\tfrac23$ certifies that the shared entanglement was used. Between $\tfrac12$ and $\tfrac23$, a phone line alone could have done it.

Three marks: doing nothing — Bob outputs $I/2$ — scores $\tfrac12$ for every $\psi$; classical, $\tfrac23$; ideal teleportation, $1$.

The scale on which a teleporter is judged; the two experiments of the next slides, as their authors report them.

With $N$ copies of $\psi$ the classical optimum is $\tfrac{N + 1}{N + 2}$ (the same paper): $\tfrac23$ is its $N = 1$ case, and the test assumes Alice gets a single copy.

What is cited. S. Massar and S. Popescu, "Optimal extraction of information from finite quantum ensembles", Phys. Rev. Lett. 74, 1259 (1995), prove that the best average fidelity of estimating a uniformly random pure qubit state from $N$ copies, and preparing the estimate, is $(N + 1)/(N + 2)$. A strategy with no entanglement is exactly such an estimate: whatever Alice does ends in a classical message, and Bob's output is a function of it. The proof needs generalised measurements (unit 21), which is why it is cited here, not proved. The upper bound is what makes $\tfrac23$ a test; that $\tfrac23$ is reached is this deck's worked example.

Doing nothing. If Bob outputs his half of the pair uncorrected, or any fixed $I/2$, his fidelity is $\braket{\psi|\tfrac I2|\psi} = \tfrac12$ for every $\psi$. If he outputs a fixed pure state $\ket\chi$, $F = \tfrac12(1 + \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf c)$ averages to $\tfrac12$ by $\langle\mathbf r\rangle = 0$. Problem P9.

The noisy pair. If the shared pair is the mixture $p\ket{\Phi^+}\!\bra{\Phi^+} + (1 - p)I/4$ (a Werner state), the standard protocol gives $\bar F = \tfrac{1 + p}{2}$; this beats $\tfrac23$ exactly when $p \gt \tfrac13$, and at $p = \tfrac12$ it is $0.75$. Problem P12 derives it. That $p \gt \tfrac13$ is also exactly when this state is entangled is a theorem about mixed-state entanglement (R. F. Werner, Phys. Rev. A 40, 4277, 1989; cited), outside this course.

07 · benchmarks · experiment

Innsbruck, 1997: the first teleported photon

After Bouwmeester et al. (1997), simplified. A coincidence of f1 and f2 is Alice's "outcome $\Psi^-$".

The pieces. A crystal turns one ultraviolet photon into a pair in the singlet (unit 03), here photons 2 and 3 — the ebit. Photon 1 carries $\psi$ in its polarisation. A beam splitter, a half-silvered mirror, mixes photons 1 and 2.

The Bell measurement: two identical photons arriving together leave by different exits only if their polarisations are in $\ket{\Psi^-}$ (cited). So the f1–f2 coincidence recognises one outcome of four: it succeeds a quarter of the time, a limit of the linear optics used (no linear-optics Bell measurement exceeds a half).

The result. Visibilities $0.57$–$0.66$ (each $\pm0.02$) for five input polarisations; $0.70 \pm 0.03$ with a fourth photon as herald. The authors' later reading: fidelity $0.80 \pm 0.05$, above $\tfrac23$.

The paper. D. Bouwmeester, J.-W. Pan, K. Mattle, M. Eibl, H. Weinfurter and A. Zeilinger, "Experimental quantum teleportation", Nature 390, 575 (1997). A visibility here is the fractional depth of a dip: when photons 1 and 2 arrive at the beam splitter together, a perfect teleporter never fires the detector analysing the polarisation orthogonal to $\psi$, so that detector's coincidence rate falls from its value with the photons made to arrive apart; the fraction by which it falls is $1$ for a perfect teleporter and $0$ if nothing was teleported. Its Table 1 lists the visibility of the dip in three-fold coincidences (f1, f2 and the analysis detector orthogonal to the input) for inputs $+45^\circ$: $0.63$, $-45^\circ$: $0.64$, $0^\circ$: $0.66$, $90^\circ$: $0.61$, circular: $0.57$, each $\pm0.02$, after subtracting spurious coincidences measured by blocking photon 1. Conditioning also on the trigger photon 4 removed that background, giving $0.70 \pm 0.03$ without subtraction. The fidelity figure $0.80 \pm 0.05$ is from D. Bouwmeester, J.-W. Pan, H. Weinfurter and A. Zeilinger, "High-fidelity teleportation of independent qubits", J. Mod. Opt. 47, 279 (2000).

Why the singlet branch needs no correction here. Innsbruck's ebit was $\ket{\Psi^-}$, not $\ket{\Phi^+}$. Problem P2 redoes the identity for that resource: the outcome $\Psi^-$ then leaves Bob with $\psi$ up to global phase, so in the one branch they could detect, Bob had nothing to do.

The beam-splitter fact, cited. Two photons identical in every respect but polarisation meet at a 50/50 beam splitter. The polarisation singlet is antisymmetric, so its spatial part must be antisymmetric, and the photons exit by different ports; the other three Bell states are symmetric and exit together. H. Weinfurter, Europhys. Lett. 25, 559 (1994); S. L. Braunstein and A. Mann, Phys. Rev. A 51, R1727 (1995). With linear optics alone no Bell measurement succeeds more than half the time: N. Lütkenhaus, J. Calsamiglia and K.-A. Suominen, Phys. Rev. A 59, 3295 (1999).

The honest caveat. The fidelity is conditioned on the f1–f2 coincidence and on photon 3 being detected; S. L. Braunstein and H. J. Kimble, "A posteriori teleportation", Nature 394, 840 (1998), argued that a teleporter must also deliver the photon, and the authors replied in the same issue. Read the $0.80$ as "when it worked, it worked well".

07 · benchmarks · experiment

Micius, 2017: from the ground to orbit

After Ren et al. (2017), not to scale. Photon 3 is sent up; Bob's lab is the satellite.

The setup. At Ngari, a source makes the ebit (photons 2, 3) and photon 1 in one of six inputs, $\ket0, \ket1, \ket\pm, \ket{\pm i}$ in unit 02's names. Alice's Bell measurement is on the ground; photon 3 crosses the atmosphere to the satellite Micius, whose analyser measures it.

The Bell measurement recognised $\Phi^+$ and $\Phi^-$ — half the outcomes. The $Z$ correction for $\Phi^-$ was applied in the data analysis, as a relabelling of the satellite's outcomes.

The result. $911$ four-photon events over $32$ passes, at $500$–$1400$ km; average fidelity $0.80 \pm 0.01$ over the six states, without background subtraction — well above $\tfrac23$.

The paper. J.-G. Ren et al., "Ground-to-satellite quantum teleportation", Nature 549, 70 (2017); preprint arXiv:1707.00934. The numbers on the slide are theirs: ground station at Ngari, Tibet, altitude $5100$ m; Micius launched 16 August 2016 into an orbit at about $500$ km; slant distance from $500$ km (satellite overhead, elevation $76.0^\circ$) to $1400$ km (elevation $14.5^\circ$); $911$ four-photon counts in $32$ orbits of $350$ s each; average fidelity $0.80 \pm 0.01$; all data without background subtraction. They name double pair emission, partial distinguishability of the photons, polarisation distortion on the uplink and dark counts as the error sources.

Why six states give the sphere average. For a teleporter whose output Bloch vector is an affine function of the input's, $\mathbf s = A\mathbf r + \mathbf b$, the fidelity is a polynomial of degree two in $(x, y, z)$, and the six axis states reproduce the sphere's averages of $x$, $x^2$ and $xy$ (Averages over the sphere, without integrals, notes; problem P11). The paper describes the six as "sampling over the whole Bloch sphere"; the lemma is why that sampling is exact.

Correction in post-processing. Bob's $Z$ followed by a measurement in some basis has the same statistics as measuring in the $Z$-rotated basis; and for each of the six inputs, $Z$ either fixes the analysis basis or exchanges its two outcomes. So relabelling outcomes is equivalent to applying $Z$. A deployed teleporter would apply it physically, which needs the bits to arrive first.

Why up, not down. Sending the photon up means the delicate multi-photon source and Bell measurement stay on the ground; the cost is that turbulence near the ground spreads the beam early in its path. The loss was $41$ to $52$ dB.

07 · benchmarks

Teleporting half a pair: entanglement swapping

Swapping: two ebits and one Bell measurement leave the outer pair entangled.

entanglement swapping (Żukowski et al. 1993)

Start with $\ket{\Phi^+}_{12}\ket{\Phi^+}_{34}$. A Bell measurement on 2 and 3 with outcome $jk$ (probability $\tfrac14$) leaves 1 and 4 in the Bell state $(I\otimes\sigma_{jk})\ket{\Phi^+}_{14} = \ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}_{14}$; correcting 4 with $\sigma_{jk}^{-1}$ gives $\ket{\Phi^+}_{14}$.

Why: it is teleportation with photon 2 as the input C — and photon 2 is half of a pair. Teleporting it moves its entanglement with 1 onto 4 (by linearity of the identity). Problem P5 checks every branch.

Why it matters: chains of short ebits joined by swapping make one long ebit — the primitive of a quantum repeater. First demonstrated with photons by Pan et al. in 1998.

Swapping from the identity. Write $\ket{\Phi^+}_{12} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_a\ket a_1\ket a_2$. For each $a$, the teleportation identity with C = 2, A = 3, B = 4 gives $\ket a_2\ket{\Phi^+}_{34} = \tfrac12\sum_{jk}\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}_{23}\,\sigma_{jk}\ket a_4$. So $\ket{\Phi^+}_{12}\ket{\Phi^+}_{34} = \tfrac12\sum_{jk}\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}_{23}\otimes\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_a\ket a_1\sigma_{jk}\ket a_4 = \tfrac12\sum_{jk}\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}_{23}\otimes(I\otimes\sigma_{jk})\ket{\Phi^+}_{14}$. Outcome $jk$ has probability $\tfrac14$ and leaves 1, 4 in $\ket{\mathcal B_{jk}}$ ✓.

References. M. Żukowski, A. Zeilinger, M. A. Horne and A. K. Ekert, "'Event-ready-detectors' Bell experiment via entanglement swapping", Phys. Rev. Lett. 71, 4287 (1993). J.-W. Pan, D. Bouwmeester, H. Weinfurter and A. Zeilinger, "Experimental entanglement swapping: entangling photons that never interacted", Phys. Rev. Lett. 80, 3891 (1998). The repeater: H.-J. Briegel, W. Dür, J. I. Cirac and P. Zoller, Phys. Rev. Lett. 81, 5932 (1998).

Why a repeater is needed at all. The probability that a photon survives a fibre falls exponentially with its length, and unit 05 forbids amplifying it by copying. Swapping lets short links be made and checked separately, then joined. Real repeaters also need the noisy links purified — which is beyond this unit.

07 · the close

One ebit and two bits buy one qubit — and nothing less will

the question, from the overview

Alice holds $\ket\psi = \cos\tfrac\theta2\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\sin\tfrac\theta2\ket1$, unknown; she shares $\ket{\Phi^+}$ with Bob and has a phone. Can Bob end up with $\ket\psi$?

the answer: yes, exactly, for the exchange $$1\text{ ebit} + 2\text{ cbits} \;\ge\; 1\text{ qubit}$$

Alice's Bell measurement, two bits, Bob's $Z^jX^k$; every branch delivers $\psi$ (section 2).

Along the way: Alice's bits are uniform and say nothing about $\theta, \varphi$; $\psi$ leaves her lab; Bob holds $I/2$ until the bits land (section 3).

Each resource is necessary:

  • the ebit — without it the message is classical, can be copied, and two receivers would clone $\psi$ (unit 05); measured fidelity drops to at most $\tfrac23$;
  • the second cbit — with one, dense coding through the teleporter would push two bits through one cbit, against the lemma;
  • the first cbit — with none, Bob holds $I/2$ forever: fidelity $\tfrac12$.
and the reverse trade is tight too

$1$ ebit $+\ 1$ qubit $\ge 2$ cbits, and not $3$: Bob's two qubits have room for four orthogonal messages. Composed, the two trades give back exactly what they took.

The running example, closed. Every claim in its hover card is now derived: the normalisation (overview); the four branches and their corrections (Worked: the identity, term by term; All four branches, on the running example); the uniform $\tfrac14$s (Alice learns nothing about ψ); Bob's $I/2$ (Before the bits arrive, Bob holds I/2, and the worked example after it); and the classical comparison, $1 - \tfrac12\sin^2\theta$ averaging to $\tfrac23$ (Worked: measure and prepare scores two-thirds).

Necessity, with numbers. No ebit: at most $\tfrac23$ (Massar–Popescu). No cbits: exactly $\tfrac12$ (problem P9). One cbit: problem P6 finds that sending just one of the two bits, with the matching half of the correction, gives exactly $\tfrac23$ — the classical benchmark, no better. (That is one protocol, not a bound on all one-cbit protocols; the impossibility proof on Teleportation cannot be cheapened does not need it.)

What the unit leaves for later. Noisy pairs as channels: unit 21. How many ebits a partly entangled pure state is worth, and the Holevo bound proved: branch 08. Teleportation as a way to apply a gate — "gate teleportation", which is how fault-tolerant machines apply the $T$ gate: unit 24.
