Entropy and the Holevo bound: how many bits a qubit holds

A qubit has a continuum of states and yields one bit. Shannon's entropy says how many bits a classical source holds, von Neumann's says the same of a quantum one, and strong subadditivity turns "discarding a system never helps" into the Holevo bound: no measurement gets more than $\chi$ bits out, and $\chi \le \log d$.

The physics, from nothing. Light is a wave whose electric field oscillates across its direction of travel; the direction of that oscillation is its polarisation. Unit 03 made it a qubit: horizontal is $\ket0$, vertical $\ket1$, and linear polarisation at angle $\alpha$ is $\cos\alpha\ket0 + \sin\alpha\ket1$, so $45^\circ$ is $\ket+$. An analyser is a birefringent crystal that sends the polarisation at its angle $\theta$ one way and the perpendicular one the other way, with a detector on each exit: it measures in the basis $\{\ket\theta, \ket{\theta + 90^\circ}\}$. Non-orthogonal encodings are not a curiosity: they are what key distribution uses (unit 09), and what any source whose states overlap delivers.

08 · Shannon

How many bits a source holds

Shannon entropy

A random variable $X$ on a finite set, with distribution $p$, has

$$H(X) = H(p) = -\sum_xp(x)\log p(x)\quad\text{bits},$$

with $0\log0 := 0$ (the limit of $t\log t$). For two outcomes, the binary entropy $h(p) = -p\log p - (1 - p)\log(1 - p)$.

Twenty questions. $X \in \{a, b, c, d\}$ with $p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \tfrac18)$, sum $1$ ✓. Ask "is it $a$?", then "is it $b$?", then "is it $c$?": $1, 2, 3, 3$ questions, on average $\tfrac12\cdot1 + \tfrac14\cdot2 + \tfrac18\cdot3 + \tfrac18\cdot3 = 1.75$. And

$$H = \tfrac12\cdot1 + \tfrac14\cdot2 + \tfrac18\cdot3 + \tfrac18\cdot3 = 1.75 .$$

Alice's coin (the running example): $h(\tfrac12) = 1$ bit in.

The binary entropy. A coin of bias $0.146$ holds $0.601$ bits a toss — the number this unit keeps meeting.

What the number means. Shannon (Bell System Technical Journal 27, 379 and 623, 1948) asked how many binary digits a message from a random source needs. The twenty-questions example is the intuition: an outcome of probability $2^{-k}$ should cost $k$ questions, and $H$ is the average of $-\log p(x)$. When the probabilities are not powers of two no questioning scheme for a single outcome achieves $H$ exactly; what is true, and what makes $H$ the answer, is the statement about long sequences on the next two slides: $n$ outcomes need about $nH$ bits, and no fewer. The twenty-questions picture is motivation; the typical-sequence theorem is the proof.

The convention $0\log0 = 0$. $t\log t \to 0$ as $t \to 0^+$ (l'Hôpital on $\ln t/(1/t)$), so the function $t \mapsto -t\log t$ extends continuously to $[0, 1]$. Outcomes of probability zero contribute nothing, as they should.

Units. Base-2 logarithms give bits. With $\ln$ the unit is the nat; $1$ nat $= 1/\ln2 \approx 1.4427$ bits. Nothing in this unit uses nats except inside proofs, where $\ln$ appears because its derivative is $1/t$.

The figure. $h$ is symmetric about $\tfrac12$, zero at $0$ and $1$, and has infinite slope at the ends: $h'(p) = \log\frac{1 - p}p$. The two marked points are $p = \sin^2\tfrac\pi8 = 0.1464$ and $\cos^2\tfrac\pi8 = 0.8536$, where $h = 0.6009$ — the eigenvalues of the running example's average state, as section 2 will show.

08 · Shannon

Gibbs' inequality, and the bounds on H

Gibbs' inequality

For distributions $p, q$ with $q > 0$ wherever $p > 0$,

$$D(p\Vert q) := \sum_xp(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \ \ge\ 0,$$

equality iff $p = q$: the classical relative entropy.

proof

Sum over $p > 0$, with $\ln t \le t - 1$ at $t = q/p$: $\ \sum p\ln\frac qp \le \sum(q - p) \le 0$. Equality forces $q = p$ wherever $p > 0$, and then $\sum q = 1$ leaves nothing elsewhere. $\blacksquare$

so $0 \le H(X) \le \log d$

Each $-p\log p \ge 0$, zero only at $p \in \{0, 1\}$: $H = 0$ iff $X$ is certain. With $q$ uniform, $D(p\Vert q) = \log d - H$: $H = \log d$ iff $p$ is uniform.

The one inequality behind every entropy bound in this unit: $\ln t \le t - 1$, equality only at $t = 1$. Klein's inequality in section 3 is the same picture.

Full proof of $\ln t \le t - 1$. $f(t) = t - 1 - \ln t$ has $f'(t) = 1 - 1/t$, negative on $(0, 1)$ and positive on $(1, \infty)$, so $f$ has its unique minimum at $t = 1$, where $f(1) = 0$. Hence $f \ge 0$, with equality only at $t = 1$. Geometrically: $\ln$ is concave, so it lies below its tangent at $1$.

Gibbs, every step. Let $A = \{x : p(x) > 0\}$; by hypothesis $q > 0$ on $A$. Then $-D(p\Vert q)\ln2 = \sum_{x\in A}p(x)\ln\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} \le \sum_{x\in A}p(x)\Big(\frac{q(x)}{p(x)} - 1\Big) = \sum_{x\in A}q(x) - 1 \le 0$. Equality in the first step needs $q(x) = p(x)$ for every $x \in A$; in the second, $\sum_Aq = 1$. Together: $q = p$ on $A$ and $q = 0$ off $A$, so $q = p$. If $q(x) = 0$ for some $x$ with $p(x) > 0$, set $D = +\infty$; the inequality then holds trivially.

The upper bound, written out. With $q(x) = 1/d$: $D(p\Vert q) = \sum p\log p - \sum p\log(1/d) = -H(p) + \log d$. Gibbs gives $H(p) \le \log d$, with equality iff $p = q$. Check: $d = 2$, the fair coin, $H = 1 = \log2$ ✓.

The name. Gibbs proved the inequality in his statistical mechanics (1902); the quantity $D$ is also called the Kullback–Leibler divergence (1951). It is not a distance: it is not symmetric, and section 3 computes an example where the two orders differ.

08 · Shannon

Typical sequences

the typical set

$X_1, \dots, X_n$ independent, each with distribution $p$. A sequence $x^n = x_1\cdots x_n$ is $\delta$-typical if

$$\Big|-\tfrac1n\log p(x^n) - H\Big| \le \delta,$$

where $p(x^n) = \prod_ip(x_i)$.

So $2^{-n(H + \delta)} \le p(x^n) \le 2^{-n(H - \delta)}$ on $T_{n,\delta}$: typical sequences are all about equally likely.

$-\log p(x^n) = \sum_i\big(-\log p(x_i)\big)$ is a sum of $n$ independent copies of $L = -\log p(X)$, with mean $\mathbb E L = H$ and some variance $\sigma^2$.

the typical-sequence lemma

(i) $\Pr(T_{n,\delta}) \ge 1 - \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n\delta^2} \to 1$.
(ii) $|T_{n,\delta}| \le 2^{n(H + \delta)}$.
(iii) If $\Pr(T_{n,\delta}) \ge 1 - \varepsilon$, then $|T_{n,\delta}| \ge (1 - \varepsilon)2^{n(H - \delta)}$.

proof

(i) Chebyshev for the mean of $n$ independent copies of $L$, whose variance is $\sigma^2/n$. (ii) $1 \ge \sum_{T}p(x^n) \ge |T|\,2^{-n(H + \delta)}$. (iii) $1 - \varepsilon \le \sum_Tp(x^n) \le |T|\,2^{-n(H - \delta)}$. $\blacksquare$

There are $d^n$ sequences; about $2^{nH}$ of them carry almost all the probability.

Full proof of (i). Chebyshev's inequality (background): for a random variable $Y$ with finite variance, $\Pr(|Y - \mathbb EY| \ge t) \le \mathrm{Var}\,Y/t^2$. Take $Y = \tfrac1n\sum_iL_i$ with $L_i = -\log p(X_i)$. The $L_i$ are independent (functions of independent variables) and identically distributed, so $\mathbb EY = H$ and $\mathrm{Var}\,Y = \tfrac1{n^2}\cdot n\sigma^2 = \sigma^2/n$. So $\Pr(|Y - H| > \delta) \le \sigma^2/(n\delta^2)$, and $T_{n,\delta}$ is exactly the event $|Y - H| \le \delta$. This is the weak law of large numbers for $L$, with its rate. $\sigma^2 = \sum_xp(x)(\log p(x))^2 - H^2$ is finite because the alphabet is finite (sum only over $p(x) > 0$; outcomes of probability zero never occur).

Chernoff–Hoeffding instead. The background's Chernoff–Hoeffding bound gives $\Pr(T^c) \le 2e^{-2n\delta^2/(L_{\max} - L_{\min})^2}$, exponentially small rather than $O(1/n)$. Nothing here needs the better rate; Chebyshev keeps the proof self-contained.

Typical is not "most probable". The single most probable sequence is usually not typical: see the next slide. Typicality is about the empirical entropy $-\tfrac1n\log p(x^n)$, which is what the law of large numbers pins down. The name and the lemma (the "asymptotic equipartition property") are Shannon's, 1948, with the general form due to McMillan (1953) and Breiman (1957).

Where the numbers come from. Summing the binomial probabilities $\binom{1000}k p^k(1 - p)^{1000 - k}$ over that range gives $0.9266$, and summing $\binom{1000}k$ gives $2^{643.9}$. The variance of $L = -\log p(X)$ is $p(1 - p)(2.7716 - 0.2284)^2 = \tfrac18\cdot2.5431^2 = 0.8084$, since here $p(1 - p) = \sin^2\tfrac\pi8\cos^2\tfrac\pi8 = \tfrac14\sin^2\tfrac\pi4 = \tfrac18$ exactly.

As $n$ grows the band, measured in $k/n$, shrinks like $1/\sqrt n$ at fixed weight — but the number of sequences inside it is still $2^{n(H \pm \delta)}$. The warning, in numbers. The all-zeros sequence is $2^{-228.4}$ against about $2^{-600.9}$ for a typical one: $2^{372}$ times likelier. But it is one sequence, and its probability is $10^{-69}$; the $2^{643.9}$ typical sequences together have $0.927$.

08 · Shannon

Compression: $H$ bits per symbol, and no fewer

Shannon's source coding theorem (1948)

A source emits $n$ independent symbols with distribution $p$. (a) If $R > H$, the sequences can be encoded in $nR$ bits and decoded with error probability $\to 0$. (b) If $R < H$, any set of $2^{nR}$ sequences has probability $\to 0$: every $nR$-bit code fails almost surely.

proof

Fix $\delta$ with $H + \delta < R$ in (a), $R < H - \delta$ in (b). (a) Number the typical sequences: $|T| \le 2^{n(H + \delta)} \le 2^{nR}$ labels suffice. Send the label, or a fixed failure label off $T$. Error $\le \Pr(T^c) \to 0$.
(b) For $|S| \le 2^{nR}$: $\ \Pr(S) \le \Pr(S\cap T) + \Pr(T^c) \le 2^{nR}2^{-n(H - \delta)} + \Pr(T^c) \to 0$. $\blacksquare$

Encode by numbering the typical set; the rest is the error.

Alice's coin: $H = 1$, so $n$ fair bits need $n$ bits; there is nothing to compress. Her qubits will be another matter (section 5).

(a) in full. Choose $\delta > 0$ with $H + \delta \le R$ (possible as $R > H$). The typical set has at most $2^{n(H + \delta)} \le 2^{nR}$ elements, so the strings of $\lceil nR\rceil$ bits can label each of them and still have one left over to mean "failure". The decoder inverts the labelling. A typical sequence is recovered exactly; an atypical one is lost, which happens with probability $\Pr(T^c) \le \sigma^2/(n\delta^2) \to 0$.

(b) in full. Choose $\delta > 0$ with $R < H - \delta$. A code using $nR$ bits decodes to at most $2^{nR}$ distinct sequences; call that set $S$. It is right only on $S$. Split $S$ into its typical and atypical parts: each typical sequence has probability at most $2^{-n(H - \delta)}$, so $\Pr(S\cap T) \le |S|\,2^{-n(H - \delta)} \le 2^{-n(H - \delta - R)} \to 0$; the atypical part has probability at most $\Pr(T^c) \to 0$. So the probability of decoding correctly tends to zero — not merely stays below one.

What "bits per symbol" means. The rate $R$ is the number of bits per source symbol in the long run. The theorem makes $H$ the exact threshold. Practical codes (Huffman, arithmetic coding) approach it with variable-length codewords; the typical-set code is the proof, not the practice.

08 · Shannon

Two variables: joint, conditional, mutual

for $(X, Y)$ with joint distribution $p(x, y)$

joint entropy: $H(XY) = -\sum_{x,y}p(x, y)\log p(x, y)$.
conditional entropy: $H(Y|X) = \sum_xp(x)\,H(Y|X = x) = H(XY) - H(X)$.
mutual information: $I(X:Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(XY)$.

three facts

1. $H(Y|X) \ge 0$: an average of entropies. So $H(XY) \ge H(X)$.
2. $I(X:Y) = D\big(p_{XY}\Vert p_Xp_Y\big) \ge 0$, zero iff independent.
3. $I(X:Y) \le \min\{H(X), H(Y)\}$, by 1.

Running example: if Bob could read $x$ exactly, $Y = X$, $H(XY) = H(X)$ and $I = 1$ bit. Section 4 asks how close a measurement gets.

The classical bookkeeping. Every region is non-negative — a fact section 3 will see fail for quantum systems.

Fact 1, every step. Write $p(x, y) = p(x)p(y|x)$. Then $H(XY) = -\sum p(x)p(y|x)\big(\log p(x) + \log p(y|x)\big) = H(X) + \sum_xp(x)\big(-\sum_yp(y|x)\log p(y|x)\big)$, using $\sum_yp(y|x) = 1$. The bracket is $H(Y|X = x)$, the entropy of a distribution, so $\ge 0$. This is the chain rule $H(XY) = H(X) + H(Y|X)$.

Fact 2, every step. $D(p_{XY}\Vert p_Xp_Y) = \sum p(x, y)\log\frac{p(x, y)}{p(x)p(y)} = -H(XY) - \sum p(x, y)\log p(x) - \sum p(x, y)\log p(y)$, and the last two sums are $-H(X)$ and $-H(Y)$ after summing out the other variable. The support condition holds: $p(x)p(y) = 0$ forces $p(x, y) = 0$. Gibbs gives $\ge 0$, equality iff $p(x, y) = p(x)p(y)$.

Fact 3. $I = H(Y) - H(Y|X) \le H(Y)$ and symmetrically $\le H(X)$.

A check. $Y = X$ a fair bit: $H(XY) = 1$, $I = 1 + 1 - 1 = 1$ ✓. $X, Y$ independent fair bits: $H(XY) = 2$, $I = 0$ ✓. The Venn picture is a mnemonic only: with three variables its central region can be negative. With two it is exact.

08 · von Neumann

The entropy of a density operator

von Neumann entropy

With $\eta(t) = -t\log t$ on $[0, 1]$ and unit 00's functional calculus,

$$\begin{aligned}S(\rho) &= \operatorname{tr}\eta(\rho) = -\operatorname{tr}\rho\log\rho\\ &= H(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_d),\end{aligned}$$

the Shannon entropy of the eigenvalues of $\rho$ — a distribution, since $\rho \ge 0$ and $\operatorname{tr}\rho = 1$.

from Shannon, at once

1. $0 \le S(\rho) \le \log d$. 2. $S = 0$ iff one eigenvalue is $1$: iff $\rho$ is pure. 3. $S = \log d$ iff every $\lambda_k = 1/d$: iff $\rho = I/d$. 4. $S(U\rho U^\dagger) = S(\rho)$: same spectrum.

A qubit: $\rho = \tfrac12(I + r\cdot\sigma)$ has eigenvalues $\tfrac12(1 \pm |r|)$ (unit 04), so $S = h\big(\tfrac12(1 + |r|)\big)$: entropy depends only on the distance from the centre of the Bloch ball.

$S = h\big(\tfrac12(1 + |r|)\big)$. The running example sits at $|r| = 1/\sqrt2$.

The functional calculus, applied. Unit 00: for a normal operator $A = \sum_\lambda\lambda P_\lambda$ and a function $f$ on its spectrum, $f(A) = \sum_\lambda f(\lambda)P_\lambda$. A density operator is Hermitian with spectrum in $[0, 1]$, and $\eta$ is defined and continuous there (with $\eta(0) = 0$), so $\eta(\rho) = \sum_\lambda\eta(\lambda)P_\lambda$ and $\operatorname{tr}\eta(\rho) = \sum_\lambda\eta(\lambda)\operatorname{tr}P_\lambda$. The trace of a spectral projector is the multiplicity, so this is $\sum_k\eta(\lambda_k)$ over eigenvalues listed with multiplicity: $H(\lambda)$. The formula $-\operatorname{tr}\rho\log\rho$ is the same thing, read with $\log0$ multiplied by $0$.

Why this and nothing else. Unit 04 proved that $\rho$ determines every measurement statistic, and that different preparation stories with the same $\rho$ cannot be told apart. An entropy that depended on the story would not be a property of the system. Ask also that a unitary — a relabelling of the space — not change it: then it is a function of the spectrum alone. And the spectrum is one of the stories: the eigen-ensemble, a mixture of orthogonal, hence perfectly distinguishable, states, where Shannon's entropy has its full classical meaning. Section 5 gives $S$ its operational meaning directly: it is the number of qubits per symbol a source needs.

History. Von Neumann defined it in 1927 (Göttinger Nachrichten, 273) from a thermodynamic thought experiment — a gas of molecules in internal state $\rho$, separated by idealised semipermeable walls — two decades before Shannon. Cited, not developed.

Full proof of 1–4. All four are the corresponding facts about $H$ applied to the spectrum: $0 \le H \le \log d$ with the equality cases (Gibbs' inequality, and the bounds on H); a density operator with spectrum $(1, 0, \dots, 0)$ is $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi$; one with every eigenvalue $1/d$ is $I/d$; and $U\rho U^\dagger$ has the eigenvectors $U\ket{e_k}$ with the same eigenvalues.

Worked: the entropy of the running mixture

$\ket0$ or $\ket+$, by a fair coin, every step

1. The state. $\rho = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} + \tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}3/4&1/4\\1/4&1/4\end{pmatrix}$, trace $1$ ✓.

$$\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12\big(1 \pm \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big) = \cos^2\tfrac\pi8,\ \sin^2\tfrac\pi8 \approx 0.8536,\ 0.1464 .$$

Sum $1$ ✓.

3. The entropy. $\log0.8536 = -0.2284$ and $\log0.1464 = -2.7716$, so

$$\begin{aligned}S(\rho) &= 0.8536\cdot0.2284 + 0.1464\cdot2.7716\\ &= 0.1950 + 0.4059 = 0.6009 .\end{aligned}$$

4. Check by the Bloch ball. $r = (\tfrac12, 0, \tfrac12)$, $|r| = 0.7071$: $\ h(\tfrac12(1 + 0.7071)) = h(0.8536)$ — the same ✓.

5. The members. $\ket0\!\bra0$ and $\ket+\!\bra+$ are pure: entropy $0$ each.

the number to keep

The average state holds $0.601$ bits; its members hold none. Section 4 names the difference $\chi$.

Each step. Step 1: $\ket+\!\bra+ = \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$, and half of it is $\tfrac14\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$. Step 2: a $2\times2$ matrix has characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr})\lambda + \det$; the discriminant is $1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$, so $\lambda_\pm = \tfrac12(1 \pm \sqrt{1/2})$; and $\cos^2\tfrac\pi8 = \tfrac12(1 + \cos\tfrac\pi4)$ by the double-angle formula. Step 3: $\log_2 0.853553 = \ln0.853553/\ln2 = -0.158353/0.693147 = -0.228447$; $\log_2 0.146447 = -1.921113/0.693147 = -2.771553$. Products $0.194991$ and $0.405885$; sum $0.600876$. Quoted as $0.601$ bits throughout.

Step 4, why it must agree. The Bloch vector of a mixture is the average of the members' vectors (unit 04): $\tfrac12(0, 0, 1) + \tfrac12(1, 0, 0)$. Its length is $\sqrt{1/4 + 1/4} = 1/\sqrt2$, and unit 04's eigenvalue formula $\tfrac12(1 \pm |r|)$ gives step 2's numbers again.

The eigenvectors (unit 04, Worked: a mixture off the centre): $\ket{e_1} = \cos\tfrac\pi8\ket0 + \sin\tfrac\pi8\ket1$ and $\ket{e_2} = -\sin\tfrac\pi8\ket0 + \cos\tfrac\pi8\ket1$ — the polarisations at $22.5^\circ$ and $112.5^\circ$. So the same $\rho$ is also "a source that sends $22.5^\circ$ with probability $0.8536$ and $112.5^\circ$ with probability $0.1464$": the eigen-ensemble, whose Shannon entropy is $S(\rho)$.

Forward. Each member being pure is what makes the second term of the Holevo quantity vanish: $\chi = S(\rho) - \tfrac12S(\ket0\!\bra0) - \tfrac12S(\ket+\!\bra+) = 0.601$. That is the number the Holevo bound will cap Bob at.

08 · von Neumann

Diagonals have more entropy than the spectrum

lemma

For any orthonormal basis $\{\ket{f_k}\}$, the diagonal $d_k = \braket{f_k|\rho|f_k}$ is a distribution and

$$H(d) \ge S(\rho).$$

Measuring in a basis and forgetting the result never lowers the entropy.

proof

With $\rho = \sum_j\lambda_j\ket{e_j}\!\bra{e_j}$: $\ d_k = \sum_jB_{kj}\lambda_j$, $\ B_{kj} = |\braket{f_k|e_j}|^2$. Rows and columns of $B$ sum to $1$ (Parseval). $\eta$ is concave, so $\eta(d_k) \ge \sum_jB_{kj}\eta(\lambda_j)$; sum over $k$. $\blacksquare$

the story's entropy is not the state's

Alice's coin has $H = 1$; her state has $S = 0.601$. For pure-state stories $H(p) \ge S(\rho)$ always, equal only for orthogonal members — problem P3.

Forgetting a $z$ measurement drops $r_x$: the point moves to a smaller circle, so the entropy rises.

Running example. $z$ diagonal $(\tfrac34, \tfrac14)$: $h(\tfrac14) = 0.811 \ge 0.601$ ✓. In the eigenbasis: equality.

Full proof. $d_k = \bra{f_k}\big(\sum_j\lambda_j\ket{e_j}\!\bra{e_j}\big)\ket{f_k} = \sum_j\lambda_j|\braket{f_k|e_j}|^2$. Each $d_k \ge 0$ and $\sum_kd_k = \operatorname{tr}\rho = 1$. The matrix $B$ is doubly stochastic: $\sum_kB_{kj} = \lVert e_j\rVert^2 = 1$ and $\sum_jB_{kj} = \lVert f_k\rVert^2 = 1$, by Parseval in each basis. $\eta(t) = -t\log t$ is concave on $[0, 1]$ ($\eta''(t) = -1/(t\ln2) < 0$), and $d_k$ is a convex combination of the $\lambda_j$ with weights $B_{kj}$ (row sums one), so Jensen's inequality for a finite average gives $\eta(d_k) \ge \sum_jB_{kj}\eta(\lambda_j)$. Summing over $k$ and using the column sums, $H(d) = \sum_k\eta(d_k) \ge \sum_j\eta(\lambda_j)\sum_kB_{kj} = S(\rho)$. $\blacksquare$

Jensen for a finite average (from the background, by induction on the number of terms): for concave $\eta$ and weights $w_j \ge 0$ summing to one, $\eta(\sum w_jt_j) \ge \sum w_j\eta(t_j)$.

The operator meaning. Measuring in $\{f_k\}$ and not recording the outcome sends $\rho$ to $\sum_k\ket{f_k}\!\bra{f_k}\rho\ket{f_k}\!\bra{f_k}$ (unit 04's non-selective measurement), whose spectrum is exactly $d$. So the lemma says: a non-selective measurement in a basis never lowers $S$. In the Bloch picture a $z$ measurement forgotten sends $(r_x, r_y, r_z) \mapsto (0, 0, r_z)$, shortening the vector, and $S$ falls with $|r|$. Running example: $|r|$ from $0.7071$ to $0.5$, entropy $h(0.8536) = 0.601$ to $h(0.75) = 0.811$.

The warning. The coin-flip story uses non-orthogonal members, and its Shannon entropy $1$ exceeds $S(\rho)$. In general, for $\rho = \sum p_i\ket{\psi_i}\!\bra{\psi_i}$, $S(\rho) \le H(p)$ with equality iff the $\psi_i$ are orthogonal; the proof, on the sheet as problem P3, purifies $\rho$ and applies this lemma.

08 · von Neumann

Concavity: mixing never lowers entropy

concavity of $S$ $$S\Big(\sum_ip_i\rho_i\Big) \ \ge\ \sum_ip_iS(\rho_i)$$

for density operators $\rho_i$ and a distribution $p$; equality iff all $\rho_i$ with $p_i > 0$ are equal.

proof

Let $\{\ket{e_k}\}$ be an eigenbasis of $\rho = \sum_ip_i\rho_i$. Then

$$\begin{aligned}S(\rho) &= \textstyle\sum_k\eta\big(\sum_ip_i\braket{e_k|\rho_i|e_k}\big)\\ &\ge \textstyle\sum_ip_i\sum_k\eta\big(\braket{e_k|\rho_i|e_k}\big)\\ &\ge \textstyle\sum_ip_iS(\rho_i),\end{aligned}$$

by concavity of $\eta$, then the diagonal lemma. $\blacksquare$

Running example. $0.601 \ge \tfrac12\cdot0 + \tfrac12\cdot0$: two pure states mixed hold $0.601$ bits — the price of not knowing which.

$\eta(t) = -t\log t$ is concave: the curve lies above every chord. That one fact, applied eigenvalue by eigenvalue, is the theorem.

Full proof. In the eigenbasis of $\rho$, $S(\rho) = \sum_k\eta(\braket{e_k|\rho|e_k})$ (the diagonal is the spectrum). Linearity gives $\braket{e_k|\rho|e_k} = \sum_ip_i\braket{e_k|\rho_i|e_k}$. Concavity of $\eta$ (Jensen with weights $p_i$) gives, for each $k$, $\eta\big(\sum_ip_i\braket{e_k|\rho_i|e_k}\big) \ge \sum_ip_i\eta(\braket{e_k|\rho_i|e_k})$. Sum over $k$ and exchange the sums. Finally $\sum_k\eta(\braket{e_k|\rho_i|e_k})$ is the entropy of $\rho_i$'s diagonal in the basis $\{e_k\}$, which is at least $S(\rho_i)$ by Diagonals have more entropy than the spectrum.

Equality. If all $\rho_i$ are equal, both sides are $S(\rho_1)$. Conversely, $\eta$ is strictly concave, so equality in the first step forces, for each $k$, the numbers $\braket{e_k|\rho_i|e_k}$ to be the same for all $i$ with $p_i > 0$. Equality in the second forces each $\rho_i$'s diagonal in $\{e_k\}$ to have entropy $S(\rho_i)$; by the equality case of the lemma (strict concavity again: each $d_k$ must be an average of equal $\lambda_j$'s) the basis $\{e_k\}$ then diagonalises $\rho_i$. So every $\rho_i$ is diagonal in one basis with one diagonal: they are equal.

The figure. Computed: $\eta(0.1) = 0.332$, $\eta(0.8) = 0.258$, their average $0.295$, and $\eta(0.45) = 0.518$ at the midpoint. The maximum is $\eta(1/e) = 1/(e\ln2) = 0.531$.

An upper bound too. Mixing can raise the entropy by at most the entropy of the mixing: $S(\sum p_i\rho_i) \le \sum p_iS(\rho_i) + H(p)$, with equality iff the $\rho_i$ have orthogonal supports. That is problem P3; the running example has $0.601 \le 0 + 1$.

Relative entropy, and Klein's inequality

relative entropy

$D(\rho\Vert\sigma) = \operatorname{tr}\rho\log\rho - \operatorname{tr}\rho\log\sigma$ if $\operatorname{supp}\rho \subseteq \operatorname{supp}\sigma$, else $+\infty$. Commuting $\rho, \sigma$: Gibbs' $D$ of the spectra.

Klein's inequality

$D(\rho\Vert\sigma) \ge 0$, with equality iff $\rho = \sigma$.

proof

$\rho = \sum_ip_i\ket{r_i}\!\bra{r_i}$, $\sigma = \sum_jq_j\ket{s_j}\!\bra{s_j}$, $P_{ij} = |\braket{r_i|s_j}|^2$, doubly stochastic. Then

$$\begin{aligned}D(\rho\Vert\sigma) &= \textstyle\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}\,p_i\log(p_i/q_j)\\ &\ge \textstyle\frac1{\ln2}\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}(p_i - q_j) = 0,\end{aligned}$$

by $a\ln\frac ab \ge a - b$ (Gibbs' tangent line). Equality forces $p_i = q_j$ whenever $P_{ij} > 0$, hence $\sigma\ket{r_i} = p_i\ket{r_i}$: $\sigma = \rho$. $\blacksquare$

Real vectors in the plane of $\mathbb C^2$ (not the Bloch disc). $P_{11} = \cos^2 22.5^\circ = 0.854$, $P_{12} = 0.146$: the double sum weighs every pair of eigenvectors by their overlap.

Full proof. Write $\operatorname{tr}\rho\log\rho = \sum_ip_i\log p_i$. For the second term, expand in the eigenbasis of $\rho$: $\operatorname{tr}\rho\log\sigma = \sum_ip_i\braket{r_i|\log\sigma|r_i} = \sum_ip_i\sum_j|\braket{r_i|s_j}|^2\log q_j$, because $\log\sigma = \sum_j\log q_j\ket{s_j}\!\bra{s_j}$ on the support of $\sigma$. Terms with $p_i = 0$ contribute nothing; if some $p_i > 0$ and $P_{ij} > 0$ had $q_j = 0$, then $\ket{r_i}$ would have a component outside $\operatorname{supp}\sigma$, which the support condition excludes. Since $\sum_jP_{ij} = 1$, $\sum_ip_i\log p_i = \sum_{i,j}P_{ij}p_i\log p_i$, and the two combine into the displayed double sum. Now $a\ln(a/b) \ge a - b$ for $a \ge 0$, $b > 0$ (put $t = b/a$ in $\ln t \le t - 1$ and multiply by $-a$; at $a = 0$ it reads $0 \ge -b$). Apply it to each term with weight $P_{ij} \ge 0$ and divide by $\ln2$. Finally $\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}p_i = \sum_ip_i = 1$ and $\sum_{i,j}P_{ij}q_j = \sum_jq_j = 1$ (column sums are one), so the bound is $0$.

Equality, in full. Equality in $a\ln(a/b) \ge a - b$ needs $a = b$. So $D = 0$ forces $p_i = q_j$ for every pair with $P_{ij} > 0$. Fix $i$: $\ket{r_i} = \sum_j\braket{s_j|r_i}\ket{s_j}$ involves only $s_j$ with $q_j = p_i$, so $\sigma\ket{r_i} = p_i\ket{r_i}$. This holds for every $i$, and $\{r_i\}$ is a basis, so $\sigma = \sum_ip_i\ket{r_i}\!\bra{r_i} = \rho$.

The spec's form. The same proof is often written with the convexity of $x\log x$: the tangent-line inequality used here is exactly the statement that $x\ln x$ lies above its tangent at $x = b$. And with $\sigma = I/d$, $D(\rho\Vert I/d) = \log d - S(\rho)$: Klein re-proves $S \le \log d$.

History. O. Klein, Z. Phys. 72, 767 (1931), for the operator inequality behind it; the quantum relative entropy itself is Umegaki's (1962). It is not a distance: next slide.

Mutual information is a relative entropy

quantum mutual information

For $\rho_{AB}$ with reduced states $\rho_A, \rho_B$ (unit 04), write $S(A) = S(\rho_A)$ and so on, and

$$I(A:B) = S(A) + S(B) - S(AB).$$
subadditivity

$I(A:B) = D(\rho_{AB}\Vert\rho_A\otimes\rho_B) \ge 0$, so $S(AB) \le S(A) + S(B)$, with equality iff $\rho_{AB} = \rho_A\otimes\rho_B$.

proof

$\log(\rho_A\otimes\rho_B) = \log\rho_A\otimes I + I\otimes\log\rho_B$ (eigenvalues $a_ib_j$, $\log a_ib_j = \log a_i + \log b_j$). By the partial trace's defining property, $\operatorname{tr}\rho_{AB}(\log\rho_A\otimes I) = \operatorname{tr}\rho_A\log\rho_A$. So $-\operatorname{tr}\rho_{AB}\log(\rho_A\otimes\rho_B) = S(A) + S(B)$. Klein. $\blacksquare$

The product of the marginals keeps each half and forgets the correlation; $I$ is how far the true state is from it.

additivity, as a corollary

$S(\rho\otimes\sigma) = S(\rho) + S(\sigma)$: the spectrum is $\{a_ib_j\}$. In particular a pure factor adds nothing: $S(\rho\otimes\ket0\!\bra0) = S(\rho)$.

The logarithm of a tensor product. If $\rho_A = \sum_ia_i\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}$ and $\rho_B = \sum_jb_j\ket{f_j}\!\bra{f_j}$, then $\rho_A\otimes\rho_B = \sum_{i,j}a_ib_j\ket{e_if_j}\!\bra{e_if_j}$ (unit 03's Kronecker rule), an eigen-decomposition in the product basis. On its support ($a_ib_j > 0$) the functional calculus gives $\log(\rho_A\otimes\rho_B) = \sum(\log a_i + \log b_j)\ket{e_if_j}\!\bra{e_if_j}$, which is $\log\rho_A\otimes I + I\otimes\log\rho_B$ restricted to $\operatorname{supp}\rho_A\otimes\operatorname{supp}\rho_B$.

The support condition holds. Let $\Pi_A$ project onto $\operatorname{supp}\rho_A$. Then $\operatorname{tr}\big(((I - \Pi_A)\otimes I)\rho_{AB}\big) = \operatorname{tr}((I - \Pi_A)\rho_A) = 0$, and a positive operator whose expectation on a projector is zero lives in the complement of that projector's range; so $\operatorname{supp}\rho_{AB} \subseteq \operatorname{supp}\rho_A\otimes H_B$, and likewise for $B$. Hence $\operatorname{supp}\rho_{AB} \subseteq \operatorname{supp}(\rho_A\otimes\rho_B)$ and $D$ is finite.

The partial-trace step. Unit 04 defined $\rho_A$ as the unique operator with $\operatorname{tr}((X\otimes I)\rho_{AB}) = \operatorname{tr}(X\rho_A)$ for all $X$. Take $X = \log\rho_A$ (on its support). So $-\operatorname{tr}\rho_{AB}\log(\rho_A\otimes\rho_B) = -\operatorname{tr}\rho_A\log\rho_A - \operatorname{tr}\rho_B\log\rho_B = S(A) + S(B)$, and $D(\rho_{AB}\Vert\rho_A\otimes\rho_B) = -S(AB) + S(A) + S(B)$. Klein gives $\ge 0$ and the equality case.

Check. A product state: $I = 0$ ✓. $\ket{\Phi^+}$: $S(AB) = 0$, $S(A) = S(B) = 1$, $I = 2$ — positive, and larger than any classical pair of bits could manage; The whole can hold less than a part returns to it.

Additivity. $\rho\otimes\sigma$ has eigenvalues $a_ib_j$, and $H(\{a_ib_j\}) = H(a) + H(b)$ — the joint entropy of independent variables. In particular a factor in a pure state, spectrum $(1, 0, \dots)$, adds $0$.

Register and qubit: the running example as one state

a classical–quantum state

Let a register $P$ keep Alice's coin: $\ \rho_{PQ} = \sum_xp_x\ket x\!\bra x\otimes\rho_x$.

lemma: its entropy

$S\big(\sum_xp_x\ket x\!\bra x\otimes\rho_x\big) = H(p) + \sum_xp_xS(\rho_x)$.

Block-diagonal: the eigenvalues are $p_x\lambda_{x,k}$, and $-\sum p_x\lambda_{x,k}\log(p_x\lambda_{x,k})$ splits. $\blacksquare$

So $S(P) = H(p)$, $\ S(Q) = S(\sum_xp_x\rho_x)$, and

$$I(P:Q) = S\big(\textstyle\sum_xp_x\rho_x\big) - \sum_xp_xS(\rho_x) =: \chi .$$

$\rho_{PQ}$ for the running example, register first. Trace $\tfrac12 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = 1$ ✓.

register and qubit, in numbers

$S(P) = 1$, $\ S(Q) = 0.601$, $\ S(PQ) = 1 + \tfrac12\cdot0 + \tfrac12\cdot0 = 1$. $\ I(P:Q) = 1 + 0.601 - 1 = 0.601$; subadditivity $1 \le 1.601$ ✓.

The lemma, in full. In a basis $\ket x\otimes\ket{e_{x,k}}$, where $\{e_{x,k}\}_k$ is an eigenbasis of $\rho_x$, the operator $\sum_xp_x\ket x\!\bra x\otimes\rho_x$ is diagonal with entries $p_x\lambda_{x,k}$: it is block-diagonal, one block $p_x\rho_x$ per value of $x$. These sum to $\sum_xp_x\sum_k\lambda_{x,k} = 1$ ✓. Then $-\sum_{x,k}p_x\lambda_{x,k}\big(\log p_x + \log\lambda_{x,k}\big) = -\sum_xp_x\log p_x\sum_k\lambda_{x,k} + \sum_xp_x\big(-\sum_k\lambda_{x,k}\log\lambda_{x,k}\big) = H(p) + \sum_xp_xS(\rho_x)$.

The marginals. $\operatorname{tr}_Q\rho_{PQ} = \sum_xp_x\operatorname{tr}(\rho_x)\ket x\!\bra x = \sum_xp_x\ket x\!\bra x$, entropy $H(p)$. And $\operatorname{tr}_P\rho_{PQ} = \sum_xp_x\rho_x$, the average state. So $I(P:Q) = H(p) + S(\bar\rho) - H(p) - \sum p_xS(\rho_x)$, the $H(p)$ cancelling.

Why bother with the register. It turns "how much does Alice's ensemble say about $x$" into the mutual information of one bipartite state, so that the inequalities of this section apply to it. The register is a bookkeeping device — "Alice writes $x$ on a piece of paper and keeps it" — and since it is diagonal it is as classical as a quantum system can be. Section 4's proof adds a third system and moves the information between them.

The numbers. With $p = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ and both members pure, $S(PQ) = H(p) = 1$: the spectrum of the figure's matrix is $\tfrac12$ (from the top block) and $\tfrac12, 0$ (from the bottom block, $\tfrac12\ket+\!\bra+$), and $0$ — that is, $(\tfrac12, \tfrac12, 0, 0)$. $I(P:Q) = 0.601$ is less than $H(p) = 1$: the qubit does not hold all of the coin.

Pure states: the entanglement entropy

both halves, one number

If $\ket\psi_{AB} = \sum_ks_k\ket{a_k}\ket{b_k}$ (unit 03's Schmidt form), then $\rho_A$ and $\rho_B$ have the same non-zero eigenvalues $s_k^2$ (unit 04), so

$$S(A) = S(B) = H(s_1^2, s_2^2, \dots).$$
entanglement entropy

$E(\psi) := S(\rho_A)$ for pure $\psi$: $0$ iff $\psi$ is a product, $\log d$ iff maximally entangled. One ebit: $E(\Phi^+) = 1$.

the unique measure (cited)

With local operations and classical communication, $n$ copies of $\psi$ convert to $\approx nE(\psi)$ Bell pairs and back, and no measure other than $E$ is consistent with this (Bennett–Bernstein–Popescu–Schumacher 1996; Popescu–Rohrlich 1997).

Squared Schmidt coefficients, which sum to one in each case ✓, and the entropy of each pair.

Running example. Purify $\rho$ (unit 04): $\sqrt{0.8536}\ket{e_1}\ket0 + \sqrt{0.1464}\ket{e_2}\ket1$. Its entanglement entropy is $S(\rho) = 0.601$: the mixedness of a state is the entanglement it would have with a purifier.

Full proof of the theorem. Unit 04 showed that for a pure state with coefficient matrix $C$, $\rho_A = CC^\dagger$ and $\rho_B$ has the same non-zero spectrum, and that in Schmidt form $\rho_A = \sum_ks_k^2\ket{a_k}\!\bra{a_k}$ and $\rho_B = \sum_ks_k^2\ket{b_k}\!\bra{b_k}$. Entropy ignores zero eigenvalues ($\eta(0) = 0$), so $S(A) = S(B) = H(s^2)$, and $\sum_ks_k^2 = \lVert\psi\rVert^2 = 1$. $E = 0$ iff one Schmidt coefficient is $1$: Schmidt rank one, a product (unit 03). $E = \log d$ iff all $d$ squared coefficients equal $1/d$.

The cited theorem, stated carefully. "Local operations and classical communication" (LOCC) means: Alice and Bob may each do anything quantum to their own systems and may telephone each other, but may not send quantum systems. Bennett, Bernstein, Popescu and Schumacher (Phys. Rev. A 53, 2046, 1996) showed that by LOCC, $n$ copies of a pure state $\psi$ can be converted into $m$ Bell pairs with $m/n \to E(\psi)$ and vanishing error ("concentration"), and $m$ Bell pairs into $n$ copies of $\psi$ with the same ratio ("dilution"). Popescu and Rohrlich (Phys. Rev. A 56, R3319, 1997) showed that any measure of pure-state entanglement that is additive and does not increase under LOCC, normalised to one ebit for $\Phi^+$, must equal $E$ asymptotically. Both are cited; LOCC is not developed in this course.

The purification. $\rho = \lambda_1\ket{e_1}\!\bra{e_1} + \lambda_2\ket{e_2}\!\bra{e_2}$, so unit 04's construction gives $\ket\Psi = \sqrt{\lambda_1}\ket{e_1}\ket0 + \sqrt{\lambda_2}\ket{e_2}\ket1$, already in Schmidt form, with squared coefficients $0.8536, 0.1464$ ✓.

The whole can hold less than a part

Classically, $H(XY) = H(X) + H(Y|X) \ge H(X)$: a pair holds at least as much as either member.

For $\ket{\Phi^+}$: $\rho_{AB}$ is pure, $S(AB) = 0$; $\ \rho_A = \rho_B = I/2$, $S(A) = S(B) = 1$. So

$$S(AB) = 0 \ \lt\ 1 = S(A).$$
quantum conditional entropy

$S(A|B) := S(AB) - S(B)$, the same formula as the classical conditional entropy. For $\ket{\Phi^+}$, $S(A|B) = -1$: negative, which no joint distribution allows.

And $I(A:B) = 1 + 1 - 0 = 2$, where two classical bits manage at most $\min\{H(X), H(Y)\} = 1$.

Perfectly correlated bits against a Bell pair. The pair's whole holds nothing; its correlation is twice the classical maximum.

Why the classical inequality fails. The classical proof of $H(XY) \ge H(X)$ used $p(x, y) = p(x)p(y|x)$: a joint distribution has conditional distributions, whose entropies are non-negative. A quantum state has no analogue of "the state of $B$ given the state of $A$" that is itself a state with an entropy. What survives is the weaker Araki–Lieb inequality on the next slide, $S(AB) \ge |S(A) - S(B)|$, which allows $S(AB) = 0$ with $S(A) = S(B)$.

Negative conditional entropy has a meaning — cited, not developed: Horodecki, Oppenheim and Winter (Nature 436, 673, 2005) showed that when $S(A|B) < 0$, Alice can transfer her share of a state to Bob using classical communication alone and gain $-S(A|B)$ ebits for later use. For $\Phi^+$ that is one ebit: Bob already "has" Alice's half, in the sense that teleportation (unit 07) can move it.

Mutual information $2$. Classically $I(X:Y) \le \min\{H(X), H(Y)\}$. Quantumly $I(A:B) \le 2\min\{S(A), S(B)\}$ (problem P7, from Araki–Lieb), and $\Phi^+$ attains it. Unit 07's dense coding spends exactly these two bits: one qubit plus the pair carries two classical bits. Section 4 makes that precise.

Araki–Lieb: how much less

the Araki–Lieb inequality (1970) $$|S(A) - S(B)| \ \le\ S(AB).$$
proof

Purify $\rho_{AB}$ by a system $R$ (unit 04). $ABR$ is pure, so each part has the entropy of its complement: $S(R) = S(AB)$, $S(AR) = S(B)$. Subadditivity on $AR$:

$$\begin{aligned}S(B) = S(AR) &\le S(A) + S(R)\\ &= S(A) + S(AB).\end{aligned}$$

So $S(B) - S(A) \le S(AB)$; swap $A$ and $B$. $\blacksquare$

Tight for every pure $\rho_{AB}$ ($0 \le 0$) and for $\rho_A\otimes\ket0\!\bra0$ ($S(A) - 0 = S(A)$).

Running example, register and qubit: $S(P) - S(Q) = 0.399 \le 1 = S(PQ)$ ✓.

Purification turns a statement about $AB$ into one about $AR$.

Full proof. Unit 04: $\rho_{AB}$ has a purification $\ket\Psi \in H_A\otimes H_B\otimes H_R$ with $\operatorname{tr}_R\ket\Psi\!\bra\Psi = \rho_{AB}$. For any split of a pure state into two parts, the two reduced states have the same non-zero spectrum (Pure states: the entanglement entropy), so equal entropies. Splitting $ABR$ as $(AB)|R$ gives $S(AB) = S(R)$; as $(AR)|B$ gives $S(AR) = S(B)$. Subadditivity for the pair $A, R$ — with reduced states $\rho_A$ and $\rho_R$ of $\rho_{AR}$ — gives $S(AR) \le S(A) + S(R)$. Substitute. The roles of $A$ and $B$ are symmetric.

When it is tight. If $\rho_{AB}$ is pure, $S(A) = S(B)$ and $S(AB) = 0$, so both sides are $0$. If $\rho_{AB} = \rho_A\otimes\ket0\!\bra0$, $S(B) = 0$ and $S(AB) = S(A)$ by additivity, so $S(A) - S(B) = S(AB)$. In general equality in $S(A) - S(B) \le S(AB)$ means equality in subadditivity for $B, R$, so $\rho_{BR} = \rho_B\otimes\rho_R$ — problem P5 explores what that forces.

The running example's numbers. $S(P) = 1$, $S(Q) = 0.601$, $S(PQ) = 1$. The other direction, $S(Q) - S(P) = -0.399 \le 1$, is weaker. And classically $S(PQ) \ge \max\{S(P), S(Q)\} = 1$, which holds with equality here: the register is classical, and for a classical–quantum state the classical inequality survives (problem P6).

History. H. Araki and E. H. Lieb, Commun. Math. Phys. 18, 160 (1970), who proved both this and subadditivity. Strong subadditivity, conjectured by Lanford and Robinson (1968), was proved by Lieb and Ruskai three years after this paper.

08 · inequalities

Strong subadditivity

strong subadditivity — cited

For every state of three systems $A, B, C$,

$$S(ABC) + S(B) \ \le\ S(AB) + S(BC).$$

Lieb and Ruskai, J. Math. Phys. 14, 1938 (1973). Not proved in this course: the proof needs Lieb's concavity theorem, which is beyond it.

the form this unit uses $$I(A:BC) \ \ge\ I(A:B)$$

Discarding a subsystem never increases mutual information. Expand: $I(A:BC) - I(A:B) = S(AB) + S(BC) - S(ABC) - S(B)$, which is $\ge 0$ exactly by strong subadditivity. $\blacksquare$

Throwing $C$ away can only lose what $A$ and $BC$ shared.

the general statement — cited

monotonicity of relative entropy: $D(\rho_{XY}\Vert\sigma_{XY}) \ge D(\rho_X\Vert\sigma_X)$ (Lindblad 1975, Uhlmann 1977). With $\sigma = \rho_A\otimes\rho_{BC}$ it is $I(A:BC) \ge I(A:B)$. The two are equivalent; neither is derived from the other here.

Why it is cited. Subadditivity took one page because $I(A:B)$ is a single relative entropy and Klein's inequality handles it. Strong subadditivity compares two relative entropies, and no proof of comparable length is known. Lieb and Ruskai derived it from Lieb's concavity theorem (Adv. Math. 11, 267, 1973): for $0 \le t \le 1$ and fixed $K$, the map $(A, B) \mapsto \operatorname{tr}(K^\dagger A^tKB^{1-t})$ is jointly concave on positive operators. Proving that needs complex interpolation or operator-convexity arguments well outside this course. Nielsen and Chuang, appendix 6, give a complete proof for the reader who wants it.

The equivalence, as a statement only. Monotonicity of relative entropy under partial trace implies strong subadditivity by the choice of $\sigma$ on the slide: $D(\rho_{ABC}\Vert\rho_A\otimes\rho_{BC}) = I(A:BC)$ by the computation on Mutual information is a relative entropy, and tracing out $C$ turns it into $D(\rho_{AB}\Vert\rho_A\otimes\rho_B) = I(A:B)$. Conversely, monotonicity can be derived from strong subadditivity (Lindblad 1975, via a further argument not given here). The course uses only the special case.

A known case: classical $A, B, C$. For diagonal states the inequality is $I(X:YZ) \ge I(X:Y)$, and the classical chain rule gives $I(X:YZ) = I(X:Y) + I(X:Z|Y)$, where $I(X:Z|Y) = \sum_yp(y)I(X:Z|Y = y)$ is an average of classical mutual informations, each $\ge 0$ by Gibbs. So the classical case is easy, and what is hard is entirely quantum.

The chain rule, proved. Conditioning on $Y = y$ gives distributions with entropies $H(X|Y = y)$ and so on, and the chain rule of Two variables, averaged over $y$, gives $\sum_yp(y)H(XZ|Y = y) = H(XYZ) - H(Y)$, $\sum_yp(y)H(X|Y = y) = H(XY) - H(Y)$, $\sum_yp(y)H(Z|Y = y) = H(YZ) - H(Y)$. So $I(X:Z|Y) = H(XY) + H(YZ) - H(XYZ) - H(Y)$, and expanding $I(X:YZ) - I(X:Y)$ in joint entropies gives the same four terms.

Where it is used. Twice in this unit: the Holevo bound, and Worked: conditioning, and a separable state.

Two moves: a local unitary, and a discard

move 1: local unitaries change nothing

For a unitary $U$ on $B$ alone, $\rho'_{AB} = (I\otimes U)\rho_{AB}(I\otimes U)^\dagger$ has $I'(A:B) = I(A:B)$.

proof

$S(AB)$: a unitary conjugation, same spectrum. $\rho_A' = \rho_A$: no-signalling (unit 04). $\rho_B' = U\rho_BU^\dagger$: the partial trace commutes with $U$ on $B$. Three entropies unchanged. $\blacksquare$

move 2: a discard can only lose

$I(A:B) \le I(A:BC)$: strong subadditivity.

So no processing on $B$'s side — unitaries on $B$ with fresh systems attached, then discards — can increase what $B$ knows about $A$. The Holevo bound is this sentence applied once.

Wires carry systems left to right; a box is a unitary; the ground symbol is a discard (a partial trace).

Full proof of move 1. $S(AB)$: $\rho'_{AB} = V\rho_{AB}V^\dagger$ with $V = I\otimes U$ unitary, so by property 4 of the von Neumann entropy it is unchanged. $\rho'_A$: unit 04's no-signalling theorem says a unitary on $B$ does not change $\rho_A$ — directly, $\operatorname{tr}((X\otimes I)V\rho V^\dagger) = \operatorname{tr}(V^\dagger(X\otimes I)V\rho) = \operatorname{tr}((X\otimes U^\dagger U)\rho) = \operatorname{tr}((X\otimes I)\rho)$ for every $X$. $\rho'_B$: for every $Y$ on $B$, $\operatorname{tr}((I\otimes Y)V\rho V^\dagger) = \operatorname{tr}((I\otimes U^\dagger YU)\rho) = \operatorname{tr}(U^\dagger YU\rho_B) = \operatorname{tr}(YU\rho_BU^\dagger)$, so by uniqueness of the partial trace $\rho'_B = U\rho_BU^\dagger$, with the same entropy. The same holds with $B$ replaced by a composite $BC$, which is how the Holevo proof uses it.

Attaching a fresh system in a pure state changes no mutual information either: if $\rho_{ABM} = \rho_{AB}\otimes\ket0\!\bra0$ then $S(ABM) = S(AB)$ and $S(BM) = S(B)$ by additivity, so $I(A:BM) = I(A:B)$.

The notation. The wires are only a picture of which operator acts on which tensor factor, as in unit 07; unit 10 makes circuits a formal model. The ground symbol, borrowed from electrical drawings, means the system is no longer looked at — mathematically, the partial trace over it.

The data-processing inequality. Moves 1 and 2 together say: if $B$ is processed by any physical operation built from them, $I(A:B)$ cannot grow. Unit 21 shows that every physical operation on $B$ — every channel — is of this form (Stinespring), which makes this the quantum data-processing inequality. Here only the special case is needed.

Worked: conditioning, and a separable state

two consequences of strong subadditivity, every step

1. Conditioning reduces entropy. Rename strong subadditivity's systems $(A, B, C) \to (B, A, X)$:

$$S(BAX) + S(A) \le S(BA) + S(AX),$$

that is, $S(B|AX) \le S(B|A)$ with $S(B|A) = S(AB) - S(A)$.

2. Conditional entropy is concave. For $\rho_{AB} = \sum_ip_i\rho^{(i)}_{AB}$, attach a register: $\rho_{ABX} = \sum_ip_i\rho^{(i)}_{AB}\otimes\ket i\!\bra i$. By the classical–quantum lemma, applied to $ABX$ and to $AX$,

$$S(B|AX) = \textstyle\sum_ip_i\,S(B|A)_{\rho^{(i)}},$$

the $H(p)$'s cancelling. With step 1: $S(B|A)_\rho \ge \sum_ip_iS(B|A)_{\rho^{(i)}}$.

3. Check on $\ket{\Phi^+}$. $S(B|A) = 0 - 1 = -1$. Mix it half-and-half with $\ket{00}$, for which $S(B|A) = 0 - 0 = 0$: concavity says $S(B|A) \ge -\tfrac12$ for the mixture.

4. Compute. $\rho = \tfrac12\ket{\Phi^+}\!\bra{\Phi^+} + \tfrac12\ket{00}\!\bra{00}$. Then $\rho_A = \mathrm{diag}(\tfrac34, \tfrac14)$, $S(A) = 0.811$. The two members have overlap $|\braket{00|\Phi^+}| = 1/\sqrt2$, so $\rho$ has eigenvalues $\tfrac12(1 \pm \tfrac1{\sqrt2})$ and $S(AB) = 0.601$.

$S(B|A) = 0.601 - 0.811 = -0.210 \ge -0.5$ ✓.

the use

For a product $\rho_A\otimes\rho_B$, $S(B|A) = S(B) \ge 0$. So a mixture of products has $S(B|A) \ge 0$: $S(AB) \ge S(A)$ — problem P6.

Step 1. Strong subadditivity reads $S(ABC) + S(B) \le S(AB) + S(BC)$ for any three systems, $B$ being the one that appears on both sides. Put the present $A$ in the middle slot, $B$ in the first, the register $X$ in the third. Subtract $S(A) + S(AX)$ from both sides: $S(ABX) - S(AX) \le S(AB) - S(A)$.

Step 2, in full. $\rho_{ABX}$ is classical–quantum with the register $X$ and the quantum part $AB$, so $S(ABX) = H(p) + \sum_ip_iS(AB)_{\rho^{(i)}}$. Tracing out $B$ leaves $\rho_{AX} = \sum_ip_i\rho^{(i)}_A\otimes\ket i\!\bra i$, also classical–quantum, so $S(AX) = H(p) + \sum_ip_iS(A)_{\rho^{(i)}}$. Subtract. And $\operatorname{tr}_X\rho_{ABX} = \rho_{AB}$, so step 1's right side is $S(B|A)_\rho$.

Step 4, every number. $\ket{\Phi^+}\!\bra{\Phi^+}$ has $\rho_A = I/2$ and $\ket{00}\!\bra{00}$ has $\rho_A = \ket0\!\bra0$; the average is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12I + \tfrac12\ket0\!\bra0 = \mathrm{diag}(\tfrac34, \tfrac14)$, entropy $h(\tfrac14) = 0.8113$. For $S(AB)$: the mixture of two pure states $\psi, \varphi$ with equal weights has, on their span, the Gram-matrix eigenvalues $\tfrac12(1 \pm |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|)$ (problem P8 proves this); here $\braket{00|\Phi^+} = 1/\sqrt2$, the same overlap as $\braket{0|+}$, so the same spectrum as the running example and $S(AB) = 0.6009$. So $S(B|A) = -0.2104$. (It is a coincidence of these numbers that this equals minus the relative entropy $0.2104$ computed on Worked: Klein, checked with numbers.)

Why the corollary matters. "The whole can hold less than a part" is a signature of entanglement: any state with $S(AB) < S(A)$ is not a mixture of product states. The converse fails: some entangled mixed states have $S(AB) \ge S(A)$ (problem P6's check gives one), so this is a test that can certify entanglement but not rule it out.

08 · Holevo

Alice's message, Bob's measurement

The protocol. Alice picks $x$ with probability $p_x$ and sends a system $Q$ in state $\rho_x$. Bob measures $Q$ with a projective measurement $\{P_y\}$ (unit 01) and gets $y$, so

$$p(x, y) = p_x\operatorname{tr}(P_y\rho_x),$$

summing to $\sum_xp_x\operatorname{tr}(\sum_yP_y\rho_x) = \sum_xp_x = 1$ ✓.

accessible information

$I_{\mathrm{acc}} = \max\,I(X:Y)$ over Bob's measurements: what one copy can tell him about $x$, at best.

the Holevo quantity $$\chi = S\Big(\sum_xp_x\rho_x\Big) - \sum_xp_xS(\rho_x).$$

Section 3: $\chi = I(P:Q)$ for register and qubit. It needs no measurement at all.

One use of the channel: a classical $x$ in, a quantum system across, a classical $y$ out.

Running example. $Q$ is the photon, $\rho_x \in \{\ket0\!\bra0, \ket+\!\bra+\}$, Bob's measurement an analyser at some angle. $\chi = 0.601 - 0 = 0.601$ (section 2).

two different questions

$\chi$ is a property of the ensemble; $I_{\mathrm{acc}}$ is what a measurement extracts. The theorem says which way they are ordered.

The joint distribution. Given $x$, Bob's outcome has the Born probabilities $\operatorname{tr}(P_y\rho_x)$ (unit 04's density-operator form of unit 01's rule); multiplying by $p_x$ gives $p(x, y)$. It sums to one because $\sum_yP_y = I$ and $\operatorname{tr}\rho_x = 1$. Its marginal on $y$ is $\operatorname{tr}(P_y\bar\rho)$ with $\bar\rho = \sum_xp_x\rho_x$: Bob's outcome statistics, ignoring $x$, depend only on the average state, as unit 04 says they must.

Which measurements. Here "measurement" means a projective one on $Q$, possibly after Bob attaches an extra system of his own in a fixed state and measures projectively on the pair. That already includes every measurement there is: unit 21 shows (Naimark's theorem) that the general measurements, POVMs, are exactly these. The proof of this section covers the extended case at no cost, because attaching a pure system changes no entropy, so $\chi$ is the same for $\{p_x, \rho_x\}$ and $\{p_x, \rho_x\otimes\ket a\!\bra a\}$.

The physics of the running example. Bob's analyser at angle $\theta$ measures in $\{\ket\theta, \ket{\theta + 90^\circ}\}$ (unit 03). Rotating the crystal is how he chooses the measurement; the two detectors are the two outcomes.

Why "accessible". It is the information accessible to Bob from one system, by the best single measurement. It is generally hard to compute — an optimisation over all measurements — which is what makes an upper bound in closed form, like $\chi$, valuable.

08 · Holevo

The Holevo bound

the Holevo bound (Holevo 1973)

For every ensemble $\{p_x, \rho_x\}$ and every measurement with outcome $Y$,

$$I(X:Y) \ \le\ \chi,$$

the Holevo quantity $S(\sum_xp_x\rho_x) - \sum_xp_xS(\rho_x)$ of the ensemble.

corollary: a qubit carries at most one bit

$\chi \le S(\bar\rho) \le \log d$, since each $S(\rho_x) \ge 0$. On a qubit, $I(X:Y) \le 1$ — however many states Alice chooses from. Unit 07's citation is discharged.

Also $\chi \ge 0$ (concavity), and $\chi \le H(p)$ (problem P3): never more than Alice put in.

Running example. $I(X:Y) \le 0.601$ for every analyser angle — the next three slides prove it, and the three after test it.

Every measurement lands below $\chi$; $\chi$ lands below the bit Alice put in.

Full proof of the corollary. $S(\rho_x) \ge 0$ gives $\chi \le S(\bar\rho)$, and $S(\bar\rho) \le \log d$ for any state on $\mathbb C^d$. So $I(X:Y) \le \log d$. For $n$ qubits, $d = 2^n$ and $I \le n$. This holds whatever the number of states in the ensemble: a continuum of polarisation angles buys nothing beyond one bit per photon.

The other bounds on the figure. $\chi \ge 0$ is concavity of $S$. $\chi \le H(p)$ is the upper bound $S(\sum p_x\rho_x) \le \sum p_xS(\rho_x) + H(p)$, proved on the sheet; so $I(X:Y) \le H(X)$ is recovered, as it must be classically. The two measured values are computed on Worked: the Helstrom measurement extracts 0.399 bits.

History. A. S. Holevo, Problems of Information Transmission 9, 177 (1973), proved it by a direct operator argument; the bound had been conjectured by Gordon (1964) and stated without proof by Levitin (1969). The proof given here, through strong subadditivity, is the one in Nielsen and Chuang §12.1.1, and it is the reason this unit develops the entropy inequalities at all: once strong subadditivity is granted, the bound is a few lines.

What it does not say. It bounds what one measured copy yields. With many copies and collective measurements across them, rates up to $\chi$ per copy are achievable — that is a different theorem (the HSW theorem, named on The gap between 0.399 and 0.601), and it does not beat $\chi$ either.

Proof, 1: three systems

$P$ keeps Alice's $x$; $Q$ is the system she sends; $M$ is Bob's apparatus, a pointer with one position per outcome. Bob's measurement is a unitary on $QM$, then he discards $Q$ and reads $M$.

At $t_0$. $\ \rho_{PQM} = \sum_xp_x\ket x\!\bra x\otimes\rho_x\otimes\ket0\!\bra0$, trace $\sum_xp_x = 1$ ✓.

$M$ is a pure factor, which adds no entropy: $\ I(P:QM)_{t_0} = I(P:Q) = \chi$, by Register and qubit.

The idea of the proof. Section 3's moves say that operations on one side never increase that side's mutual information with the other. So: start with a state in which $Q$'s mutual information with Alice's register is exactly $\chi$; model Bob's measurement as operations on his side only — a unitary on $Q$ and his apparatus, then throwing $Q$ away — and end with a state whose mutual information is exactly $I(X:Y)$. The inequality falls out.

$I(P:QM)_{t_0} = I(P:Q)$ in full. By additivity, $S(PQM) = S(PQ) + S(\ket0\!\bra0) = S(PQ)$ and $S(QM) = S(Q)$; $S(P)$ is the same in both. So $I(P:QM) = S(P) + S(Q) - S(PQ) = I(P:Q)$, which is $\chi$ by the classical–quantum lemma.

The pointer. $M$ has an orthonormal basis $\{\ket y\}$, one vector per outcome of Bob's measurement — a dial with one position per result. Unit 01 modelled its which-path detector simply as a measurement, and warned that a detector which lets the photon through must mark the path on some other system; this is that other system, made explicit: it ends up in $\ket y$, correlated with the branch $y$ of the measured system. Unit 05's CNOT, which copies the $z$ basis, is the simplest example. Here the pointer is the whole of Bob's apparatus, idealised; the next slide builds the unitary that moves it.

Running example, at $t_0$. $\rho_{PQM} = \tfrac12\ket0\!\bra0\otimes\ket0\!\bra0\otimes\ket0\!\bra0 + \tfrac12\ket1\!\bra1\otimes\ket+\!\bra+\otimes\ket0\!\bra0$, and $I(P:QM) = 0.601$.

Proof, 2: a measurement is a unitary, then a look

the pointer unitary

$V\ket\psi = \sum_yP_y\ket\psi\otimes\ket y$ is an isometry $H_Q \to H_Q\otimes H_M$:

$$\begin{aligned}\lVert V\psi\rVert^2 &= \textstyle\sum_y\lVert P_y\psi\rVert^2\\ &= \braket{\psi|\textstyle\sum_yP_y|\psi} = \lVert\psi\rVert^2 .\end{aligned}$$

It extends to a unitary $U$ on $QM$: $U(\ket\psi\ket0) = V\ket\psi$.

At $t_1$, by linearity in $\rho_x$:

$$\rho_{PQM} = \textstyle\sum_xp_x\ket x\!\bra x\otimes\sum_{y,y'}P_y\rho_xP_{y'}\otimes\ket y\!\bra{y'} .$$

At $t_2$, trace out $Q$: $\operatorname{tr}(P_y\rho_xP_{y'}) = \operatorname{tr}(P_{y'}P_y\rho_x) = 0$ for $y \ne y'$, so

$$\rho_{PM} = \sum_{x,y}p(x, y)\,\ket x\!\bra x\otimes\ket y\!\bra y .$$

Each branch of $\psi$ is tagged by a pointer position; reading $M$ in $\{\ket y\}$ reads the outcome.

$\rho_{PM}$ is diagonal: its entropies are Shannon entropies of $p(x, y)$ and its marginals, so $I(P:M)_{t_2} = I(X:Y)$.

Check. For the $z$ measurement on a qubit, $U$ is CNOT from $Q$ to $M$ (unit 05): $\ket j\ket0 \mapsto \ket j\ket j$.

The isometry, in full. For $\psi, \varphi \in H_Q$: $\braket{V\psi|V\varphi} = \sum_{y,y'}\braket{P_y\psi|P_{y'}\varphi}\braket{y|y'} = \sum_y\braket{\psi|P_y\varphi} = \braket{\psi|\varphi}$, using $P_y^\dagger = P_y = P_y^2$ and $\sum_yP_y = I$. So $V$ preserves inner products.

Extending to a unitary. The map $\ket\psi\otimes\ket0 \mapsto V\ket\psi$ is a linear isometry from the subspace $H_Q\otimes\ket0$ (dimension $d$) into $H_Q\otimes H_M$ (dimension $dm$, with $m$ outcomes). Its image has dimension $d$. Choose orthonormal bases of the two orthogonal complements, both of dimension $dm - d$, and send one to the other. The combined map sends an orthonormal basis to an orthonormal basis, so it is unitary. What $U$ does off $H_Q\otimes\ket0$ never matters: $M$ starts in $\ket0$.

$t_1$ in full. For a pure $\rho_x = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$: $U(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\otimes\ket0\!\bra0)U^\dagger = V\ket\psi\!\bra\psi V^\dagger = \sum_{y,y'}P_y\ket\psi\!\bra\psi P_{y'}\otimes\ket y\!\bra{y'}$. Both sides are linear in $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi$, and every $\rho_x$ is a combination of such projectors (its spectral decomposition), so the formula holds for every $\rho_x$. $U$ acts on $QM$ only, so the register factor is untouched.

$t_2$ in full. The partial trace over $Q$ of $X\otimes\ket y\!\bra{y'}$ is $\operatorname{tr}(X)\ket y\!\bra{y'}$; with $X = P_y\rho_xP_{y'}$, cyclicity gives $\operatorname{tr}(P_{y'}P_y\rho_x)$, which is $\delta_{yy'}\operatorname{tr}(P_y\rho_x)$ since distinct spectral projectors multiply to zero. A diagonal state $\sum p(x, y)\ket{xy}\!\bra{xy}$ has spectrum $p(x, y)$; its marginals are $\sum_xp(x)\ket x\!\bra x$ and $\sum_yp(y)\ket y\!\bra y$. So the three von Neumann entropies are $H(XY)$, $H(X)$, $H(Y)$.

The CNOT check. For $P_0 = \ket0\!\bra0$, $P_1 = \ket1\!\bra1$: $V(\alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1) = \alpha\ket0\ket0 + \beta\ket1\ket1$, which is CNOT$_{QM}$ applied to $(\alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1)\ket0$. Problem P9 follows the running example through this circuit.

The cited result. L. B. Levitin, "Optimal quantum measurements for two pure and mixed states", in Quantum Communications and Measurement (Plenum, 1995), 439–448: for two pure states with equal priors the accessible information is attained by the projective measurement symmetric about the pair — here the Helstrom basis — and equals $1 - h\big(\tfrac12(1 - \sqrt{1 - c^2})\big)$ with $c = |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|$. The HSW theorem, as a statement. A. S. Holevo, IEEE Trans. Inf. Theory 44, 269 (1998), and B. Schumacher and M. D. Westmoreland, Phys. Rev. A 56, 131 (1997): if Alice may use long codewords $x_1\cdots x_n$ (each $x_i$ sent as $\rho_{x_i}$) and Bob may make one entangled measurement on all $n$ systems, the achievable rate is $\chi$ bits per system, maximised over ensembles. For the running ensemble that is $0.601$ bits per photon, $50\%$ more than any photon-by-photon reading. The proof uses typical subspaces of the kind section 5 builds, plus a decoding argument this course omits.

The point for this unit. Holevo's bound is about one use and one measurement, and it is not tight there; the reason $\chi$ is the right quantity is that it becomes tight in the many-copy limit.

Worked: dense coding saturates Holevo

unit 07's protocol, measured against $\chi$

1. The ensemble. Alice applies $I, X, Z$ or $XZ$ to her half of $\ket{\Phi^+}$, each with probability $\tfrac14$ (sum $1$ ✓), making the four Bell states $\ket{\beta_x}$ on Bob's two qubits at the end.

2. The average. The Bell states are an orthonormal basis, so $\tfrac14\sum_x\ket{\beta_x}\!\bra{\beta_x} = \tfrac14I$: $\ S = \log4 = 2$.

3. $\chi$. Each $\ket{\beta_x}$ is pure: $\chi = 2 - 0 = 2$ — the maximum, $\log d$ with $d = 4$.

4. Attained. Bob's Bell measurement returns $y = x$ surely: $I(X:Y) = H(X) = 2 = \chi$ ✓.

5. The qubit in flight, alone: each Bell state has reduced state $I/2$, so $\chi_{\text{sent}} = S(I/2) - 1 = 0$. Intercepted, it tells nothing.

Unit 07's dense coding. Bob ends with two qubits, so $\chi \le \log4 = 2$, and the protocol attains it.

unit 07's debt, paid

One qubit alone: $\le 1$ bit. One qubit and one ebit: Bob holds two qubits, $\le 2$ bits — so dense coding cannot be improved, even with more entanglement (notes), and unit 07's citation now rests on a proved theorem.

Each step. Step 1: unit 07, $(\sigma\otimes I)\ket{\Phi^+}$ for $\sigma \in \{I, X, Z, XZ\}$ gives $\ket{\Phi^+}, \ket{\Psi^+}, \ket{\Phi^-}$ and $\ket{\Psi^-}$ up to sign. Step 2: resolution of the identity in the Bell basis (unit 00's $\sum\ket e\!\bra e = I$ for any orthonormal basis); $I/4$ has spectrum $(\tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14, \tfrac14)$ and entropy $2$. Step 4: the Bell measurement is a projective measurement whose projectors are the four $\ket{\beta}\!\bra\beta$, so $p(y|x) = |\braket{\beta_y|\beta_x}|^2 = \delta_{xy}$; then $H(Y|X) = 0$ and $I = H(Y) = 2$.

Step 5. Unit 04: every Bell state has both reduced states $I/2$. So the ensemble on the travelling qubit alone is $I/2$ for every $x$: $\chi = S(I/2) - \sum_x\tfrac14S(I/2) = 1 - 1 = 0$. By the Holevo bound an eavesdropper who captures it, without Bob's half, learns nothing about $x$ — the information is in the correlation, not in either qubit.

The resource count. Before the protocol, Bob's half is uncorrelated with $x$ (Alice has not chosen yet). At the end, the $x$-dependent state is on Bob's two qubits, and the Holevo bound on $\mathbb C^4$ caps the information at $2$ bits. Without the ebit, Bob would end with the one received qubit: $\le 1$ bit. Unit 07's "1 ebit + 1 qubit $\ge$ 2 cbits" is therefore exact. (Unit 07's proof that teleportation needs both classical bits rested on its guessing lemma, not on this bound.)

Full proof: no amount of entanglement beats $2n$. Unit 07 also cited that with any shared entangled state, $n$ qubits carry at most $2n$ bits. Let Bob hold $B'$ of the shared state, and let Alice, after acting on her side according to $x$, send $n$ qubits $Q$; Bob ends with $\rho_x$ on $QB'$. Nothing Alice does changes $\rho_{B'}$ (no-signalling, unit 04), so $S(B')$ is the same for every $x$ and for the average $\bar\rho$. Subadditivity gives $S(\bar\rho) \le S(Q)_{\bar\rho} + S(B') \le n + S(B')$; Araki–Lieb gives $S(\rho_x) \ge S(B') - S(Q)_{\rho_x} \ge S(B') - n$. Subtract: $\chi \le 2n$, and the Holevo bound on $QB'$ finishes it. With $n = 1$ that is dense coding's $2$.

Schumacher compression

Shannon's theorem, with qubits: a source of quantum states needs $S(\rho)$ qubits per symbol, and the typical sequences become a typical subspace.

08 · Schumacher

Typical subspaces

$\rho = \sum_k\lambda_k\ket{e_k}\!\bra{e_k}$. By the Kronecker rule (unit 03), $\rho^{\otimes n}$ has eigenvectors $\ket{e_{k_1}\cdots e_{k_n}}$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_{k_1}\cdots\lambda_{k_n}$: its spectrum is the distribution of $n$ independent draws from $\lambda$.

the typical subspace

$\Pi_{n,\delta}$ projects onto the span of the $\ket{e_{k_1}\cdots e_{k_n}}$ whose index sequence is $\delta$-typical for $\lambda$.

the typical-subspace lemma

(i) $\operatorname{tr}(\Pi\rho^{\otimes n}) = \Pr(T_{n,\delta}) \to 1$. (ii) $\operatorname{rank}\Pi \le 2^{n(S + \delta)}$. (iii) A projector $P$ of rank $\le 2^{nR}$ with $R < S - \delta$ has $\operatorname{tr}(P\rho^{\otimes n}) \le 2^{-n(S - \delta - R)} + \Pr(T^c) \to 0$.

(i) and (ii) are section 1's lemma for the distribution $\lambda$, whose entropy is $S(\rho)$; (iii) is in the notes.

The running example's $\rho^{\otimes n}$: weight held by the subspace of its largest eigenvalues, against that subspace's size. As $n$ grows the curve becomes a step at $S(\rho)$.

At $n = 1000$ this is section 1's coin: $k$ counts the factors $e_2$, each of weight $0.1464$.

The spectrum of $\rho^{\otimes n}$. $\rho^{\otimes n}\ket{e_{k_1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{e_{k_n}} = \rho\ket{e_{k_1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\rho\ket{e_{k_n}} = \lambda_{k_1}\cdots\lambda_{k_n}\ket{e_{k_1}\cdots e_{k_n}}$, and these $d^n$ product vectors form an orthonormal basis (unit 03). So the eigenvalue attached to the index sequence $k_1\cdots k_n$ is exactly the probability of that sequence under $n$ independent draws from $\lambda$.

(i) and (ii). $\operatorname{tr}(\Pi\rho^{\otimes n})$ is the sum of the eigenvalues of the typical index sequences, which is $\Pr(T_{n,\delta})$ for the distribution $\lambda$; the rank of $\Pi$ is the number of typical sequences. Both are the classical lemma.

(iii), full proof. $\Pi$ is a sum of eigenprojectors of $\rho^{\otimes n}$, so it commutes with it, and $\rho^{\otimes n} = \Pi\rho^{\otimes n}\Pi + \Pi^\perp\rho^{\otimes n}\Pi^\perp$ with $\Pi^\perp = I - \Pi$. First term: $\Pi\rho^{\otimes n}\Pi$ is positive with largest eigenvalue at most $2^{-n(S - \delta)}$ (typical eigenvalues), so $\operatorname{tr}(P\Pi\rho^{\otimes n}\Pi) \le 2^{-n(S - \delta)}\operatorname{tr}P \le 2^{-n(S - \delta)}2^{nR}$, using $\operatorname{tr}(PA) = \sum_i\braket{p_i|A|p_i} \le \operatorname{rank}(P)\lVert A\rVert$ for $A \ge 0$ and an orthonormal basis $p_i$ of $P$'s range. Second term: $\operatorname{tr}(P\Pi^\perp\rho^{\otimes n}\Pi^\perp) \le \operatorname{tr}(\Pi^\perp\rho^{\otimes n}) = \Pr(T^c)$, since $0 \le P \le I$. Add.

The figure. For each $n$, order the eigenvalues of $\rho^{\otimes n}$ from largest down — all sequences with $k$ factors $e_2$ share the eigenvalue $0.8536^{n-k}0.1464^k$ and there are $\binom nk$ of them — and plot the cumulative weight against $\tfrac1n\log_2$ of the cumulative count. For $n = 1000$: weight $0.5$ at $0.595$, $0.9$ at $0.631$, $0.99$ at $0.659$. At $n = 100\,000$, weight $0.99$ needs $0.607$. The convergence to $0.601$ is slow, like $1/\sqrt n$.

08 · Schumacher

Schumacher's theorem

Schumacher compression (Schumacher 1995)

A source emits pure states $\ket{\psi_x}$ with probabilities $p_x$, independently, $n$ times; $\rho = \sum_xp_x\ket{\psi_x}\!\bra{\psi_x}$. A scheme of rate $R$ encodes the $n$ systems into $nR$ qubits and decodes back; its fidelity is the average of $\braket{\psi_{x^n}|\sigma_{x^n}|\psi_{x^n}}$ over the source, $\sigma_{x^n}$ the decoded state. (a) If $R > S(\rho)$, schemes with fidelity $\to 1$ exist. (b) If $R < S(\rho)$, every scheme has fidelity $\to 0$.

The achievability half: keep the typical subspace, throw the rest. With $R > S + \delta$ the typical subspace fits in $nR$ qubits.

(a), its structure. Fidelity $\ge 2\operatorname{tr}(\Pi\rho^{\otimes n}) - 1 \to 1$, by the lemma's (i) and (ii); proof in the notes.

(b), cited. Its core is the lemma's (iii): no $2^{nR}$-dimensional subspace holds much of $\rho^{\otimes n}$. Turning that into a bound on every scheme needs unit 21's channels.

(a), the sketch made complete for this encoder. Fix $\delta$ with $S + \delta < R$; then $\operatorname{rank}\Pi \le 2^{n(S + \delta)} \le 2^{nR}$. Choose a unitary $W$ on the $n$ qubits sending the range of $\Pi$ into the span of $\ket{z}\otimes\ket{0\cdots0}$ with $z$ ranging over $nR$-qubit strings (possible since the target has dimension $2^{nR} \ge \operatorname{rank}\Pi$). The encoder measures $\{\Pi, I - \Pi\}$. On $\Pi$ it applies $W$ and sends the first $nR$ qubits (the rest are in $\ket{0\cdots0}$); on $I - \Pi$ it sends a fixed state $\ket j$ that decodes into the typical subspace. The decoder appends $\ket{0\cdots0}$ and applies $W^\dagger$. For input $\psi$, with probability $\braket{\psi|\Pi|\psi}$ the output is $\Pi\psi/\lVert\Pi\psi\rVert$, contributing fidelity $\braket{\psi|\Pi|\psi}\cdot|\braket{\psi|\Pi\psi}|^2/\braket{\psi|\Pi|\psi} = \braket{\psi|\Pi|\psi}^2$; the failure branch contributes $\ge 0$. And $a^2 \ge 2a - 1$ (as $(a - 1)^2 \ge 0$). So fidelity $\ge 2\braket{\psi|\Pi|\psi} - 1$; averaging over the source, $\sum_{x^n}p(x^n)\braket{\psi_{x^n}|\Pi|\psi_{x^n}} = \operatorname{tr}(\Pi\rho^{\otimes n})$ because the average of $\ket{\psi_{x^n}}\!\bra{\psi_{x^n}}$ is $\rho^{\otimes n}$. The lemma's (i) sends this to $1$.

(b), what is cited. B. Schumacher, Phys. Rev. A 51, 2738 (1995), with R. Jozsa and B. Schumacher, J. Mod. Opt. 41, 2343 (1994), for the simplified proof. For encoders that are unitaries plus discards of the kind drawn, the decoded states live in a subspace of dimension $2^{nR}$; with $P$ its projector, each decoded $\sigma$ satisfies $\sigma = P\sigma P \le P$, so $\braket{\psi|\sigma|\psi} \le \braket{\psi|P|\psi}$ and the average fidelity is at most $\operatorname{tr}(P\rho^{\otimes n})$, which (iii) sends to $0$. General encoders, which may entangle with an environment, are channels — unit 21's subject — and the converse for them is cited.

What "qubits" buys. Classical compression may copy and inspect the source's output; here the states are unknown to the encoder and cannot be copied (unit 05), and the measurement of $\{\Pi, I - \Pi\}$ is allowed precisely because it almost never disturbs a typical input.

1000 symbols in about 601 qubits

Compressing the source: Alice emits $\ket0$ or $\ket+$ by fair coins, $n = 1000$ times.

  • Uncompressed: $1000$ qubits.
  • Leading order: $nS(\rho) = 1000 \times 0.601 = 601$ qubits.
  • At $n = 1000$ exactly: the subspace of $\rho^{\otimes1000}$'s largest eigenvalues holding weight $0.99$ has dimension $2^{659.2}$ — so $660$ qubits, fidelity $\ge 2(0.99) - 1 = 0.98$.
  • Below rate: any $2^{500}$-dimensional subspace holds weight at most $2^{-50.9} + \Pr(T^c)$ (lemma (iii), $\delta = 0.05$), and $\to 0$ as $n$ grows.
not the coin

Alice's coins carry $1000$ bits, and Bob could not recover them from $601$ qubits — nor from the uncompressed $1000$ (section 4). Compression reproduces the states, not the coins.

Overlapping states compress; orthogonal ones, with $S(I/2) = 1$, do not.

The orthogonal source $\ket0$ or $\ket1$ has $\rho = I/2$, $S = 1$: no compression at all.

The numbers. Leading order: $1000 \times 0.6009 = 600.9$, quoted as about $601$. The exact count at $n = 1000$: take the eigenvectors of $\rho^{\otimes1000}$ in decreasing order of eigenvalue — all $\binom{1000}k$ sequences with $k$ factors $e_2$ share the eigenvalue $0.8536^{1000-k}0.1464^k$ — until the weight reaches $0.99$. That happens inside the class $k = 173$, after $2^{659.19}$ eigenvectors. The fidelity bound $2w - 1$ is from Schumacher's theorem's notes. The "below rate" line is the lemma's (iii) with $R = 0.5$ and $\delta = 0.05$: $2^{-n(0.5509 - 0.5)}$, which for $n = 1000$ is $2^{-50.9}$; the atypical weight is $\Pr(T^c) = 0.073$.

Why fidelity, not exact recovery. A pure state cannot be compressed exactly into fewer dimensions than its source spans; what Schumacher compresses is the typical behaviour, with an error that vanishes as $n \to \infty$. That is the quantum version of Shannon's vanishing error probability.

The warning, in Holevo's terms. Each of Alice's qubits carries at most $\chi = 0.601$ bits about her coin, and the $601$ compressed qubits carry at most $601$ bits — less than the $1000$ coin bits. The compressed qubits are as good as the originals for anything Bob might do with them; they are not as good as Alice's notebook. That $S(\rho)$ is both the compression rate and (for pure-state ensembles) the Holevo quantity is no accident: both count the dimension the source effectively occupies.
