The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: sixteen photons. Alice's bits \(0110\,1001\,1100\,1010\) in
bases \(\mathtt{zzxz\,xxzx\,zxxz\,zxxz}\); Bob's bases
\(\mathtt{zxxx\,xzzz\,xxzz\,xxzz}\). The bases agree at photons 1, 3, 5, 7, 10,
12, 14, 16, giving the sifted key \(01101000\); a sample at 3, 7, 12, 16 shows
no error, leaving the raw key \(0110\). With Eve measuring every photon in a
random basis, Bob's bit is wrong at 7 and 14 (\(Q = 25\%\)), the sample reads
\(\hat Q = \frac14\), and the rate \(1 - 2h(\frac14) = -0.623\) says abort. At
\(Q = 5\%\), \(10^6\) sifted bits would leave \(427\,206\) secret ones.

* Overview

- quantum key distribution (QKD) :: A protocol by which two parties, joined by
  a quantum channel Eve may tamper with and an authenticated public channel she
  may only read, agree on a shared random key whose secrecy rests on quantum
  mechanics rather than on a computational assumption. /Slide: Sixteen photons,
  and someone may be listening./

* The protocol

- one-time pad :: Encryption \(c = m\oplus k\) with a uniformly random key
  \(k\) as long as the message and used once. The ciphertext is uniform and
  independent of the message (Shannon 1949): perfect secrecy, at the price of
  distributing the key. /Slide: What a shared key buys: the one-time pad./
- photon :: An indivisible lump of light: a sensitive detector clicks, never
  half-clicks, and dimming the light makes clicks rarer, not smaller. A single
  photon at a polarising beam splitter reaches exactly one detector; its
  polarisation is a qubit. /Slide: Single photons, from nothing./
- BB84 states :: \(\ket0, \ket1\) (the \(\mathtt z\) basis, H and V) and
  \(\ket\pm = \frac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 \pm \ket1)\) (the \(\mathtt x\) basis,
  \(\pm45^\circ\)), with bit 0 sent as \(\ket0\) or \(\ket+\) and bit 1 as
  \(\ket1\) or \(\ket-\). The two bases are mutually unbiased: measured in the
  right basis a state gives its bit surely, in the wrong one a fair coin.
  /Slide: Four states, two bases./
- BB84 :: Bennett and Brassard's 1984 protocol: transmission of the BB84 states
  with random bits and bases, sifting, parameter estimation, error correction
  and privacy amplification, over an authenticated public channel. /Slide:
  BB84, step by step./
- sifting :: Announcing the bases (never the bits) and keeping only the
  photons where Alice's and Bob's bases agree — half of them on average.
- sifted key :: The bits kept by sifting. Where the bases agree, Bob's result
  equals Alice's bit unless something disturbed the photon.
- parameter estimation :: Revealing a random sample of the sifted bits, chosen
  after the transmission, to estimate the error rate; the sample is then
  discarded, and the run aborted if the rate is too high.
- quantum bit error rate (QBER) :: \(Q\), the probability that Bob's sifted
  bit differs from Alice's, estimated as \(\hat Q\) from the sample. The only
  number by which Eve's presence is measured.
- error correction (information reconciliation) :: Public discussion that
  makes Bob's string equal to Alice's, at a cost of at least about \(h(Q)\)
  published bits per bit (Slepian–Wolf, cited); Cascade does it with parities
  and binary search. Every published parity is heard by Eve.
- privacy amplification :: Shrinking the corrected string with a publicly
  chosen random hash from a universal family, so that Eve's partial knowledge
  of the input becomes negligible knowledge of the output (Bennett, Brassard
  and Robert 1988, cited).
- binary entropy :: \(h(Q) = -Q\log Q - (1 - Q)\log(1 - Q)\), base 2: the
  bits of uncertainty per symbol in independent flips of probability \(Q\), and
  the price per bit of correcting them or hiding them. \(h(0) = 0\),
  \(h(\frac12) = 1\), \(h(0.05) = 0.2864\). /Slide: The binary entropy: how
  many bits an error rate hides./
- universal hashing :: A family of functions \(f : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^m\)
  with \(\Pr_f(f(x) = f(y)) \le 2^{-m}\) for all \(x \ne y\) (Carter and Wegman
  1979); \(m\) parities of independent random subsets is one. Keeping \(n - t -
  s\) bits leaves an Eve who knew \(t\) bits' worth an advantage exponentially
  small in \(s\) (leftover hash lemma, cited). /Slide: Privacy amplification:
  hashing Eve's knowledge away./
- authenticated channel (authenticated) :: A public channel on which Eve may
  read every message but cannot alter or invent one undetected; made from an
  ordinary channel by Wegman–Carter tags, which need a short shared secret.
  /Slide: The public line must be authenticated./
- key expansion :: What QKD actually does: it turns a short pre-shared secret,
  needed to authenticate the public channel, into a long one. It cannot create
  a key from nothing.

* What looking costs Eve

- information–disturbance trade-off (information–disturbance) :: For the BB84
  states: if an interaction \(U(\ket s\ket e) = \ket s\ket{e_s}\) leaves all
  four undisturbed, then \(e_0 = e_1 = e_+ = e_-\) and Eve learns nothing. Any
  information gained shows up as errors. /Slide: Undisturbed means
  uninformed./
- intercept-resend attack :: Eve measures each photon in a randomly chosen
  basis and sends Bob a fresh photon in the state she found. On the sifted key
  it causes \(Q = \frac14\) and lets Eve guess each bit with probability
  \(\frac34\); on a fraction \(f\) of photons, \(Q = f/4\). /Slide: The
  simplest attack: measure, then resend./
- Breidbart basis :: The analyser halfway between \(\mathtt z\) and \(\mathtt
  x\), at \(22.5^\circ\) and \(112.5^\circ\), each direction \(22.5^\circ\)
  from both states encoding one bit value. Intercept-resend in it guesses each
  sifted bit with probability \(\cos^2\frac\pi8 \approx 0.8536\) at the same
  \(Q = 25\%\). /Slide: Gentler attacks do better./

* From error rate to key rate

- Shor–Preskill rate :: \(r(Q) = 1 - 2h(Q)\) secret bits per sifted bit for
  BB84 with one-way post-processing, against every attack, for long keys (Shor
  and Preskill 2000, cited). One \(h(Q)\) pays for error correction, the other
  for privacy amplification. \(r(0.05) = 0.427\); \(r = 0\) at \(Q =
  0.1100\). /Slide: The Shor–Preskill rate./
- phase error rate :: The error rate in the basis conjugate to the key's. It
  bounds Eve's knowledge of the key; with random bases it is estimated by the
  same \(Q\). /Slide: Why the second h(Q)./

* The same key from Bell pairs

- E91 :: Ekert's 1991 protocol: a source sends singlets, Alice and Bob measure
  in randomly chosen settings, key comes from matching settings and a CHSH test
  from the others. Here Alice uses Bloch angles \(0, \frac\pi2\) and Bob \(0,
  \frac\pi2, \pm\frac\pi4\). /Slide: E91: a source in the middle./
- key round, test round :: In E91, a round with equal settings (perfectly
  anticorrelated outcomes, so key after Bob flips his bit), and a round with
  Alice's setting against one of Bob's \(\pm\frac\pi4\) (feeding the CHSH value
  \(S\), \(-2\sqrt2\) on the singlet). /Slide: Key rounds and test rounds./
- BBM92 :: Bennett, Brassard and Mermin's 1992 entanglement version of BB84:
  singlets measured in \(\mathtt z\) or \(\mathtt x\), no Bell test. Equivalent
  to BB84, since Alice's measurement prepares a BB84 state on Bob's side.
  /Slide: BBM92, and devices nobody trusts./
- self-testing :: The theorem (Summers and Werner 1987; Popescu and Rohrlich
  1992; cited) that devices reaching \(|S| = 2\sqrt2\) must, up to local
  isometries, hold a singlet and measure unit 06's observables — without
  trusting how they were built.
- device-independent QKD :: Key distribution whose security rests only on the
  observed CHSH value, with the devices treated as black boxes. Its key rate
  dies at \(Q \approx 7.1\%\) for uniform noise (cited); it was first
  demonstrated in 2022.

* What QKD does not do

- man-in-the-middle attack :: Without authentication, Eve runs BB84 separately
  with Alice (posing as Bob) and with Bob (posing as Alice), holding both keys;
  every check passes. /Slide: Without authentication: the man in the middle./
- photon-number-splitting attack :: On a pulse carrying two photons in Alice's
  state, Eve keeps one and forwards the other untouched, then measures hers in
  the announced basis: the bit with certainty, and no error (Brassard,
  Lütkenhaus, Mor and Sanders 2000, cited). /Slide: Real sources sometimes send
  two photons./
- decoy states :: Pulses of randomly varied intensity, announced afterwards
  (Hwang 2003; Lo, Ma and Chen 2005). Eve's action can depend on the photon
  number but not the intensity, so comparing detection rates across intensities
  exposes a photon-number-splitting attack. /Slide: Decoy states, and the gaps
  that remain./
