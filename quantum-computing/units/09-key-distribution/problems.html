Quantum key distribution: disturbance as a security guarantee

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. On paper and without notes, you prove that intercept-resend in a random basis causes a 25 per cent error rate on the sifted key — all eight cases, probabilities summing to one — and that Eve then guesses each sifted bit with probability \(\tfrac34\); and you produce the 11.0 per cent threshold from the Shor–Preskill rate \(1 - 2h(Q)\), solving \(2h(Q) = 1\) to three places, with \(r(0.05) = 0.427\) on the way.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about four and a half hours. Work them on paper first.

Conventions, as in the deck: \(\braket{u|v}\) is conjugate-linear in the first slot; \(\ket0 = (1, 0)\), \(\ket1 = (0, 1)\) are horizontal and vertical polarisation, the \(\mathtt z\) basis; \(\ket\pm = (\ket0 \pm \ket1)/\sqrt2\) are \(\pm45^\circ\), the \(\mathtt x\) basis. Bit 0 is sent as \(\ket0\) or \(\ket+\), bit 1 as \(\ket1\) or \(\ket-\). In \(\ket{ab}\) the first symbol is qubit 0. \(Q\) is the error rate on the sifted key. \(h(Q) = -Q\log Q - (1 - Q)\log(1 - Q)\) with \(\log\) base 2, and the Shor–Preskill rate is \(r(Q) = 1 - 2h(Q)\) secret bits per sifted bit (cited). The singlet is \(\ket{\Psi^-} = (\ket{01} - \ket{10})/\sqrt2\), \(A(\theta) = \cos\theta\,Z + \sin\theta\,X\), and \(S = E(a, b) + E(a, b') + E(a', b) - E(a', b')\) with E91's settings \(a = 0\), \(a' = \tfrac\pi2\), \(b = \tfrac\pi4\), \(b' = -\tfrac\pi4\).

Some solutions end with an optional check by the course's referee, qclib, run from the course directory with uv run python. Every solution is complete without it.

The protocol

P1 Twelve more photons routine

exercises: sifting, parameter estimation on a sample, the rate as a decision, why small samples decide nothing

A second run of BB84, with no eavesdropper but a slightly noisy channel:

photon123456789101112
Alice's bit100111010010
Alice's basisxzzxzxxzzxzz
Bob's basisxxzzzxzzxxzx
Bob's result110011101010
  1. Sift: which photons are kept, and what are Alice's and Bob's sifted strings? Where do they differ?
  2. Alice announces the sample \(3, 8, 11\). Compute \(\hat Q\), and the raw key that remains.
  3. Compute \(r(\hat Q)\). What do Alice and Bob do — and was that the right decision about this run?
  4. How likely was it that sifting kept exactly as many photons as it did?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every answer here is read off the table, except the last two, which are one evaluation of \(h\) and one binomial probability.

Rung 2

Compare the two basis rows column by column. Where they agree, Bob's result should equal Alice's bit; where it does not, that is an error. The sample's error rate is (mismatches)/(sample size).

Rung 3

First line: "The bases agree at photons 1, 3, 5, 6, 8, 10, 11, so seven of twelve are kept, and Alice's sifted string is \(1011101\)."

Worked solution

Idea. Sifting and sampling are bookkeeping; the rate turns the sample into a decision, and the decision is only as good as the sample.

1. Bases agree at photons \(1\) (\(\mathtt x\)), \(3\) (\(\mathtt z\)), \(5\) (\(\mathtt z\)), \(6\) (\(\mathtt x\)), \(8\) (\(\mathtt z\)), \(10\) (\(\mathtt x\)), \(11\) (\(\mathtt z\)): seven kept. Alice's bits there: \(1, 0, 1, 1, 1, 0, 1\), so \(1011101\). Bob's: \(1, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1\), so \(1011001\). They differ only at photon 8: one error in seven, a true sifted error rate of \(1/7 = 0.143\).

2. At \(3, 8, 11\) Alice reveals \(0, 1, 1\) and Bob has \(0, 0, 1\): one mismatch, so \(\hat Q = 1/3\). The remaining positions \(1, 5, 6, 10\) give the raw key \(1110\), the same on both sides.

3. \(h(\tfrac13) = \tfrac13\log3 + \tfrac23\log\tfrac32 = \log3 - \tfrac23 = 1.584963 - 0.666667 = 0.918296\), so \(r(\tfrac13) = 1 - 1.836592 = -0.8366 \lt 0\): they abort. As a decision about this run it was unlucky — there was no Eve, and the four remaining bits agree — but the protocol charges every error to Eve, and a three-bit sample that happens to contain the run's only error cannot tell noise from attack. That is the deck's warning that sixteen, or twelve, photons are far too few; the sample size needed for a useful estimate is problem P8's.

4. Each photon is kept with probability \(\tfrac12\), independently, so the number kept is binomial\((12, \tfrac12)\) and \(\Pr(7) = \binom{12}7/2^{12} = 792/4096 = 0.193\).

Check. Kept plus discarded: \(7 + 5 = 12\) ✓. Sample plus raw key: \(3 + 4 = 7\) ✓. And at the five discarded photons, Bob's results \(1, 0, 1, 1, 0\) against Alice's \(0, 1, 0, 0, 0\) agree only at photon 12 — irrelevant, as wrong-basis results should be.

P2 Sifting efficiency medium

exercises: the sifting probability, biased bases, estimating error rates per basis, why an Eve who prefers one basis is caught

  1. With uniform, independent basis choices, what fraction of photons survives sifting?
  2. Suppose Alice and Bob each choose \(\mathtt z\) with probability \(p\) and \(\mathtt x\) with \(1 - p\). Find the sifting rate, and evaluate it at \(p = 0.9\).
  3. With \(p = 0.9\), Eve measures every photon in \(\mathtt z\) and resends what she finds. Compute the error rate in the sifted \(\mathtt z\) rounds, in the sifted \(\mathtt x\) rounds, and pooled over all sifted rounds. What does Eve learn?
  4. Explain why biased bases remain secure, provided the two error rates are estimated separately (Lo, Chau and Ardehali 2005, cited).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answers to 1 and 2 are probabilities that two independent choices agree. Part 3 is the eight-case table of the deck with Eve's basis fixed.

Rung 2

Two bases agree if both are \(\mathtt z\) or both are \(\mathtt x\). In part 3, Eve's \(\mathtt z\) measurement leaves \(\mathtt z\) states alone; on an \(\mathtt x\) state it resends \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), which Bob, measuring in \(\mathtt x\), reads as a coin.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "\(\Pr(\text{agree}) = p\cdot p + (1 - p)(1 - p) = p^2 + (1 - p)^2\)."

Worked solution

Idea. Biasing the bases raises the sifting rate towards one, but makes the rare basis a place where a lopsided Eve hides — unless each basis's error rate is checked on its own.

1. \(\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12\).

2. \(p^2 + (1 - p)^2\). At \(p = 0.9\): \(0.81 + 0.01 = 0.82\). As \(p \to 1\) it tends to \(1\); its minimum is \(\tfrac12\), at \(p = \tfrac12\) (derivative \(2p - 2(1 - p) = 0\)).

3. Sifted \(\mathtt z\) rounds: Eve measures in Alice's basis, reads the bit, resends Alice's own state: error \(0\). Sifted \(\mathtt x\) rounds: Eve's \(\mathtt z\) outcome is a coin, she resends \(\ket0\) or \(\ket1\), and Bob's \(\mathtt x\) result is a coin: error \(\tfrac12\). Sifted rounds are \(\mathtt z\) with probability \(0.81/0.82\) and \(\mathtt x\) with \(0.01/0.82 = 0.0122\), so the pooled rate is \[ Q_{\text{pooled}} = \frac{0.81\cdot0 + 0.01\cdot\tfrac12}{0.82} = 0.0061 . \] Eve learns every \(\mathtt z\) key bit exactly — \(98.8\%\) of the sifted key — while the pooled error rate, \(0.6\%\), looks like a quiet channel and gives a Shor–Preskill rate of \(1 - 2h(0.0061) = 0.89\). Pooling would be a disaster.

4. Estimate \(Q_{\mathtt z}\) and \(Q_{\mathtt x}\) separately, each on a sample of its own basis. In part 3, \(Q_{\mathtt x} = \tfrac12\) and they abort. In general Eve does not know the basis of any photon, so her attack is the same on every photon, and the \(\mathtt x\) rounds — though rare — sample her effect on the conjugate basis fairly. Lo, Chau and Ardehali (J. Cryptology 18, 133, 2005) prove the protocol secure with the key rate computed from the separately estimated rates, at a sifting efficiency approaching one as \(p \to 1\) for long keys (the rare basis still needs enough rounds for its own estimate).

Check. At \(p = \tfrac12\), part 3's attack gives \(Q_{\mathtt z} = 0\), \(Q_{\mathtt x} = \tfrac12\), pooled \(\tfrac14\) — an Eve who always uses \(\mathtt z\), the case in the notes to the deck's The simplest attack: measure, then resend ✓. The weights \(0.81/0.82 + 0.01/0.82 = 1\) ✓.

P3 Intercept-resend causes 25 per cent medium

exercises: conditioning on the sifted event, the eight cases, the law of total probability, the trade-off for a fraction \(f\)

  1. Prove that if Eve measures every photon in a uniformly random basis and resends the state she found, then on the sifted key \(Q = \tfrac14\), and Eve guesses each sifted bit correctly with probability \(\tfrac34\).
  2. Prove that attacking each photon independently with probability \(f\) gives \(Q = f/4\), and that after the basis announcement Eve knows exactly a fraction \(f/2\) of the sifted bits.
  3. Alice and Bob observe \(Q = 3\%\). If Eve is doing partial intercept-resend, what \(f\), and what fraction of the sifted key does she know?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is an average over cases with known probabilities. The first thing to settle is which variables are independent once you condition on "this photon was sifted".

Rung 2

Sifting depends only on Alice's and Bob's bases, so given sifting, Bob's basis equals Alice's, while Alice's basis, her bit and Eve's basis remain independent and uniform. Split on "Eve's basis = Alice's basis".

Rung 3

First line: "Condition on Eve's basis. If it equals Alice's (probability \(\tfrac12\)), Eve's outcome is Alice's bit and she resends Alice's state, so Bob errs with probability \(0\)."

Worked solution

Idea. Half the time Eve is in the right basis and invisible; half the time she is in the wrong one, and then two independent coins — hers and Bob's — decide everything.

1. Condition on the photon being sifted. Sifting is a function of Alice's and Bob's bases only, which are independent of Alice's bit and of Eve's choices; so conditionally, Bob's basis is Alice's, and Alice's basis \(A\), her bit \(a\) and Eve's basis \(E\) are independent and uniform.

Case \(E = A\) (probability \(\tfrac12\)). Eve measures Alice's state \(\ket s\) in its own basis: outcome \(a\) with probability \(|\braket{s|s}|^2 = 1\). She resends \(\ket s\); Bob, in the same basis, reads \(a\). Error \(0\); Eve right with probability \(1\).

Case \(E \ne A\) (probability \(\tfrac12\)). The bases are mutually unbiased, so Eve's two outcomes each have probability \(\tfrac12\), whatever \(a\) is: her guess is right with probability \(\tfrac12\). She resends a state \(\ket t\) of basis \(E\), and Bob measures it in basis \(A\): wrong with probability \(|\braket{\bar s|t}|^2 = \tfrac12\), where \(\bar s\) is the other state of basis \(A\), again by mutual unbiasedness.

By total probability: \(Q = \tfrac12\cdot0 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14\) and \(\Pr(\text{right}) = \tfrac12\cdot1 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac34\). \(\blacksquare\) Written as the deck's table: eight cases \((A, a, E)\), weight \(\tfrac18\) each, summing to \(1\); four with error \(0\), four with error \(\tfrac12\).

2. Let \(F\) be "attacked", probability \(f\), independent of everything else. Unattacked sifted photons have no error. So \(Q = f\cdot\tfrac14 + (1 - f)\cdot0 = f/4\). Eve knows a bit exactly iff she attacked and used the right basis, which she can tell after the announcement: probability \(f\cdot\tfrac12\). On the rest she has a coin (wrong basis) or nothing (unattacked). \(\blacksquare\)

3. \(f = 4Q = 0.12\), and Eve knows \(f/2 = 2Q = 6\%\) of the sifted bits. For comparison the Shor–Preskill rate charges \(h(0.03) = 0.194\) bits per bit for privacy amplification — over three times what this Eve knows, because it is priced for the worst attack (problem P5 is a worse one).

Check. \(f = 1\) recovers part 1 ✓; \(f = 0\) gives \(Q = 0\) ✓. Within the case \(E \ne A\) Bob's outcomes \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1\) ✓.

With the referee

from qclib import Statevector
label = {("z", 0): "0", ("z", 1): "1", ("x", 0): "+", ("x", 1): "-"}
ket = lambda basis, bit: Statevector.from_label(label[basis, bit])
Q = right = 0.0
for A in "zx":                 # Alice's basis; Bob measures in it too (sifted)
    for a in (0, 1):           # Alice's bit
        for E in "zx":         # Eve's basis
            for e in (0, 1):   # Eve's outcome, resent as ket(E, e)
                p_e = abs(ket(E, e).inner(ket(A, a))) ** 2
                p_wrong = abs(ket(A, 1 - a).inner(ket(E, e))) ** 2
                Q += p_e * p_wrong / 8
                right += p_e * (e == a) / 8
print(round(Q, 6), round(right, 6))   # 0.25 0.75

P4 Eve in the Breidbart basis medium

exercises: a measurement chosen for a bit rather than a state, the Born rule on four states, the error a resent state causes, unit 05's Helstrom value

Eve measures every photon in the basis \(\ket{b_0} = \cos\tfrac\pi8\ket0 + \sin\tfrac\pi8\ket1\), \(\ket{b_1} = -\sin\tfrac\pi8\ket0 + \cos\tfrac\pi8\ket1\) (an analyser at \(22.5^\circ\)), guesses bit 0 on \(b_0\) and bit 1 on \(b_1\), and resends the state she found.

  1. Check that \(\{\ket{b_0}, \ket{b_1}\}\) is orthonormal, and that \(\ket{b_0}\) is \(22.5^\circ\) from both \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\).
  2. Compute Eve's probability of guessing a sifted bit correctly.
  3. Compute the error rate she causes on the sifted key.
  4. Unit 05's best measurement for \(\ket0\) against \(\ket+\) is the analyser at \(-22.5^\circ\). Show that Eve does equally well with it, once the bases are announced.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Both answers are averages over the four states Alice may send, each with probability \(\tfrac14\), of squared overlaps with \(\ket{b_0}\) and \(\ket{b_1}\). Symmetry will make all four terms equal.

Rung 2

Each BB84 state is at angle \(22.5^\circ\) from one Breidbart vector and \(67.5^\circ\) from the other. The error needs two steps: Eve's outcome, then Bob's measurement of the resent \(\ket{b_e}\) in Alice's basis.

Rung 3

First line of part 3: "If Alice sent \(\ket0\), Eve finds \(b_0\) with probability \(c^2\) and \(b_1\) with \(s^2\), where \(c = \cos\tfrac\pi8\), \(s = \sin\tfrac\pi8\); then Bob, measuring \(\ket{b_0}\) in \(\mathtt z\), reads \(1\) with probability \(s^2\)."

Worked solution

Idea. Eve wants the bit, not the state. The bit-0 states \(\ket0\) and \(\ket+\) sit at \(0^\circ\) and \(45^\circ\); their bisector is the best single direction for both — and the price, in errors, turns out to be exactly intercept-resend's.

1. \(\braket{b_0|b_0} = c^2 + s^2 = 1\), \(\braket{b_1|b_1} = s^2 + c^2 = 1\), \(\braket{b_0|b_1} = -cs + sc = 0\) ✓, with \(c = \cos\tfrac\pi8 = 0.92388\), \(s = \sin\tfrac\pi8 = 0.38268\). \(\braket{0|b_0} = c = \cos22.5^\circ\); \(\braket{+|b_0} = (c + s)/\sqrt2 = \cos(\tfrac\pi4 - \tfrac\pi8) = \cos22.5^\circ\) ✓.

2. \(|\braket{b_0|0}|^2 = c^2\), \(|\braket{b_0|+}|^2 = c^2\), \(|\braket{b_1|1}|^2 = c^2\), \(|\braket{b_1|-}|^2 = \tfrac12(-s - c)^2 = c^2\) (using \((c + s)^2/2 = c^2\) from part 1). Each state yields the right guess with probability \(c^2\), so \[ \Pr(\text{right}) = \cos^2\tfrac\pi8 = \tfrac12\big(1 + \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big) = 0.8536, \] unit 05's Helstrom value, on every sifted bit.

3. Alice sends \(\ket0\): Eve finds \(b_0\) (probability \(c^2\)) and resends it; Bob's \(\mathtt z\) measurement gives \(1\) with \(|\braket{1|b_0}|^2 = s^2\). Or Eve finds \(b_1\) (probability \(s^2\)); Bob gives \(1\) with \(|\braket{1|b_1}|^2 = c^2\). So \(\Pr(\text{error}) = c^2s^2 + s^2c^2 = 2c^2s^2 = \tfrac12\sin^2\tfrac\pi4 = \tfrac14\). The other three states give the same by symmetry (each is \(22.5^\circ\) from one Breidbart vector and \(67.5^\circ\) from the other, and so is the state of the same basis with the other bit). \(Q = \tfrac14\).

4. The analyser at \(-22.5^\circ\) has vectors \(e_+ = c\ket0 - s\ket1\) and \(e_- = s\ket0 + c\ket1\). \(\ket0\) and \(\ket-\) are \(22.5^\circ\) from \(e_+\); \(\ket1\) and \(\ket+\) from \(e_-\). So with "\(e_+\) means bit 0" Eve is right with probability \(c^2\) on \(\mathtt z\) rounds and wrong with probability \(c^2\) on \(\mathtt x\) rounds. After the announcement she flips her \(\mathtt x\)-round guesses: right with \(c^2\) everywhere, and the errors are unchanged, \(\tfrac14\).

Check. Against intercept-resend: same \(Q = \tfrac14\), guess \(0.8536\) instead of \(0.75\) — a better attack, as the deck's information–disturbance plot shows. Eve's two outcome probabilities for \(\ket0\): \(c^2 + s^2 = 1\) ✓.

Nothing measured on the photon does better, even using the announcement. Let Eve measure in any orthonormal basis \(\{\ket{f_0}, \ket{f_1}\}\) and, having seen \(f_j\), guess \(g_{\mathtt z}(j)\) on \(\mathtt z\) rounds and \(g_{\mathtt x}(j)\) on \(\mathtt x\) rounds. The four (basis, bit) pairs are equally likely, so \[ \Pr(\text{right}) = \sum_j\braket{f_j|\sigma_j|f_j}, \qquad \sigma_j = \tfrac14\big(\ket{u_j}\!\bra{u_j} + \ket{v_j}\!\bra{v_j}\big), \] with \(\ket{u_j}\) the \(\mathtt z\) state of bit \(g_{\mathtt z}(j)\) and \(\ket{v_j}\) the \(\mathtt x\) state of bit \(g_{\mathtt x}(j)\). Every such pair has \(|\braket{u|v}| = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\), and \(\ket u\!\bra u + \ket v\!\bra v\) has trace \(2\) and determinant \(1 - |\braket{u|v}|^2\) (it is \(WW^\dagger\) for the matrix \(W\) with columns \(u, v\), and \(\det WW^\dagger = \det W^\dagger W\) is the Gram determinant), so eigenvalues \(1 \pm |\braket{u|v}|\). Hence \(\braket{f_j|\sigma_j|f_j} \le \tfrac14(1 + \tfrac1{\sqrt2})\), and \(\Pr(\text{right}) \le \tfrac12(1 + \tfrac1{\sqrt2}) = c^2\): the Breidbart basis is optimal. (Unit 21's general measurements obey the same bound, with \(\braket{f_j|\sigma_j|f_j}\) replaced by \(\operatorname{tr}(M_j\sigma_j) \le \tfrac14(1 + \tfrac1{\sqrt2})\operatorname{tr}M_j\) and \(\sum_j\operatorname{tr}M_j = 2\).)

import numpy as np
from qclib import Statevector
c, s = np.cos(np.pi / 8), np.sin(np.pi / 8)
b = [Statevector([c, s]), Statevector([-s, c])]          # guess 0, guess 1
label = {("z", 0): "0", ("z", 1): "1", ("x", 0): "+", ("x", 1): "-"}
ket = lambda basis, bit: Statevector.from_label(label[basis, bit])
Q = right = 0.0
for A in "zx":
    for a in (0, 1):
        for e in (0, 1):
            p_e = abs(b[e].inner(ket(A, a))) ** 2
            Q += p_e * abs(ket(A, 1 - a).inner(b[e])) ** 2 / 4
            right += p_e * (e == a) / 4
print(round(right, 4), round(Q, 4))   # 0.8536 0.25

P5 A gentler attack hard

exercises: an interaction defined on a basis and extended to a unitary, the Helstrom bound, reduced states, the trade-off as a curve

Eve couples each photon to a probe qubit of her own, starting in \(\ket0\), by the unitary with

\[ U\ket0\ket0 = \ket0\ket0, \qquad U\ket1\ket0 = \ket1\ket{e_\varphi}, \qquad \ket{e_\varphi} = \cos\varphi\ket0 + \sin\varphi\ket1, \]

for a fixed \(0 \le \varphi \le \tfrac\pi2\); the photon goes on to Bob, and Eve measures her probe after the basis announcement.

  1. Show that such a unitary exists.
  2. On sifted \(\mathtt z\) rounds: find Bob's error rate, and Eve's best probability of guessing the bit.
  3. On sifted \(\mathtt x\) rounds: find Bob's error rate, and show Eve learns nothing.
  4. Deduce \(Q = (1 - \cos\varphi)/4\) and \(\Pr(\text{right}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14\sqrt{8Q - 16Q^2}\). Evaluate at \(Q = 0.05\) and compare with intercept-resend.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1: a map defined on part of an orthonormal basis extends to a unitary when it sends that part to an orthonormal family. Part 2 is unit 05's Helstrom bound for Eve's two possible probe states. Part 3 is a reduced state.

Rung 2

By linearity \(U\ket+\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket0 + \ket1\ket{e_\varphi})\). Bob's \(\mathtt x\) error is the probability that the photon is found in \(\ket-\); Eve's probe state is the partial trace over the photon, which you can compute in the \(\mathtt z\) basis of the photon.

Rung 3

First line of part 3: "\(\Pr(\text{Bob reads }-) = \lVert(\bra-\otimes I)\,U\ket+\ket0\rVert^2 = \tfrac14\lVert\ket0 - \ket{e_\varphi}\rVert^2\)."

Worked solution

Idea. A weak, partial copy of the \(\mathtt z\) value: the probe barely moves, so Bob's \(\mathtt x\) states are barely disturbed, but the information it gains grows like the square root of the disturbance. That is why gentle attacks beat intercept-resend.

1. \(\ket0\ket0\) and \(\ket1\ket{e_\varphi}\) are orthonormal (different first factors). Send the other two basis vectors to \(\ket0\ket1\) and \(\ket1\ket{e_\varphi^\perp}\) with \(\ket{e_\varphi^\perp} = -\sin\varphi\ket0 + \cos\varphi\ket1\): four orthonormal images of four orthonormal vectors, so \(U\) is unitary. (It is a controlled rotation: \(\ket1\) on the photon turns the probe by \(\varphi\).)

2. On \(\mathtt z\) rounds the photon leaves in Alice's state, so Bob's error is \(0\). Eve's probe is \(\ket0\) (bit 0) or \(\ket{e_\varphi}\) (bit 1), equally likely, with overlap \(\cos\varphi\). Unit 05's Helstrom bound: \[ \Pr(\text{right}\mid\mathtt z) = \tfrac12\big(1 + \sqrt{1 - \cos^2\varphi}\big) = \tfrac12(1 + \sin\varphi). \]

3. \(U\ket\pm\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket0 \pm \ket1\ket{e_\varphi})\). Take \(\ket+\): Bob reads \(-\) with probability \(\lVert(\bra-\otimes I)U\ket+\ket0\rVert^2 = \lVert\tfrac12(\ket0 - \ket{e_\varphi})\rVert^2 = \tfrac14(2 - 2\cos\varphi) = \tfrac12(1 - \cos\varphi)\), using \(\braket{-|0} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\), \(\braket{-|1} = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\). The same for \(\ket-\). Eve's probe: tracing out the photon in its \(\mathtt z\) basis, \(\rho_E = \tfrac12\ket0\!\bra0 + \tfrac12\ket{e_\varphi}\!\bra{e_\varphi}\) for both \(\ket+\) and \(\ket-\) — the sign sits on the cross terms, which the partial trace removes. Identical states: by unit 04, no measurement of her probe tells the bit, so \(\Pr(\text{right}\mid\mathtt x) = \tfrac12\).

4. Sifted rounds are \(\mathtt z\) or \(\mathtt x\) with probability \(\tfrac12\) each: \[ Q = \tfrac12\cdot0 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12(1 - \cos\varphi) = \tfrac14(1 - \cos\varphi), \qquad \Pr(\text{right}) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12(1 + \sin\varphi) + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14\sin\varphi . \] From the first, \(\cos\varphi = 1 - 4Q\), so \(\sin\varphi = \sqrt{1 - (1 - 4Q)^2} = \sqrt{8Q - 16Q^2}\). At \(Q = 0.05\): \(\cos\varphi = 0.8\), \(\varphi = 36.87^\circ\), \(\sin\varphi = \sqrt{0.4 - 0.04} = 0.6\), and \(\Pr(\text{right}) = 0.5 + 0.15 = 0.65\). Intercept-resend at the same \(Q\) gets \(\tfrac12 + Q = 0.55\).

Check. \(\varphi = 0\): \(U = I\), \(Q = 0\), guess \(\tfrac12\) ✓ (and Undisturbed means uninformed). \(\varphi = \tfrac\pi2\): \(U\) acts as \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\) on every input with the probe in \(\ket0\), which is all that is used (it differs from \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\) only by the sign of \(U\ket1\ket1 = -\ket1\ket0\)), \(Q = \tfrac14\), guess \(\tfrac34\) — intercept-resend's numbers, from a different attack ✓. Near \(Q = 0\), \(\Pr(\text{right}) - \tfrac12 \approx \tfrac14\sqrt{8Q}\): a square root, so the first percent of error buys Eve \(\tfrac14\sqrt{0.08} = 0.071\) of guessing advantage, against intercept-resend's \(0.01\).

import numpy as np
from qclib import Statevector, gates
phi = np.arccos(1 - 4 * 0.05)            # cos φ = 1 - 4Q, aiming at Q = 0.05
def attack(label):                       # photon is qubit 0, Eve's probe qubit 1
    return Statevector.from_label(label + "0").apply_controlled(gates.ry(2 * phi), [0], [1])
err_x = attack("+").apply(gates.H, 0).probabilities([0])[1]   # Bob reads − on a + round

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is single-variable calculus; part 3 asks for a ratio that should tend to \(1\).

Rung 2

Differentiate \(-Q\log Q\) as \(-\frac1{\ln2}(Q\ln Q)\): its derivative is \(-\log Q - \frac1{\ln2}\). Part 3 needs \(\log\binom nk\), which a calculator or \(\ln k! \approx k\ln k - k\) handles.

Rung 3

Worked solution

Idea. \(h\) is a smooth hump on \([0, 1]\), steep at the ends; its value is a count of patterns in disguise.

1. Swapping \(Q\) and \(1 - Q\) swaps the two terms: symmetric. The derivative is as in the last rung; it is positive on \((0, \tfrac12)\), zero at \(\tfrac12\), negative after. \(h''(Q) = \frac1{\ln2}\big(-\frac1Q - \frac1{1 - Q}\big) = -\frac{1}{\ln2\,Q(1 - Q)} \lt 0\): strictly concave, maximum at \(\tfrac12\), where \(h = -\tfrac12\log\tfrac12 - \tfrac12\log\tfrac12 = 1\).

3. With \(k = Qn\):

\(n\)\(\log\binom n{0.05n}\)\(h(0.05)\,n\)ratio
\(100\)\(26.17\)\(28.64\)\(0.914\)
\(1000\)\(282.28\)\(286.40\)\(0.986\)
\(10\,000\)\(2858.20\)\(2863.97\)\(0.998\)

Rung 1

Part 1 is \(h(0.05)\) times \(10^6\), twice. Part 2: the sample's errors are independent indicators with mean \(Q\).

Rung 2

P9 A CHSH value of \(2\sqrt2\) leaves Eve nothing hard

exercises: the CHSH operator at fixed settings, uniqueness of the maximiser, purification, the product criterion for pure states, independence

Alice, Bob and Eve share a state \(\rho_{ABE}\), with Alice and Bob each holding a qubit and Eve anything. Alice measures \(Z\) or \(X\), Bob \((Z + X)/\sqrt2\) or \((Z - X)/\sqrt2\), and the test rounds give \(S = -2\sqrt2\).

  1. Show that \(\rho_{AB} = \ket{\Psi^-}\!\bra{\Psi^-}\).
  2. Prove: if the reduced state \(\rho_{AB}\) of any \(\rho_{ABE}\) is pure, \(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\), then \(\rho_{ABE} = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi\otimes\rho_E\).
  3. Deduce that the outcomes of every measurement Eve makes are independent of Alice's and Bob's outcomes.
  4. Why does the argument say nothing if the test rounds give \(S = -2.8\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is unit 06's uniqueness theorem; part 2 is unit 04's purification plus unit 04's problem P7. Part 3 is a trace of a tensor product.

Rung 2

Write \(S = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{AB}C)\) with \(C\) the CHSH operator, and find \(C\)'s spectrum. For part 2, purify \(\rho_{ABE}\) on \(ABER\) and apply P7 to the split \(AB \mid ER\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: "With \(B = (Z + X)/\sqrt2\), \(B' = (Z - X)/\sqrt2\): \(B + B' = \sqrt2Z\) and \(B - B' = \sqrt2X\), so \(C = Z\otimes(B + B') + X\otimes(B - B') = \sqrt2(Z\otimes Z + X\otimes X)\)."

Worked solution

Idea. The maximal value pins Alice and Bob's state down to one pure state, and a pure state of \(AB\) has no room for correlations with anything else — so whatever Eve holds is a separate world.

1. \(C = \sqrt2(Z\otimes Z + X\otimes X)\) (last rung). \(Z\otimes Z\) and \(X\otimes X\) commute and are diagonal in the Bell basis with eigenvalues \((+1, +1)\) on \(\ket{\Phi^+}\), \((+1, -1)\) on \(\ket{\Phi^-}\), \((-1, +1)\) on \(\ket{\Psi^+}\), \((-1, -1)\) on \(\ket{\Psi^-}\) (unit 06). So \(C\) has eigenvalues \(2\sqrt2, 0, 0, -2\sqrt2\), the last only on \(\ket{\Psi^-}\). Eve's system does not enter: \(S = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{ABE}(C\otimes I_E)) = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{AB}C)\) by the definition of the partial trace. With \(w_j = \braket{f_j|\rho_{AB}|f_j} \ge 0\) the weights on the eigenvectors, \(\sum w_j = 1\), \(S + 2\sqrt2 = \sum_jw_j(c_j + 2\sqrt2)\), a sum of non-negative terms; it vanishes only if \(w_j = 0\) for the three other eigenvectors. A positive operator with \(\braket{f|\rho|f} = 0\) has \(\rho f = 0\), so \(\rho_{AB}\) is supported on the line of \(\ket{\Psi^-}\), and with trace one, \(\rho_{AB} = \ket{\Psi^-}\!\bra{\Psi^-}\). \(\blacksquare\)

2. Purify (unit 04): a unit vector \(\ket\Omega \in H_{AB}\otimes H_E\otimes H_R\) with \(\operatorname{tr}_R\ket\Omega\!\bra\Omega = \rho_{ABE}\). Its reduced state on \(AB\) is \(\operatorname{tr}_E\operatorname{tr}_R\ket\Omega\!\bra\Omega = \rho_{AB} = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi\), pure. By unit 04's P7, for the split \(AB \mid ER\) a pure state whose reduced state is pure is a product: \(\ket\Omega = \ket{\psi'}\otimes\ket\varphi\), and \(\ket{\psi'}\!\bra{\psi'} = \operatorname{tr}_{ER}\ket\Omega\!\bra\Omega = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi\). Then \(\rho_{ABE} = \operatorname{tr}_R(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\otimes\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi) = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi\otimes\rho_E\) with \(\rho_E = \operatorname{tr}_R\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi\). \(\blacksquare\)

3. For projections \(P_a\), \(Q_b\), \(R_e\) of Alice's, Bob's and Eve's measurements, \[ \Pr(a, b, e) = \operatorname{tr}\big((P_a\otimes Q_b\otimes R_e)(\ket{\Psi^-}\!\bra{\Psi^-}\otimes\rho_E)\big) = \braket{\Psi^-|P_a\otimes Q_b|\Psi^-}\,\operatorname{tr}(R_e\rho_E), \] a product of a function of \((a, b)\) and a function of \(e\), each summing to one: independence. In particular the key bits, conditioned on anything Eve sees, are uniform and anticorrelated. \(\blacksquare\)

4. At \(S = -2.8\) the weight on the other eigenvectors need not vanish: \(S + 2\sqrt2 = 0.028\) allows, for example, \(w = 0.01\) on \(\ket{\Phi^-}\) (eigenvalue \(0\)) — \(0.01\times2.828 = 0.028\), to rounding (exactly, \(w = 0.0284/2.8284 = 0.0101\)) — and that small weight can be correlated with Eve. Part 1's exact argument gives nothing; a robust version, bounding Eve's information by a function of \(S\) that tends to zero as \(|S| \to 2\sqrt2\), is what device-independent proofs supply (cited in the deck).

Check. On \(\ket{\Psi^-}\) itself, \(\braket{\Psi^-|C|\Psi^-} = \sqrt2(-1 - 1) = -2\sqrt2\) ✓, and \(\operatorname{tr}C = 0\) ✓ (traceless Paulis). Part 2 with \(\rho_{ABE}\) a product to begin with returns it unchanged ✓.

P10 What a lower \(S\) allows medium

exercises: the Werner state's CHSH value and error rate, two thresholds compared, what a Bell violation does and does not certify

A noisy source emits the Werner state \(\rho_p = p\ket{\Psi^-}\!\bra{\Psi^-} + (1 - p)\tfrac I4\) of unit 06's sheet, \(0 \le p \le 1\).

  1. Recall why \(S = -2\sqrt2\,p\) at E91's settings, and find \(p\) at which \(|S| = 2\).
  2. Show that the error rate on E91's key rounds is \(Q = (1 - p)/2\), and evaluate it at that \(p\).
  3. Find \(p\) and \(|S|\) at the BB84 threshold \(Q = 0.110\). Explain why \(|S| = 2\) is not the threshold for key.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Everything is linear in \(\rho_p\): the singlet's value times \(p\), plus the maximally mixed state's times \(1 - p\). The answers are three numbers and one comparison.

Rung 2

\(\operatorname{tr}(\tfrac I4 M) = 0\) for every traceless \(M\). An error on a \(Z\)–\(Z\) round is "outcomes equal", with probability \(\tfrac12(1 + E)\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: "\(E_{ZZ} = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_p\,Z\otimes Z) = p\braket{\Psi^-|Z\otimes Z|\Psi^-} + (1 - p)\tfrac14\operatorname{tr}(Z\otimes Z) = -p\)."

Worked solution

Idea. Noise lowers \(|S|\) and raises \(Q\) together, and the two thresholds — no local model, and positive key — fall at different places, because they answer different questions.

1. \(S = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_pC) = p(-2\sqrt2) + (1 - p)\tfrac14\operatorname{tr}C = -2\sqrt2\,p\), since \(C\) is traceless (unit 06, P9). \(|S| = 2\) at \(p = 1/\sqrt2 = 0.7071\).

2. \(E_{ZZ} = -p\) (last rung) and likewise \(E_{XX} = -p\). Bob flips his bit, so an error is "outcomes equal": probability \(\tfrac12(1 + E) = \tfrac12(1 - p)\). At \(p = 0.7071\): \(Q = 0.1464\).

3. \(Q = 0.110028\) needs \(p = 1 - 2Q = 0.7799\), where \(|S| = 2\sqrt2\times0.7799 = 2.206\). So between \(|S| = 2\) (\(Q = 14.6\%\)) and \(|S| = 2.206\) (\(Q = 11.0\%\)) the source violates CHSH — no local model explains it — and yet one-way BB84 post-processing extracts no key. The two thresholds answer different questions. \(|S| \gt 2\) says the correlations were not pre-programmed; a positive key rate needs them to be strong enough that Alice and Bob's shared information, \(1 - h(Q)\), exceeds what Eve can have. With untrusted devices it is worse: the device-independent rate (Acín et al. 2007; Pironio et al. 2009; cited) vanishes at \(Q = 0.0715\), \(|S| = 2.424\). The Bell threshold is necessary for device-independent key, never sufficient.

Check. \(p = 1\): \(S = -2\sqrt2\), \(Q = 0\) ✓. \(p = 0\): \(S = 0\), \(Q = \tfrac12\) — Bob's bits are coins ✓. And the three thresholds are ordered: \(7.1\% \lt 11.0\% \lt 14.6\%\).

P11 Authentication medium

exercises: what each check in BB84 compares, the man-in-the-middle attack, the one-time pad relayed, why a seed is unavoidable

  1. Suppose the public line is not authenticated. Describe how Eve, controlling both channels, runs BB84 separately with Alice and with Bob, and show that every check Alice and Bob perform passes with the statistics of an honest run.
  2. Alice then sends \(c = m\oplus k_A\). Show how Eve reads \(m\) and delivers to Bob a ciphertext he decrypts correctly.
  3. Argue that no protocol at all — quantum or classical — lets two parties with no shared secret rule this out.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every test in BB84 compares data held by the two ends of one session. Ask who the two ends are.

Rung 2

Eve plays Bob to Alice and Alice to Bob, following the protocol honestly in each role. For part 3, think about what distinguishes Bob from anyone who behaves exactly as Bob would.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: "In session 1 Eve measures Alice's photons in random bases, exactly as Bob would, and answers on the public line as Bob would; Alice's view of session 1 is that of an honest BB84 run with Eve as Bob."

Worked solution

Idea. The quantum part certifies that nobody tampered with the photons between the two ends; it cannot certify who the other end is. Only a shared secret can.

1. Eve cuts both channels. In session 1 she plays Bob: she receives Alice's photons, measures in random bases, and answers Alice's sifting and sampling messages as Bob would; she ends with a key \(k_A\) shared with Alice. In session 2 she plays Alice to Bob, sending fresh BB84 photons and answering as Alice would, ending with \(k_B\) shared with Bob. Each session has two honest-behaving ends and an untouched quantum channel between them, so its statistics are exactly those of an honest run: the estimated error rate is the channel's own, zero in principle, and every check passes. Neither Alice nor Bob ever compares data with the other.

2. Eve intercepts \(c\), computes \(m = c\oplus k_A\) (she holds \(k_A\)), and sends Bob \(c' = m\oplus k_B\); Bob computes \(c'\oplus k_B = m\). The message arrives correctly and Eve has read it. She can also alter it, since she re-encrypts.

3. With no shared secret, everything Alice can know about her partner is what the partner sends. An impostor who runs Bob's program — whatever it is — sends messages with exactly the distribution Bob's would have, so no test Alice applies can separate the two. This is a statement about identity, independent of physics. Hence authentication needs a secret shared in advance; with one, Wegman–Carter tags make forging exponentially unlikely, and QKD turns the short seed into a long key: key expansion.

Check. With authentication, Eve's forged "Bob" messages in session 1 carry no valid tag: the attack fails at the first message. And the same attack breaks unauthenticated Diffie–Hellman, so it is not a weakness peculiar to QKD.

P12 Two-photon pulses medium

exercises: reduced states of product states, the photon-number-splitting attack, Poisson probabilities, a loss budget

  1. A pulse carries two photons in Alice's state, \(\ket s\otimes\ket s\). Eve keeps the second and forwards the first. Show that Bob's statistics are those of an honest single photon, and that after the basis announcement Eve learns Alice's bit with certainty. Why does this not contradict no-cloning?
  2. Photon numbers are Poisson with mean \(\mu = 0.1\). What fraction of non-empty pulses carry two or more?
  3. The channel transmits a fraction \(\eta\) of photons, and without Eve Bob detects about \(\mu\eta\) of the pulses. Eve, holding a lossless line, blocks every single-photon pulse and forwards one photon from each multi-photon pulse. Below what \(\eta\) can she keep Bob's detection rate normal while knowing every key bit? How many kilometres of \(0.2\) dB/km fibre is that?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a partial trace of a product state; parts 2 and 3 are arithmetic with the Poisson distribution.

Rung 2

The reduced state of \(\ket s\otimes\ket s\) on the first factor is \(\ket s\!\bra s\). For part 3, Eve can supply at most \(\Pr(n \ge 2)\) detections per pulse; compare with \(\mu\eta\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: "Eve's attack keeps Bob's rate normal iff \(\Pr(n \ge 2) \ge \mu\eta\), i.e. \(\eta \le \Pr(n\ge2)/\mu = 0.004679/0.1\)."

Worked solution

Idea. A second photon in the same state is a copy Alice made for Eve. Loss in the channel then lets Eve hide the fact that she only lets copied pulses through.

1. \(\operatorname{tr}_2(\ket s\!\bra s\otimes\ket s\!\bra s) = \ket s\!\bra s\,\operatorname{tr}\ket s\!\bra s = \ket s\!\bra s\) (unit 04): Bob's photon is in exactly Alice's state, so every outcome probability is the honest one and no error is introduced. After the announcement Eve measures her photon in the announced basis and gets Alice's bit with probability \(|\braket{s|s}|^2 = 1\). No-cloning forbids a machine that copies an unknown state; here Alice's laser made two photons in the same state, and Eve only separated them — by measuring the photon number, which does not touch polarisation.

2. \(\Pr(0) = e^{-0.1} = 0.904837\), \(\Pr(1) = 0.090484\), \(\Pr(n \ge 2) = 0.004679\). Among non-empty pulses: \(0.004679/0.095163 = 0.0492\), about \(4.9\%\).

3. Eve can deliver at most one detection per multi-photon pulse: rate \(\Pr(n\ge2) = 0.004679\) per pulse. She matches Bob's expected \(\mu\eta\) if \(\eta \le 0.004679/0.1 = 0.0468\), a loss of \(-10\log_{10}0.0468 = 13.3\) dB, which is \(13.3/0.2 = 66.5\) km of fibre. Beyond that distance a plain BB84 link with these pulses gives Eve the whole key with no errors and a normal count rate — which is why every practical system uses decoy states (deck, Decoy states, and the gaps that remain).

Check. The Poisson probabilities sum to one: \(0.904837 + 0.090484 + 0.004679 = 1\) ✓. At \(\eta = 1\) (no loss) Eve cannot hide: blocking all single-photon pulses would cut Bob's rate from \(\approx0.095\) to \(0.0047\) per pulse.
