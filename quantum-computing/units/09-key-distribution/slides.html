09

Quantum key distribution: disturbance as a security guarantee

An eavesdropper who does not know the basis can neither copy a photon nor read it without leaving errors behind. Intercept-resend costs her a 25 per cent error rate; Shor and Preskill's rate $1 - 2h(Q)$ says how much secret key survives any error rate below 11 per cent.

needs 06, 05~2½ h slides + 4½ h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 09

Unit 05 proved two limitations: no machine copies an unknown state drawn from a non-orthogonal set, and no measurement tells two non-orthogonal states apart with certainty. This unit turns both into a weapon. Alice sends Bob single photons in one of four polarisations from two mutually unbiased bases; an eavesdropper who does not know the basis cannot copy the photon, and anything she does to learn its bit leaves errors that Alice and Bob can count. From the counted error rate a classical calculation decides how many secret bits remain. Then the same key is made from unit 06's singlets, where the CHSH test of unit 06 becomes the alarm.

What the unit does not do: it is not a cryptography course, and it does not prove security against every possible attack. That proof (Shor and Preskill 2000; Renner 2005 for the composable, finite-size version) is long and uses quantum error-correcting codes, which are Part E's subject; it is cited, stated exactly, and read. What is proved here is the information–disturbance trade-off for the attacks one can write down by hand.

Sixteen photons, and someone may be listening

The running example, sixteen photons. Alice picks a bit and a basis for each; Bob picks a basis and records what his detectors say. A bar is the polarisation sent: $\mathtt z$ is horizontal or vertical, $\mathtt x$ is $\pm45^\circ$.

The plan. 1 BB84, step by step, on the sixteen · 2 what looking costs Eve: intercept-resend, priced exactly · 3 from error rate to key rate: $1 - 2h(Q)$ · 4 the same key from Bell pairs · 5 what QKD does not do.

the question

Alice and Bob want a secret shared key. They have a channel for photons, which Eve may tamper with as she likes, and a public telephone line, which she may listen to but not alter.

How would they notice Eve? And if she has been listening a little, how much of the key is still secret?

The answer in one line: where Bob measured in Alice's basis, he must get her bit — and an Eve who does not know the basis cannot help breaking that.

A dotted underline is a glossary term (click, or G); a solid one like the sixteen is the running example (hover); N shows the notes, where the longer proofs live.

The table, read column by column. Photon 1: Alice's bit is $0$, her basis $\mathtt z$, so she sends horizontal polarisation, $\ket0$; Bob measures in $\mathtt z$ and reads $0$. Photon 2: Alice sends $\ket1$ (vertical) but Bob measures in $\mathtt x$, and his result, $0$, is a fair coin that tells him nothing about her bit. Where the two bases agree — eight of the sixteen here — Bob's result always equals Alice's bit. That is the whole mechanism, and the next section proves it.

What a shared key buys: the one-time pad

the one-time pad (Vernam 1917)

Alice and Bob share a key $k \in \{0, 1\}^n$, uniformly random and used once. To send $m \in \{0, 1\}^n$, Alice publishes $c = m \oplus k$ (bitwise XOR); Bob computes $c \oplus k = m$.

perfect secrecy (Shannon 1949)

Whatever the distribution of $m$, the ciphertext $c$ is uniform on $\{0, 1\}^n$ and independent of $m$.

proof

For fixed $m$, $k \mapsto m \oplus k$ is a bijection of $\{0, 1\}^n$, so $\Pr(c = y \mid m) = \Pr(k = y \oplus m) = 2^{-n}$ for every $y$. $\blacksquare$

One column at a time: $c_j = m_j \oplus k_j$. Without $k$, every $c$ is equally likely, whatever $m$ said.

The catch. The key is as long as the message and must never be reused: $c_1 \oplus c_2 = m_1 \oplus m_2$ leaks. So the problem is distributing keys — which is what this unit's protocols do.

Check: $n = 1$, $m = 0$ with probability $0.9$: $\Pr(c = 0) = 0.9\cdot\tfrac12 + 0.1\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$ ✓.

Independence, in full. $\Pr(c = y, m = x) = \Pr(m = x)\Pr(k = y\oplus x) = \Pr(m = x)\,2^{-n}$, using that $k$ is independent of $m$. Summing over $x$ gives $\Pr(c = y) = 2^{-n}$, so the joint probability is the product of the marginals. Eve, seeing $c$, has exactly the distribution of $m$ she had before.

The figure's XOR. $1\oplus0 = 1$, $0\oplus1 = 1$, $1\oplus1 = 0$, $1\oplus0 = 1$, $0\oplus1 = 1$, $0\oplus1 = 1$, $1\oplus0 = 1$, $0\oplus0 = 0$: $11011110$ ✓.

How keys are distributed today. By public-key methods (Diffie–Hellman 1976, RSA 1977), whose security rests on the assumption that discrete logarithms or factoring are hard — unproved, and, as unit 16 will show, false for a large quantum computer. Quantum key distribution makes a different promise: security from the laws of quantum mechanics, whatever the eavesdropper can compute. It does not make the one-time pad cheaper — the key still has to be as long as the message — and in practice its output often keys a conventional cipher instead.

History. Vernam's 1917 teleprinter cipher added a key tape to the message tape; that the key must be random and never reused was understood soon after. Shannon proved perfect secrecy in "Communication theory of secrecy systems" (1949), and also that it needs a key at least as long as the message.

09 · protocol

Single photons, from nothing

Light comes in lumps. A sensitive detector lit by very faint light does not register a weak, steady signal: it clicks, at random moments, and each click is the same size. Dimming the light makes clicks rarer, never smaller. Each indivisible lump is a photon.

One photon at an analyser. Unit 03's polarising beam splitter sends a single photon to exactly one of its two detectors. Its polarisation is unit 03's qubit, and the click is a measurement in the analyser's basis: where bright light splits by Malus's fraction $\cos^2(\alpha - \theta)$, one photon goes one way with that probability (Born rule, unit 01).

The apparatus. Alice: a pulsed laser, dimmed so far that a pulse holds one photon or none (section 5 counts how often two), and a rotator that sets each pulse's polarisation. Bob: a rotator that chooses his basis, an analyser, two detectors. Between them, optical fibre or open air.

The apparatus. Bob's analyser transmits one polarisation of its basis to detector $0$ and reflects the other to detector $1$; his rotator, set at random each time, picks the basis.

The evidence for photons. The photoelectric effect (Einstein 1905, cited): light ejects electrons from a metal only above a threshold frequency, however bright, which a smooth wave cannot explain and a lump of energy proportional to the frequency can. The cleaner modern evidence is the one on the slide: a single-photon source at a beam splitter gives a click in one detector or the other, never both at once (Grangier, Roger and Aspect 1986, cited). A course in quantum optics derives the photon from a quantised field; this course needs only its polarisation, which is a qubit.

Unit 03, recalled. Polarisation along the line at angle $\alpha$ is $\ket\alpha = \cos\alpha\ket0 + \sin\alpha\ket1$, with $\ket0 = $ horizontal (H) and $\ket1 = $ vertical (V). An analyser turned to $\theta$ measures in the basis $\{\ket\theta, \ket{\theta + 90^\circ}\}$, and the Born rule gives transmission with probability $|\braket{\theta|\alpha}|^2 = \cos^2(\alpha - \theta)$: Malus's law, one photon at a time.

The rotator. In practice an electro-optic crystal (a Pockels cell) whose effect on polarisation is switched by a voltage in nanoseconds, so the basis can be chosen afresh for each pulse. The first demonstration used Pockels cells on both sides (Bennett et al., J. Cryptology 5, 3, 1992, cited); many later systems use passive beam splitters instead, letting the photon itself pick Bob's basis at random.

"One photon or none". Dimming a laser does not make a one-photon source; it makes pulses whose photon number is random. Section 5 gives the distribution and the attack that its two-photon pulses invite.

Four states, two bases

the BB84 states

The $\mathtt z$ basis $\ket0$ (H), $\ket1$ (V); the $\mathtt x$ basis $\ket\pm = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 \pm \ket1)$, polarised at $\pm45^\circ$. A bit is encoded as

$$0 \mapsto \ket0 \text{ or } \ket+, \qquad 1 \mapsto \ket1 \text{ or } \ket-,$$

the basis chosen at random. $\lVert\ket\pm\rVert^2 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ ✓.

sentmeasured $\mathtt z$: $0$, $1$measured $\mathtt x$: $0$, $1$
$\ket0$$1$, $0$$\tfrac12$, $\tfrac12$
$\ket1$$0$, $1$$\tfrac12$, $\tfrac12$
$\ket+$$\tfrac12$, $\tfrac12$$1$, $0$
$\ket-$$\tfrac12$, $\tfrac12$$0$, $1$

Every row sums to one in each basis ✓. Right basis: certain. Wrong basis: a fair coin.

The four states as vectors of real amplitudes. Each $\mathtt x$ vector is $45^\circ$ from each $\mathtt z$ vector, so every overlap is $\cos^2 45^\circ = \tfrac12$.

The two bases are mutually unbiased (unit 00): $|\braket{0|\pm}|^2 = |\braket{1|\pm}|^2 = \tfrac12$.

The table, computed. $\braket{0|+} = \braket{1|+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ and $\braket{0|-} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$, $\braket{1|-} = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$; each squared modulus is $\tfrac12$. Measured in its own basis a state gives its own outcome with probability $|\braket{s|s}|^2 = 1$. The $\mathtt x$ basis is orthonormal: $\braket{+|-} = \tfrac12(1 - 1) = 0$.

Vectors, not polarisation angles. In the figure the angle between $\ket0$ and $\ket+$ is $45^\circ$, and for linear polarisation the physical angle is the same (unit 03: no doubling for polarisation). On the Bloch sphere of unit 02 the four states sit at the ends of two perpendicular diameters, and "mutually unbiased" means "perpendicular axes". $\ket-$ at $-45^\circ$ is the same polarisation line as $135^\circ$, since $\ket{\alpha + 180^\circ} = -\ket\alpha$ differs by a global phase.

Why these four. Any two mutually unbiased bases would do; these are the ones a polarisation analyser reaches by a $45^\circ$ turn. A six-state protocol adds unit 00's third basis, circular polarisation, and tolerates slightly more noise (Bruß 1998, cited) at the cost of sifting away two thirds of the photons.

Wiesner's idea. Stephen Wiesner proposed "conjugate coding" around 1970, for banknotes that cannot be forged; his paper was published only in 1983 (SIGACT News 15, 78). Bennett and Brassard turned it into key distribution in 1984.

Why Eve can neither copy nor read the photon

from unit 05

No-cloning. A machine copying every state of a set exists iff its members are pairwise orthogonal or equal. $|\braket{0|+}| = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ is neither $0$ nor $1$: no machine copies the four.

Helstrom. The best single measurement telling $\ket0$ from $\ket+$ succeeds with probability

$$\tfrac12\big(1 + \sqrt{1 - \tfrac12}\big) \approx 0.8536 .$$

So Eve can neither keep a copy to read after the bases are announced, nor read the photon reliably now.

not yet enough

Neither says that trying leaves a trace. Section 2 proves it: on these four states, any information Eve gains costs errors Bob can see.

Unit 05's best measurement for $\ket0$ against $\ket+$: each state is $22.5^\circ$ from its own measurement vector, so each is identified with probability $\cos^2\tfrac\pi8 \approx 0.8536$ — and misidentified with $0.1464$.

Both results are unit 05's, used as proved there. The cloning criterion: a unitary $U$ with $U(\psi\otimes s) = e^{i\gamma}\psi\otimes\psi$ for two states preserves inner products, so $\braket{\psi|\varphi} = \braket{\psi|\varphi}^2$ up to a phase, forcing $|\braket{\psi|\varphi}| \in \{0, 1\}$. Helstrom's bound for two equiprobable pure states is $\tfrac12(1 + \sqrt{1 - |\braket{\psi|\varphi}|^2})$, proved there by reducing to the plane the two states span. Here $1 - \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$ and $\sqrt{1/2} = 0.70711$, so the bound is $\tfrac12\cdot1.70711 = 0.85355$.

Why Eve's problem is about pairs. After sifting, the basis is public. If Eve could keep the photon (or a copy) until then, she would measure in the right basis and learn the bit for certain. No-cloning forbids the copy; she could keep the photon itself, but then Bob receives nothing, notices the loss, and the round is discarded. So she must decide before the announcement, facing four states — and unit 05's bound for $\ket0$ against $\ket+$ is the shape of her difficulty. Problem P4 finds her best guess at the bit, measuring in the basis halfway between the two: again $0.8536$.

What is still missing. A machine might, in principle, learn something and pass the photon on unchanged. Unit 05's results do not exclude that; the theorem Undisturbed means uninformed, in section 2, does.

09 · protocol

BB84, step by step

BB84 (Bennett and Brassard 1984)
  1. Transmission. For each photon Alice picks a random bit and a random basis and sends the BB84 state; Bob picks a random basis and records his result. Job: put the key where Eve cannot copy it.
  2. Sifting. Over the public line both announce their bases — never the bits — and keep only the photons where they agree: the sifted key. Job: discard the coin flips.
  3. Parameter estimation. Alice reveals a random sample of sifted bits; Bob compares and estimates the quantum bit error rate $Q$; the sample is then discarded. If $Q$ is too high, they abort. Job: measure Eve.
  4. Error correction. They fix Bob's errors by public discussion. Job: make the strings equal.
  5. Privacy amplification. They shrink the string with a public random hash. Job: make Eve's knowledge negligible.

Throughout, the public line is authenticated: Eve may read it, not alter it.

The funnel. The numbers are the sixteen's. Steps 4 and 5 are priced in section 3: about $2h(Q)$ bits per sifted bit.

Why each step is there. Step 1 is the only quantum one: everything after it is classical processing of two correlated bit strings. Step 2 is free of risk, because bases carry no information about bits: given the basis, the bit is still uniform. Step 3 is where Eve's presence becomes a number: the sample is chosen at random after the transmission, so Eve cannot know which bits will be checked and must disturb all of them alike to disturb any. Step 4 is needed even without Eve — real channels and detectors make errors — and costs public parity bits, which Eve hears. Step 5 removes what Eve knows, including what she heard in step 4.

The rule for aborting. Section 3 gives it: a key survives only if $1 - 2h(Q) > 0$, i.e. $Q$ below about $11\%$. In practice the threshold is set lower, to leave room for the statistical uncertainty in the estimate of $Q$.

The source. C. H. Bennett and G. Brassard, "Quantum cryptography: public key distribution and coin tossing", Proceedings of the IEEE International Conference on Computers, Systems and Signal Processing, Bangalore, pp. 175–179 (1984). The steps after sifting were made precise later: error correction and privacy amplification by Bennett, Brassard and Robert (1988) and Bennett, Bessette, Brassard, Salvail and Smolin (1992).

Worked: sifting the sixteen

worked example: steps 1 and 2 on the sixteen photons

1. Announce. Bob reads out his bases, $\mathtt{zxxx\,xzzz\,xxzz\,xxzz}$; Alice answers which agree with hers. 2. Compare. Column by column, the outlined ones: photons $1, 3, 5, 7, 10, 12, 14, 16$ — eight of sixteen.

3. Keep. The sifted key is Alice's bits there: $01101000$, and Bob's results there are the same, since a measurement in the preparation basis is certain. 4. Check. The other eight results are fair coins, carrying nothing: Bob's eight read $0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 0, 1, 0$ against Alice's $1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1$.

Each step. Step 2 compares the strings $\mathtt{zzxz\,xxzx\,zxxz\,zxxz}$ (Alice) and $\mathtt{zxxx\,xzzz\,xxzz\,xxzz}$ (Bob) position by position: agreement at 1 ($\mathtt z$), 3 ($\mathtt x$), 5 ($\mathtt x$), 7 ($\mathtt z$), 10 ($\mathtt x$), 12 ($\mathtt z$), 14 ($\mathtt x$), 16 ($\mathtt z$). Step 3 reads Alice's bits at those positions: $a_1 = 0$, $a_3 = 1$, $a_5 = 1$, $a_7 = 0$, $a_{10} = 1$, $a_{12} = 0$, $a_{14} = 0$, $a_{16} = 0$. Bob's results there are the same, column by column.

The discarded eight. At photons 2, 4, 6, 8, 9, 11, 13, 15 Bob's result agrees with Alice's bit at 9, 11 and 13 — three of eight, where a coin would agree four times on average. The point is that it does not matter: by the table on Four states, two bases, a wrong-basis result is independent of the bit, so it is discarded without loss.

How many are kept, on average. Each photon's two bases agree with probability $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$, independently, so the number kept is binomial with mean $n/2$. Eight of sixteen is the mean exactly; any number from $0$ to $16$ was possible. Problem P2 computes what biased basis choices buy.

What Eve learns from the announcement. The bases, nothing else: the bit was chosen independently of the basis, so after the announcement each sifted bit is still uniform to anyone who has not touched the photon.

Worked: estimating the error rate on a sample

worked example: step 3 on the sifted eight

Half the sifted bits are sacrificed to measure the error rate; the other half go on.

1. Choose. After the transmission, Alice picks four sifted positions at random — photons $3, 7, 12, 16$ — and announces them with her bits there: $1, 0, 0, 0$.

2. Compare. Bob's bits there: $1, 0, 0, 0$. Mismatches: none. The estimate of the quantum bit error rate is $\hat Q = 0/4 = 0$.

3. Discard and keep. The revealed bits are public now; the four left, at photons $1, 5, 10, 14$, are the raw key $0110$.

4. Check. $4 + 4 = 8$ sifted bits ✓; the kept bits are the sifted key $01101000$ at its positions 1, 3, 5, 7 ✓.

The definition. The quantum bit error rate is the probability that Bob's sifted bit differs from Alice's. It is estimated by the fraction of mismatches in a random sample; the name says "quantum" only because it is measured on the quantum channel. Real links run at a few per cent even without Eve, from detector noise and imperfect optics.

How much four bits can say. Very little. Section 2 shows that a full intercept-resend attack makes each sifted bit wrong independently with probability $\tfrac14$, so a four-bit sample is clean with probability $(\tfrac34)^4 = 0.3164$ even under attack. Real runs sample tens of thousands of bits. By Hoeffding's inequality (assumed background), a sample of $k$ bits estimates $Q$ to within $\varepsilon$ except with probability $2e^{-2k\varepsilon^2}$; for $\varepsilon = 0.01$ and failure probability $10^{-6}$ that asks for $k \ge \ln(2\times10^6)/(2\times10^{-4}) = 72\,544$ — problem P8.

Why the sample must be chosen afterwards. If Eve knew in advance which photons would be checked, she would leave those alone and attack the rest. Chosen at random after transmission, the sample is a fair sample of all the sifted bits.

The binary entropy: how many bits an error rate hides

the binary entropy $$h(Q) = -Q\log Q - (1 - Q)\log(1 - Q),$$

$\log$ base $2$, with $0\log0 := 0$. Defined for $0 \le Q \le 1$.

What it means. If each of $n$ bits is flipped independently with probability $Q$, the flip pattern is one of about $2^{h(Q)n}$ "typical" patterns, each about equally likely: $h(Q)$ bits of uncertainty per symbol. It is the number of bits per symbol it costs to correct the flips, and the number it costs to hide them from someone who knows less.

The counting is Shannon's (1948), the price of correcting is Slepian and Wolf's theorem (1973), and the price of hiding is privacy amplification's, below — all cited. This unit uses $h$ as a price.

The binary entropy. Steep at small $Q$: a $5\%$ error rate already hides $0.286$ bits per bit.

Checks. $h(0) = h(1) = 0$: no uncertainty. $h(\tfrac12) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$: a fair coin is one full bit. $h(Q) = h(1 - Q)$ ✓.

The counting behind "typical". A pattern with $k$ flips among $n$ has probability $Q^k(1 - Q)^{n - k}$. By the law of large numbers (or Chernoff–Hoeffding, assumed background) $k$ is close to $Qn$ with high probability, and a pattern with exactly $k = Qn$ has probability $Q^{Qn}(1 - Q)^{(1 - Q)n} = 2^{-h(Q)n}$. There are $\binom{n}{Qn}$ of them, and $\log\binom{n}{Qn} = h(Q)n + O(\log n)$ by Stirling. So the flips are spread over about $2^{h(Q)n}$ patterns of about equal weight: naming one takes $h(Q)n$ bits.

The two theorems, cited. Shannon (1948): a binary channel that flips each bit with probability $Q$ carries $1 - h(Q)$ bits per use, and no more. Slepian and Wolf (1973), in the form needed here: if Bob holds a copy of Alice's $n$-bit string with each bit flipped independently with probability $Q$, Alice can let him correct it by publishing about $h(Q)n$ bits, and no scheme uses fewer as $n \to \infty$. Proofs: Cover and Thomas, Elements of Information Theory, chapters 7 and 15. The price of hiding is a different theorem: the leftover hash lemma of Privacy amplification: hashing Eve's knowledge away, with Shor and Preskill's argument (section 3) saying how much there is to hide. Branch 08 states the quantum analogue of Shannon's counting (Schumacher compression, with its proof's structure); this unit needs only the classical prices.

The numbers on the figure. $h(0.05) = 0.286397$, $h(0.110028) = 0.500000$, $h(0.25) = \tfrac14\cdot2 + \tfrac34\log\tfrac43 = 0.5 + 0.311278 = 0.811278$. If you took branch 08: this is its $h(p)$, the Shannon entropy of a two-outcome distribution.

09 · protocol

Error correction: Bob's errors, found in public

The task. Bob's string differs from Alice's in about $Qn$ unknown places. Over the public line, they must make the two equal.

the price (Slepian and Wolf 1973, cited)

Alice must publish at least about $h(Q)\,n$ bits about her string, and about $h(Q)\,n$ suffice, as $n \to \infty$.

A practical scheme (Cascade, Brassard and Salvail 1993): split into blocks, compare parities; a block whose parities differ holds an odd number of errors, and halving it repeatedly finds one with $\log_2(\text{block length})$ more parities.

every parity is heard

Each published parity is a bit of information Eve now has about the key. That is the first $h(Q)$ of section 3's price, and step 5 must remove it too.

A parity mismatch, then halving: four public parities find the one error in a block of eight.

The figure, step by step. Alice's block $10110100$ has four ones, parity $0$; Bob's $10110000$ has three, parity $1$. They differ, so the block holds an odd number of errors. First half, positions 1–4: $1011$ on both sides, parity $1$ and $1$, agree; so the odd count is in 5–8. Then 5–6: Alice $01$, parity $1$; Bob $00$, parity $0$; differ. Then position 5: $0$ and $0$, agree; so the error is at 6, and Bob flips it. Four parities in all: one for the block and $\log_2 8 = 3$ for the search.

Why Cascade "cascades". A block with an even number of errors passes the parity test. Cascade therefore reshuffles the string and repeats with larger blocks; each error found in a later pass reveals a partner error in an earlier block, which is then found too. It publishes about $1.1$ to $1.2$ times $h(Q)n$ bits at the error rates of interest (Brassard and Salvail, EUROCRYPT 1993, cited). Modern systems use low-density parity-check codes, which come closer to $h(Q)n$.

The lower bound, in one sentence. Bob's uncertainty about Alice's string, given his own, is the flip pattern, one of about $2^{h(Q)n}$ (previous slide's notes); public messages of fewer than $h(Q)n$ bits cannot single it out. Making this exact is Slepian and Wolf's theorem, cited.

Without Eve. Error correction is needed even on a perfect day, since detectors have dark counts and optics are imperfect. The protocol cannot tell those errors from Eve's, so it charges them all to Eve: every error is treated as hers.

Privacy amplification: hashing Eve's knowledge away

The task. Alice and Bob now hold the same $n$-bit string $x$, and Eve may know part of it — up to $t$ bits' worth. They want a shorter string Eve knows nothing about.

one bit Eve does not know hides a parity

If $x_j$ is uniform and independent of everything Eve has, then for every set $T \ni j$ the parity $\bigoplus_{i\in T}x_i$ is uniform and independent of it too.

Proof: it is $x_j \oplus (\text{the rest})$ — the one-time pad, with $x_j$ as key. $\blacksquare$

universal hashing (Carter and Wegman 1979)

A family of functions $f : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}^m$ such that $\Pr_f(f(x) = f(y)) \le 2^{-m}$ for all $x \ne y$. Example: $m$ parities of independent random subsets.

Each output bit is the parity of a random subset. A subset that contains even one bit Eve is ignorant of gives her a fair coin.

the guarantee (cited)

Keeping $m = n - t - s$ bits from a random universal hash leaves Eve's knowledge of the result exponentially small in $s$ (Bennett, Brassard and Robert 1988; the leftover hash lemma).

Universal hashing, in one paragraph. Choose the hash at random, publicly, after Eve has done her listening. If Eve's knowledge of $x$ amounts to $t$ bits, about $2^{n - t}$ strings remain plausible to her; a function drawn from a universal family sends plausible strings that differ to equal outputs only with probability $2^{-m}$, so as long as $2^m$ is much smaller than $2^{n - t}$ the outputs are spread almost evenly, whichever plausible $x$ is the true one. Taking $m = n - t - s$ makes the leftover bias at most about $2^{-s/2}$. Precisely, with "$t$ bits' worth" meaning that Eve's best guess of the whole of $x$ succeeds with probability at most $2^{-(n - t)}$: the output is within total-variation distance $2^{-s/2}$ of uniform and independent of Eve's data (Impagliazzo, Levin and Luby 1989; Bennett, Brassard, Crépeau and Maurer 1995, cited). Against an Eve holding quantum information the same statement holds with a quantum version of the guessing probability (Renner 2005, cited).

Random parities are universal, proved. Let $f(x)_r = \bigoplus_{i\in T_r}x_i$ with $T_1, \dots, T_m$ independent uniformly random subsets. For $x \ne y$ put $z = x\oplus y \ne 0$ and pick $j$ with $z_j = 1$. Then $f(x)_r = f(y)_r$ iff $\bigoplus_{i\in T_r}z_i = 0$, and the involution $T \mapsto T \triangle \{j\}$ pairs subsets with parity $0$ with subsets of parity $1$; so each output bit agrees with probability exactly $\tfrac12$, independently over $r$, and $\Pr(f(x) = f(y)) = 2^{-m}$. $\blacksquare$

The one-bit theorem, in full. Let $R = \bigoplus_{i\in T, i\ne j}x_i$ and let $E$ be Eve's data. By hypothesis $x_j$ is independent of $(R, E)$ jointly. Then $\Pr(x_j\oplus R = b,\ R = r,\ E = e) = \Pr(x_j = b\oplus r)\Pr(R = r, E = e) = \tfrac12\Pr(R = r, E = e)$; summing over $r$ gives $\Pr(\text{parity} = b, E = e) = \tfrac12\Pr(E = e)$. That is the one-time pad's proof with $n = 1$.

09 · protocol

The public line must be authenticated

The two channels. Every message on the lower one carries a tag only the holder of a short shared secret can make.

an authenticated channel

One on which Eve may read every message, but any message she alters or invents is detected.

How. Wegman and Carter (1981, cited): Alice attaches to each message a tag computed with a secret, randomly chosen universal hash; a forger succeeds with probability exponentially small in the tag length. The secret consumed grows only logarithmically in the message length.

key expansion, not key creation

So Alice and Bob must start with a short shared secret. QKD turns it into a long one, and part of each new key authenticates the next round: key expansion.

Why authentication cannot be skipped. Every security argument in this unit assumes that the bases, the sample and the parities Alice and Bob exchange are the ones they sent. If Eve could alter them, she could simply impersonate each to the other; section 5 works out this man-in-the-middle attack, and problem P11 proves it goes undetected.

The Wegman–Carter scheme. M. N. Wegman and J. L. Carter, "New hash functions and their use in authentication and set equality", J. Comput. Syst. Sci. 22, 265 (1981). The tag is $f(\text{message})$, with $f$ drawn secretly from a suitable family of hash functions — a stronger property than the universality of the last slide is needed, so that seeing one tag tells the forger nothing about another. Its security is information-theoretic, like the one-time pad's: no computational assumption. The key it consumes is of order $\log$ of the message length plus the tag length, which is why a short initial secret suffices for long sessions.

What "start with a secret" means in practice. The first secret must reach both parties some other way: a courier, a meeting. QKD's contribution is that this secret can be short and need never be renewed by courier, since each session produces more key than it uses.

09 · intercept-resend

Undisturbed means uninformed

Eve in general. She lets the photon interact with apparatus of her own, in some state $\ket e$: by unit 01's evolution postulate, a unitary $U$ on $\mathbb C^2\otimes H_E$. She may measure the apparatus whenever she likes.

information–disturbance, for the BB84 states

Suppose Bob, measuring in the sending basis, never sees an error, so that for each $s \in \{0, 1, +, -\}$

$$U(\ket s\otimes\ket e) = \ket s\otimes\ket{e_s}.$$

Then $\ket{e_0} = \ket{e_1} = \ket{e_+} = \ket{e_-}$: Eve's apparatus ends the same whatever Alice sent.

proof

By linearity, $U(\ket+\ket e) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket{e_0} + \ket1\ket{e_1})$; by hypothesis it is $\ket+\ket{e_+} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket{e_+} + \ket1\ket{e_+})$. The expansion $\ket0\otimes u + \ket1\otimes v$ is unique (unit 03), so $e_0 = e_+ = e_1$. With $\ket-$: $e_0 = e_-$. $\blacksquare$

The most general attack on one photon: a unitary with apparatus, then a measurement of the apparatus. Qubit 0, the photon, on top.

why two bases

With the $\mathtt z$ states alone, $\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}$ into Eve's $\ket0$ gives $\ket s\ket0 \mapsto \ket s\ket s$: undisturbed, and Eve holds a perfect copy (unit 05: orthogonal states clone). The $\mathtt x$ states are what forbid it.

So any information Eve gains shows up as errors on some of the four.

Full proof

Why "no error" means a product. Write $U(\ket0\otimes\ket e) = \ket0\otimes\ket u + \ket1\otimes\ket v$ (unit 03's unique expansion). Bob measures in $\mathtt z$ and reads $1$ with probability $\lVert(\bra1\otimes I)U(\ket0\ket e)\rVert^2 = \lVert v\rVert^2$ (the Born rule on the joint system, unit 03). No error means $v = 0$, so $U(\ket0\ket e) = \ket0\otimes\ket{e_0}$ with $\ket{e_0} := \ket u$, and $\lVert u\rVert = 1$ since $U$ is unitary. The same for $\ket1$ (with roles swapped) and for $\ket\pm$ in the $\mathtt x$ basis.

The comparison. $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\otimes\ket{e_0} + \ket1\otimes\ket{e_1}) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\otimes\ket{e_+} + \ket1\otimes\ket{e_+})$ and uniqueness of the $u, v$ in $\ket0\otimes u + \ket1\otimes v$ give $\ket{e_0} = \ket{e_+}$ and $\ket{e_1} = \ket{e_+}$. From $\ket- = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - \ket1)$: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket{e_0} - \ket1\ket{e_1}) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket{e_-} - \ket1\ket{e_-})$, so $\ket{e_-} = \ket{e_0}$. All four are one vector. Note that only three of the four states were needed for $e_0 = e_1$; the fourth then comes free.

What this proves, and what it does not. It proves the extreme case: zero errors forces zero information, for every attack, however clever, on a single photon. It does not say how much information a small error rate allows; that quantitative trade-off is what the rest of the section computes for particular attacks, and what Shor and Preskill's theorem (section 3) controls for all of them. The argument is the one in Bennett, Brassard and Mermin, PRL 68, 557 (1992), and Nielsen and Chuang §12.6.3.

Measurements inside $U$. If Eve measures and then acts on the result, the whole process is still a unitary on photon, apparatus and the record of the result (unit 05 modelled cloners with "a machine with parts of its own" the same way). Unit 21 makes this "every process is a unitary on a larger system" precise; here it is the evolution postulate applied to the photon and Eve's laboratory together.

09 · intercept-resend

The simplest attack: measure, then resend

Intercept-resend. Eve's photon to Bob is a fresh one, prepared in the basis state her detectors reported.

the intercept-resend attack

On each photon Eve picks $\mathtt z$ or $\mathtt x$ with probability $\tfrac12$, measures in it, notes her outcome, and sends Bob a new photon in the state she found.

Eve's best case. Right basis (probability $\tfrac12$): she reads the bit for sure and resends Alice's very state. Bob sees nothing.

Eve's worst case. Wrong basis: her outcome is a coin, and she resends a state of the other basis — which, measured by Bob in Alice's basis, is a coin again.

The next slide counts both cases on the sifted key.

In the language of the last slide. Intercept-resend in the $\mathtt z$ basis is the unitary "copy the $\mathtt z$ value into a record, then re-prepare the photon from the record", followed by reading the record. It satisfies the hypothesis of Undisturbed means uninformed on $\ket0$ and $\ket1$ but not on $\ket\pm$, and that is where its errors come from.

Why random bases for Eve. Eve does not know Alice's basis, so any fixed choice is right half the time on average. Choosing at random makes her attack symmetric between the two bases, so the error rates Alice and Bob estimate in the $\mathtt z$ and $\mathtt x$ rounds come out equal. An Eve who always measures in $\mathtt z$ makes no errors on $\mathtt z$ rounds and twice as many on $\mathtt x$ rounds — the same average with uniform bases, but visible if the two error rates are estimated separately, which problem P2 shows matters once the bases are biased.

Why "resend". Eve cannot simply keep the photon: Bob would receive nothing, and photons Bob never detects are dropped from the protocol, so Eve learns nothing useful from them. She must send Bob something, and her best guess at what Alice sent is the state she found.

09 · intercept-resend

The trade-off, priced

intercept-resend on a fraction $f$

If Eve attacks each photon independently with probability $f$, in a random basis, then on the sifted key

$$\begin{aligned}Q &= \tfrac f4,\\ \Pr(\text{Eve right}) &= \tfrac12 + \tfrac f4 = \tfrac12 + Q,\end{aligned}$$

and Eve knows a fraction $\tfrac f2 = 2Q$ of the sifted bits exactly, and nothing of the rest.

proof

Condition on "attacked": the eight-case table gives error $\tfrac14$ and Eve right $\tfrac34$, a known bit half the time. Unattacked: no error, Eve's guess a coin. Average with weights $f$, $1 - f$. $\blacksquare$

Checks. $f = 0$: $Q = 0$, Eve at chance ✓. $f = 1$: $Q = \tfrac14$, Eve right $\tfrac34$ ✓, the table.

Information against disturbance. Intercept-resend buys Eve least per error; problems P4 and P5 compute the other two.

Full proof

Let $F$ be the event "Eve attacks this photon", independent of everything Alice and Bob do, with $\Pr(F) = f$. By the law of total probability, $Q = \Pr(\text{error}\mid F)f + \Pr(\text{error}\mid F^c)(1 - f) = \tfrac14f + 0$, using the eight-case table for the first term and, for the second, that an untouched photon measured in its own basis gives Alice's bit. Likewise $\Pr(\text{Eve right}) = \tfrac34f + \tfrac12(1 - f) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac f4$: on an untouched photon Eve has no data and guesses with a coin. Eve knows the bit exactly iff she attacked and chose the right basis, probability $f\cdot\tfrac12$. With $Q = f/4$ these are $\tfrac12 + Q$ and $2Q$. $\blacksquare$

Reading the line. Each percentage point of error buys intercept-resend Eve one more percentage point of guessing probability, and two of exactly known bits. The line ends at $f = 1$: $Q = \tfrac14$ is the most this attack can produce.

The other two marks, both proved on the sheet. The Breidbart point: measuring in the basis halfway between $\mathtt z$ and $\mathtt x$ gives the same $Q = \tfrac14$ but guesses right with $\cos^2\tfrac\pi8 = 0.8536$ (problem P4). The curve: a "gentle" partial copy of each photon's $\mathtt z$ value, tuned by an angle $\varphi$, gives $Q = (1 - \cos\varphi)/4$ and $\Pr(\text{right}) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14\sin\varphi$, that is $\tfrac12 + \tfrac14\sqrt{8Q - 16Q^2}$ (problem P5). At $Q = 0.05$: $\sqrt{0.4 - 0.04} = 0.6$, so $0.65$, against intercept-resend's $0.55$. The curve has infinite slope at $Q = 0$: a small disturbance buys a square-root amount of information, which is why gentle attacks are the dangerous ones.

Why the angle $22.5^\circ$. Eve wants the bit, not the state: bit 0 is $\ket0$ or $\ket+$, bit 1 is $\ket1$ or $\ket-$. 09 · the rate

The Shor–Preskill rate

the Shor–Preskill rate (2000, cited)

Run BB84 with one-way error correction and privacy amplification. Against every attack quantum mechanics allows — on single photons or on all of them jointly, with any quantum memory — Alice and Bob can extract secret key at

$$r(Q) = 1 - 2h(Q)$$

bits per sifted bit, in the limit of long keys, whenever $r(Q) > 0$.

Where the proof is. P. W. Shor and J. Preskill, Phys. Rev. Lett. 85, 441 (2000), by reduction to an entanglement-purification protocol built on quantum error-correcting codes (Part E); Mayers (2001) first; Renner (2005) for the composable, finite-key form.

Where a sifted bit at $Q = 5\%$ goes. $0.286 + 0.286 + 0.427 = 1$, to rounding.

the reading

One $h(Q)$ is the public parity information for error correction (Slepian–Wolf), which Eve hears. The other bounds what Eve can know about the corrected string by any attack, removed by privacy amplification.

Checks. $Q = 0$: $r = 1$, every sifted bit is key ✓. $Q = \tfrac12$: $r = -1$ — Bob's bits are coins, nothing to share ✓.

Stated exactly. The theorem concerns the protocol with one-way post-processing (only Alice speaks during error correction), with the error rate estimated from a random sample, and with the key length taken to infinity: the fraction of sifted bits that can be turned into key that is secret against all attacks tends to $1 - 2h(Q)$. Secret means: Eve's information about the final key, and the probability that Alice's and Bob's keys differ, both tend to zero exponentially. Rates for finite keys are lower, by terms of order $1/\sqrt n$ (Renner 2005; Tomamichel, Lim, Gisin and Renner, Nature Commun. 3, 634, 2012, cited).

The proof's structure, for orientation only. Shor and Preskill imagine that Alice prepares Bell pairs and sends half of each, so that BB84 becomes a protocol that distils nearly perfect Bell pairs from noisy ones using a quantum error-correcting code (a CSS code, unit 23's stabilisers). A pure Bell pair is unentangled with Eve (the theorem of section 4's A maximal violation leaves Eve nothing, in another guise), so the key from distilled pairs is secret. Then they show that Alice and Bob need not actually do the quantum operations: measuring first and doing the code's classical shadow afterwards gives the same key. Bit errors cost $h(Q)$ through the code's bit-flip part, which becomes error correction; phase errors cost another $h(Q)$ through its phase-flip part, which becomes privacy amplification. The details need Part E.

Mayers. D. Mayers, "Unconditional security in quantum cryptography", J. ACM 48, 351 (2001), preprint 1996: the first complete proof, long and hard. Lo and Chau (Science 283, 2050, 1999) proved security of an entanglement-based variant. Shor and Preskill's contribution was a short proof and the clean rate.

09 · the rate

Why the second $h(Q)$

Eve's knowledge shows up in the other basis. Undisturbed means uninformed said it at $Q = 0$: learning the $\mathtt z$ value costs errors on the $\mathtt x$ states. Quantitatively, Shor and Preskill bound Eve's knowledge of the $\mathtt z$-basis key by the error rate in the $\mathtt x$ basis — the phase error rate — as $h(\text{phase error rate})$ bits per bit.

Random bases make the two rates equal. Alice chooses each basis independently of Eve's attack, and Eve cannot tell which basis a photon carries, so the error rate she causes in the $\mathtt x$ rounds estimates the one in the $\mathtt z$ rounds: both are $Q$, measured on the sample.

priced for the worst Eve

For intercept-resend, Eve knows exactly $2Q$ bits per sifted bit — less than $h(Q)$. The second $h(Q)$ is the price of the best attack, and Alice and Bob pay it whatever Eve actually did.

What privacy amplification removes ($h(Q)$) against what intercept-resend actually learns ($2Q$). At $5\%$: $0.286$ against $0.10$.

Check: $h(Q) \ge 2Q$ on $[0, \tfrac14]$ — equal at $0$; $h' = \log\frac{1 - Q}{Q} \ge 2$ up to $Q = \tfrac15$; and $h(\tfrac14) = 0.811 > \tfrac12$ ✓.

"Phase error", in this unit's words. In the entanglement picture of the proof, a sifted $\mathtt z$ round with an error in the $\mathtt x$ basis is a phase flip on the shared pair ($Z$ applied to one half), and a bit error is a bit flip ($X$). Unit 21 names these noise models and Part E corrects them. Here the content is: the more Eve learns about $\mathtt z$ values, the more she disturbs $\mathtt x$ values, and the price of hiding what she learnt is $h$ of the disturbance — the information–disturbance trade-off, made quantitative for every attack at once.

Why the two rates are equal. Eve's operation on a photon cannot depend on the basis, which she does not know. So the probability of an error on a photon sent in $\mathtt x$ is the same function of her attack whether that photon ends up as a key bit or a sample bit. A careful version (problem P2) estimates the $\mathtt z$ and $\mathtt x$ error rates separately and uses each where it belongs; with uniform bases and a symmetric attack they coincide.

The check, in full. $f(Q) = h(Q) - 2Q$ has $f(0) = 0$ and $f'(Q) = \log\frac{1 - Q}{Q} - 2$, which is $\ge 0$ iff $\frac{1 - Q}Q \ge 4$, iff $Q \le \tfrac15$. So $f$ rises on $[0, \tfrac15]$ and falls after; at $Q = \tfrac14$, $f = 0.811 - 0.5 = 0.311 > 0$, so $f \ge 0$ on the whole interval.

Against intercept-resend alone a one-way protocol could keep $1 - h(Q) - 2Q$ bits per sifted bit (Csiszár and Körner 1978, cited), positive up to $Q = 0.1705$. Alice and Bob cannot use that rate: they do not know that Eve restricted herself.

Where 5 per cent comes from. It is typical of working systems: Micius reported error rates of a few per cent, fibre systems a few per cent. At $1\%$ the rate is $1 - 2\times0.080793 = 0.838$; at $3\%$, $0.611$ — every point of error is expensive.

The abort is the success. The protocol cannot stop Eve from listening; what it guarantees is that if she listens enough to matter, Alice and Bob find out before they use the key. A denial of service — Eve cutting the fibre, or attacking every run — is always possible, and no protocol prevents it.

E91: a source in the middle

Polariser angles are half the Bloch angles (unit 03): Alice's two settings are Bloch $0$ and $\tfrac\pi2$, Bob's dashed pair is $\pm\tfrac\pi4$ — unit 06's optimal CHSH directions.

E91 (Ekert 1991), in unit 06's settings

A source sends singlets $\ket{\Psi^-} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} - \ket{10})$, $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$. Each round, independently and uniformly, Alice measures $A(\theta)$ at $\theta \in \{0, \tfrac\pi2\}$, Bob at $\theta \in \{0, \tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi4, -\tfrac\pi4\}$, with

$$A(\theta) = \cos\theta\,Z + \sin\theta\,X .$$

Then they announce settings, never outcomes.

Physics: unit 03's crystal makes the pairs, and each side is the analyser of Single photons, from nothing. Nobody prepares a bit: the key is made by the measurements.

Ekert's own choice. A. K. Ekert, "Quantum cryptography based on Bell's theorem", Phys. Rev. Lett. 67, 661 (1991), used spin-½ particles with three analyser directions on each side, at azimuthal angles $0, \tfrac\pi4, \tfrac\pi2$ for Alice and $\tfrac\pi4, \tfrac\pi2, \tfrac{3\pi}4$ for Bob; the two shared directions give key, and the rest the CHSH test. The version here keeps unit 06's four CHSH directions exactly and lets the key come from Alice's two directions, which are BB84's two bases — so the comparison with section 1 is direct. $A(\theta)$, recalled. Unit 06: $A(\theta)$ is Hermitian and unitary ($A^2 = \cos^2\theta\,I + \sin^2\theta\,I + \sin\theta\cos\theta(ZX + XZ) = I$, since $ZX = -XZ$), with eigenvalues $\pm1$ — a $\pm1$-valued observable, measured by an analyser at polariser angle $\theta/2$. $A(0) = Z$ is the $\mathtt z$ basis, $A(\tfrac\pi2) = X$ the $\mathtt x$ basis, and $A(\pm\tfrac\pi4) = (Z \pm X)/\sqrt2$.

Why the key does not exist in advance. Before measurement, each photon's reduced state is $I/2$ (unit 04): there is no bit sitting on it for Eve to read in transit. Key rounds and test rounds

Eight equally likely setting pairs, $\tfrac18$ each: key $\tfrac28$, test $\tfrac48$, discard $\tfrac28$; sum $1$ ✓.

Key rounds. Unit 06: on the singlet, $E(\theta_a, \theta_b) = -\cos(\theta_a - \theta_b)$. Equal settings give $E = -1$: the outcomes are always opposite, and each alone is a fair coin ($\rho_A = \rho_B = I/2$). Bob flips his bit: a shared uniform bit.

Test rounds. The four cells give unit 06's CHSH quantity,

$$\begin{aligned}S = {}&E(0, \tfrac\pi4) + E(0, -\tfrac\pi4)\\ &+ E(\tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi4) - E(\tfrac\pi2, -\tfrac\pi4),\end{aligned}$$

and on the singlet $S = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1 + 1 + 1 + 1) = -2\sqrt2$.

check, against BB84

The key rounds are BB84's sifted rounds: $Z$ on both sides, or $X$ on both. With a perfect source the error rate is $Q = 0$ in both ✓.

The four test correlations. $E(0, \tfrac\pi4) = -\cos(-\tfrac\pi4) = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$; $E(0, -\tfrac\pi4) = -\cos\tfrac\pi4 = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$; $E(\tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi4) = -\cos\tfrac\pi4 = -\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$; $E(\tfrac\pi2, -\tfrac\pi4) = -\cos\tfrac{3\pi}4 = +\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$. So $S = -\tfrac3{\sqrt2} - \tfrac1{\sqrt2} = -\tfrac4{\sqrt2} = -2\sqrt2 = -2.828427$. This is unit 06's four directions exactly; the sign is negative because the singlet anticorrelates, and $|S|$ is what matters.

Key bits are uniform. In a $Z$–$Z$ round the outcomes $01$ and $10$ each have probability $|\tfrac1{\sqrt2}|^2 = \tfrac12$, and $00$, $11$ have probability $0$; sum $1$ ✓. In an $X$–$X$ round the same holds, because $\ket{\Psi^-} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{+-} - \ket{-+})$ up to global phase (unit 03's $U\otimes U$ invariance: $(H\otimes H)\ket{\Psi^-} = \det(H)\ket{\Psi^-} = -\ket{\Psi^-}$, global phase). Alice's bit is her outcome; Bob's is the complement of his.

Error rate. Here $Q$ is the probability, on key rounds, that the outcomes are equal (so that after Bob's flip the bits differ): $Q = \tfrac12(1 + E)$ at equal settings, which is $0$ on the singlet. Problem P10 computes it for a noisy source.

A maximal violation leaves Eve nothing

the alarm that cannot be fooled

Let Alice, Bob and Eve share any state $\rho_{ABE}$, and suppose the test rounds give $S = -2\sqrt2$ with these observables. Then

$$\rho_{ABE} = \ket{\Psi^-}\!\bra{\Psi^-}\otimes\rho_E ,$$

so every measurement of Eve's is independent of Alice's and Bob's outcomes — and of the key.

proof, in three steps

1. Unit 06: at these settings $S = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{AB}C)$ with $C = \sqrt2(Z\otimes Z + X\otimes X)$, whose eigenvalue $-2\sqrt2$ is non-degenerate, on $\ket{\Psi^-}$; so $\rho_{AB} = \ket{\Psi^-}\!\bra{\Psi^-}$, pure.
2. A pure marginal splits off (lemma in the notes, from unit 04's purification).
3. $\Pr(a, b, e) = \operatorname{tr}\big((P_a\otimes Q_b\otimes R_e)\,\rho_{ABE}\big) = \braket{\Psi^-|P_a\otimes Q_b|\Psi^-}\operatorname{tr}(R_e\rho_E)$, a product. $\blacksquare$

A pure state of $AB$ has nothing left over to share with $E$: the whole is a product.

what it assumes

That Alice's and Bob's devices really measure $Z$, $X$, $(Z \pm X)/\sqrt2$ on qubits; that rounds are independent; that $S$ is known exactly. Each can be removed — the last two by statistics, the first by self-testing (BBM92, and devices nobody trusts) — at a cost.

Full proof of the lemma

Lemma. If $\rho_{ABE}$ is a density operator whose reduced state $\rho_{AB} = \operatorname{tr}_E\rho_{ABE}$ is pure, $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi$, then $\rho_{ABE} = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi\otimes\rho_E$ with $\rho_E = \operatorname{tr}_{AB}\rho_{ABE}$.

Proof. By unit 04, $\rho_{ABE}$ has a purification: a unit vector $\ket\Omega \in H_{AB}\otimes H_E\otimes H_R$ with $\operatorname{tr}_R\ket\Omega\!\bra\Omega = \rho_{ABE}$. Its reduced state on $AB$ is $\operatorname{tr}_{ER}\ket\Omega\!\bra\Omega = \operatorname{tr}_E\rho_{ABE} = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$, pure. Unit 04's problem P7 (a pure bipartite state is a product iff its reduced state is pure), applied to the split $AB \mid ER$, gives $\ket\Omega = \ket{\psi'}\otimes\ket\varphi$ with $\ket{\psi'}\!\bra{\psi'} = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi$; absorbing a phase, $\ket{\psi'} = \ket\psi$. Then $\rho_{ABE} = \operatorname{tr}_R(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\otimes\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi) = \ket\psi\!\bra\psi\otimes\operatorname{tr}_R\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi$ (unit 04: $\operatorname{tr}_R(X\otimes Y) = X\operatorname{tr}_RY$ for $X$ on the kept systems). $\blacksquare$

Step 1 in full. With $B = A(\tfrac\pi4)$, $B' = A(-\tfrac\pi4)$: $B + B' = \sqrt2Z$ and $B - B' = \sqrt2X$, so the CHSH operator $C = Z\otimes(B + B') + X\otimes(B - B') = \sqrt2(Z\otimes Z + X\otimes X)$. Unit 06 (The bound is reached, and by exactly one state) diagonalised it in the Bell basis, eigenvalues $2\sqrt2, 0, 0, -2\sqrt2$, and proved that $\operatorname{tr}(\rho C) = -2\sqrt2$ forces $\rho = \ket{\Psi^-}\!\bra{\Psi^-}$. Here $\rho = \rho_{AB}$, since Eve's system does not enter $C$: $\operatorname{tr}(\rho_{ABE}(C\otimes I_E)) = \operatorname{tr}(\rho_{AB}C)$ by the definition of the partial trace.

Step 3 in full. $\operatorname{tr}((X\otimes Y)(\sigma\otimes\tau)) = \operatorname{tr}(X\sigma)\operatorname{tr}(Y\tau)$, with $X = P_a\otimes Q_b$, $\sigma = \ket{\Psi^-}\!\bra{\Psi^-}$, $Y = R_e$, $\tau = \rho_E$. Summing over $e$ gives Alice's and Bob's distribution, summing over $a, b$ gives Eve's, and the joint is their product: independence. In particular, conditioned on anything Eve observes, the key bits are still uniform and perfectly anticorrelated.

The word for this. Entanglement is monogamous: a maximally entangled pair cannot be correlated with a third party. Problem P9 asks for the whole argument.

09 · limits

Without authentication: the man in the middle

Two honest-looking BB84 runs. Alice ends with $k_A$, Bob with $k_B$; Eve has both.

The man-in-the-middle attack. If Eve can alter the public line, she cuts both channels and runs BB84 twice: with Alice, posing as Bob; with Bob, posing as Alice. Each run is a genuine, error-free BB84 session, so every check passes.

Then Alice encrypts with $k_A$; Eve decrypts, reads, re-encrypts with $k_B$ and forwards. Neither notices anything (problem P11).

the remedy, and its cost

Authenticate every public message (Wegman–Carter): a forged "I am Bob" is caught. That needs a pre-shared secret — short, but there from the start. QKD is key expansion.

Every key-exchange method has this gap: Diffie–Hellman without authentication falls to the same attack.

Why no check catches it. Every statistical test in BB84 compares data between the two ends of one session. In the attack each session has two honest ends — Alice and Eve, or Eve and Bob — and Eve, being an end, follows the protocol exactly, so the error rates are whatever the channel gives, zero in principle. Nothing in the quantum part identifies who is at the other end; only a secret shared in advance can.

How much secret. Wegman–Carter authentication of an $L$-bit message with forging probability $2^{-t}$ needs a key of order $t + \log L$ bits, and the key used for the tag of one message can be partly reused for the next if a one-time pad protects the tags. A QKD session producing $10^5$ bits needs a few hundred for its own authentication, so the expansion is large.

"From nothing" is impossible, not just unsolved. With no shared secret, Alice cannot distinguish Bob from anyone who behaves as Bob does. That is a statement about identity, not about physics, and no protocol, quantum or classical, gets around it.

09 · limits

Real sources sometimes send two photons

A dimmed laser does not emit exactly one photon: the number in a pulse is random, Poisson with mean $\mu$ (cited, from the quantum theory of light),

$$\Pr(n) = e^{-\mu}\frac{\mu^n}{n!}, \qquad \textstyle\sum_n\Pr(n) = e^{-\mu}e^{\mu} = 1 .$$

At $\mu = 0.1$: $\Pr(0) = 0.905$, $\Pr(1) = 0.0905$, $\Pr(n \ge 2) = 0.0047$ — about $4.9\%$ of the pulses that carry anything carry two or more.

the photon-number-splitting attack

On a two-photon pulse — two copies $\ket s\otimes\ket s$ of Alice's state — Eve splits off one photon, stores it, and forwards the other untouched. After sifting she measures hers in the announced basis: the bit, surely, and Bob's photon is undisturbed.

Photon-number splitting. No cloning is involved: Alice made the copy.

why it hurts

It leaves no errors, so $Q$ does not see it. With loss in the channel Eve can also block some single-photon pulses and forward multi-photon ones over a better line, keeping Bob's count rate normal.

The Poisson numbers. $e^{-0.1} = 0.904837$; $\Pr(1) = 0.1e^{-0.1} = 0.090484$; $\Pr(n\ge2) = 1 - 0.904837 - 0.090484 = 0.004679$. Among non-empty pulses, $0.004679/(1 - 0.904837) = 0.049167$. Lowering $\mu$ cuts the multi-photon fraction (roughly $\mu/2$) but also the rate, since most pulses are then empty. Why Poisson: an attenuated laser pulse is a "coherent state" of the light field, whose photon-number distribution is Poisson; this is quantum optics, cited, not derived here.

The attack in this course's terms. Two photons in the same polarisation are the product state $\ket s\otimes\ket s$ (unit 03). Eve takes the second factor; the first's reduced state is still $\ket s\!\bra s$ (unit 04), so Bob's statistics are exactly those of an honest single-photon pulse. After the announcement Eve measures her copy in the right basis and gets Alice's bit with probability $|\braket{s|s}|^2 = 1$. Physically, she needs to split off one photon without measuring polarisation — possible in principle by measuring photon number alone, which commutes with polarisation. Problem P12 writes it out. (G. Brassard, N. Lütkenhaus, T. Mor and B. C. Sanders, PRL 85, 1330 (2000), cited.)

Why no-cloning is not violated. No-cloning forbids a machine that copies an unknown state; here the copy was made by Alice's laser, which put two photons in the same state. That is the whole problem.

Decoy states, and the gaps that remain

Two intensities, the same photon numbers in different proportions. Eve sees only the photon number.

decoy states (Hwang 2003; Lo, Ma and Chen 2005)

Alice varies the intensity $\mu$ at random from pulse to pulse and announces each pulse's $\mu$ only afterwards. A pulse with $n$ photons looks the same to Eve whatever its $\mu$, so she must treat $n$-photon pulses alike; comparing Bob's detection rates across intensities then exposes any excess on multi-photon pulses.

The other gaps, all cited: detectors can be "blinded" by bright light and steered by Eve (Lydersen et al. 2010); timing and wavelength side channels; imperfect random number generators. Some are closed by measurement-device-independent protocols (Lo, Curty and Qi 2012), all by device independence — at a cost.

the lesson

A security proof covers its model. The model is a qubit and the four states; hardware is never exactly that.

The decoy argument, in one paragraph. Let $Y_n$ be the probability that Bob detects a pulse given that it carried $n$ photons. Eve's strategy can depend on $n$ (she can measure it without disturbing polarisation) but not on $\mu$, which she never sees. So the overall detection rate at intensity $\mu$ is $\sum_ne^{-\mu}\frac{\mu^n}{n!}Y_n$ with the same $Y_n$ for every $\mu$. Measuring it at several intensities gives several linear equations in the $Y_n$, enough to bound $Y_1$ and the error rate on single-photon pulses from below. The key is then computed from single-photon pulses only, at a rate close to the ideal protocol's. W.-Y. Hwang, PRL 91, 057901 (2003); H.-K. Lo, X. Ma and K. Chen, PRL 94, 230504 (2005); X.-B. Wang, PRL 94, 230503 (2005).

The bar values. $\mu = 0.1$: $0.9048, 0.0905, 0.0045, 0.0002$. $\mu = 0.5$: $e^{-0.5} = 0.6065$, $0.3033$, $0.0758$, and $0.0144$ for three or more; each sums to $1$. A pulse with two photons is about seventeen times likelier at $\mu = 0.5$ than at $0.1$ ($0.0758/0.0045$), which is why a decoy at high intensity amplifies anything Eve does to multi-photon pulses.

09 · limits

The experiments: from 32 centimetres to 1200 kilometres

Three landmarks, cited. Each step on the axis is a factor of ten.

  • 1992 — Bennett, Bessette, Brassard, Salvail and Smolin run BB84 over 32 cm of open air (J. Cryptology 5, 3).
  • 2017 — the Micius satellite sends decoy-state BB84 to a ground station over up to 1200 km, at a kilohertz-scale key rate (Liao et al., Nature 549, 43).
  • 2022 — device-independent QKD, from a CHSH violation alone (Nadlinger et al.; Zhang et al.).
why a satellite

Fibre loses about $0.2$ dB per km (cited). Over $1200$ km that is $240$ dB: a transmission of $10^{-24}$. A source of $10^9$ photons a second would get one through every $3\times10^7$ years. Most of a satellite's path is vacuum.

The fibre arithmetic. A loss of $L$ dB means transmission $10^{-L/10}$. At $0.2$ dB/km (typical telecom fibre at $1550$ nm), $1200$ km gives $L = 240$ and transmission $10^{-24}$. At $10^9$ photons per second, the expected number arriving per second is $10^{-15}$, i.e. one per $10^{15}$ seconds, and $10^{15}/(3.156\times10^7\ \text{s per year}) = 3.17\times10^7$ years. No-cloning forbids the obvious fix, an amplifier on the way; proposed "quantum repeaters" would use entanglement swapping (unit 07's teleportation, applied to pairs) and quantum memories, and do not yet exist at useful scale.

Micius. S.-K. Liao et al., "Satellite-to-ground quantum key distribution", Nature 549, 43 (2017): a low-Earth-orbit satellite launched in 2016, decoy-state BB84 with the satellite-to-ground distance varying from about 645 km to 1200 km during a pass, with error rates of a few per cent. Most of the loss is diffraction of the beam, which grows only as the square of the distance, and the atmosphere, which is crossed only near the ground.

1992. C. H. Bennett, F. Bessette, G. Brassard, L. Salvail and J. Smolin, "Experimental quantum cryptography", J. Cryptology 5, 3 (1992), describing an apparatus first run in 1989. It worked; its authors later recalled that the Pockels cells' power supplies hummed audibly at different pitches for different polarisations, so that an eavesdropper with a microphone could have listened — the first side channel, years before any proof of security (G. Brassard, "Brief history of quantum cryptography: a personal perspective", 2005, cited).

The sixteen photons, closed

The story of the sixteen. Sifting kept $8$; a sample of $4$ showed no error; the raw key was $0110$. With Eve on the line the same sample read $\hat Q = \tfrac14$, the rate went negative, and they aborted — rightly, since Eve knew all four bits.

09 · checkpoint

Done when

checkpoint

On paper and without notes, you can:

  • prove that intercept-resend in a random basis causes a $25\%$ error rate on the sifted key — all eight cases, probabilities summing to one — and that Eve then guesses each sifted bit with probability $\tfrac34$;
  • produce the $11.0\%$ threshold from the Shor–Preskill rate $1 - 2h(Q)$, by solving $2h(Q) = 1$ to three places, with $r(0.05) = 0.427$ on the way.

Failing it is unambiguous

An error rate of $50\%$ (counting every wrong-basis round as an error, forgetting Bob's coin); a count over all photons instead of the sifted ones; natural logarithms in $h$ (giving $Q^* = 0.1997$, which solves $2h_e(Q) = 1$ in nats instead of bits); a threshold off in the third decimal.
