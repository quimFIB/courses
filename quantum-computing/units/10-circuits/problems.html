the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. On paper and without notes, you prove CNOT plus one-qubit gates exactly universal — the two-level decomposition with its count \(\tfrac{d(d-1)}2\), the Gray-code step, many controls from one, and the ABC construction with its phase gate — and you build the seven-T Toffoli circuit and verify it on all eight basis inputs by its phase table: the seven terms sum to \(4abx\).

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, the last optional, about five hours. Work them on paper first.

Conventions, as in the deck: \(\braket{u|v}\) is conjugate-linear in the first slot; \(\ket0 = (1, 0)\), \(\ket1 = (0, 1)\), \(\ket\pm = (\ket0 \pm \ket1)/\sqrt2\). Qubit 0 is the leftmost symbol of a ket, the most significant bit of an index and the top wire of a circuit, so \(\ket{abc}\) is basis vector \(4a + 2b + c\). A circuit drawn \(G_1\) then \(G_2\) is the operator \(G_2G_1\). \(X, Y, Z\) are the Pauli matrices, \(H = (X + Z)/\sqrt2\), \(S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i)\), \(T = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4})\), \(R_P(\theta) = e^{-i\theta P/2}\); \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{01} = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes X\) has control 0; \(C(U) = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes U\), and \(C^k(U)\) has \(k\) controls. \(P(\alpha) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\alpha})\). The Toffoli is \(\mathrm{TOF}\ket{a, b, c} = \ket{a, b, c\oplus ab}\).

The model, and measurement

P1 Three CNOTs make a SWAP, and one cannot medium

exercises: circuits as products on basis states, conjugating a CNOT by Hadamards, the Schmidt rank of an operator as a lower bound

\(\mathrm{SWAP}\ket{x, y} = \ket{y, x}\) on two qubits.

  1. Show that \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{10}\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{01} = \mathrm{SWAP}\), where \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{10}\) has control 1 and target 0. Does the order of the three matter?
  2. Show that \((H\otimes H)\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\,(H\otimes H) = \mathrm{CNOT}_{10}\), and so write SWAP using CNOTs of one orientation only. Give the gate count.
  3. Every two-qubit operator can be written \(M = \sum_{i=1}^rA_i\otimes B_i\); the least such \(r\) is its operator Schmidt rank. Show that \(\mathrm{CNOT}\) has rank 2 and \(\mathrm{SWAP}\) has rank 4, and that multiplying by one-qubit gates on either side, \((L_1\otimes L_2)M(R_1\otimes R_2)\), does not change the rank. Conclude that no circuit with a single CNOT, however many one-qubit gates surround it, is SWAP.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1–2 are identities between \(4\times4\) unitaries: check them on the four basis states (part 1) or by a one-line matrix identity (part 2). Part 3 is a statement about a tensor in a space of the form \(V\otimes W\), with \(V = W\) the \(2\times2\) matrices — unit 03's Schmidt rank, applied to operators instead of states.

Rung 2

Part 1: track the pair of bits \((x, y)\) through the three gates; each CNOT replaces one bit by the XOR of both. Part 2: \(HXH = Z\), and \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{01} = \tfrac12(I\otimes I + Z\otimes I + I\otimes X - Z\otimes X)\). Part 3: write SWAP in the Pauli basis, and use that the four Paulis are a basis of the \(2\times2\) matrices (unit 02).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \((x, y)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}}(x, x\oplus y)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{CNOT}_{10}}(x\oplus x\oplus y,\ x\oplus y) = (y, x\oplus y)\). For part 3, \(\mathrm{SWAP} = \tfrac12(I\otimes I + X\otimes X + Y\otimes Y + Z\otimes Z)\).

Worked solution

Idea. A circuit of CNOTs is a reversible map of bit pairs, so part 1 is three XORs. Part 3 needs an invariant that one-qubit gates cannot change: how many product terms an operator needs.

1. On basis states, \((x, y)\mapsto(x, x\oplus y)\mapsto(y, x\oplus y)\mapsto(y, y\oplus x\oplus y) = (y, x)\). A linear map is fixed by the basis, so the product is SWAP. The order does matter in general, but here the circuit is a palindrome: reversed it is the same three gates. The other alternation, \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{10}\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\mathrm{CNOT}_{10}\), is also SWAP (the same computation with the roles of the qubits exchanged).

2. Write \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{01} = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes X = \tfrac12(I + Z)\otimes I + \tfrac12(I - Z)\otimes X\), i.e. \(\tfrac12(I\otimes I + Z\otimes I + I\otimes X - Z\otimes X)\). Conjugating by \(H\otimes H\) conjugates each factor: \(HIH = I\), \(HZH = X\), \(HXH = Z\). So the result is \(\tfrac12(I\otimes I + X\otimes I + I\otimes Z - X\otimes Z) = \tfrac12(I + Z)\otimes I + \tfrac12(I - Z)\otimes X\) with the factors exchanged — \(I\otimes\ket0\!\bra0 + X\otimes\ket1\!\bra1 = \mathrm{CNOT}_{10}\). Hence

\[ \mathrm{SWAP} = \mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\,(H\otimes H)\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\,(H\otimes H)\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}: \]

3 CNOTs and 4 Hadamards, 7 gates (as one-qubit gates on the same wire in the same layer, \(H\otimes H\) is two gates).

3. The ranks. \(\mathrm{CNOT} = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes X\) has \(r\le2\); it is not a single product \(A\otimes B\), since then every block \(A_{ij}B\) would be a multiple of one matrix \(B\), but its diagonal blocks are \(I\) and \(X\), not proportional. So \(r = 2\). For SWAP, expand in Paulis: \(\mathrm{SWAP} = \tfrac12\sum_{P\in\{I, X, Y, Z\}}P\otimes P\) (checked on basis states below). With the four Paulis linearly independent, unit 03's theorem says the rank of \(\sum_iA_i\otimes B_i\) with \(\{A_i\}\) and \(\{B_i\}\) both linearly independent is the number of terms: 4.

Invariance. The map \(M\mapsto(L_1\otimes L_2)M(R_1\otimes R_2)\) sends \(A\otimes B\) to \(L_1AR_1\otimes L_2BR_2\), so it sends an \(r\)-term expression to an \(r\)-term one; its inverse (with \(L_i^{-1}, R_i^{-1}\)) does the same. So the rank is unchanged.

Conclusion. A circuit with one CNOT is \((L_1\otimes L_2)\,\mathrm{CNOT}\,(R_1\otimes R_2)\) after merging one-qubit gates on each wire (a CNOT of either orientation; \(\mathrm{CNOT}_{10}\) also has rank 2, by part 2). Its rank is 2, and SWAP's is 4. Two CNOTs are not enough either, and three are optimal — that needs more than ranks (cited: G. Vidal and C. M. Dawson, Phys. Rev. A 69, 010301, 2004).

Check. The Pauli expansion of SWAP on \(\ket{01}\): \(I\otimes I\) gives \(\ket{01}\); \(X\otimes X\) gives \(\ket{10}\); \(Y\otimes Y\ket{01} = (i\ket1)(-i\ket0) = \ket{10}\); \(Z\otimes Z\ket{01} = -\ket{01}\). Sum \(\tfrac12(\ket{01} + \ket{10} + \ket{10} - \ket{01}) = \ket{10}\) ✓. On \(\ket{00}\): \(\tfrac12(\ket{00} + \ket{11} - \ket{11} + \ket{00}) = \ket{00}\) ✓.

P2 Deferred measurement, in general medium

exercises: the measurement postulate as a product of projectors, commuting a projector past gates it only controls, the chain rule for outcome probabilities

A circuit on \(\ket\psi\) alternates blocks of gates and meters: \(U_1\), meter on qubit \(q_1\) (outcome \(m_1\)), \(U_2\), meter on \(q_2\), …, meter on \(q_r\), \(U_{r+1}\), then a final measurement \(\{Q_k\}\) of the qubits never metered. The block \(U_j\) may depend on the earlier outcomes \(m_1, \dots, m_{j-1}\) (classical control), and a qubit already metered is used afterwards only as a control: every gate of \(U_j\) is block diagonal in the computational basis of each \(q_i\), \(i \lt j\).

  1. Write the probability of the outcomes \((m_1, \dots, m_r, k)\) as a squared norm of a single vector.
  2. Define the deferred circuit: each classically controlled block replaced by the quantum-controlled \(\tilde U_j = \sum_{m'}\ket{m'}\!\bra{m'}_{q_1\dots q_{j-1}}\otimes U_j(m')\), all meters moved to the end. Prove it gives the same joint distribution.
  3. Prove that, for every outcome, the two circuits also leave the same post-measurement state.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is \(\lVert Q_k\beta\rVert^2\) for an unnormalised branch vector \(\beta\) built from the \(U_j\) and the projectors \(P_{m_j}\) on \(q_j\); part 2 is an operator identity showing the deferred circuit produces the same \(\beta\).

Rung 2

Each meter multiplies the probability by \(\lVert P_m\phi\rVert^2\) and divides the state by its square root, so the normalisations telescope. For part 2, two facts: \(P_{m_i}\) on \(q_i\) commutes with every later block, because those blocks only control on \(q_i\); and \(\ket{m}\!\bra m\tilde U_j = \ket m\!\bra m U_j(m)\), the projector picks the branch.

Rung 3

First line: \(\Pr(m_1, \dots, m_r, k) = \lVert Q_k\beta_m\rVert^2\) with \(\beta_m = U_{r+1}(m)\,P_{m_r}\,U_r(m)\cdots P_{m_1}\,U_1\psi\), where \(U_j(m)\) uses only \(m_1..m_{j-1}\).

Worked solution

Idea. The early meters cut the state into branches and the later gates act branch by branch; a controlled block does exactly the same thing to the same branches, it just never normalises them. Moving each projector to the end of the circuit is legal because nothing after a meter touches that qubit except as a control.

1. Let \(\phi_0 = \psi\). By unit 01's rule, the first meter gives \(m_1\) with probability \(\lVert P_{m_1}U_1\psi\rVert^2\) and leaves \(P_{m_1}U_1\psi\) divided by its norm. Conditioned on that, the second meter gives \(m_2\) with probability \(\lVert P_{m_2}U_2(m_1)P_{m_1}U_1\psi\rVert^2/\lVert P_{m_1}U_1\psi\rVert^2\), and so on: the joint probability is the product of conditionals, and the denominators cancel the previous numerators. So

\[ \Pr(m_1, \dots, m_r, k) = \lVert Q_k\,\beta_m\rVert^2,\qquad \beta_m = U_{r+1}(m)\,P_{m_r}\,U_r(m)\cdots U_2(m)\,P_{m_1}\,U_1\,\psi . \]

(If some branch has probability zero, both sides are zero.)

2. Let \(\mathcal P_m = P_{m_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{m_r}\) on the metered qubits and \(W = \tilde U_{r+1}\cdots\tilde U_1\) (with \(\tilde U_1 = U_1\)). The deferred circuit measures everything at the end, so its probability is \(\lVert(\mathcal P_m\otimes Q_k)W\psi\rVert^2 = \lVert Q_k\,\mathcal P_mW\psi\rVert^2\) (\(Q_k\) acts on other qubits and commutes with \(\mathcal P_m\)). Now peel the blocks off from the left, carrying the projectors through them. Write \(\Pi_{\lt j} = P_{m_1}\cdots P_{m_{j-1}}\), the projectors on the qubits metered before block \(j\). Two facts:

  • Selection. \(\Pi_{\lt j}\tilde U_j = \Pi_{\lt j}U_j(m)\), because \(\Pi_{\lt j}\ket{m'}\!\bra{m'} = 0\) unless \(m' = (m_1, \dots, m_{j-1})\).
  • Passing. \(\Pi_{\lt j}U_j(m) = U_j(m)\,\Pi_{\lt j}\): every gate of \(U_j(m)\) is block diagonal in the basis of each \(q_i\), \(i \lt j\), by hypothesis.

Start with \(\mathcal P_m\tilde U_{r+1} = \Pi_{\lt r+1}\tilde U_{r+1} = U_{r+1}(m)\,\mathcal P_m\). Next, \(\mathcal P_m\tilde U_r = P_{m_r}\,\Pi_{\lt r}\tilde U_r = P_{m_r}\,U_r(m)\,\Pi_{\lt r}\): \(P_{m_r}\) stays where it is (\(U_r\) may act on \(q_r\), which is metered only after it), and the rest pass through. Each step leaves one projector behind and carries the others on, down to \(\tilde U_1 = U_1\), which only \(P_{m_1}\) reaches. So

\[ \mathcal P_mW\psi = U_{r+1}(m)\,P_{m_r}\,U_r(m)\cdots P_{m_1}\,U_1\,\psi = \beta_m , \]

and both distributions are \(\lVert Q_k\beta_m\rVert^2\). \(\blacksquare\)

3. The early circuit ends, given the outcomes, in \(Q_k\beta_m\) normalised; the deferred one in \((\mathcal P_m\otimes Q_k)W\psi = Q_k\beta_m\) normalised. The same vector.

Check. With \(r = 1\), \(U_1 = I\) and \(U_2(m) = U_m\) this is the deck's theorem: \(\beta_m = U_mP_m\psi = (P_m\otimes U_m)\psi\) when \(U_m\) acts on the other qubits. And the deck's worked example: \(\psi = \ket\chi\ket+\), \(U_2(1) = H\) on qubit 1, gives \(\beta_1 = s\ket1\ket0\), \(\lVert\beta_1\rVert^2 = s^2 = \Pr(10)\) ✓.

Where the hypothesis was used. Only in the first bullet. If a later gate acted on \(q_i\) as anything but a control — an \(H\), say — \(P_{m_i}\) could not be moved past it, and the counterexample on the deck's warning (measure \(\ket+\), then \(H\)) shows the statement fails.

P3 XOR the long way routine

exercises: compiling a NAND circuit into Toffoli gadgets, Bennett's compute–copy–uncompute, counting gates and work qubits

XOR is four NANDs: \(n_1 = \mathrm{NAND}(a, b)\), \(n_2 = \mathrm{NAND}(a, n_1)\), \(n_3 = \mathrm{NAND}(b, n_1)\), \(a\oplus b = \mathrm{NAND}(n_2, n_3)\).

  1. Check the four-NAND formula on all four inputs.
  2. Compile it into Toffolis with the deck's gadget, then apply Bennett's trick to produce the clean oracle \(\ket{a, b}\ket{0000}\ket y\mapsto\ket{a, b}\ket{0000}\ket{y\oplus a\oplus b}\). Count the gates of each kind and the work qubits.
  3. Compare with the best circuit you can find for the same oracle.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a circuit on 7 qubits and a gate count — a number for each of \(X\), Toffoli and CNOT.

Rung 2

The NAND gadget needs its target at \(\ket1\): an \(X\) on a fresh \(\ket0\). Each NAND gets its own work qubit \(w_1, \dots, w_4\). Bennett: the forward circuit, one CNOT from \(w_4\) to \(y\), the forward circuit reversed — including the \(X\)'s, so the work qubits return to \(\ket0\), not \(\ket1\).

Rung 3

First lines: \(X\) on \(w_1..w_4\); Toffoli \((a, b; w_1)\), Toffoli \((a, w_1; w_2)\), Toffoli \((b, w_1; w_3)\), Toffoli \((w_2, w_3; w_4)\).

Worked solution

Idea. One gadget per gate, one work qubit per gate, then compute–copy–uncompute: the general recipe, at its general price.

1. \(ab = 00\): \(n_1 = 1\), \(n_2 = \mathrm{NAND}(0, 1) = 1\), \(n_3 = 1\), output \(\mathrm{NAND}(1, 1) = 0\) ✓. \(01\): \(n_1 = 1\), \(n_2 = 1\), \(n_3 = \mathrm{NAND}(1, 1) = 0\), output \(1\) ✓. \(10\): symmetric, \(1\) ✓. \(11\): \(n_1 = 0\), \(n_2 = n_3 = 1\), output \(0\) ✓.

2. Forward circuit \(C\) on \(a, b, w_1, w_2, w_3, w_4\): \(X\) on each \(w_i\) (they become \(\ket1\)); then Toffolis \((a, b; w_1)\), \((a, w_1; w_2)\), \((b, w_1; w_3)\), \((w_2, w_3; w_4)\). Each Toffoli sets its target to \(1\oplus(\text{AND of controls}) = \) NAND, and never changes a control, so \(w_4 = a\oplus b\) at the end. Copy: CNOT \((w_4; y)\). Uncompute: \(C^{-1}\), the same gates in reverse order (each is its own inverse), ending with the four \(X\)'s, which return the \(w_i\) from \(\ket1\) to \(\ket0\).

Count. 8 \(X\), 8 Toffolis, 1 CNOT: 17 gates, 4 work qubits, 7 qubits in all.

3. Two CNOTs, \((a; y)\) then \((b; y)\), with no work qubits: \(y\mapsto y\oplus a\oplus b\). XOR is linear, and reversible circuits do linear functions with CNOTs alone. Bennett's recipe is general, not economical; its value is that it works for every circuit at a constant factor — here \(2\times\) the 8-gate forward circuit, plus one.

Check. On \(ab = 11\), \(y = 0\): forward gives \(w = (0, 1, 1, 0)\); the copy leaves \(y = 0 = 1\oplus1\) ✓; reversing restores \(w = (1, 1, 1, 1)\) and the \(X\)'s give \(0000\) ✓.

P4 A two-bit adder, uncomputed medium

exercises: chaining the deck's one-bit adder, deciding when a carry can be uncomputed, counting work qubits and gates

Two-bit numbers \(a = 2a_1 + a_0\) and \(b = 2b_1 + b_0\) sit on four wires. Build a circuit of Toffolis and CNOTs with work qubits starting at \(\ket0\) that leaves \(a\) unchanged and the three-bit sum \(a + b\) on wires \(b_1, b_0\) and one extra wire \(z\) (as \(4z + 2b_1 + b_0\)), with every other work qubit returned to \(\ket0\). Give the number of work qubits and of gates, and check \(a = b = 3\).

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a circuit on six wires — \(a_1, a_0, b_1, b_0\), a carry \(c\) and the top bit \(z\) — with about seven gates.

Rung 2

Bit 0 is a half adder: carry \(c = a_0b_0\), sum \(a_0\oplus b_0\). Bit 1 is the deck's full adder with \(c_{\rm in} = c\), but arranged so the sum lands on \(b_1\) and \(c\) is left unchanged. Then \(c\) is garbage: uncompute it while \(b_0\) still holds \(b_0\), and only then overwrite \(b_0\).

Rung 3

First lines: Toffoli \((a_0, b_0; c)\); Toffoli \((a_1, b_1; z)\); CNOT \((a_1; b_1)\); Toffoli \((c, b_1; z)\); CNOT \((c; b_1)\).

Worked solution

Idea. A ripple of the deck's adder. The intermediate carry is needed by bit 1 and by nobody afterwards, so it is garbage, and the order of the last steps is chosen so that it can be uncomputed from inputs that are still intact.

The circuit, gate by gate on basis states:

  1. Toffoli \((a_0, b_0; c)\): \(c = a_0b_0\), the carry out of bit 0.
  2. Toffoli \((a_1, b_1; z)\): \(z = a_1b_1\).
  3. CNOT \((a_1; b_1)\): \(b_1\to a_1\oplus b_1\).
  4. Toffoli \((c, b_1; z)\): \(z = a_1b_1\oplus c(a_1\oplus b_1) = \mathrm{maj}(a_1, b_1, c)\), the top bit (the deck's carry argument).
  5. CNOT \((c; b_1)\): \(b_1\to a_1\oplus b_1\oplus c = s_1\).
  6. Toffoli \((a_0, b_0; c)\): \(c\to a_0b_0\oplus a_0b_0 = 0\) — legal because \(a_0\) and \(b_0\) are untouched so far.
  7. CNOT \((a_0; b_0)\): \(b_0\to a_0\oplus b_0 = s_0\).

Count. 4 Toffolis and 3 CNOTs, 7 gates; 2 work qubits, of which \(c\) returns to \(\ket0\) and \(z\) carries the top bit of the sum. Six wires in all.

Why it is right. \(a + b = (a_0 + b_0) + 2(a_1 + b_1) = s_0 + 2c + 2(a_1 + b_1)\), and \(a_1 + b_1 + c = s_1 + 2\,\mathrm{maj}(a_1, b_1, c)\): so \(a + b = s_0 + 2s_1 + 4z\).

Check \(a = b = 3\) (\(a_1 = a_0 = b_1 = b_0 = 1\)): \(c = 1\); \(z = 1\); \(b_1 = 0\); \(z = 1\oplus1\cdot0 = 1\); \(b_1 = 0\oplus1 = 1\); \(c = 1\oplus1 = 0\); \(b_0 = 1\oplus1 = 0\). Output \(z b_1 b_0 = 110 = 6 = 3 + 3\) ✓, \(c = 0\) ✓. Also \(a = 2\), \(b = 1\): \(c = 0\), \(z = 0\), \(b_1 = 1\), \(z = 0\), \(b_1 = 1\), \(c = 0\), \(b_0 = 1\): \(011 = 3\) ✓.

What goes wrong in the other order. Writing \(s_0\) onto \(b_0\) first and uncomputing \(c\) afterwards needs \(a_0b_0 = a_0\,\overline{s_0}\): an extra pair of \(X\) gates around the Toffoli. Still possible, but the order above is cheaper.

P5 Garbage destroys interference medium

exercises: the oracle on \(\ket-\) as a phase, the probability formula with a garbage register, the overlap of garbage states

Take one input bit and \(f(x) = x\), so \(U_f\ket x\ket y = \ket x\ket{y\oplus x}\) is a CNOT.

  1. Show \(U_f\ket x\ket- = (-1)^{f(x)}\ket x\ket-\). Prepare \(\ket+\ket-\), apply \(U_f\), then \(H\) on the first qubit and measure it. What is \(\Pr(1)\)?
  2. Now use a careless oracle that leaves a garbage qubit holding a copy of \(x\): \(\ket x\ket y\ket0\mapsto\ket x\ket{y\oplus x}\ket x\). Repeat. What is \(\Pr(1)\)?
  3. In general the garbage is \(\ket{g(x)}\), unit vectors with overlap \(\gamma = \braket{g(0)|g(1)}\). Show \(\Pr(1) = \tfrac12(1 + \mathrm{Re}\,\gamma)\), and read off parts 1 and 2.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Each answer is a probability; part 3's formula interpolates between \(1\) and \(\tfrac12\).

Rung 2

\(X\ket- = -\ket-\), so XORing a bit into \(\ket-\) multiplies by \((-1)^{\text{bit}}\). With garbage, the state before the last \(H\) is \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_x(-1)^x\ket x\ket{g(x)}\ket-\); expand \(H\) on the first qubit and collect the \(\ket1\) part.

Rung 3

First line of part 3: after \(H\), the \(\ket1\) component is \(\tfrac12\big(\ket{g(0)} + \ket{g(1)}\big)\ket-\), since \((-1)^x\) and the \(H\) sign \((-1)^x\) multiply to \(1\).

Worked solution

Idea. The oracle writes \(f(x)\) as a sign — a relative phase between the branches \(x = 0\) and \(x = 1\). A relative phase is visible only if the branches can interfere, and a garbage record of \(x\) stops them, exactly as unit 01's which-path detector did.

1. \(U_f\ket x\ket- = \ket x\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{f(x)} - \ket{1\oplus f(x)})\), which is \(\ket x\ket-\) if \(f(x) = 0\) and \(-\ket x\ket-\) if \(f(x) = 1\) ("phase kickback"; unit 13 makes it the engine of every query algorithm).   1. Prove that every circuit of \(X\), CNOT, Toffoli and SWAP gates, on any number of qubits, is a permutation matrix.
  2. Conclude that such a circuit, started on a basis state, ends on a basis state, and every measurement of it is deterministic. In particular it never produces \(\ket+\) on any qubit.
  3. Add the diagonal gates \(Z, S, T\) and \(CZ\). Show the conclusion of part 2 survives: no superposition is created. Which gate of the deck breaks it?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a closure statement: a set of matrices closed under multiplication and under extension by identities.

Rung 2

A permutation matrix sends each basis vector to a basis vector. A monomial matrix sends each basis vector to a basis vector times a unit complex number. Check each gate, then that placing it on chosen wires and multiplying keep the property.

Rung 3

First line: if \(G\ket u = \ket{\pi(u)}\) on its wires, then on the whole register \(G\ket{x}\) is the basis state with the bits on those wires replaced by \(\pi\) of them.

Worked solution

Idea. Classical gates move basis states to basis states; phases only decorate them. A superposition needs a gate that sends one basis state to a combination of several.

1. Each gate is a bijection of bit strings on its wires: \(X\) flips, CNOT XORs, Toffoli XORs an AND, SWAP exchanges. Placed on wires \(Q\) of an \(n\)-qubit register (the deck's definition) it maps \(\ket x\) to \(\ket{x'}\), where \(x'\) is \(x\) with the bits on \(Q\) replaced by their image — a bijection of \(\{0, 1\}^n\), so a permutation matrix. A product of permutation matrices is the matrix of the composite bijection, again a permutation matrix.

2. So \(U\ket x = \ket{\sigma(x)}\) for some bijection \(\sigma\). Measuring any qubit of \(\ket{\sigma(x)}\) gives its bit with probability 1. \(\ket+\) on a qubit would mean probability \(\tfrac12\) of each outcome on it — impossible.

3. \(Z, S, T, CZ\) are diagonal with unit-modulus entries: \(D\ket x = e^{i\theta(x)}\ket x\). Call a matrix monomial if \(M\ket x = e^{i\theta(x)}\ket{\sigma(x)}\) for a bijection \(\sigma\). Permutation and diagonal unitary gates are monomial; extending to more wires keeps the form; and products of monomial matrices are monomial (\(M'M\ket x = e^{i\theta(x)}e^{i\theta'(\sigma(x))}\ket{\sigma'(\sigma(x))}\)). So from \(\ket x\) the output is \(e^{i\phi}\ket{\sigma(x)}\): a basis state up to global phase, and all measurements are again deterministic. The deck's gate that breaks this is \(H\) (and, in the ABC construction, \(R_y\)): \(H\ket0 = \ket+\) has two non-zero amplitudes.

Check. The seven-T Toffoli circuit contains two \(H\)'s, and between them its state is a superposition (on \(\ket{110}\), \(H\) on \(c\) gives \(\ket{11}\ket+\)); the \(H\)'s are what let \(T\) phases, which alone are monomial, build a permutation that phases alone could not.

What adding \(H\) buys (cited, for later): Toffoli and \(H\) together are universal for quantum computation in the sense of approximation with real amplitudes (Y. Shi, Quantum Inf. Comput. 3, 84, 2003; D. Aharonov, 2003). Unit 11 is about what "approximate" means there.

  2. Prove that, in time order, \(C(V)\) from \(b\) onto the target, \(\mathrm{CNOT}\) \(a\to b\), \(C(V^\dagger)\) from \(b\), \(\mathrm{CNOT}\) \(a\to b\), \(C(V)\) from \(a\), is \(C^2(U)\) with controls \(a, b\).

Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is the functional calculus of unit 00; part 2 a sum of exponents; part 3 two integers; part 4 a named gate.

Rung 2

A unitary is normal with eigenvalues \(e^{i\theta_j}\). In part 2, on basis states the target receives a power of \(V\) from each controlled gate, and all these powers commute. In part 3, each controlled gate costs two CNOTs and at most four one-qubit gates.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: the exponents are \(b\), then \(-(a\oplus b)\) (the middle gate sees \(b\) after the first CNOT), then \(a\); and \(b - (a\oplus b) + a = 2ab\).

Worked solution

Idea. Split \(U\) into two halves and let each control apply one half; the CNOTs arrange that the halves cancel unless both controls are on.

1. By the spectral theorem (unit 00) \(U = \sum_je^{i\theta_j}P_j\) with orthogonal projectors summing to \(I\). Put \(V = \sum_je^{i\theta_j/2}P_j\): it is unitary (\(V^\dagger V = \sum|e^{i\theta_j/2}|^2P_j = I\)) and \(V^2 = \sum e^{i\theta_j}P_j = U\). (There are several square roots; any one works.)

2. On \(\ket{a, b}\ket\psi\): the first gate applies \(V^b\); the CNOT makes the second wire \(a\oplus b\); the middle gate applies \(V^{-(a\oplus b)}\); the CNOT restores \(b\); the last gate applies \(V^a\). Powers of one operator commute, so the target receives \(V^{b - (a\oplus b) + a}\). For bits, \(b - (a\oplus b) + a = 2ab\) (\(00\): \(0\); \(01\): \(1 - 1 + 0 = 0\); \(10\): \(0 - 1 + 1 = 0\); \(11\): \(1 - 0 + 1 = 2\)). So the target receives \(V^{2ab} = U^{ab}\), and the controls end as they began: this is \(C^2(U)\) on the basis, hence everywhere. \(\blacksquare\)

3. Three controlled gates, each 2 CNOTs and at most 4 one-qubit gates (\(A, B, C\) and \(P(\alpha)\)), plus the 2 CNOTs between them: 8 CNOTs and at most 12 one-qubit gates, so at most 20 gates. (Adjacent one-qubit gates on the same wire can be merged, which can lower the 12.) For \(U = X\) the deck's version uses \(V = HSH\) and gets 8 CNOTs, 9 \(T\)-type and 2 \(H\): 19.

4. \(U = Z\): \(V = S\), since \(S^2 = \mathrm{diag}(1, i^2) = Z\). The circuit is \(C(S)\) from \(b\), CNOT, \(C(S^\dagger)\) from \(b\), CNOT, \(C(S)\) from \(a\) — and with problem P7's \(C(S)\) it becomes a CNOT–\(T\) network, a phase polynomial for \(4abx\) once more. It is \(C^2(Z)\), the middle of the seven-T Toffoli.

Check. On \(\ket{11}\ket\psi\): \(V\), then \(b\to0\) so the middle gate is idle, then \(b\to1\), then \(V\): \(V^2\ket\psi = U\ket\psi\) ✓. On \(\ket{01}\ket\psi\) the CNOTs do nothing (\(a = 0\)), so \(b = 1\) throughout: \(V\), then \(V^\dagger\), and the last gate is idle: \(V^\dagger V = I\) ✓.

P9 How many two-level factors hard

exercises: running the two-level construction by hand, a connectivity lower bound, a parameter-count lower bound

Let \(U = \tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 2\\ 2 & 1 & -2\\ 2 & -2 & 1\end{pmatrix}\).

  1. Check that \(U\) is unitary, and run the deck's construction on it. Show that it uses all \(\tfrac{3\cdot2}2 = 3\) factors, and write \(U\) as their product.
  2. Prove that if a product of two-level unitaries has every entry non-zero, the pairs \(\{s, t\}\) they act on, as edges on the vertices \(1, \dots, d\), form a connected graph — so at least \(d - 1\) factors are needed. Exhibit a \(d\times d\) unitary with every entry non-zero, for every \(d\ge3\).
  3. Using the deck's counting argument, show that a generic \(d\times d\) unitary needs at least \(d^2/4\) two-level factors in any decomposition. How far is the construction's \(\tfrac{d(d-1)}2\) from this?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is three \(3\times3\) matrices with entries involving \(\sqrt5\); part 2 a graph argument; part 3 a dimension count ending in a ratio below 2.

Rung 2

Part 1: the first factor acts on rows 1, 2 with \((a, b) = (\tfrac13, \tfrac23)\), so \(n = \tfrac{\sqrt5}3\). Part 2: a two-level unitary on \(\{s, t\}\) maps the span of the basis vectors of any union of graph components to itself. Try \(I - \tfrac2dJ\), \(J\) the all-ones matrix. Part 3: each two-level factor is a point of \(U(2)\), 4 real parameters, and the choice of pair is discrete.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(W_1 = \tfrac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 2 & -1\end{pmatrix}\) on rows 1, 2, and \(W_1U\) has first column \((\tfrac{\sqrt5}3, 0, \tfrac23)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The construction's count is an upper bound. Two lower bounds bracket the truth: connectivity gives \(d - 1\) for some unitaries, and counting parameters gives \(d^2/4\) for almost all — so the construction is within a factor 2 of optimal.

1. Unitary. Rows \((1, 2, 2), (2, 1, -2), (2, -2, 1)\) have squared length \(9\) and pairwise dot products \(2 + 2 - 4 = 0\), \(2 - 4 + 2 = 0\), \(4 - 2 - 2 = 0\); divided by 3 they are orthonormal, and \(U\) is real, so \(UU^\dagger = I\) ✓.

Step 1 (rows 1, 2 of column 1): \(a = \tfrac13\), \(b = \tfrac23\), \(n = \tfrac{\sqrt5}3\); \(W_1 = \tfrac1n\begin{pmatrix}a & b\\ b & -a\end{pmatrix} = \tfrac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 2 & -1\end{pmatrix}\). New rows: \(r_1' = (r_1 + 2r_2)/\sqrt5 = \tfrac1{3\sqrt5}(5, 4, -2)\), \(r_2' = (2r_1 - r_2)/\sqrt5 = \tfrac1{3\sqrt5}(0, 3, 6) = \tfrac1{\sqrt5}(0, 1, 2)\), \(r_3\) unchanged.

Step 2 (rows 1, 3): \(a = \tfrac{\sqrt5}3\), \(b = \tfrac23\), \(n = 1\); \(W_2 = \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt5/3 & 2/3\\ 2/3 & -\sqrt5/3\end{pmatrix}\) on rows 1, 3. New row 1: \(\tfrac{\sqrt5}3r_1' + \tfrac23r_3 = \tfrac19(5, 4, -2) + \tfrac19(4, -4, 2) = (1, 0, 0)\). New row 3: \(\tfrac23r_1' - \tfrac{\sqrt5}3r_3 = \tfrac1{9\sqrt5}\big((10, 8, -4) - (10, -10, 5)\big) = \tfrac1{\sqrt5}(0, 2, -1)\).

Step 3. Now \(W_2W_1U = 1\oplus\tfrac1{\sqrt5}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 2 & -1\end{pmatrix} =: W_3\), two-level on rows 2, 3. Each \(W_i\) is real symmetric and orthogonal, so \(W_i^{-1} = W_i\), and

\[ U = W_1\,W_2\,W_3 , \]

all three non-trivial: no entry the construction zeroed was zero to begin with.

2. Connectivity. Let the factors' pairs form a graph on \(\{1, \dots, d\}\), and suppose it is disconnected; let \(K\) be one component, not everything. Each factor acts on a pair inside one component, so it maps \(\mathrm{span}\{\ket j : j\in K\}\) into itself (a pair inside \(K\) is mixed within \(K\); a pair outside \(K\) leaves \(K\)'s vectors fixed). So the product does too: its entries \(\braket{i|M|j}\) with \(j\in K\), \(i\notin K\) vanish. Hence every-entry-non-zero forces a connected graph, and a connected graph on \(d\) vertices has at least \(d - 1\) edges. Example: \(R = I - \tfrac2dJ\) with \(J\) the all-ones matrix. It is real symmetric, and \(J^2 = dJ\) gives \(R^2 = I - \tfrac4dJ + \tfrac4{d^2}dJ = I\), so \(R\) is orthogonal. Its entries are \(1 - \tfrac2d\) and \(-\tfrac2d\), all non-zero for \(d\ge3\).

3. Counting. Fix the sequence of pairs used by \(m\) factors (finitely many choices). The product is the image of the smooth map \(U(2)^m\to U(d)\), from a manifold of dimension \(4m\). If \(4m \lt d^2 = \dim U(d)\) the image has measure zero (Sard, easy case, as in the deck), and so does the finite union over pair sequences. So a generic unitary needs \(m\ge d^2/4\). The construction uses \(\tfrac{d(d-1)}2\), and \(\tfrac{d(d-1)/2}{d^2/4} = \tfrac{2(d-1)}d \lt 2\): within a factor of 2.

Check. \(d = 3\): bounds \(d - 1 = 2\) and \(\lceil 9/4\rceil = 3\); the construction used 3 on \(U\), so for a generic \(3\times3\) unitary three factors are exactly optimal. \(d = 2\): \(\lceil 4/4\rceil = 1 = \tfrac{2\cdot1}2\) ✓.

P11 Counting parameters routine

exercises: the dimension \(4^n - 1\), the counting lower bound, the same bound for CNOT and one-qubit gates

  1. For \(n = 1, 2, 3, 10\), give the number of real parameters of an \(n\)-qubit unitary up to global phase, and the deck's lower bound on the number of gates (each on at most two qubits) that a generic one needs.
  2. In a circuit of CNOTs and one-qubit gates only the one-qubit gates carry parameters. Show that a generic \(n\)-qubit unitary then needs at least \((4^n - 1)/3\) one-qubit gates, and evaluate this for the same \(n\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Eight numbers in part 1 and four in part 2; the last of each is in the tens or hundreds of thousands.

Rung 2

Part 1: \(4^n - 1\) and \(\lceil(4^n - 1)/16\rceil\). Part 2: a one-qubit gate is a point of \(U(2)\), 4 parameters, but its phase is a global phase of the whole circuit; so the circuit with \(g\) one-qubit gates is the image of \(SU(2)^g\times U(1)\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: the image of a smooth map from a manifold of dimension \(3g + 1\) into \(U(2^n)\), of dimension \(4^n\), has measure zero unless \(3g + 1\ge4^n\).

Worked solution

Idea. Compare the dimension of what a circuit can reach with the dimension of all unitaries.

1. \(4^n - 1\) and the bound \(\lceil(4^n - 1)/16\rceil\):

  • \(n = 1\): \(3\) parameters; bound \(\lceil 3/16\rceil = 1\).
  • \(n = 2\): \(15\); bound \(\lceil 15/16\rceil = 1\).
  • \(n = 3\): \(63\); bound \(\lceil 63/16\rceil = \lceil3.94\rceil = 4\).
  • \(n = 10\): \(4^{10} - 1 = 1\,048\,575\); bound \(\lceil 65\,535.94\rceil = 65\,536 = 4^8\approx6.6\times10^4\) gates.

2. Fix the positions of the CNOTs and one-qubit gates. Write each one-qubit gate as \(e^{i\varphi_j}V_j\) with \(V_j\in SU(2)\); the phases commute out of the product as one global phase \(e^{i\sum\varphi_j}\). So the reachable set is the image of \(SU(2)^g\times U(1)\to U(2^n)\), a smooth map from dimension \(3g + 1\). If \(3g + 1 \lt 4^n\) its image has measure zero (Sard, as in the deck), and there are finitely many layouts for each \(g\) and each number of CNOTs — countably many in all, and a countable union of measure-zero sets has measure zero. So generically \(g\ge(4^n - 1)/3\):

  • \(n = 1\): \(1\). \(n = 2\): \(5\). \(n = 3\): \(21\). \(n = 10\): \(349\,525\approx3.5\times10^5\).

(\(4^n - 1\) is divisible by 3, since \(4\equiv1\bmod 3\), so these are whole numbers.)

Check. \(n = 1\): one one-qubit gate makes every one-qubit unitary, so the bound 1 is attained ✓. \(n = 2\): the deck's controlled-\(H\) used four one-qubit gates — fewer than 5, which is no contradiction, because controlled-\(H\) is a special unitary, not a generic one. The optimal circuits for a generic two-qubit gate use three CNOTs with one-qubit gates around and between them (cited: Vatan and Williams, Phys. Rev. A 69, 032315, 2004).

Reading the numbers. At \(n = 10\), ten qubits, a generic unitary already needs tens of thousands of gates; the bound grows by a factor 4 per qubit. The unitaries algorithms use are the rare exceptions with polynomial circuits.

exercises: a gate on chosen wires as a tensor contraction, the qubit order, testing against an independent implementation (optional; nothing later depends on it)

An \(n\)-qubit state is a vector of \(2^n\) amplitudes. Reshape it to an array with \(n\) axes of length 2 — axis \(j\) is qubit \(j\), qubit 0 first — and a \(k\)-qubit gate to an array with \(2k\) axes (\(k\) output, then \(k\) input). Applying the gate to wires \(q_1, \dots, q_k\) is contracting its input axes with the state's axes \(q_1, \dots, q_k\).

  1. Explain, from the definition of a gate on chosen wires in the deck, why that contraction is the right operation.
  2. Write a function apply(psi, gate, qubits, n) in NumPy that does it in about ten lines.
  3. Test it against qclib's Circuit(...).run() on 200 random circuits of \(H, T, S\), CNOT and Toffoli gates on 3 to 6 qubits.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a short function using reshape, np.tensordot and np.moveaxis, plus a test loop.

Rung 2

\(\braket{y|G_{(Q)}|x} = \braket{y_Q|G|x_Q}\prod_{j\notin Q}\delta_{y_jx_j}\) says: the new amplitude at \(y\) is \(\sum_{x_Q}G_{y_Q, x_Q}\,\psi_{x_Q, y_{\text{rest}}}\) — a sum over the gate's input indices only. tensordot puts the gate's output axes first; moveaxis must put them back on wires \(q_1, \dots, q_k\).

Rung 3

First lines: t = psi.reshape((2,) * n); g = gate.reshape((2,) * (2 * k)); t = np.tensordot(g, t, axes=(list(range(k, 2 * k)), list(qubits))).

Worked solution

Idea. A gate on \(k\) wires is a matrix in those \(k\) indices and the identity in the rest; as arrays, that is a contraction over \(k\) axes. It costs \(O(2^n\cdot2^k)\) operations instead of the \(4^n\) of building the full matrix.

1. With \(\psi_x = \braket{x|\psi}\), \((G_{(Q)}\psi)_y = \sum_x\braket{y|G_{(Q)}|x}\psi_x = \sum_{x_Q}\braket{y_Q|G|x_Q}\,\psi_{x_Q\cup y_{\text{rest}}}\): the delta functions fix every index off \(Q\). Reshaping a vector of length \(2^n\) to shape \((2, \dots, 2)\) in C order makes the first axis the most significant bit — qubit 0, the course's convention — so the index \(x\) of the vector is the multi-index \((x_0, \dots, x_{n-1})\) of the array. The gate's matrix, reshaped to \((2,)\times2k\), has entry \(G[y_{q_1}, \dots, y_{q_k}, x_{q_1}, \dots, x_{q_k}]\) — again most significant first, matching the order qubits is given in.

2.

import numpy as np

def apply(psi, gate, qubits, n):
    """Apply a 2^k x 2^k gate to the listed qubits (in that order) of an n-qubit state."""
    k = len(qubits)
    t = psi.reshape((2,) * n)                    # one axis per qubit, qubit 0 first
    g = gate.reshape((2,) * (2 * k))             # k output axes, then k input axes
    t = np.tensordot(g, t, axes=(list(range(k, 2 * k)), list(qubits)))
    t = np.moveaxis(t, list(range(k)), list(qubits))   # new axes back to their wires
    return t.reshape(-1)

tensordot returns the gate's \(k\) output axes followed by the state's untouched axes in their old order; moveaxis sends output axis \(i\) to position \(q_i\), and the untouched axes slide into the remaining positions in order — where they were.
