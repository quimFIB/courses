Circuits: reversible classical computation, and exact universality

A quantum computer is a recipe for building a large unitary from small ones. CNOT and one-qubit gates build every unitary exactly; every classical computation fits inside, reversibly; and most unitaries still need exponentially many gates.

needs 03, 02~2½ h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 10

Parts A and B used unitaries one at a time: a beam splitter, a rotation of a spin, a CNOT in a teleporter. This unit asks how a large unitary — one acting on $n$ qubits, a $2^n\times2^n$ matrix — is built from small ones, each touching one or two qubits. The answer comes in three parts. Circuits are a formal model, and measurement may as well wait until the end. Every classical computation can be run inside the model, reversibly and with its scratch work cleaned up, which is what makes every oracle of Part D a polynomial-size circuit. And CNOT plus one-qubit gates build every unitary exactly — but a count of parameters shows that almost all unitaries need exponentially many gates, so the real question is efficiency, not possibility.

What this unit is not: a compiler course. Finite gate sets, approximation and the price of the $T$ gate are unit 11's; the complexity class the model defines is unit 12's; which gates a real device has is unit 26's.

10 · overview

A reversible AND, and how to build it

the running example: the Toffoli gate

On three qubits, for bits $a, b, c$, extended linearly:

$$\mathrm{TOF}\ket{a, b, c} = \ket{a,\, b,\, c\oplus ab}.$$

It flips $c$ exactly when $a = b = 1$: an AND whose inputs survive, so nothing is lost and it can be undone. It permutes the eight basis states, so it is unitary.

The question. Real devices apply gates to one or two qubits at a time. How is a three-qubit gate built from those — and is every gate buildable?

The plan. 1 the circuit model · 2 classical computation, reversibly · 3 deferred measurement · 4 exact universality · 5 counting.

Two controls, one target. The answer, in section 4: fifteen gates, seven of them $T$ or $T^\dagger$.

10 · the model

Wires, gates, and the product they denote

a quantum circuit

On $n$ qubits ($(\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes n}$, qubit 0 the leftmost tensor factor): a finite list of gates $G_1, \dots, G_m$, each a unitary on one, two or three named qubits, the wires it acts on. Gate $G_j$ on wires $Q$ acts as $G_j$ on those factors and $I$ on the rest. The circuit denotes

$$U = G_m\cdots G_2G_1 .$$
two directions

The diagram is read left to right (time); the formula is read right to left (the rightmost factor acts first). $G_1$ is drawn first and written last.

Conventions (fixed for the course): qubit 0 is the top wire and the most significant bit, so $\ket{01}$ is basis vector 1. (Qiskit numbers the other way.)

The reversal, shown once: drawn $H$ then CNOT; written $\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}(H\otimes I)$.

Check on $\ket{00}$: $(H\otimes I)\ket{00} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{10})$, then CNOT flips the second qubit of the second term: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11}) = \ket{\Phi^+}$, of norm $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ ✓.

A gate on chosen wires, precisely. A $k$-qubit gate $G$ placed on wires $q_1, \dots, q_k$ is the operator on $(\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes n}$ with matrix entries $\braket{y|G_{(q_1\dots q_k)}|x} = \braket{y_{q_1}\cdots y_{q_k}|G|x_{q_1}\cdots x_{q_k}}\prod_{j\notin Q}\delta_{y_jx_j}$, for bit strings $x, y$. When the wires are $0, \dots, k-1$ this is $G\otimes I$ (unit 03's Kronecker rule); in general it is $\Pi^{-1}(G\otimes I)\Pi$ for the permutation $\Pi$ of tensor factors that brings the chosen wires to the front. A permutation of factors sends the product basis to itself, so it is unitary, and so is $G_{(Q)}$ — a product of unitaries.

Why a product is the right meaning. Unit 01's evolution postulate says a closed system evolves by a unitary; applying one after another composes them. Gates on disjoint wires commute (unit 03: operators on different factors commute), so gates drawn in the same column may be multiplied in either order.

Unit 07's promise. Unit 07 drew the teleportation circuit as a picture of a product of operators and said this unit would make circuits a formal model; this slide is that definition.

What counts as a gate. A gate set is the list of unitaries allowed. This unit uses two: all one-qubit gates together with CNOT, and, as a stepping stone, the Toffoli. Unit 11 replaces "all one-qubit gates" by a finite list.

10 · the model

Controlled gates

controlled-U

For a unitary $U$ on some qubits, with one control qubit in front:

$$C(U) = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes U,$$

in blocks $\mathrm{diag}(I, U)$. So $\ket0\ket\psi\mapsto\ket0\ket\psi$ and $\ket1\ket\psi\mapsto\ket1 U\ket\psi$. With $k$ controls, $C^k(U)$ applies $U$ exactly when all $k$ read 1.

Unitary: $C(U)^\dagger C(U) = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes U^\dagger U = I$, the cross terms dying because $\braket{0|1} = 0$ ✓.

CNOT is $C(X)$: $\ \ket{c, t}\mapsto\ket{c, t\oplus c}$ (unit 03, problem P2). The Toffoli gate is $C^2(X)$.

● a control; ⊕ a target of $X$; ○ an open control, which acts on 0: an $X$ before and after an ordinary control.

a controlled phase is not a global phase

$e^{i\alpha}I$ is invisible, but $C(e^{i\alpha}I) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\alpha})\otimes I$: a relative phase on the control. Section 4 pays for it with a gate on the control wire.

Full proof of unitarity. $C(U)^\dagger = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes U^\dagger$, since $(A\otimes B)^\dagger = A^\dagger\otimes B^\dagger$ (unit 03) and projectors are Hermitian. Multiplying, $(\ket a\!\bra a\otimes X)(\ket b\!\bra b\otimes Y) = \braket{a|b}\ket a\!\bra b\otimes XY$, so the two cross terms vanish and the diagonal terms give $\ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes U^\dagger U = (\ket0\!\bra0 + \ket1\!\bra1)\otimes I = I$.

Multiply controlled. With $P = \ket{1\cdots1}\!\bra{1\cdots1}$ on the $k$ controls, $C^k(U) = (I - P)\otimes I + P\otimes U$; the same computation shows it is unitary. In block form it is the identity on every control pattern but the last, and $U$ there.

The open control. "Act when the control reads 0" is $\ket0\!\bra0\otimes U + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes I = (X\otimes I)\,C(U)\,(X\otimes I)$: conjugating by $X$ swaps the two projectors, since $X\ket0\!\bra0X = \ket1\!\bra1$. So an open control costs two $X$ gates, and nothing new is needed.

The controlled phase, computed. $C(e^{i\alpha}I) = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + e^{i\alpha}\ket1\!\bra1\otimes I = (\ket0\!\bra0 + e^{i\alpha}\ket1\!\bra1)\otimes I$. On $(\ket0 + \ket1)\ket\psi$ it produces $(\ket0 + e^{i\alpha}\ket1)\ket\psi$, a different state for $\alpha\ne0$ — measurable in the $x$ basis of the control. So "$U$ up to global phase" is good enough for a gate applied unconditionally, and not for one that will be controlled.

The Toffoli gate, as a matrix

the Toffoli, column by column

Column $j$ is the image of basis vector $j$ (bits $abc$, $a$ most significant). Only $\ket{110}$ (index 6) and $\ket{111}$ (index 7) move, to each other:

$$\mathrm{TOF} = \begin{pmatrix}I_6 & 0\\ 0 & X\end{pmatrix},\qquad X = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}.$$

The $X$ block sits on rows and columns 6, 7. Every column has a single 1: column $j$ has it in row $j$ for $j\le5$, and columns 6 and 7 have theirs in rows 7 and 6.

Checks. A permutation matrix — one 1 in each row and column — has orthonormal columns, so it is unitary ✓. This one is also symmetric, so Hermitian, and $\mathrm{TOF}^2 = I$: it is its own inverse ✓.

The Toffoli as a permutation of the eight basis states: one transposition.

On a superposition it acts linearly: $\mathrm{TOF}\,\ket{11}\ket+ = \ket{11}\ket+$, since $X\ket+ = \ket+$; and $\mathrm{TOF}\,\ket{11}\ket- = -\ket{11}\ket-$.

Index arithmetic. With qubit 0 most significant, $\ket{abc}$ is basis vector $4a + 2b + c$. So $\ket{110}$ is 6 and $\ket{111}$ is 7, and the block $X$ sits in rows and columns 6–7 (counting from 0). This is the block form of $C^2(X)$ on the previous slide: identity on the control patterns $00, 01, 10$, and $X$ on $11$.

Permutation matrices are unitary, in full. If $P\ket j = \ket{\pi(j)}$ for a bijection $\pi$, then $\braket{Pi|Pj} = \braket{\pi(i)|\pi(j)} = \delta_{ij}$ because $\pi$ is injective: the columns are orthonormal, which is $P^\dagger P = I$. Being square, $P$ is then invertible with $P^{-1} = P^\dagger$.

The sign on $\ket-$. $X\ket- = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket1 - \ket0) = -\ket-$. So on $\ket{ab}\ket-$ the Toffoli multiplies by $(-1)^{ab}$ and leaves the target alone: the bit $ab$ has become a phase. Unit 13 calls this phase kickback and builds every query algorithm on it; problem P5 sees what garbage does to it.

Measurement and classical control

a meter, and a double line

A meter on qubit $q$ is the measurement $\{\ket0\!\bra0_q, \ket1\!\bra1_q\}$ (each $\otimes I$ on the rest). Outcome $m$ has probability $p_m = \lVert(\ket m\!\bra m_q\otimes I)\psi\rVert^2$, and the state becomes that vector over $\sqrt{p_m}$ (unit 01's rule, unit 03's form for a subsystem). A double line carries the bit $m$.

a classically controlled gate

A gate fed by a double line is applied when the bit is 1. It is not one operator: it is $U^m$, a different one in each branch. A circuit with meters denotes a family of products, one per string of outcomes.

Measure, then decide. Section 3 proves this can always be replaced by one unitary and a meter at the end.

Check on $\ket\psi = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)\otimes\ket0$ with $U = X$: outcome $0$ (prob. $\tfrac12$) leaves $\ket{00}$; outcome $1$ (prob. $\tfrac12$) leaves $\ket{10}$, then $X$ gives $\ket{11}$. Probabilities $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ ✓.

Why the computational basis is enough. A measurement in any other orthonormal basis $\{V\ket0, V\ket1\}$ of one qubit is a unitary $V^\dagger$ followed by a meter: $|\braket{Vm|\psi}|^2 = |\braket{m|V^\dagger\psi}|^2$. So circuits lose nothing by allowing only $z$ meters. The same holds on several qubits: to measure in the basis $\{V\ket x\}$, apply $V^\dagger$ and then meters.

The check, step by step. $(\ket0\!\bra0\otimes I)\psi = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ket{00}$, of squared norm $\tfrac12$; normalised, $\ket{00}$. Likewise for outcome 1, giving $\ket{10}$. Then $U^m$ acts on qubit 1: nothing for $m = 0$, $X$ for $m = 1$.

In unit 04's language (not needed here): the family of branches, weighted by their probabilities, is a density operator, and the measured circuit is a channel. This unit never needs that: every argument below computes branch probabilities from pure states, as unit 01 does.

Size and depth

size and depth

The size of a circuit is its number of gates. Its depth is the least number of layers it can be cut into, each layer a set of gates on disjoint wires — equivalently, the longest chain of gates each sharing a wire with the next.

Why two measures. Size is total work. Depth is time on a machine that applies gates on disjoint qubits at once — and time is what noise charges for (unit 21).

The running example. The textbook circuit for the Toffoli in section 4 has size 15 and depth 11: the target wire alone carries 10 gates in a row.

efficient means polynomial

A family of unitaries $U_n$ on $n$ qubits is efficiently implementable if circuits of size polynomial in $n$, from a fixed gate set, build them — the same standard as classical circuit families.

Size 4, depth 3. $H$ and $X$ act on different wires, so they share a layer.

Check the denoted product: layer by layer, $U = \mathrm{CNOT}_{12}\,\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\,(H\otimes I\otimes X)$. On $\ket{000}$: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{001} + \ket{101})$, then $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{001} + \ket{111})$, then $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{001} + \ket{110})$; norm 1 ✓.

The two definitions of depth agree. Assign each gate the length of the longest chain of gates ending at it, where consecutive gates in a chain share a wire and are in drawn order. Gates with the same value act on disjoint wires (two gates sharing a wire are ordered, so the later one has a strictly larger value): this cuts the circuit into as many layers as the longest chain. And no cut does better, because the gates of a chain must lie in different, increasing layers.

The check in full. $(H\otimes I\otimes X)\ket{000} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)\ket0\ket1$. $\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}$ flips qubit 1 when qubit 0 is 1: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{001} + \ket{111})$. $\mathrm{CNOT}_{12}$ flips qubit 2 when qubit 1 is 1: the second term becomes $\ket{110}$.

The Toffoli's depth. Of the fifteen gates in section 4, the ten on the target wire form a chain, and the final CNOT, $T$, $T^\dagger$, CNOT on wires $a, b$ must follow the last gate on $a$ (the fourth CNOT, eighth gate overall): a longest chain has 11 gates. Reordering commuting gates does not shorten it.

10 · reversible computation

Reversible gates are permutations

a reversible gate

A bijection $g$ of $\{0, 1\}^k$. Its matrix $\ket x\mapsto\ket{g(x)}$ is a permutation matrix, hence unitary: every reversible gate is a quantum gate.

AND is not one. $\{0, 1\}^2\to\{0, 1\}$ loses a bit, and three inputs share the output 0. No unitary can implement $\ket{a, b}\mapsto\ket{ab}$: it would map four orthonormal vectors into a 2-dimensional space.

lemma: every function, embedded

For any $f:\{0, 1\}^n\to\{0, 1\}^m$, the map $(x, y)\mapsto(x, y\oplus f(x))$ is a bijection of $\{0, 1\}^{n+m}$ — its own inverse.

proof

Applying it twice gives $(x, y\oplus f(x)\oplus f(x)) = (x, y)$. $\blacksquare$

AND forgets. The Toffoli is the lemma applied to AND: $(a, b, c)\mapsto(a, b, c\oplus ab)$.

Check on the Toffoli: with $f(a, b) = ab$, $n = 2$, $m = 1$, the lemma's map is exactly $\mathrm{TOF}$, and "its own inverse" is $\mathrm{TOF}^2 = I$ ✓.

Why no unitary computes AND in place. A unitary is injective. The four basis states $\ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}, \ket{11}$ are orthonormal, so their images are orthonormal too, and four orthonormal vectors do not fit in $\mathbb C^2$. More simply: $\ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}$ would all map to $\ket0$, contradicting injectivity.

The cost of reversibility, in bits. The embedding keeps the input. So a reversible computation of $f$ ends holding $x$ as well as $f(x)$ — which is no loss for a quantum computer, since the oracles of Part D are exactly this map.

History. Landauer (1961) argued that erasing a bit has an unavoidable physical cost, which made reversibility interesting as physics; Bennett (1973) showed it costs almost nothing as computation. This course needs only the second fact.

The Toffoli is universal for reversible computation

theorem (Toffoli 1980)

With work bits preset to 0 or 1, Toffoli gates compute NAND, NOT and FANOUT. So every circuit of $g$ NAND gates becomes a reversible circuit of $g$ Toffolis and at most $g + 1$ work bits, with each output bit on its own wire.

proof: three gadgets
  • NAND: $\ket{a, b, 1}\mapsto\ket{a, b, 1\oplus ab}$, and $1\oplus ab = \lnot(ab)$.
  • FANOUT: $\ket{1, b, 0}\mapsto\ket{1, b, b}$: a copy of $b$.
  • NOT: $\ket{1, 1, c}\mapsto\ket{1, 1, \lnot c}$.

NAND alone builds every Boolean function (background: $\lnot x = \mathrm{NAND}(x, x)$, and AND, OR follow). Replace each gate by its gadget on a fresh work bit. $\blacksquare$

The three gadgets. Constant inputs are work bits, prepared as $\ket0$ or $X\ket0 = \ket1$.

fan-out is mostly free

A wire can control many gates without being copied: a control is read, not consumed. The FANOUT gadget is needed only when a copy will be changed.

Full proof of the count. A Boolean circuit is a list of gates, each NAND of two earlier wires (inputs or earlier gate outputs), after rewriting AND, OR, NOT into NANDs at a constant factor. Gate $j$ becomes: a fresh work bit $w_j$ preset to 1, and a Toffoli with controls the two wires NAND reads and target $w_j$. Since controls are not changed, every earlier wire still holds its value, so $w_j$ ends holding exactly what gate $j$ computed. One Toffoli and one work bit per gate. A Toffoli's two controls must be different wires, so a gate that reads one wire twice, $\mathrm{NAND}(x, x)$, is written $\mathrm{NAND}(x, 1)$ instead, with a single extra work bit preset to 1 serving as the second control for every such gate (controls are never changed, so one suffices): hence $g + 1$.

NAND is complete (background, recalled): $\lnot x = \mathrm{NAND}(x, x)$, $x\wedge y = \lnot\mathrm{NAND}(x, y)$, $x\vee y = \mathrm{NAND}(\lnot x, \lnot y)$; and every Boolean function is an OR of ANDs of literals (its disjunctive normal form).

On the gate set. The theorem needs constants. No reversible gate set on two bits is universal even with constants (every reversible map of two bits is affine over $\mathbb F_2$ — cited: Toffoli 1980), which is why a three-bit gate is needed classically. Quantumly, section 4 removes that need: the Toffoli is built from two-qubit gates, at the price of leaving the classical world in the middle of the circuit.

Garbage destroys interference

What the gadgets leave behind. Translating a circuit gate by gate maps $\ket x\ket{0\cdots0}$ to $\ket x\ket{f(x)}\ket{g(x)}$, where $g(x)$ is every intermediate value: garbage. Classically harmless. Quantumly, on a superposition of inputs, the garbage is entangled with $x$ — and an entangled record of $x$ is a which-path detector (unit 01).

the smallest case: one bit of garbage

Clean. $H$ then $H$: $\ket0\mapsto\ket+\mapsto\ket0$. Outcome 0 with probability $1$.

With garbage. After $H$ and a CNOT copying $x$: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})$. Then $H$ on qubit 0:

$$\tfrac12\big(\ket{00} + \ket{10} + \ket{01} - \ket{11}\big),$$

so each outcome of qubit 0 has probability $\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$; total $1$ ✓. The two paths no longer meet.

Top: a copy of $x$ left behind turns certainty into a coin toss. Bottom: uncompute the copy first, and the interference returns.

Why it happens. The two terms reaching the meter carry orthogonal garbage, $\ket0$ and $\ket1$, so their amplitudes cannot add: probabilities add instead.

The computation in full. $H\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)$; with the garbage qubit, $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{10})$; the CNOT gives $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})$. Then $H\otimes I$: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)\ket0 + \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - \ket1)\ket1\big)$, the slide's state. Measuring qubit 0 with $\ket0\!\bra0\otimes I$ leaves $\tfrac12(\ket{00} + \ket{01})$, of squared norm $\tfrac12$.

Bottom circuit. The second CNOT returns the garbage to $\ket0$: the state is $\ket+\ket0$ again, and $H$ gives $\ket0\ket0$.

The general principle. If two branches $\ket{\phi_0}\ket{g_0}$ and $\ket{\phi_1}\ket{g_1}$ with orthogonal records $g_0\perp g_1$ are measured on the first factor only, the probabilities are $\lVert(P\otimes I)(\alpha\phi_0 g_0 + \beta\phi_1 g_1)\rVert^2 = |\alpha|^2\lVert P\phi_0\rVert^2 + |\beta|^2\lVert P\phi_1\rVert^2$: the cross term carries $\braket{g_0|g_1} = 0$. This is unit 01's Worked: the fringes vanish, with the garbage playing the which-path detector. Problem P5 shows it wrecks phase kickback, the engine of Part D.

Bennett's uncomputation

theorem: uncomputation (Bennett 1973)

Let $C$ be a reversible circuit with $C\ket x\ket0\ket0 = \ket x\ket{f(x)}\ket{g(x)}$ (input, work, garbage registers). Append $m$ CNOTs copying the work register onto a target $\ket y$, then $C^{-1}$. The result is

$$\ket x\ket0\ket0\ket y\ \mapsto\ \ket x\ket0\ket0\ket{y\oplus f(x)} .$$

Cost: $2\,\mathrm{size}(C) + m$ gates, no extra work bits.

proof, on a basis state

$C$ gives $\ket x\ket{f(x)}\ket{g(x)}\ket y$. The CNOTs change only the target: $\ket x\ket{f(x)}\ket{g(x)}\ket{y\oplus f(x)}$. $C^{-1}$ does not touch the target and sees exactly what $C$ produced, so it returns $\ket x\ket0\ket0$. Extend linearly. $\blacksquare$

Compute, copy, uncompute. The work bits are borrowed at 0 and returned at 0.

Work qubits (or ancillas) are qubits that start in a known state and, after uncomputation, end in it — so they are unentangled with everything and may be reused.

Full proof. Write $W = C^{-1}\,K\,C$ with $K$ the CNOT layer, all acting on $\ket x\ket w\ket z\ket y$, with $C$ acting as $C\otimes I_y$. On $\ket x\ket0\ket0\ket y$: $C$ gives $\ket x\ket{f(x)}\ket{g(x)}\otimes\ket y$. $K$ adds the work register into the target bit by bit: $\ket x\ket{f(x)}\ket{g(x)}\ket{y\oplus f(x)}$. Now $C^{-1}\otimes I$ acts on the first three registers, which hold $C(\ket x\ket0\ket0)$, so it returns $\ket x\ket0\ket0$; the target factor is untouched. A linear map is fixed by the basis, so the theorem holds for every superposition $\sum_{x,y}\alpha_{xy}\ket x\ket0\ket0\ket y$: it goes to $\sum\alpha_{xy}\ket x\ket0\ket0\ket{y\oplus f(x)}$, with the work registers factored off — unentangled, which is the whole point.

$C^{-1}$ is a circuit. $(G_m\cdots G_1)^{-1} = G_1^{-1}\cdots G_m^{-1}$: the gates in reverse order, each inverted. Toffolis, CNOTs and $X$ are their own inverses, so $C^{-1}$ is $C$ drawn backwards. It has the same size.

Time, and space. The cost in time is a factor about 2. Bennett's paper (C. H. Bennett, "Logical reversibility of computation", IBM J. Res. Dev. 17, 525, 1973) also handles space — the garbage of a long computation can be huge — by uncomputing in nested stages; that refinement (Bennett 1989, cited) trades a little time for much less space, and this course does not need it.

Every polynomial-time function is a polynomial-size oracle

corollary: the standard oracle

If $f:\{0, 1\}^n\to\{0, 1\}^m$ is computable in time polynomial in $n$, there are circuits of Toffoli, CNOT and $X$ gates, of size polynomial in $n$, implementing

$$U_f\ket x\ket y\ket{0\cdots0} = \ket x\ket{y\oplus f(x)}\ket{0\cdots0}.$$

Polynomial time gives Boolean circuits of polynomial size (background: the tableau construction of Cook–Levin). Toffoli gadgets make them reversible at the same size; Bennett's trick cleans the garbage at a factor 2. $\blacksquare$

The oracle of every Part D algorithm. Its work qubits leave as they came, unentangled.

what this buys

A quantum computer can do anything a classical one can, at polynomial overhead — so whatever it adds, it adds on top. And "query $f$ on a superposition" is a circuit, not magic.

The background step, recalled. A Turing machine running in time $T(n)$ has a computation tableau of $T(n)\times T(n)$ cells, each determined by three cells of the row above through a fixed finite function; wiring those functions as constant-size Boolean circuits gives a circuit of size $O(T(n)^2)$ computing the output (the same construction proves Cook–Levin). The family is uniform: a classical machine writes it down in polynomial time.

Counting the result. If the Boolean circuit has $g$ gates and $m$ outputs, the clean oracle has at most $2g$ Toffolis, $m$ CNOTs, and the $X$ gates that preset and reset work bits to 1, with at most $g + 1$ work qubits. For a $T(n)$-time $f$ that is $O(T(n)^2)$ of everything.

What it does not buy. Simulating classical computation gives no speed-up by itself; a quantum algorithm earns its keep by what it does between oracle calls (interference, unit 13 onward). And unit 13 will feed the oracle $\ket y = \ket-$, where it becomes a phase — which only works because the work qubits were cleaned (problem P5).

10 · deferred measurement

The principle of deferred measurement

theorem: deferred measurement

Qubit 0 of $\ket\psi$ is measured, with outcome $m$, and afterwards used only as the control of a gate $U_m$ on the other qubits; then the others are measured with projectors $\{Q_k\}$. Replace this by the unitary

$$W = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes U_0 + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes U_1$$

followed by measuring qubit 0 and $\{Q_k\}$ at the end. Every joint probability $\Pr(m, k)$ is the same.

For a classically controlled $U$: $U_0 = I$, $U_1 = U$, and $W = C(U)$.

The double line becomes a wire. The meter moves to the end.

the hypothesis matters

Only if the measured qubit is afterwards a control and nothing else. Measure $\ket+$ then apply $H$: outcomes uniform. Apply $H$ then measure: $\ket0$ with certainty.

Why $W$ is unitary. It is block diagonal, $\mathrm{diag}(U_0, U_1)$, and $W^\dagger W = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes U_0^\dagger U_0 + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes U_1^\dagger U_1 = I$, as for $C(U)$.

The counterexample in the warning. Measuring $\ket+$ leaves $\ket0$ or $\ket1$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each; $H$ makes them $\ket\pm$, and a final meter reads 0 or 1 with probability $\tfrac12$ in both branches. Without the early meter, $H\ket+ = \ket0$. The measured qubit was used as the input of $H$, not as a control, so the theorem does not apply.

Where this is used. In teleportation (unit 07, if you have taken it): Bob's classically controlled $X$ and $Z$ can be replaced by a controlled-$X$ and controlled-$Z$ from Alice's qubits, measured at the end — which is how teleportation is often run on hardware without fast feed-forward. The semiclassical Fourier transform (Griffiths and Niu, 1996; cited) uses it in the other direction: it replaces controlled rotations by measurements and classically controlled ones, saving qubits.

10 · deferred measurement

Deferred measurement, proved

proof: both distributions from unit 01's rules

Write $P_m = \ket m\!\bra m$ on qubit 0.

Measure early. Outcome $m$ with probability $p_m = \lVert(P_m\otimes I)\psi\rVert^2$, leaving $\phi_m = (P_m\otimes I)\psi/\sqrt{p_m}$. Then $I\otimes U_m$, then $Q_k$:

$$\begin{aligned}\Pr(m, k) &= p_m\,\lVert(I\otimes Q_kU_m)\phi_m\rVert^2\\ &= \lVert(P_m\otimes Q_kU_m)\psi\rVert^2 .\end{aligned}$$

Measure late. $(P_m\otimes Q_k)W = P_m\otimes Q_kU_m$, because $P_mP_{m'} = \delta_{mm'}P_m$. So

$$\begin{aligned}\Pr(m, k) &= \lVert(P_m\otimes Q_k)W\psi\rVert^2\\ &= \lVert(P_m\otimes Q_kU_m)\psi\rVert^2 .\end{aligned}$$

The same number. $\blacksquare$

Read the proof. The early meter splits $\psi$ into the branches $(P_m\otimes I)\psi$ and applies $U_m$ to branch $m$. The controlled gate does the same thing to the same branches — it just does not normalise them. Probabilities are squared norms, and normalising then multiplying by $p_m$ undoes itself.

the post-measurement states agree too

Given $(m, k)$, both procedures leave the state proportional to $(P_m\otimes Q_kU_m)\psi$. So the equivalence survives anything done afterwards.

Check with $\psi = \ket1\ket0$, $U_1 = X$: early, $m = 1$ surely, then $X$: $\ket{11}$. Late, $C(X)\ket{10} = \ket{11}$, then $m = 1$ surely ✓.

Full proof, the operator identity. $(P_m\otimes Q_k)W = \sum_{m'}(P_m\otimes Q_k)(P_{m'}\otimes U_{m'}) = \sum_{m'}P_mP_{m'}\otimes Q_kU_{m'} = P_m\otimes Q_kU_m$, using the product rule for tensor products of operators (unit 03) and $P_0P_1 = 0$.

The early side, carefully. When $p_m = 0$ the branch never happens, and the formula's right side is $0$ too, since $(P_m\otimes I)\psi = 0$. When $p_m > 0$: the later measurement of $Q_k$ on the normalised state $\phi_m$ gives $\lVert(I\otimes Q_k)(I\otimes U_m)\phi_m\rVert^2$, and multiplying by $p_m$ clears the normalisation. The joint probability of a sequence of outcomes is the product of conditional ones — the only rule of probability used.

Problem P2 extends this to many mid-circuit measurements, each steering any later gates, by the same projector-pushing argument.

In unit 04's language (not needed): the early-measured circuit and the deferred one give the same channel once the control is measured, and the proof above is the statement that measuring a qubit commutes with any gate it only controls.

read backwards, it is a saving

The theorem runs both ways. A quantum control that will only be measured can be measured at once, and its gates become classical decisions — no three-qubit gate needed.

Measure at the end, or not at all

corollary: measuring only at the end loses nothing

Any circuit with mid-circuit meters, each outcome used only to control later gates, has the same output statistics as a circuit of unitaries followed by meters at the end.

Proof. Apply the theorem to the last mid-circuit meter, then the one before, and so on: each step removes one double line (problem P2 proves the general case directly). $\blacksquare$

implicit measurement

A qubit that is no longer acted on may be measured or not: the statistics of the other qubits are the same.

proof

$\sum_m\lVert(P_m\otimes Q_k)\phi\rVert^2 = \big\langle\phi\big|\textstyle\sum_mP_m\otimes Q_k\big|\phi\big\rangle = \lVert(I\otimes Q_k)\phi\rVert^2$, using $Q_k^\dagger Q_k = Q_k$ and $\sum_mP_m = I$. $\blacksquare$

Measuring an idle qubit changes nothing the other meters can see.

Check on $\ket{\Phi^+}$: qubit 1 alone reads 0 with probability $\tfrac12$. Measuring qubit 0 first: $\tfrac12\cdot1 + \tfrac12\cdot0 = \tfrac12$ ✓.

So from here on a circuit is unitaries then meters. Unit 12 defines $\mathsf{BQP}$ with circuits of exactly this shape.

The implicit-measurement proof, step by step. $\lVert(P_m\otimes Q_k)\phi\rVert^2 = \braket{\phi|(P_m\otimes Q_k)^\dagger(P_m\otimes Q_k)|\phi} = \braket{\phi|P_m\otimes Q_k|\phi}$, since projectors are Hermitian and idempotent. Summing over $m$ gives $\braket{\phi|I\otimes Q_k|\phi} = \lVert(I\otimes Q_k)\phi\rVert^2$. The left side is the probability of $k$ when the idle qubit is measured too (summed over its outcome); the right side, when it is not. If gates $W$ act on the other qubits after the idle qubit's meter, as in the figure, $P_m\otimes I$ commutes with $I\otimes W$ (different factors, unit 03), so the meter may be moved to the end and the argument applied with $\phi = (I\otimes W)\psi$.

The corollary's induction. If the circuit has $r$ mid-circuit meters, apply the theorem to the last one: everything after it is gates and final meters, and the theorem replaces the meter and its double lines by a controlled gate and a final meter. The new circuit has $r - 1$ mid-circuit meters. The earlier meters' outcomes may also steer which gates come later; the theorem's $U_m$ can be anything, including a product that depends on earlier outcomes, so the induction goes through.

The cost. Deferring keeps the measured qubits alive until the end — more qubits held coherent for longer. On real hardware that is a genuine cost; on paper it is free.

Two-level unitaries, and the step that zeroes an entry

a two-level unitary

A unitary on $\mathbb C^d$ that acts non-trivially only on the span of two basis vectors $\ket s, \ket t$: it fixes every other basis vector, and on $\mathrm{span}(\ket s, \ket t)$ it is some $2\times2$ unitary $\tilde U$.

lemma: zeroing one entry

For $(a, b)\ne(0, 0)$ with $n = \sqrt{|a|^2 + |b|^2}$, the matrix

$$\tilde W = \frac1n\begin{pmatrix}\bar a & \bar b\\ b & -a\end{pmatrix}$$

is unitary and sends $(a, b)^{\mathsf T}$ to $(n, 0)^{\mathsf T}$.

proof

First row: $(\bar aa + \bar bb)/n = n$. Second: $(ba - ab)/n = 0$. Columns $(\bar a, b)/n$ and $(\bar b, -a)/n$ have norm 1 and inner product $(a\bar b - \bar ba)/n^2 = 0$. $\blacksquare$

For real $a, b$, $\tilde W$ is the reflection in the line halfway between $(a, b)$ and the first axis.

Check $(a, b) = (\tfrac35, \tfrac45)$: $n = 1$, $\tilde W = \begin{pmatrix}0.6 & 0.8\\0.8 & -0.6\end{pmatrix}$, and $\tilde W(0.6, 0.8)^{\mathsf T} = (0.36 + 0.64,\ 0.48 - 0.48) = (1, 0)$ ✓.

Placed in $\mathbb C^d$, acting on rows $s, t$: left-multiplying a matrix by it replaces rows $s, t$ by combinations of themselves and leaves every other row alone.

The proof, with the convention. The inner product is conjugate-linear in the first slot, so the inner product of columns $u = (\bar a, b)/n$ and $v = (\bar b, -a)/n$ is $\braket{u|v} = (a\bar b + \bar b(-a))/n^2 = 0$. Norms: $\lVert u\rVert^2 = (|a|^2 + |b|^2)/n^2 = 1$, and the same for $v$.

The reflection. For real $a = n\cos\varphi$, $b = n\sin\varphi$, $\tilde W = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi & \sin\varphi\\ \sin\varphi & -\cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}$, the reflection in the line at angle $\varphi/2$: it sends the direction $\varphi$ to $0$. Its determinant is $-1$. A rotation by $-\varphi$ would do the same job with determinant $+1$; either is fine, since only unitarity is needed.

Left multiplication acts on rows. If $W$ is two-level on $s, t$, then $(WM)_{rj} = \sum_kW_{rk}M_{kj}$, and for $r\ne s, t$ the row $W_{r\cdot}$ is the standard basis row, so $(WM)_{rj} = M_{rj}$. For $r = s, t$ the new rows are $\tilde W$ applied to the pair of old rows, column by column.

10 · exact universality

Every unitary is a product of two-level unitaries

theorem: the two-level decomposition

Every unitary $U$ on $\mathbb C^d$ is a product of at most $\tfrac{d(d-1)}2$ two-level unitaries.

proof, by induction on $d$

$d = 2$: $U$ is itself two-level; $\tfrac{2\cdot1}2 = 1$.

$d > 2$: apply the lemma to rows $1$ and $k$ of column 1, for $k = 2, \dots, d$ in turn: $d - 1$ two-level factors, product $V$, with $(VU)_{k1} = 0$ for $k\ge2$ and $(VU)_{11} > 0$. Column 1 of the unitary $VU$ is a unit vector, so $(VU)_{11} = 1$; row 1 is a unit vector too, so the rest of row 1 is $0$. Hence

$$VU = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & U'\end{pmatrix},\quad U'\in U(d-1).$$

By induction $U'$ is a product of $\tfrac{(d-1)(d-2)}2$ two-level unitaries; so $U = V^{-1}(1\oplus U')$, with $(d-1) + \tfrac{(d-1)(d-2)}2 = \tfrac{d(d-1)}2$. $\blacksquare$

$d = 4$: the entries zeroed, in order. The last factor is the whole remaining $2\times2$ block.

Skip when zero. If $(VU)_{k1}$ is already 0 and the diagonal entry already positive, that factor is the identity — so "at most".

For $n$ qubits, $d = 2^n$: at most $2^{n-1}(2^n - 1)$ factors: $6$ when $n = 2$ and $28$ when $n = 3$.

Full proof of the column step. Start with $M_1 = U$. For $k = 2, \dots, d$: let $a = (M_{k-1})_{11}$, $b = (M_{k-1})_{k1}$. If $b \ne 0$, apply the lemma on rows $1, k$: the new $(k, 1)$ entry is $0$, the new $(1, 1)$ entry is $n > 0$, and rows other than $1, k$ are unchanged — so zeros made earlier stay zero. If $b = 0$, take the identity, with one exception: at the last step, $k = d$, if $a$ is not yet real and positive, use $\mathrm{diag}(\bar a/|a|, a/|a|)$ on rows $1, d$, a two-level unitary that makes the $(1, 1)$ entry $|a| > 0$ (here $a \ne 0$, since column 1 is a unit vector whose other entries are by then 0). So $(1, 1)$ ends real and positive. Column 1 of the unitary $VU$ is then $(r, 0, \dots, 0)$ with $r > 0$ and norm 1, so $r = 1$. Row 1 has norm 1 (rows of a unitary are orthonormal, as $UU^\dagger = I$) and first entry 1, so its other entries vanish.

Extending factors from $U(d-1)$. A two-level unitary on $\mathbb C^{d-1}$ (basis vectors $2, \dots, d$) becomes, with a 1 in the corner, a two-level unitary on $\mathbb C^d$. Inverses of two-level unitaries are two-level. So $U = V^{-1}(1\oplus U')$ is the required product.

Is the count sharp? The construction uses all $\tfrac{d(d-1)}2$ factors unless it meets a zero. No decomposition uses fewer than $d^2/4$ factors for a generic unitary, by section 5's counting (each two-level factor carries 4 real parameters, and $U(d)$ needs $d^2$). Problem P9 does both, and an explicit $3\times3$ case.

Two-level on neighbours is a controlled one-qubit gate

lemma

If $s, t\in\{0, 1\}^n$ differ only in bit $j$, with $s_j = 0$, the two-level unitary acting as $\tilde U$ on $\ket s, \ket t$ is $\tilde U$ on qubit $j$, controlled by every other qubit $i$ — a filled control where $s_i = 1$, an open one where $s_i = 0$.

proof

A basis state whose bits off $j$ differ from $s$'s fails some control: fixed, as by the two-level unitary. The two that pass are $\ket s, \ket t$, and on them the gate is $\tilde U$ on bit $j$, $\ket0\leftrightarrow\ket s$, $\ket1\leftrightarrow\ket t$. $\blacksquare$

(If $s_j = 1$, swap the roles: the gate is $X\tilde UX$.) Such a gate is a multiply-controlled gate, $C^{n-1}(\tilde U)$ up to $X$ gates for the open controls.

The basis states as a cube; an edge joins neighbours. The Toffoli lives on one edge.

the running example: the Toffoli is two-level

It fixes six basis states and acts as $X$ on $\ket{110}, \ket{111}$, which differ only in bit 2. The lemma: $X$ on qubit 2, filled controls on qubits 0 and 1 — $C^2(X)$, the symbol we drew ✓.

The proof in operators. Let $P$ be the projector onto basis states whose bits off $j$ agree with $s$'s; it is a tensor product of $\ket{s_i}\!\bra{s_i}$ on each $i\ne j$ and $I$ on $j$. The controlled gate is $(I - P) + P(\tilde U\text{ on }j)$. Its range of action is $P$'s range, spanned by $\ket s$ and $\ket t$ — two-level, as claimed — and it acts there as $\tilde U$ in the basis $(\ket s, \ket t)$.

Open controls cost $X$ gates. An open control on qubit $i$ is an ordinary control conjugated by $X_i$ (Controlled gates). So any such gate is $C^{n-1}(\tilde U)$ sandwiched by $X$'s on the qubits where $s_i = 0$.

The cube. The basis states of $n$ qubits are the vertices of the $n$-cube; neighbours differ in one bit. A two-level unitary on an edge is cheap (this lemma). On a non-edge it is not yet a gate — the next slide walks it to an edge.

Gray codes: walking two states together

a Gray code from $s$ to $t$

A sequence $s = g_1, g_2, \dots, g_r = t$ in which consecutive strings differ in one bit. Flipping the differing bits one at a time gives one with $r \le n + 1$.

lemma

Let $\Pi$ swap $\ket{g_1}\leftrightarrow\ket{g_2}$, then $\ket{g_2}\leftrightarrow\ket{g_3}$, …, up to $\ket{g_{r-2}}\leftrightarrow\ket{g_{r-1}}$. Then a two-level $\tilde U$ on $\ket s, \ket t$ equals $\Pi^{-1}\,U'\,\Pi$, where $U'$ is $\tilde U$ on the neighbours $\ket{g_{r-1}}, \ket t$.

Proof. $\Pi$ sends $\ket s\mapsto\ket{g_{r-1}}$ and fixes $\ket t$; each swap is two-level on neighbours, so a multiply-controlled $X$. Check the three kinds of basis vector in the notes. $\blacksquare$

$W_1$ of the worked example: $h$ on $\ket{00}, \ket{11}$, via $00\to01\to11$.

The worked example's $W_1$. Path $00, 01, 11$. Swap $\ket{00}\leftrightarrow\ket{01}$: $X$ on $q_1$ when $q_0 = 0$. Now $\ket{01}, \ket{11}$ differ in $q_0$: $h$ on $q_0$, controlled by $q_1$. Swap back. Three gates, checked on all four basis states in the notes ✓.

On three qubits: $001\to000\to010\to110$, two swaps, then one controlled gate on an edge.

Full proof of the lemma. The swaps involve only $g_1, \dots, g_{r-1}$, so $\Pi\ket t = \ket t$; and they carry $\ket s = \ket{g_1}$ to $\ket{g_2}$, then to $\ket{g_3}$, …, to $\ket{g_{r-1}}$. Three cases. (i) $\Pi^{-1}U'\Pi\ket s = \Pi^{-1}U'\ket{g_{r-1}} = \Pi^{-1}(\tilde u_{00}\ket{g_{r-1}} + \tilde u_{10}\ket t) = \tilde u_{00}\ket s + \tilde u_{10}\ket t$. (ii) The same for $\ket t$. (iii) Any other basis vector $\ket v$: $\Pi\ket v$ is a basis vector other than $\ket{g_{r-1}}$ and $\ket t$ (as $\Pi$ is a bijection), which $U'$ fixes, and $\Pi^{-1}$ brings back $\ket v$. These are exactly the columns of the two-level unitary $\tilde U$ on $\ket s, \ket t$.

The two-qubit example on all four inputs. $\ket{00}\to\ket{01}\to h$ gives $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} + \ket{11})$, swap back: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})$ ✓. $\ket{11}$: the swap ignores it ($q_0 = 1$); $h$ gives $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{01} - \ket{11})$; swap back: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} - \ket{11})$ ✓. $\ket{01}\to\ket{00}$, which $h$ ignores ($q_1 = 0$), then back to $\ket{01}$ ✓. $\ket{10}$: nothing fires ✓. That is $h$ on $\ket{00}, \ket{11}$.

The cost. At most $2(n - 1)$ swaps and one controlled $\tilde U$: $2n - 1$ multiply-controlled gates per two-level factor. The name: Frank Gray's 1953 patent on reflected binary codes, in which consecutive integers differ in one bit. Problem P10 does $\ket{000}$ to $\ket{111}$.

Many controls from Toffolis and one controlled gate

lemma: $C^k(U)$ with $k - 1$ work qubits

Toffolis compute $c_1c_2$, then $c_1c_2c_3$, …, then $c_1\cdots c_k$ onto work qubits starting at $\ket0$; one $C(U)$ fires from the last; the Toffolis run again in reverse. Cost: $2(k-1)$ Toffolis and one $C(U)$.

proof

On a basis state the last work qubit holds $c_1\cdots c_k$, so $U$ acts exactly when every control is 1. The reverse Toffolis restore the work qubits (Bennett's trick, with the controlled gate as the "copy"). Linear extension. $\blacksquare$

Without work qubits it can still be done (Barenco's recursion, in the notes): from $C(V)$, $C^{k-1}(X)$ and $C^{k-1}(V)$ with $V^2 = U$. The universality theorem uses that version, so it needs no extra qubits.

$C^3(U)$: four Toffolis, one $C(U)$, two work qubits borrowed at $\ket0$ and returned.

Check $c = 110$: the first Toffoli sets $w_1 = 1$, the second leaves $w_2 = c_3w_1 = 0$, $U$ does not fire; reversed, $w_1\to0$ ✓.

Full proof (with work qubits). On $\ket{c_1\dots c_k}\ket{0\dots0}\ket\psi$ the forward Toffolis give $w_1 = c_1c_2$, $w_i = c_{i+1}w_{i-1}$, so $w_{k-1} = c_1\cdots c_k$. Then $C(U)$ from $w_{k-1}$ applies $U^{c_1\cdots c_k}$ to $\ket\psi$, leaving all the $c$'s and $w$'s alone. The reversed Toffolis then see the same controls and targets they produced, and undo them: $w_i\to0$. Result $\ket c\ket0U^{c_1\cdots c_k}\ket\psi$, which is $C^k(U)$ on the basis, hence everywhere.

Full proof (without work qubits): Barenco's recursion. Let $V$ be a unitary with $V^2 = U$ — it exists by the functional calculus (unit 00): $U = \sum e^{i\theta_j}P_j$, $V = \sum e^{i\theta_j/2}P_j$. Write $p = c_1\cdots c_{k-1}$ and $q = c_k$. Apply, in time order: $C(V)$ from $c_k$ on the target ($V^q$); $C^{k-1}(X)$ from $c_1..c_{k-1}$ onto $c_k$ ($c_k\to q\oplus p$); $C(V^\dagger)$ from $c_k$ ($V^{-(q\oplus p)}$); $C^{k-1}(X)$ again ($c_k\to q$); $C^{k-1}(V)$ from $c_1..c_{k-1}$ ($V^p$). All the powers of $V$ commute, and the exponents add to $q - (q\oplus p) + p = 2pq$ (check the four cases), so the target gets $V^{2pq} = U^{pq}$: exactly $C^k(U)$. Each piece has fewer controls, so by induction everything reduces to $C(\cdot)$ of one-qubit gates and CNOTs. For $k = 2$ this is the circuit on The Toffoli by the general construction.

Cost without work qubits. This recursion is exponential in $k$ as written; Barenco et al. give $O(k^2)$ gates without work qubits, and $O(k)$ with a single one (cited from their paper). Only finiteness matters for exactness.

Controlled-U from two CNOTs: the ABC construction

theorem: the ABC construction

Every one-qubit $U$ can be written $U = e^{i\alpha}AXBXC$ with $ABC = I$, and then

$$\begin{aligned}C(U) = {}&\big(P(\alpha)\otimes A\big)\,\mathrm{CNOT}\\ &\cdot(I\otimes B)\,\mathrm{CNOT}\,(I\otimes C),\end{aligned}$$

$P(\alpha) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\alpha})$. Explicitly, from unit 02's $U = e^{i\alpha}R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta)$:

$$A = R_z(\beta)R_y(\tfrac\gamma2),\quad C = R_z(\tfrac{\delta-\beta}2),$$ $$B = R_y(-\tfrac\gamma2)R_z(-\tfrac{\delta+\beta}2).$$
proof

Control 0: the CNOTs are idle; target gets $ABC = I$, control gets $1$. Control 1: target gets $AXBXC = e^{-i\alpha}U$, control gets $e^{i\alpha}$: total $U$. $\blacksquare$

Time order: $C$, CNOT, $B$, CNOT, $A$, and the phase gate on the control.

Why $ABC = I$ and $AXBXC = R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta)$. $ABC = R_z(\beta)R_y(\tfrac\gamma2)R_y(-\tfrac\gamma2)R_z(-\tfrac{\delta+\beta}2)R_z(\tfrac{\delta-\beta}2) = R_z(\beta)R_z(-\beta) = I$. And $XR_y(\theta)X = R_y(-\theta)$, $XR_z(\theta)X = R_z(-\theta)$, so $XBX = R_y(\tfrac\gamma2)R_z(\tfrac{\delta+\beta}2)$ and the angles add up.

The phase gate is the warning on Controlled gates, paid.

Full proof of the circuit identity. Write $C(U)$'s candidate as $\Gamma = (P(\alpha)\otimes A)\,\mathrm{CNOT}\,(I\otimes B)\,\mathrm{CNOT}\,(I\otimes C)$ and $\mathrm{CNOT} = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes X$. Multiplying the block-diagonal factors, $\Gamma = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes ABC + e^{i\alpha}\ket1\!\bra1\otimes AXBXC = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes U = C(U)$.

The conjugation rules. $XYX = -Y$ and $XZX = -Z$ (unit 02's Pauli algebra: $XY = iZ$, so $XYX = iZX = i\cdot iY = -Y$; and $XZX = -Z$ likewise). Since $X = X^{-1}$, conjugation by $X$ commutes with the functional calculus: $Xe^{-i\theta Y/2}X = e^{-i\theta XYX/2} = e^{i\theta Y/2}$, i.e. $XR_y(\theta)X = R_y(-\theta)$. The same for $Z$. On the Bloch sphere: $X$ is a half-turn about $x$, which reverses the $y$ and $z$ axes.

The angle sum. $AXBXC = R_z(\beta)R_y(\tfrac\gamma2)\cdot R_y(\tfrac\gamma2)R_z(\tfrac{\delta+\beta}2)\cdot R_z(\tfrac{\delta-\beta}2) = R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta) = e^{-i\alpha}U$, using $R_P(\theta)R_P(\theta') = R_P(\theta + \theta')$ (exponentials of commuting operators, unit 00).

Cost. Two CNOTs and at most four one-qubit gates. Two CNOTs are necessary for a generic $U$: a single CNOT with one-qubit gates around it cannot make $C(U)$ unless $U$ is a multiple of $I$ (no CNOT needed) or, up to phase, conjugate to $X$ (cited: Shende, Bullock and Markov, Phys. Rev. A 70, 012310, 2004, who classify two-qubit gates by the CNOTs they need).

CNOT and one-qubit gates are exactly universal

theorem (Barenco et al. 1995)

Every unitary on $n$ qubits is exactly a product of CNOTs and one-qubit gates acting on those $n$ qubits.

proof: compose the four arrows
  1. $U$ is a product of at most $2^{n-1}(2^n - 1)$ two-level unitaries.
  2. Each is $\Pi^{-1}U'\Pi$ with $\Pi$ a product of two-level swaps on neighbours and $U'$ two-level on neighbours (Gray codes): all multiply-controlled one-qubit gates, with $X$'s for open controls.
  3. Each $C^{k}(\tilde U)$ is built from $C(\cdot)$ of one-qubit gates and CNOTs by Barenco's recursion (no work qubits).
  4. Each $C(\tilde U)$ is two CNOTs and one-qubit gates (ABC). $\blacksquare$
check: $n = 1$ and $n = 2$

$n = 1$: one two-level factor, a one-qubit gate; no CNOT needed ✓. $n = 2$: the Bell-state circuit became $W_1W_2W_3$; $W_3$ is a CNOT, $W_1$ and $W_2$ each become three gates by a Gray code, and each controlled $h$ is two CNOTs by ABC ✓.

What it costs on this route: $O(4^n)$ two-level factors, $O(n)$ multiply-controlled gates each, $O(n)$ gates each with work qubits: $O(n^24^n)$.

exact, not efficient

The theorem says every unitary can be built. Section 5 proves that almost every one must cost exponentially many gates — by any route.

Each step's justification, by slide: 1, Every unitary is a product of two-level unitaries; 2, Gray codes: walking two states together and Two-level on neighbours is a controlled one-qubit gate; 3, Many controls from Toffolis and one controlled gate (its notes prove the recursion); 4, Controlled-U from two CNOTs: the ABC construction. lemma: a CNOT–T network is a phase polynomial

In a circuit of CNOTs, $T$ and $T^\dagger$, each wire always holds an XOR of the inputs. If the CNOTs return every wire to its input, the circuit is diagonal: $\ket{a, b, x}\mapsto e^{i\pi f/4}\ket{a, b, x}$, where $f$ sums $\pm$(the XOR on the wire) over the $T$'s ($+$) and $T^\dagger$'s ($-$).

Full proof of the lemma. On a basis state, a CNOT maps $\ket{u, v}\mapsto\ket{u, v\oplus u}$: if both wires held XORs of the inputs, they still do. A $T$ on a wire holding the bit $\ell$ multiplies by $e^{i\pi\ell/4}$ and changes no bit; $T^\dagger$ by $e^{-i\pi\ell/4}$. So a basis state is carried to a basis state times a product of such phases; if the final bits equal the input bits, the basis state is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $e^{i\pi f/4}$. A linear map with an eigenbasis of basis vectors is diagonal.

Do the CNOTs return the wires? On $c$: $x\to x\oplus b\to x\oplus a\oplus b\to x\oplus a\to x$ after the four CNOTs from $b, a, b, a$. On $b$: $b\to a\oplus b\to b$. On $a$: never a target. Yes.

The identity behind the table. For bits, $u\oplus v = u + v - 2uv$ and $u\oplus v\oplus w = u + v + w - 2uv - 2uw - 2vw + 4uvw$. Substituting, $a + b + x - (a\oplus b) - (a\oplus x) - (b\oplus x) + (a\oplus b\oplus x) = 4abx$: the linear and quadratic terms cancel. The circuit is a way of evaluating the cubic $4abx$ using only XORs, each weighted $\pm1$. That is why the circuit has seven $T$-type gates: one for each of $a, b, x$ and their four XORs.

Step 3 on a basis input $\ket{a, b, c}$. $H$ on $c$ gives $\ket{a, b}\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_x(-1)^{cx}\ket x$; $D$ multiplies the $x = 1$ term by $(-1)^{ab}$; the second $H$ returns $\ket{a, b}\ket{c\oplus ab}$, because $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + (-1)^{c\oplus ab}\ket1) = H\ket{c\oplus ab}$.

Numerically checked: the 15 gates multiply to exactly the $8\times8$ Toffoli matrix, and the CNOT–$T$ middle to $\mathrm{diag}(1, \dots, 1, -1)$.

A truth table checks bits, not phases

The same fifteen gates, regrouped: $T$ on $b$ commutes with everything on $c$, so it can move right.

The right box is $C(S)$ on $a, b$: its phases are $b + a - (a\oplus b) = 2ab$, so $i^{ab}$. The left box therefore has phase sum $4abx - 2ab$: it is $\mathrm{TOF}\cdot C(S^\dagger)_{ab}$ — the Toffoli times $-i$ on $\ket{11c}$.

the trap

The left box alone passes the classical test — each $\ket{abc}$ goes to $\ket{a, b, c\oplus ab}$ up to a phase — but it is not the Toffoli: on $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})\ket+$ the relative phase shows. Verify circuits on states, with phases, as the table did.

The left box's phase sum. It contains ①, ②, ③ and ⑤. From the table's identity, ① + ② + ③ + ⑤ $= 4abx - (④ + ⑥ + ⑦) = 4abx - (b + a - (a\oplus b)) = 4abx - 2ab$. So its middle is $\mathrm{diag}((-1)^{abx}(-i)^{ab})$, and with the $H$'s it is $\mathrm{TOF}\cdot(C(S^\dagger)\otimes I)$ (the two commute: one is diagonal on $a, b$, the other acts on $c$ controlled by $a, b$). Numerically checked.

Relative-phase Toffolis are useful. When a Toffoli is later undone — as in Bennett's uncomputation — the phase $C(S^\dagger)$ cancels against its inverse, so a cheaper gate that is the Toffoli up to a phase on the controls is enough. This one costs 4 $T$-type gates instead of 7; a three-CNOT version with real entries is Margolus's gate (discussed in Barenco et al. 1995; cited).

The test that would catch it. Prepare $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} + \ket{11})\ket0$ on $a, b, c$, apply the box, then undo a true Toffoli and measure $a, b$ in the Bell basis: the Toffoli gives $\ket{\Phi^+}$ for certain, the box gives $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{00} - i\ket{11})$, which is not $\ket{\Phi^+}$ and fails the test with probability $\tfrac12$.

section 5

Counting: most unitaries are out of reach

A unitary on $n$ qubits takes $4^n - 1$ real numbers to specify; a gate supplies at most 16. Exact synthesis of a generic unitary needs at least $4^{n-2}$ gates, whatever the method.

10 · counting

How many numbers a unitary takes

theorem: the dimension of $U(d)$

$U(d)$ is a smooth manifold of real dimension $d^2$; up to global phase, $d^2 - 1$. For $n$ qubits, $d = 2^n$: $4^n$, or $4^n - 1$ up to phase.

proof sketch (full in the notes)

Coordinates. Hermitian $d\times d$ matrices: $d$ real diagonal entries and $\tfrac{d(d-1)}2$ complex ones above it — $d + d(d-1) = d^2$ real numbers. Every unitary is $e^{iK}$, $K$ Hermitian (spectral theorem, unit 00).

Not fewer. Near $K = 0$, $K\mapsto e^{iK}$ is a smooth bijection with smooth inverse (inverse function theorem: its derivative there is $K\mapsto iK$, injective). Translating by $U_0$ gives coordinates near every $U_0$.

Near $I$, the unitaries look like $\mathbb R^{d^2}$: the Hermitian matrices, through the exponential.

Check $n = 1$: $4 = 1 + 3$ — a global phase and three Euler angles (unit 02) ✓. Up to phase, $3$: a rotation of the Bloch sphere ✓.

For the running example's size, $n = 3$: $4^3 - 1 = 63$ real parameters.

Full proof. (i) $\mathrm{Herm}(d)$ is a real vector space (closed under real combinations, not complex ones), with basis the matrices $E_{jj}$, $E_{jk} + E_{kj}$ and $i(E_{jk} - E_{kj})$ for $j < k$: $d + 2\binom d2 = d^2$ elements. (ii) Surjectivity: a unitary is normal with eigenvalues of modulus 1, so $U = \sum_je^{i\theta_j}P_j$ and $U = e^{iK}$ with $K = \sum_j\theta_jP_j$ Hermitian. (iii) $\exp(iK) = I + iK + O(\lVert K\rVert^2)$, so the derivative at $0$ is $K\mapsto iK$, injective on $\mathrm{Herm}(d)$; and its image, $i\,\mathrm{Herm}(d)$, is exactly the set of skew-Hermitian matrices, which is the tangent space of $U(d)$ at $I$ (differentiate $U(t)^\dagger U(t) = I$). By the inverse function theorem, $\exp$ restricted to a neighbourhood of 0 in $i\,\mathrm{Herm}(d)$ is a diffeomorphism onto a neighbourhood of $I$ in $U(d)$. (iv) $V\mapsto U_0V$ is a smooth bijection of $U(d)$ with smooth inverse, carrying a chart at $I$ to one at $U_0$.

Up to phase. The phases $e^{i\varphi}I$ form a circle inside $U(d)$, and $K = \varphi I$ is one direction of $\mathrm{Herm}(d)$; dropping it leaves the traceless Hermitian matrices, of dimension $d^2 - 1$ — the dimension of $SU(d)$. In section 5's argument we avoid quotients altogether by putting the phase into the parameter count instead.

Level of rigour. As in unit 03's counting, "dimension" is meant as a manifold dimension, and the only facts used are the inverse function theorem (background) and Sard's theorem in its easy case (cited, next slide).

The counting lower bound

theorem: most unitaries need $\Omega(4^n)$ gates

Fix $g$ with $16g + 1 < 4^n$. The $n$-qubit unitaries that are products of at most $g$ gates, each on at most two qubits, form a set of measure zero in $U(2^n)$. So a generic unitary needs

$$g\ \ge\ \frac{4^n - 1}{16},\qquad\text{i.e. } g\ge4^{n-2}\ (n\ge2).$$
proof: count parameters

Fix which wires each gate acts on — finitely many choices. Then the circuit is the image of $U(4)^g\times U(1)\to U(2^n)$, $(G_1, \dots, G_g, e^{i\varphi})\mapsto e^{i\varphi}G_g\cdots G_1$: a smooth map from a manifold of dimension $16g + 1 < 4^n$. Its image has measure zero (Sard, easy case, cited as in unit 03); a finite union of such sets too. $\blacksquare$

The image of a lower-dimensional space is thin: a curve in a square, a surface in a solid.

Rounding. $\tfrac{4^n - 1}{16} = 4^{n-2} - \tfrac1{16}$, so the least whole number satisfying it is $4^{n-2}$. Check $n = 2$: $1$ — and one two-qubit gate does suffice ✓.

One-qubit gates are two-qubit gates with an $I$ factor, so they are covered.

Full proof. A layout assigns to each of the $g$ gates an ordered pair of distinct wires: at most $(n(n-1))^g$ layouts. For a fixed layout, the map $\Phi(G_1, \dots, G_g, e^{i\varphi}) = e^{i\varphi}\,G_g^{(Q_g)}\cdots G_1^{(Q_1)}$ is polynomial in the matrix entries, hence smooth, on the manifold $U(4)^g\times U(1)$ of dimension $16g + 1$. If $16g + 1 < 4^n = \dim U(2^n)$, the image of $\Phi$ has measure zero: the easy case of Sard's theorem, that a smooth map from a manifold of lower dimension has image of measure zero (cited: J. M. Lee, Introduction to Smooth Manifolds, 2nd ed., chapter 6 — the reference unit 03 used). Circuits of fewer gates are included by padding with identities. The union over layouts is a finite union of measure-zero sets. The phase factor was put in so that "up to global phase" circuits are covered too.

Measure zero in $U(2^n)$ means measure zero in every chart (How many numbers a unitary takes); it does not depend on the chart, since charts differ by smooth maps, which preserve measure-zero sets.

"Generic". A property holds for a generic unitary if it fails only on a set of measure zero. A unitary drawn at random (from any distribution given by a density in charts) needs at least $4^{n-2}$ gates with probability 1.

The bound for CNOT and one-qubit gates is problem P11: there, only the one-qubit gates carry parameters.

10 · the close

The Toffoli, closed: fifteen gates, seven of them T

the question, from the overview

How is $\ket{a, b, c}\mapsto\ket{a, b, c\oplus ab}$ built from one- and two-qubit gates?

the answer, twice
  • By the general route: two-level already, on neighbours; Barenco's $C^2(X)$ with $V = HSH$; 8 CNOTs, 9 $T$-type, 2 $H$ — 19 gates.
  • By the textbook circuit: $H\cdot C^2(Z)\cdot H$, with $C^2(Z)$ a phase polynomial: 6 CNOTs, 7 $T$-type, 2 $H$ — 15 gates, verified on all eight inputs.

Against counting. A generic three-qubit unitary has 63 parameters. The Toffoli has none: eight rows fix it. It is one of the exceptions.

Seven of the fifteen gates are $T$ or $T^\dagger$.

the bill unit 11 will send

With a finite gate set, $H$, $S$ and CNOT are cheap and $T$ is the expensive gate — fault-tolerant machines make each one from a distilled resource state (unit 24). Seven is the least $T$-count for an exact Toffoli without work qubits (cited).
