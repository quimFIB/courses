Conventions, as in the deck: \(\braket{u|v}\) is conjugate-linear in the first slot; \(\ket0 = (1, 0)\), \(\ket1 = (0, 1)\); qubit 0 is the leftmost symbol and the control of \(\mathrm{CNOT}\). \(R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) = e^{-i\theta\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2} = \cos\frac\theta2 I - i\sin\frac\theta2\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\); \(H = \frac1{\sqrt2}(X + Z)\), \(S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i)\), \(T = \mathrm{diag}(1, \omega)\) with \(\omega = e^{i\pi/4} = \frac{1 + i}{\sqrt2}\), so \(T = e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\frac\pi4)\). Logarithms are base 2 unless written \(\ln\).

Distance and budgets

P1 The budget routine

exercises: errors add, splitting a budget, the logarithmic price of accuracy; ends in a number

A circuit of \(N = 10^9\) gates must come out within \(d(U, V)\le10^{-2}\) of the ideal unitary.

  1. Spread the budget evenly over all \(N\) gates. What accuracy must each gate meet?
  2. One of the gates is \(R_z(\theta)\), approximated by a \(z\)-rotation \(R_z(\theta')\). How close must \(\theta'\) be to \(\theta\)?
  3. Take the T-count of an \(\varepsilon\)-approximation of a \(z\)-rotation to be \(3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\) (Ross–Selinger's leading term). What is the T-count of that one rotation?
  4. Now suppose only \(R = 10^8\) of the gates are arbitrary rotations, and the rest are Clifford + \(T\) gates used exactly. Spread the budget over the rotations only, and redo parts 1 and 3. What is the T-count the rotations contribute in total?
  5. By how much does the per-rotation T-count change when the total budget is tightened by a factor of 10?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Every answer is a number: an accuracy, an angle, a count. The only theorem needed is that errors add; the rest is arithmetic with logarithms.

Rung 2

If every gate is within \(\varepsilon\) up to phase, the whole circuit is within \(N\varepsilon\). For part 2 use \(\lVert R_z(\theta) - R_z(\theta')\rVert = 2\sin\frac{|\theta - \theta'|}4\). For part 3, \(\log_2(10^k) = k\log_210\) with \(\log_210 = 3.3219\).

Rung 3

First line: \(N\varepsilon\le10^{-2}\) gives \(\varepsilon = 10^{-2}/10^9 = 10^{-11}\), and then \(3\log_2(10^{11}) = 33\log_210\).

Worked solution

Idea. Errors add along a circuit, so a total budget divides among the gates; the cost of accuracy is logarithmic, so the division hardly matters.

1. If \(d(U_i, V_i)\le\varepsilon\) for every gate, then choosing the minimising phases and applying the theorem that errors add, \(d(U, V)\le\sum_i d(U_i, V_i)\le N\varepsilon\). So \(\varepsilon = 10^{-2}/10^9 = \mathbf{10^{-11}}\) suffices.

2. \(\lVert R_z(\theta) - R_z(\theta')\rVert = \lVert R_z(\theta - \theta') - I\rVert = 2\sin\frac{|\theta - \theta'|}4\le10^{-11}\) iff \(|\theta - \theta'|\le4\arcsin(5\times10^{-12})\), which is \(2\times10^{-11}\) rad to within one part in \(10^{22}\). (Up to phase changes nothing here: \(d(R_z(x), I) = 2\sin\frac{|x|}4\) for \(|x|\le\pi\), the identity's own phase being the best.) Eleven significant figures.

3. \(3\log_2(10^{11}) = 33\times3.32193 = 109.62\), so about 110 \(T\) gates.

4. Exact gates contribute zero error, so the \(R\) rotations may share all of it: \(\varepsilon = 10^{-2}/10^8 = 10^{-10}\), and \(3\log_2(10^{10}) = 30\times3.32193 = 99.66\), about 100 each. In total \(10^8\times99.66 = 9.97\times10^9\), about \(\mathbf{10^{10}}\) \(T\) gates. With the even split of part 1 it would have been \(10^8\times109.6 = 1.10\times10^{10}\): ten per cent more, for being careless with the budget.

5. Tightening by 10 multiplies \(1/\varepsilon\) by 10 and adds \(3\log_210 = 9.97\), about 10 \(T\) gates per rotation, whatever the starting point.

Check. One gate, \(N = 1\): the budget is the whole \(10^{-2}\), and the price \(3\log_2(100) = 19.9\) — a rotation to two decimal places costs about 20 \(T\) gates. And \(10^9\times10^{-11} = 10^{-2}\) ✓.

P2 Operator norms by hand routine

exercises: the operator norm, its formula for normal operators, distance up to phase, idle qubits

  1. Prove that \(\lVert A\rVert = \max_i|\lambda_i|\) for a normal \(A\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_i\). Give a \(2\times2\) matrix for which the formula fails.
  2. Show \(\lVert R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) - I\rVert = 2\sin\frac\theta4\) for \(0\le\theta\le2\pi\).
  3. Compute \(\lVert T - R_z(\frac\pi4)\rVert\) and \(d(T, R_z(\frac\pi4))\).
  4. Compute \(\lVert S - T\rVert\) and \(d(S, T)\).
  5. Show that \(\lVert A\otimes I\rVert = \lVert A\rVert\) for every square \(A\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Parts 1 and 5 are short proofs; parts 2–4 are numbers. Every operator in parts 2–4 is diagonal in some orthonormal basis, so its norm is its largest entry in modulus there.

Rung 2

Part 1: expand a unit vector in an orthonormal eigenbasis (unit 00's spectral theorem). Part 5: use \(\lVert A\rVert^2 = \max_{\lVert v\rVert = 1}\braket{v|A^\dagger A|v}\), the largest eigenvalue of \(A^\dagger A\). Parts 3–4: \(|e^{ia} - e^{ib}| = 2|\sin\frac{a - b}2|\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: \(T - R_z(\frac\pi4) = \mathrm{diag}(1 - e^{-i\pi/8},\ e^{i\pi/4} - e^{i\pi/8})\). For the distance up to phase, \(T = e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\frac\pi4)\) exactly.

Worked solution

Idea. For a normal operator, the norm is read off the spectrum; everything here is normal except the counterexample, and part 5 reduces the general case to a Hermitian one.

1. By the spectral theorem \(A = \sum_i\lambda_i\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}\) with \(\{e_i\}\) orthonormal. For \(v = \sum_ic_ie_i\) with \(\sum|c_i|^2 = 1\), \(\lVert Av\rVert^2 = \sum_i|\lambda_i|^2|c_i|^2\le\max_i|\lambda_i|^2\), with equality at the eigenvector of the largest \(|\lambda_i|\). Counterexample: \(N = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\) has both eigenvalues \(0\) but \(N\ket1 = \ket0\), so \(\lVert N\rVert = 1\). It is not normal: \(N^\dagger N = \mathrm{diag}(0, 1)\ne NN^\dagger = \mathrm{diag}(1, 0)\).

2. \(R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) - I\) is a function of the Hermitian \(\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) (eigenvalues \(\pm1\)), so normal with eigenvalues \(e^{\mp i\theta/2} - 1\). Their modulus is \(|e^{i\theta/2} - 1| = 2|\sin\frac\theta4| = 2\sin\frac\theta4\) on \([0, 2\pi]\).

3. \(\lVert T - R_z(\frac\pi4)\rVert = \max(|1 - e^{-i\pi/8}|, |e^{i\pi/8}(e^{i\pi/8} - 1)|) = 2\sin\frac\pi{16} = \mathbf{0.3902}\). But \(T = e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\frac\pi4)\), so \(d(T, R_z(\frac\pi4)) = \mathbf 0\).

4. \(S - T = \mathrm{diag}(0, i - \omega)\) and \(|e^{i\pi/2} - e^{i\pi/4}| = 2\sin\frac\pi8 = \mathbf{0.7654}\). Up to phase, \(S - e^{i\alpha}T = \mathrm{diag}(1 - e^{i\alpha},\ e^{i\pi/2} - e^{i(\alpha + \pi/4)})\), whose entries have moduli \(2|\sin\frac\alpha2|\) and \(2|\sin\frac{\alpha - \pi/4}2|\). The larger is smallest when they are equal, at \(\alpha = \frac\pi8\), giving \(d(S, T) = 2\sin\frac\pi{16} = \mathbf{0.3902}\). Sensibly: \(S = T\cdot T\), so \(d(S, T) = d(T, I)\), and \(T\) is a turn by \(\frac\pi4\).

5. For any \(A\), \(\lVert Av\rVert^2 = \braket{v|A^\dagger A|v}\), and by part 1 applied to the Hermitian \(A^\dagger A\) (eigenvalues \(\ge0\)), \(\lVert A\rVert^2 = \lambda_{\max}(A^\dagger A)\). Now \((A\otimes I)^\dagger(A\otimes I) = A^\dagger A\otimes I\), whose eigenvectors \(e\otimes f\) have the eigenvalues of \(A^\dagger A\), each repeated. So the largest eigenvalue is the same, and \(\lVert A\otimes I\rVert = \lVert A\rVert\).

Check. Part 2 at \(\theta = \frac\pi4\) gives \(2\sin\frac\pi{16} = 0.390\), the same number as part 3: \(T\) and \(R_z(\frac\pi4)\) differ by the phase \(e^{i\pi/8}\), and \(\lVert e^{i\pi/8}I - I\rVert = 2\sin\frac\pi{16}\) ✓.

P3 Probabilities from distance medium

exercises: a proof from Cauchy–Schwarz; what the operator norm means operationally; a case where the bound is nearly attained

Let \(U, V\) be unitary, \(\psi\) a unit vector, \(\{P_i\}\) a projective measurement (orthogonal projections with \(\sum_iP_i = I\)), \(p_i = \lVert P_iU\psi\rVert^2\) and \(q_i = \lVert P_iV\psi\rVert^2\).

  1. Prove \(|p_i - q_i|\le2\lVert U - V\rVert\) for each \(i\).
  2. Prove the stronger \(\sum_i|p_i - q_i|\le2\lVert U - V\rVert\), so the total variation distance \(\frac12\sum_i|p_i - q_i|\) is at most \(\lVert U - V\rVert\).
  3. Take \(U = I\), \(V = R_y(0.1)\), \(\psi = \ket0\), and measure in the basis \(\{\ket+, \ket-\}\). Compute the total variation distance and \(\lVert U - V\rVert\), and compare.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two inequalities and a number. Set \(a = U\psi\), \(b = V\psi\): everything is about two unit vectors with \(\lVert a - b\rVert\le\lVert U - V\rVert\).

Rung 2

Part 1: write \(p_i - q_i\) as \(\braket{a|P_i|a - b} + \braket{a - b|P_i|b}\). Part 2: write instead \(p_i - q_i = (x_i - y_i)(x_i + y_i)\) with \(x_i = \lVert P_ia\rVert\), \(y_i = \lVert P_ib\rVert\), and use Cauchy–Schwarz on the sum over \(i\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\sum_i|p_i - q_i|\le\sum_i\lVert P_i(a - b)\rVert\,(x_i + y_i)\le\big(\sum_i\lVert P_i(a - b)\rVert^2\big)^{1/2}\big(\sum_i(x_i + y_i)^2\big)^{1/2}\). Now \(\sum_i\lVert P_iv\rVert^2 = \lVert v\rVert^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. A distance between unitaries bounds the distance between output vectors, and that in turn bounds every difference of probabilities — one outcome at a time by Cauchy–Schwarz, and all together by Cauchy–Schwarz on the sum.

1. \(\lVert a - b\rVert = \lVert(U - V)\psi\rVert\le\lVert U - V\rVert\). Since \(P_i\) is Hermitian with \(P_i^2 = P_i\), \(p_i = \braket{a|P_i|a}\), and

\[ \braket{a|P_i|a - b} + \braket{a - b|P_i|b} = \braket{a|P_i|a} - \braket{a|P_i|b} + \braket{a|P_i|b} - \braket{b|P_i|b} = p_i - q_i . \]

Each term has modulus at most \(\lVert a\rVert\,\lVert P_i\rVert\,\lVert a - b\rVert\le\lVert a - b\rVert\) (Cauchy–Schwarz, \(\lVert P_i\rVert\le1\)). So \(|p_i - q_i|\le2\lVert a - b\rVert\le2\lVert U - V\rVert\).

2. With \(x_i, y_i\) as in the hint, \(p_i - q_i = x_i^2 - y_i^2 = (x_i - y_i)(x_i + y_i)\), and \(|x_i - y_i|\le\lVert P_ia - P_ib\rVert\) by the triangle inequality. Cauchy–Schwarz in \(\mathbb R^k\):

\[ \sum_i|p_i - q_i|\le\Big(\sum_i\lVert P_i(a - b)\rVert^2\Big)^{1/2}\Big(\sum_i(x_i + y_i)^2\Big)^{1/2}. \]

The \(P_i\) are orthogonal projections summing to \(I\), so \(\sum_i\lVert P_iv\rVert^2 = \sum_i\braket{v|P_i|v} = \lVert v\rVert^2\): the first factor is \(\lVert a - b\rVert\). For the second, Minkowski's inequality gives \(\big(\sum(x_i + y_i)^2\big)^{1/2}\le\big(\sum x_i^2\big)^{1/2} + \big(\sum y_i^2\big)^{1/2} = \lVert a\rVert + \lVert b\rVert = 2\). So \(\sum_i|p_i - q_i|\le2\lVert a - b\rVert\le2\lVert U - V\rVert\).

3. \(a = \ket0\), \(b = R_y(0.1)\ket0 = \cos0.05\ket0 + \sin0.05\ket1\). Then \(p_+ = \frac12\) and \(q_+ = \frac12(\cos0.05 + \sin0.05)^2 = \frac12(1 + \sin0.1)\), so \(|p_+ - q_+| = \frac12\sin0.1 = 0.04992\), and the same for \(-\). Total variation \(= \frac12(0.04992 + 0.04992) = \mathbf{0.04992}\). And \(\lVert I - R_y(0.1)\rVert = 2\sin0.025 = \mathbf{0.04999}\). The bound is attained to within 0.16 per cent.

Check. Distributions: \(p_+ + p_- = 1\) and \(q_\pm = \frac12(1\pm\sin0.1)\) also sum to one ✓. For small \(\delta\), \(\frac12\sin\delta\approx\frac\delta2\) and \(2\sin\frac\delta4\approx\frac\delta2\): the bound is sharp to first order, so the constant in part 2 cannot be improved.

P4 The bound is tight up to constants routine

exercises: when errors align, the error-accumulation bound is nearly attained; ends in a number

Take \(U_1 = \dots = U_n = R_z(\delta)\) and \(V_1 = \dots = V_n = I\).

  1. Compute both sides of \(\lVert U_n\cdots U_1 - V_n\cdots V_1\rVert\le\sum_i\lVert U_i - V_i\rVert\) exactly.
  2. Evaluate both for \(n = 1000\), \(\delta = 10^{-3}\).
  3. Prove that whenever \(n\delta\le2\pi\), the left side is at least \(\frac2\pi\) times the right side.
  4. For which \(n\delta\) is the left side zero? What does that say about the bound for large \(n\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Both sides are closed-form functions of \(n\) and \(\delta\), each a chord of the unit circle.

Rung 2

\(R_z(\delta)^n = R_z(n\delta)\), and \(\lVert R_z(x) - I\rVert = 2\sin\frac x4\) for \(0\le x\le2\pi\). For part 3, \(\sin x\le x\) and, for \(0\le x\le\frac\pi2\), \(\sin x\ge\frac2\pi x\) (the sine lies above its chord there).

Rung 3

First line: left \(= 2\sin\frac{n\delta}4\), right \(= 2n\sin\frac\delta4\).

Worked solution

Idea. Rotations about one axis compose by adding angles, so every gate's error points the same way and nothing cancels: the worst case for the bound.

1. Left: \(\lVert R_z(n\delta) - I\rVert = 2\sin\frac{n\delta}4\) (for \(n\delta\le2\pi\)). Right: \(n\lVert R_z(\delta) - I\rVert = 2n\sin\frac\delta4\).

2. \(2\sin0.25 = \mathbf{0.4948}\) against \(2000\sin(2.5\times10^{-4}) = \mathbf{0.5000}\). The bound is off by 1 per cent.

3. With \(x = n\delta/4\le\frac\pi2\): left \(= 2\sin x\ge2\cdot\frac2\pi x = \frac2\pi\cdot\frac{n\delta}2\), and right \(= 2n\sin\frac\delta4\le2n\frac\delta4 = \frac{n\delta}2\). So left \(\ge\frac2\pi\times\) right. The error-accumulation bound is tight to within a factor \(\frac\pi2\) as long as the total angle stays below a full turn: linear growth in \(n\) is the truth, not an artefact of the telescoping.

4. The left side is \(\lVert R_z(n\delta) - I\rVert\), zero exactly when \(R_z(n\delta) = I\), i.e. \(n\delta\in4\pi\mathbb Z\). (At \(n\delta = 2\pi\), \(R_z(2\pi) = -I\), which is zero only up to phase.) Errors that go all the way round cancel; the bound, which only adds, cannot see that. So for large \(n\delta\) the bound can be arbitrarily loose — but a budget has to hold in the worst case, and part 3 shows the worst case is linear.

Check. \(n = 1\): both sides are \(2\sin\frac\delta4\) ✓. Small \(\delta\) at fixed \(n\delta\to0\): both \(\approx\frac{n\delta}2\) ✓.

P5 A third irrational rotation medium

exercises: multiplying rotation operators in the Pauli algebra, reading off angle and axis, reusing the irrationality proof

  1. Show that \(T\cdot HT^\dagger H = R_z(\frac\pi4)R_x(-\frac\pi4)\) exactly — no phase.
  2. Multiply out to find its rotation angle \(\theta'\) and axis \(\mathbf n'\).
  3. Show \(\theta' = \theta\), so \(\theta'/\pi\) is irrational by the deck's proof, with nothing new to do.
  4. Find the angle between \(\mathbf n'\) and the deck's \(\mathbf n\). Conclude that \(T\cdot HTH\) and \(T\cdot HT^\dagger H\) alone already give two non-parallel irrational rotations.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer is a rotation operator: a half-angle cosine and a unit axis. Follow the deck's worked example on \(T\cdot HTH\), with one sign changed.

Rung 2

\(T^\dagger = e^{-i\pi/8}R_z(-\frac\pi4)\) and \(HR_z(\varphi)H = R_x(\varphi)\), so \(HT^\dagger H = e^{-i\pi/8}R_x(-\frac\pi4)\); the phases of \(T\) and \(HT^\dagger H\) cancel. Then use \(ZX = iY\).

Rung 3

First line: \(R_z(\frac\pi4)R_x(-\frac\pi4) = (c - isZ)(c + isX) = c^2 + iscX - iscZ + s^2ZX\).

Worked solution

Idea. Write each gate as a phase times a rotation, cancel the phases, and multiply two rotations by quarter-turn-halves in the Pauli algebra.

1. \(T = e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\frac\pi4)\) and \(T^\dagger = e^{-i\pi/8}R_z(-\frac\pi4)\). Conjugation by \(H\) swaps \(x\) and \(z\), so \(HT^\dagger H = e^{-i\pi/8}R_x(-\frac\pi4)\), and \(T\cdot HT^\dagger H = e^{i\pi/8}e^{-i\pi/8}R_z(\frac\pi4)R_x(-\frac\pi4) = R_z(\frac\pi4)R_x(-\frac\pi4)\). As a check, \(\det(T\cdot HT^\dagger H) = \det T\,\det T^\dagger(\det H)^2 = 1\), as an element of \(SU(2)\) must have.

2. \((c - isZ)(c + isX) = c^2 + iscX - iscZ + s^2ZX\), and \(ZX = iY\), so

\[ T\cdot HT^\dagger H = c^2 - i\big(-scX - s^2Y + scZ\big). \]

So \(\cos\frac{\theta'}2 = c^2\) and \(\sin\frac{\theta'}2\,\mathbf n' = s(-c, -s, c)\), giving \(\mathbf n' = (-c, -s, c)/\sqrt{1 + c^2} = (-0.679, -0.281, 0.679)\).

3. \(\cos\frac{\theta'}2 = c^2 = \cos\frac\theta2\) with both half-angles in \([0, \pi]\), so \(\theta' = \theta\). The irrationality proof used only \(2\cos\frac\theta2 = 1 + \frac{\sqrt2}2\), which is unchanged.

4. \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf n' = \frac{(c, s, c)\cdot(-c, -s, c)}{1 + c^2} = \frac{-s^2}{1 + c^2} = -\frac{0.14645}{1.85355} = -0.0790\), so the angle is \(\mathbf{94.53^\circ}\). Not parallel; by the deck's lemma, rotations about \(\mathbf n\) and \(\mathbf n'\) generate \(SU(2)\), and by density of powers both families are in the closure of \(\langle\tilde H, \tilde T\rangle\). This pair is almost perpendicular, which is why it needs no doubling.

Check. Normalisation: \(c^4 + s^2(c^2 + s^2 + c^2) = (c^2 + s^2)^2 = 1\) ✓. Unitarity: a product of unitaries ✓. And the trace, \(2c^2 = 1.7071 = 1 + \frac{\sqrt2}2\) ✓.

P6 Density of powers medium

exercises: the pigeonhole principle, density on a circle; a number; why density is not efficiency

  1. Prove: if \(\alpha/\pi\) is irrational, the multiples \(k\alpha\), \(k = 0, 1, 2, \dots\), are dense modulo \(2\pi\).
  2. The powers of \(T\cdot HTH\) turn the Bloch sphere by \(\theta = 62.80^\circ\) each. Find every \(k\le30\) for which \(k\theta\) is within \(20^\circ\) of a whole number of turns, and the least \(k\ge1\) for which it is within \(5^\circ\).
  3. Explain why part 1 gives no useful bound on the number of powers needed to land within \(\varepsilon\) of a given rotation.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an existence proof in three moves: the multiples are distinct, two are close, and multiples of their difference march round. Part 2 is a short table.

Rung 2

Divide the circle into \(N\) arcs of length \(2\pi/N\) and place \(N + 1\) points. For part 2, \(6\theta = 376.80^\circ\): from there, add or subtract multiples of \(6\theta\) and \(\theta\).

Rung 3

P7 Clifford gates map Paulis to Paulis medium

exercises: conjugation as a group automorphism, checking generators, the Pauli algebra; a proof

  1. Prove that a unitary \(U\) on \(n\) qubits normalises the Pauli group \(\mathcal P_n\) as soon as \(UX_jU^\dagger\) and \(UZ_jU^\dagger\) lie in \(\mathcal P_n\) for every qubit \(j\).
  2. Show that \(H\) and \(S\) (on one qubit) and CNOT (on two) are Clifford, by computing the images of the generators.
  3. Show that \(T\) is not Clifford. Is \(T^2\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a short group-theory argument; parts 2–3 are tables of matrix products. Use only the Pauli algebra (\(XY = iZ\) and cyclic, anticommutation) and \(\ket{a, b}\mapsto\ket{a, a\oplus b}\) for CNOT.

Rung 2

\(c_U(P) = UPU^\dagger\) is a homomorphism: \(c_U(PQ) = c_U(P)c_U(Q)\). Every element of \(\mathcal P_n\) is a product of \(X_j\)s, \(Z_j\)s and \(iI\). For CNOT, compute \(\mathrm{CNOT}\,P\,\mathrm{CNOT}\) on a basis state \(\ket{a, b}\).

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(UPQU^\dagger = UPU^\dagger\,UQU^\dagger\), so \(\{P : UPU^\dagger\in\mathcal P_n\}\) is closed under products and contains the generators.

Worked solution

Idea. Conjugation respects products, so it is enough to follow the generators; then \(H\), \(S\) and CNOT turn out to permute Paulis up to sign, and \(T\) produces a superposition of two.

1. The set \(K = \{P\in\mathcal P_n : UPU^\dagger\in\mathcal P_n\}\) is closed under products, since \(UPQU^\dagger = (UPU^\dagger)(UQU^\dagger)\) and \(\mathcal P_n\) is a group. It contains every \(X_j\), \(Z_j\) by hypothesis, and \(iI\) since \(U(iI)U^\dagger = iI\). These generate \(\mathcal P_n\): an element is \(i^k\) times a tensor product of letters, \(Y = iXZ\), and letters on different qubits are products of \(X_j, Z_j\). So \(K = \mathcal P_n\), i.e. \(U\mathcal P_nU^\dagger\subseteq\mathcal P_n\). Conjugation is injective and \(\mathcal P_n\) is finite, so the inclusion is an equality.

2. \(H\): \(HXH = Z\), \(HZH = X\) (the deck's worked example: \((X + Z)X(X + Z) = 2Z\)). \(S\): \(SXS^\dagger = Y\) by multiplying the matrices, and \(SZS^\dagger = Z\) as both are diagonal. CNOT, on \(\ket{a, b}\):

  • \(X\otimes I\): \(\ket{a, b}\to\ket{a, a\oplus b}\to\ket{a\oplus1, a\oplus b}\to\ket{a\oplus1, b\oplus1}\), so the image is \(X\otimes X\);
  • \(I\otimes X\): the two additions to \(b\) commute, so the image is \(I\otimes X\);
  • \(Z\otimes I\): the phase \((-1)^a\) is untouched by CNOT, so the image is \(Z\otimes I\);
  • \(I\otimes Z\): the phase is taken when the target holds \(a\oplus b\): \((-1)^{a\oplus b} = (-1)^a(-1)^b\), so the image is \(Z\otimes Z\).

All in \(\mathcal P_2\), so CNOT is Clifford by part 1.

3. \(TXT^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}0&\bar\omega\\ \omega&0\end{pmatrix} = \frac1{\sqrt2}(X + Y)\), whose coordinates in the basis \(\{I, X, Y, Z\}\) are \((0, \frac1{\sqrt2}, \frac1{\sqrt2}, 0)\): two nonzero, so it is not a multiple of a single Pauli. \(T\) is not Clifford. But \(T^2 = S\) is. So the Clifford group is not closed under square roots, and \(T\), a square root of the Clifford gate \(S\), lies just outside it.

Check. Conjugation preserves commutation relations. \(X\otimes I\) and \(I\otimes Z\) commute, and so do their images \(X\otimes X\) and \(Z\otimes Z\) (two anticommuting pairs). \(X\) and \(Z\) anticommute, and so do \(HXH = Z\) and \(HZH = X\) ✓.

  1. Show that up to phase there are at most 24 one-qubit Clifford unitaries, by counting where \(X\) and \(Z\) can go.
  2. Show that \(\langle H, S\rangle\) contains at least 24 up to phase.
  3. Classify \(H\), \(S\), \(Z\), \(SH\) and \(HSH\) by the type of cube axis they turn about (face, vertex or edge) and the angle, and check the census \(1 + 9 + 8 + 6 = 24\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A count from above and one from below. Work on the Bloch sphere: by unit 02, \(U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger = (\Phi(U)\mathbf r)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), and \(\Phi(U)\) determines \(U\) up to sign.

Rung 2

The image of \(X\) is Hermitian, squares to \(I\) and is traceless: one of \(\pm X, \pm Y, \pm Z\). The image of \(Z\) must also anticommute with it. For part 2, find the orbit of \(+z\) under \(\Phi(H), \Phi(S)\), and its stabiliser.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(\Phi(U)e_x\in\{\pm e_x, \pm e_y, \pm e_z\}\) (6 choices), \(\Phi(U)e_z\perp\Phi(U)e_x\) among the same six (4 choices), and \(\Phi(U)e_y = \Phi(U)e_z\times\Phi(U)e_x\).

Worked solution

Idea. A Clifford gate is a rotation of the Bloch sphere that permutes the six points \(\pm x, \pm y, \pm z\); such rotations are counted by where two axes go.

1. \(UXU^\dagger\in\mathcal P_1\) is Hermitian, squares to \(I\), and has trace \(0\): among \(i^kP\) that leaves \(\pm X, \pm Y, \pm Z\) (\(\pm iP\) is not Hermitian, \(\pm I\) has trace \(\pm2\)). Same for \(UZU^\dagger\). So \(\Phi(U)\) sends \(e_x\) and \(e_z\) to two of the six points, and since \(\Phi(U)\) is a rotation they are perpendicular and \(\Phi(U)e_y = \Phi(U)(e_z\times e_x) = \Phi(U)e_z\times\Phi(U)e_x\). Choices: \(6\times4 = 24\) rotations. \(\Phi(U) = \Phi(U')\) forces \(U' = \pm e^{i\alpha}U\) (unit 02's kernel, after removing determinants), so at most 24 Cliffords up to phase.

2. \(\Phi(H)\) swaps \(e_x\leftrightarrow e_z\) and sends \(e_y\to-e_y\); \(\Phi(S)\) sends \(e_x\to e_y\to-e_x\), fixing \(e_z\). Orbit of \(e_z\): \(e_z\xrightarrow{H}e_x\xrightarrow{S}e_y\xrightarrow{S}-e_x\xrightarrow{H}-e_z\), and \(e_y\xrightarrow{S^2}-e_y\): all six. The stabiliser of \(e_z\) contains \(\Phi(S)^k\), \(k = 0, 1, 2, 3\), four distinct rotations. Orbit–stabiliser: \(|\Phi(\langle H, S\rangle)|\ge6\times4 = 24\). With part 1, exactly 24.

3.

  • \(S\): a quarter-turn about \(z\) — a face axis, \(90^\circ\).
  • \(Z = S^2\): a half-turn about \(z\) — face, \(180^\circ\).
  • \(HSH\): conjugating by \(H\) moves \(S\)'s axis from \(z\) to \(x\) — face, \(90^\circ\) about \(x\).
  • \(H\): a half-turn about \((x + z)/\sqrt2\), the midpoint direction of an edge — edge, \(180^\circ\).
  • \(SH\): \(\Phi(SH) = \Phi(S)\Phi(H)\), applying \(\Phi(H)\) first: \(e_x\to e_z\to e_z\), \(\ e_z\to e_x\to e_y\), \(\ e_y\to-e_y\to e_x\). So \(x\to z\to y\to x\), a cyclic permutation of the axes, fixing the diagonal through \(\pm(1, 1, 1)\): a third of a turn about a vertex axis.

Census: identity \(1\); face axes \(3\), each with turns of \(90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ\): \(9\); vertex diagonals \(4\), turns of \(120^\circ, 240^\circ\): \(8\); edge axes \(6\), half-turns: \(6\). \(1 + 9 + 8 + 6 = 24\) ✓.

Check. \(\Phi(SH)^3 = I\): following \(x\to z\to y\to x\) three times returns every axis ✓. And \(SH\) on matrices: \(\mathrm{tr}(SH) = \frac{1 - i}{\sqrt2} = e^{-i\pi/4}\), \(\det(SH) = -i\); dividing by \(\sqrt{\det} = e^{-i\pi/4}\) gives an \(SU(2)\) element of trace \(1 = 2\cos\frac{\vartheta}2\), so \(\vartheta = \frac{2\pi}3\) ✓.

Rung 1

Parts 1–2 are closure arguments; part 3 is a proof by contradiction that ends in "\(\sqrt{2 + \sqrt2}\) is not of the form \(a + b\sqrt2\)".

Rung 2

If the entries were in the ring, the ratio of the two diagonal entries, \(e^{i\pi/16}\), would be in the field. Then take real parts, and use \(\cos\frac\pi8 = 2\cos^2\frac\pi{16} - 1\) and \(\cos\frac\pi8 = \frac12\sqrt{2 + \sqrt2}\).

Rung 3

First line of the last step: suppose \((a + b\sqrt2)^2 = 2 + \sqrt2\) with \(a, b\in\mathbb Q\); comparing coefficients (as \(\sqrt2\) is irrational), \(a^2 + 2b^2 = 2\) and \(2ab = 1\).

Idea. Clifford + \(T\) can only produce numbers from one ring; \(e^{i\pi/16}\) needs a square root that the ring's field does not contain.

1. Entries: \(H\) has \(\pm\frac1{\sqrt2}\); \(S\) has \(0, 1, i\); \(T\) has \(0, 1, \omega = \frac1{\sqrt2} + i\frac1{\sqrt2}\); CNOT has \(0, 1\). Then \(F\) is a field: \(\frac1{u + vi} = \frac{u - vi}{u^2 + v^2}\), with \(u^2 + v^2\in\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\) nonzero for real \(u, v\) not both zero. Conjugation sends \(u + vi\) to \(u - vi\in F\). And \(u + vi\) is real iff \(v = 0\), so \(F\cap\mathbb R = \mathbb Q(\sqrt2)\).

3. Suppose \(e^{i\alpha}R_z(\frac\pi{16}) = \mathrm{diag}(e^{i(\alpha - \pi/32)}, e^{i(\alpha + \pi/32)})\) had both entries in the ring. exercises: SU(2) as the three-sphere, covering by balls, a counting lower bound; ends in a number

  1. Show that \(U = a_0I - i\mathbf a\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\) with \((a_0, \mathbf a)\in\mathbb R^4\), \(a_0^2 + |\mathbf a|^2 = 1\), parametrises \(SU(2)\), and that for two such, \(\lVert U - V\rVert\) is the Euclidean distance between their points of the unit sphere \(S^3\subset\mathbb R^4\).
  2. There are at most \(2^{L+1}\) words of length at most \(L\) in \(H\) and \(T\). Each gives two points \(\pm\tilde W\) of \(SU(2)\). Suppose every \(U\in SU(2)\) is within \(\varepsilon\) of one of these points. Using \(\mathrm{vol}(S^3) = 2\pi^2\) and, for small \(\varepsilon\), volume \(\frac43\pi\varepsilon^3\) for each ball, show \(L\ge3\log_2(1/\varepsilon) + \log_2\frac{3\pi}4 - 1\).
  3. Evaluate at \(\varepsilon = 10^{-11}\), and compare with Ross–Selinger's \(3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A norm identity, then a volume inequality, then a logarithm. The \(3\) is the dimension of \(S^3\).

Rung 2

For real \((c_0, \mathbf c)\), \((c_0I - i\mathbf c\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^\dagger(c_0I - i\mathbf c\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = (c_0^2 + |\mathbf c|^2)I\), by \((\mathbf c\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2 = |\mathbf c|^2I\). Apply it to the difference \(U - V\). For part 2, the balls must cover \(S^3\), so their volumes add up to at least its volume.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(2\cdot2^{L+1}\cdot\frac43\pi\varepsilon^3\ge2\pi^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. \(SU(2)\) is a round three-sphere in the operator norm, so \(\varepsilon\)-balls have volume \(\propto\varepsilon^3\), and covering needs \(\propto\varepsilon^{-3}\) of them; a doubling word count then needs \(3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)\) letters.

1. Unit 02: every element of \(SU(2)\) is \(\cos\frac\vartheta2 I - i\sin\frac\vartheta2\,\mathbf k\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), i.e. \(a_0 = \cos\frac\vartheta2\), \(\mathbf a = \sin\frac\vartheta2\mathbf k\), and conversely any unit \((a_0, \mathbf a)\) gives such a rotation. \(U - V = (a_0 - b_0)I - i(\mathbf a - \mathbf b)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma\), and for real \((c_0, \mathbf c)\),

\[ (c_0I + i\mathbf c\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)(c_0I - i\mathbf c\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma) = c_0^2 + (\mathbf c\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2 = (c_0^2 + |\mathbf c|^2)I . \]

So \(\lVert(U - V)v\rVert^2 = (c_0^2 + |\mathbf c|^2)\lVert v\rVert^2\) for every \(v\), and \(\lVert U - V\rVert = \sqrt{(a_0 - b_0)^2 + |\mathbf a - \mathbf b|^2}\): the Euclidean distance in \(\mathbb R^4\).

2. The points of \(S^3\) within \(\varepsilon\) of a given point form a cap. Its volume is \(\frac43\pi\varepsilon^3\) to leading order, and never more: a flat ball of radius \(\varepsilon\) has that volume, and the cap is slightly smaller because the sphere curves away (exactly, \(4\pi\int_0^\rho\sin^2t\,dt\) with \(\varepsilon = 2\sin\frac\rho2\); comparing derivatives in \(\rho\), \(\sin^2\rho = 4\sin^2\frac\rho2\cos^2\frac\rho2\le4\sin^2\frac\rho2\cos\frac\rho2\), which is the derivative of \(\frac{8}{3}\sin^3\frac\rho2 = \frac13\varepsilon^3\)). At most \(2\cdot2^{L+1}\) caps cover \(S^3\), and covering sets have volumes adding up to at least the whole, so

\[ 2^{L+2}\cdot\tfrac43\pi\varepsilon^3\ge2\pi^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad2^{L+1}\ge\frac{3\pi}4\varepsilon^{-3}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad L\ge3\log_2\frac1\varepsilon + \log_2\frac{3\pi}4 - 1 . \]

3. \(3\log_2(10^{11}) = 109.62\), \(\log_2\frac{3\pi}4 = 1.236\), so \(L\ge109.62 + 1.24 - 1 = \mathbf{109.86}\): the words of length at most 109 cannot cover \(SU(2)\) to within \(10^{-11}\), so some rotation needs a word of at least 110 letters. Ross–Selinger's T-count is \(3\log_2(1/\varepsilon) = 109.6\) plus lower-order terms: the same leading term. (Strictly, the bound here counts letters, the \(T\)s and \(H\)s together; the Matsumoto–Amano normal form, cited in the deck, gives at most a constant times \(2^n\) operators of T-count \(n\), and the same argument gives T-count \(\ge3\log_2(1/\varepsilon) - O(1)\).)
