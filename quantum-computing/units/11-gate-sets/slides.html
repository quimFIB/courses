Finite gate sets: density, Solovay–Kitaev, and why the T gate is the expensive one

A real machine offers finitely many gates, so every other gate is approximated. Errors add along a circuit, two gates reach everything, the approximation costs only logarithmically — and the cost is counted in T gates.

needs 10~3 h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 11

Unit 10 proved that CNOT and the single-qubit gates build every unitary exactly. But "the single-qubit gates" is a continuum: one gate for every point of $SU(2)$. No machine offers that, and above all no error-corrected machine does — unit 24 will show that error correction protects a finite menu of operations. So universality must be approximate, and this unit prices the approximation.

Four results do it. Errors add along a circuit, so a total budget splits into a per-gate budget. Two gates, $H$ and $T$, generate a dense subgroup of $SU(2)$ up to phase, so nothing is out of reach. The Solovay–Kitaev theorem says that reaching a gate to accuracy $\varepsilon$ costs only polylogarithmically many gates. And modern exact synthesis says the true price of a $z$-rotation is about $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ $T$ gates. Along the way the Clifford group appears: the gates that map Paulis to Paulis, which will turn out to be the cheap ones, with $T$ the one that is not.

One rotation in a billion gates

the running example: one rotation in a billion gates

A circuit of $10^9$ gates — the size of a serious quantum algorithm — must produce output statistics within 1 per cent of the ideal ones. One of its gates is a $z$-rotation $R_z(\theta)$ by an angle $\theta$ that is nothing special: $0.3$, say, or $2\pi/7$.

The question: the machine offers only $H$, $S$, $T$ and CNOT. How many $T$ gates does that one rotation cost?

Why a finite menu

A gate on real hardware is a control pulse: unit 02's proton is turned by a field switched on for a time $t$, and the angle is $\Omega t$. The angle is set by an analogue knob, and every analogue knob has finite precision. Errors in each gate creep into the next.

The way out is the one classical computers took: encode, and protect a finite set of operations so well that they are effectively exact. That is error correction, units 22–24, and it protects only a finite menu — unit 24 says which.

a finite gate set

A finite set $\mathcal G$ of unitaries on one or two qubits. A circuit over $\mathcal G$ is a product of gates from $\mathcal G$, each acting on chosen wires and extended by identities (unit 10). The menu here: $\{H, S, T, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$.

Using $T$ alone, the menu's $z$-rotations are its eight powers, up to phase. Every other angle must be approximated — and the first job is to say what that means.

All rotations about $z$, as a circle. Up to phase, $T^k = R_z(k\pi/4)$ gives eight of them; the running example's $R_z(\theta)$ is not among them.

The menu's $z$-rotations. $T = e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\frac\pi4)$ (unit 02), so $T^k = e^{ik\pi/8}R_z(\frac{k\pi}4)$ and $T^8 = I$ exactly, since $T^8 = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{2\pi i})$. $S = T^2$ and $Z = T^4$. Products of $T$s alone never leave this set of eight; anything else needs $H$ to change axis, which is section 2's business. Words that use $H$ as well can come back to the $z$ axis, but not onto a generic angle exactly: section 5 proves that $R_z(\frac\pi{16})$, for one, is out of exact reach.

Why approximation is forced, and not just convenient. The words in a finite gate set are countable, and $SU(2)$ is not: almost every single-qubit gate is not exactly any circuit over any finite set. Section 5 says exactly which ones are, for Clifford + $T$.

The classical analogy, and where it breaks. A classical computer also offers a finite instruction set and approximates $\pi$ or $\sqrt2$ to a chosen number of bits. The difference is that a classical bit error is a flip, and a gate either happened or did not; a quantum gate error is a continuous mis-rotation. Unit 22 shows why correcting a discrete set of errors is nevertheless enough (the discretisation of errors); this unit takes the corrected, finite menu as given and asks what it costs to use.

The operator norm

the operator norm $$\lVert A\rVert = \max_{\lVert v\rVert = 1}\lVert Av\rVert .$$

The most $A$ stretches a unit vector. The maximum exists: the unit sphere of $\mathbb C^d$ is compact and $v\mapsto\lVert Av\rVert$ is continuous.

four properties

(a) $\lVert AB\rVert\le\lVert A\rVert\,\lVert B\rVert$. (b) $\lVert UAV\rVert = \lVert A\rVert$ for unitary $U, V$. (c) for normal $A$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_i$, $\ \lVert A\rVert = \max_i|\lambda_i|$. (d) $\lVert A\otimes I\rVert = \lVert A\rVert$.

proof

(a) $\lVert ABv\rVert\le\lVert A\rVert\,\lVert Bv\rVert\le\lVert A\rVert\,\lVert B\rVert$. (b) $U$ preserves lengths, and $V$ maps the unit sphere onto itself. (c) Unit 00: $A = \sum_i\lambda_i\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis, so for $v = \sum c_ie_i$,

$$\lVert Av\rVert^2 = \sum_i|\lambda_i|^2|c_i|^2\le\max_i|\lambda_i|^2,$$

with equality at the top eigenvector. (d) in the notes.

Check: $\lVert U\rVert = 1$ for unitary $U$, by (c) or directly. $\lVert X + Z\rVert = \sqrt2$: $X + Z = \sqrt2H$ is Hermitian with eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt2$.

Full proof of (d)

For any $A$, $\lVert A\rVert^2$ is the largest eigenvalue of the Hermitian operator $A^\dagger A$: $\lVert Av\rVert^2 = \braket{v|A^\dagger A|v}$, and by (c)'s argument applied to $A^\dagger A$ (whose eigenvalues are $\ge0$) the maximum over unit $v$ is its largest eigenvalue. Now $(A\otimes I)^\dagger(A\otimes I) = A^\dagger A\otimes I$ (unit 03's rule $(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D) = AC\otimes BD$), whose eigenvalues are those of $A^\dagger A$, each repeated: if $A^\dagger Ae = \mu e$ then $(A^\dagger A\otimes I)(e\otimes f) = \mu\,e\otimes f$, and these vectors span. So $\lVert A\otimes I\rVert = \lVert A\rVert$. The same holds with $I$ on the left or the gate's wires anywhere in the register, since reordering qubits is a permutation unitary and (b) applies.

Why (d) matters. A circuit's gate acts on two of $n$ wires. Its error as an operator on all $2^n$ dimensions is exactly its error on its own two wires: idle qubits neither add nor hide error.

The SVD view. From linear algebra over $\mathbb R$ you know $\lVert A\rVert$ as the largest singular value; the complex SVD (unit 03 used it for the Schmidt decomposition) gives the same statement here, since $\lVert A\rVert^2 = \lambda_{\max}(A^\dagger A) = \sigma_{\max}^2$. Unit 00's notes met this norm once already, to make $e^{A}$ converge.

(c) needs normality. $A = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ has both eigenvalues $0$ and norm $1$.

How far a rotation is from doing nothing

distance between gates

$\lVert U - V\rVert$, the operator norm of the difference. By (b), $\lVert U - V\rVert = \lVert V^\dagger U - I\rVert$: the distance from $V$ to $U$ is how far the "error" $V^\dagger U$ is from the identity.

a rotation's distance from the identity $$\lVert R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) - I\rVert = 2\sin\tfrac{\theta}{4}\qquad(0\le\theta\le2\pi).$$
proof

$R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) - I$ is a function of the Hermitian $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, so normal, with eigenvalues $e^{\mp i\theta/2} - 1$ (unit 02). By (c) its norm is $|e^{i\theta/2} - 1| = 2\sin\frac\theta4$: a chord of the unit circle.

Check: $\theta = 0$ gives $0$; $\ \theta = 2\pi$ gives $2$, the largest possible, and indeed $R_{\mathbf n}(2\pi) = -I$. Small $\theta$: $\approx\theta/2$.

The eigenvalues of $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$ sit at angle $\pm\theta/2$ on the unit circle; the distance from $I$ is the chord to $1$.

The eigenvalues. $\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$ (unit 02), so $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) = e^{-i\theta\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}$ has $e^{\mp i\theta/2}$ by the functional calculus of unit 00, and $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) - I$ has $e^{\mp i\theta/2} - 1$ on the same eigenvectors. $|e^{i\alpha} - 1|^2 = (\cos\alpha - 1)^2 + \sin^2\alpha = 2 - 2\cos\alpha = 4\sin^2\frac\alpha2$, so with $\alpha = \theta/2$ the modulus is $2\sin\frac\theta4$ for $0\le\theta\le2\pi$.

Two rotations about one axis. $\lVert R_{\mathbf n}(\theta) - R_{\mathbf n}(\theta')\rVert = \lVert R_{\mathbf n}(\theta - \theta') - I\rVert = 2\sin\frac{|\theta - \theta'|}{4}$, by (b) with $V = R_{\mathbf n}(\theta')^\dagger$. Small angle errors cost about half their size in this norm. Section 1's budget uses this to turn a distance into an angle.

Why the operator norm and not another. Any two norms on a finite-dimensional space are equivalent, so "converges" means the same in all of them; but constants matter for budgets. The operator norm is the one that bounds measurement errors with constant 2 (next slide), is unitarily invariant, and ignores idle qubits. The Frobenius norm $\sqrt{\mathrm{tr}(A^\dagger A)}$ fails the last: it grows by $\sqrt2$ with every idle qubit.

What a distance between gates means

Same input, same measurement; only the gate differs.

theorem: distance bounds every probability

For any unit $\psi$ and any projective measurement $\{P_i\}$ (unit 01), with $p_i = \lVert P_iU\psi\rVert^2$ and $q_i = \lVert P_iV\psi\rVert^2$:

$$|p_i - q_i|\le2\,\lVert U - V\rVert\quad\text{for every }i.$$
proof

Write $a = U\psi$, $b = V\psi$, so $\lVert a - b\rVert\le\lVert U - V\rVert$ and $\lVert a\rVert = \lVert b\rVert = 1$. Then

$$p_i - q_i = \braket{a|P_i|a - b} + \braket{a - b|P_i|b},$$

as expanding the right side shows ($P_i$ Hermitian). Each term is at most $\lVert a - b\rVert$ by Cauchy–Schwarz and $\lVert P_i\rVert\le1$. $\square$

Check: $U = V$ gives $0$ ✓. $\ U = I$, $V = X$, $\psi = \ket0$, $P_0 = \ket0\!\bra0$: $p_0 - q_0 = 1$, and $\lVert I - X\rVert = 2$ (eigenvalues $0, 2$), so $1\le4$ ✓.

So "a gate within $\varepsilon$" means: no experiment tells it from the ideal with probability shifts beyond $2\varepsilon$.

Full proof, the expansion

$\braket{a|P_i|a - b} + \braket{a - b|P_i|b} = \braket{a|P_i|a} - \braket{a|P_i|b} + \braket{a|P_i|b} - \braket{b|P_i|b} = p_i - q_i$, since $p_i = \lVert P_ia\rVert^2 = \braket{a|P_i^\dagger P_i|a} = \braket{a|P_i|a}$ for a projection. Cauchy–Schwarz: $|\braket{a|P_i|a - b}|\le\lVert a\rVert\,\lVert P_i(a - b)\rVert\le\lVert a - b\rVert$, and similarly for the other term. Finally $\lVert a - b\rVert = \lVert(U - V)\psi\rVert\le\lVert U - V\rVert$ by definition of the norm.

A stronger form, for the whole distribution. $\sum_i|p_i - q_i|\le2\lVert U - V\rVert$ as well, so the total variation distance $\frac12\sum_i|p_i - q_i|$ is at most $\lVert U - V\rVert$. Problem P3 proves it: write $p_i - q_i = (\lVert P_ia\rVert + \lVert P_ib\rVert)(\lVert P_ia\rVert - \lVert P_ib\rVert)$ and apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the sum. This is the form the running example's "1 per cent" means: a circuit within $10^{-2}$ of the ideal gives an output distribution within $10^{-2}$ in total variation, so any event — including "the algorithm's answer is right" — changes probability by at most $10^{-2}$.

Mixed inputs. If the input is not a fixed $\psi$ but one of several, chosen at random with probabilities $w_j$, each outcome's probability is the average $\sum_jw_jp_i^{(j)}$, and averaging preserves the bound. (Unit 04's density operators are the general language for this; nothing here needs them.)

History. This is the bound of Nielsen and Chuang §4.5.3, with their error $E(U, V)$ defined as a maximum over states; for unitaries that maximum is the operator norm. The same bound appears in Bernstein and Vazirani (1997), who used it to show that a quantum computation tolerates small errors in its gates — the robustness unit 12 relies on.

Global phase: when it may be dropped

distance up to phase $$d(U, V) = \min_{\alpha}\lVert U - e^{i\alpha}V\rVert .$$

Probabilities depend on $V$ only up to phase, so the theorem holds with $d(U, V)$ in place of $\lVert U - V\rVert$.

Example. $T = e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\frac\pi4)$, so $d(T, R_z(\frac\pi4)) = 0$ — but $\lVert T - R_z(\frac\pi4)\rVert = |1 - e^{-i\pi/8}| = 2\sin\frac\pi{16} = 0.390$.

safe: a gate on its wires

A phase on one gate, $e^{i\alpha}U\otimes I$, is a phase on the whole circuit's unitary. So each gate may be approximated up to its own phase, and the circuit is then right up to one global phase.

Controlled, a phase is no longer global: it becomes a relative phase on the control.

not safe: a gate that will be controlled

Controlled-$T$ and controlled-$R_z(\frac\pi4)$ differ by $\mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/8})$ on the control, and $d = 2\sin\frac\pi{32} = 0.196$, not $0$. Approximate the controlled gate itself, never "its target up to phase".

Why the theorem survives. $\lVert P_ie^{i\alpha}V\psi\rVert^2 = \lVert P_iV\psi\rVert^2$, so $q_i$ is the same for every representative $e^{i\alpha}V$; apply the theorem to the closest one.

The controlled computation. In the basis $\ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}, \ket{11}$ (unit 10's controlled gates: identity unless the control is $1$), controlled-$T = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 1, e^{i\pi/4})$ and controlled-$R_z(\frac\pi4) = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, e^{-i\pi/8}, e^{i\pi/8})$. Their ratio is $\mathrm{diag}(1, 1, e^{i\pi/8}, e^{i\pi/8})$: the phase gate $\mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/8})$ on the control. For a diagonal difference the norm is the largest entry's modulus, so $d = \min_\alpha\max\big(|1 - e^{i\alpha}|,\ |e^{i\pi/8} - e^{i\alpha}|\big)$, minimised at $\alpha = \pi/16$, where both are $2\sin\frac\pi{32} = 0.196$. Without the minimisation, $\lVert\cdot\rVert = 2\sin\frac\pi{16} = 0.390$.

When the unit uses which. Sections 2–5 approximate single-qubit gates placed on wires, so they work up to phase, and in $SU(2)$ where convenient: every $U\in U(2)$ is $e^{i\alpha}V$ with $V\in SU(2)$ (unit 02). Unit 10's exact constructions of controlled gates from CNOTs and single-qubit gates come first, and only then is each single-qubit gate approximated — which is safe by the green box.

Errors add along a circuit

theorem: errors add

For unitaries $U_1, \dots, U_n$ and $V_1, \dots, V_n$,

$$\lVert U_n\cdots U_1 - V_n\cdots V_1\rVert\le\textstyle\sum_i\lVert U_i - V_i\rVert .$$
proof, n = 2 $$\begin{aligned}U_2U_1 - V_2V_1 &= U_2(U_1 - V_1)\\ &\quad + (U_2 - V_2)V_1,\end{aligned}$$

and $\lVert U_2(U_1 - V_1)\rVert = \lVert U_1 - V_1\rVert$, $\ \lVert(U_2 - V_2)V_1\rVert = \lVert U_2 - V_2\rVert$ by (b). Induct, peeling one gate at a time.

Check, $n = 1$: equality. Check, all $V_i = U_i$: $0\le0$.

The hybrid argument: swap one gate at a time. Neighbouring rows differ in one gate, so their distance is that gate's error; the triangle inequality adds them.

Full proof

Let $W_k = U_n\cdots U_{k+1}V_k\cdots V_1$, row $k$ of the figure: the first $k$ gates replaced. $W_0 = U_n\cdots U_1$ and $W_n = V_n\cdots V_1$. Then

$$U_n\cdots U_1 - V_n\cdots V_1 = \sum_{k=1}^n(W_{k-1} - W_k),\qquad W_{k-1} - W_k = (U_n\cdots U_{k+1})(U_k - V_k)(V_{k-1}\cdots V_1),$$

a telescoping sum. Each term has norm exactly $\lVert U_k - V_k\rVert$ by unitary invariance, and the triangle inequality finishes. With the gates acting on a few of $n$ wires, property (d) makes each term the gate's own error.

Unitarity is used. For general matrices the same telescoping gives $\sum_k\lVert U_n\rVert\cdots\lVert U_{k+1}\rVert\,\lVert U_k - V_k\rVert\,\lVert V_{k-1}\rVert\cdots\lVert V_1\rVert$, which can grow exponentially. Unitaries have norm one, so errors add and never multiply — the reason a quantum computation of $10^9$ gates is conceivable at all.

The bound is nearly attained. Take every $U_i = R_z(\delta)$ and every $V_i = I$: the errors all point the same way. Then the left side is $\lVert R_z(n\delta) - I\rVert = 2\sin\frac{n\delta}4$ and the right is $n\cdot2\sin\frac\delta4$. At $n = 1000$, $\delta = 10^{-3}$: $0.4948$ against $0.5000$. So linear growth in $n$ is the truth in the worst case, not an artefact of the proof (problem P4). Errors in random directions partly cancel and do better; the budget below does not count on it.

Worked: the budget

worked example 1 · a billion gates, one per cent

Setup. The ideal circuit is $U = U_N\cdots U_1$, $N = 10^9$. The machine runs $V = V_N\cdots V_1$, each $V_i$ a circuit over the finite menu approximating $U_i$ up to phase. The target: $d(U, V)\le10^{-2}$, an error budget.

Step 1, errors add. Choose phases so that $\lVert U_i - e^{i\alpha_i}V_i\rVert = d(U_i, V_i)$. Then by the theorem,

$$\begin{aligned}d(U, V) &\le\big\lVert U - e^{i\sum\alpha_i}V\big\rVert\\ &\le\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^N d(U_i, V_i).\end{aligned}$$

Step 2, split evenly. It suffices that every gate meets

$$d(U_i, V_i)\le\frac{10^{-2}}{10^9} = 10^{-11}.$$

Step 3, what that means for the running example. For $U_i = R_z(\theta)$ approximated by a $z$-rotation $R_z(\theta')$:

$$2\sin\frac{|\theta - \theta'|}4\le10^{-11}$$

exactly when $|\theta - \theta'|\le2\times10^{-11}$ rad, to within one part in $10^{22}$.

Eleven significant figures in an angle. No pulse is calibrated to that; a circuit over an exact finite menu can be.

Step 4, what the user sees. Total variation distance at most $10^{-2}$: any event, such as "the output is correct", has its probability moved by at most 1 per cent.

Check. $10^9\times10^{-11} = 10^{-2}$ ✓. And one gate, $N = 1$: the budget is the whole $10^{-2}$ ✓.

Step 1 in detail. $e^{i\sum\alpha_i}V = (e^{i\alpha_N}V_N)\cdots(e^{i\alpha_1}V_1)$, a product of unitaries, so the theorem applies to it and to $U$ gate by gate. The minimum over phases defining $d(U, V)$ is at most the value at the particular phase $\sum\alpha_i$. This is the green box of the global-phase slide in action.

Only the rotations really need it. In practice most of the $10^9$ gates are exact — Cliffords, CNOTs and $T$s — and contribute nothing. Spreading the whole $10^{-2}$ over only the $R$ rotations allows $10^{-2}/R$ each. Section 5 shows the cost grows like $\log(1/\varepsilon)$, so this hardly matters: with $10^8$ rotations the per-rotation budget is $10^{-10}$ and the cost drops from about 110 to about 100 $T$ gates. The even split is the conservative choice, and it is the one the rest of the deck carries.

Step 3, the approximation sign. $2\sin(x/4)\le\varepsilon$ with $x = |\theta - \theta'|$ small is $x\le4\arcsin(\varepsilon/2) = 2\varepsilon(1 + O(\varepsilon^2))$, so $|\theta - \theta'|\le2\times10^{-11}$ to within one part in $10^{22}$.

Where the budget goes next. Section 2 shows the menu can meet $10^{-11}$ at all; sections 4 and 5 say how many gates it takes.

Dense subgroups, and the plan

dense, and approximately universal

A subset $D\subseteq SU(2)$ is dense if every $U\in SU(2)$ is a limit of elements of $D$: for every $\varepsilon\gt0$ some $W\in D$ has $\lVert U - W\rVert\lt\varepsilon$.

A finite gate set is approximately universal if circuits over it approximate every unitary on every number of qubits, up to phase, to every $\varepsilon\gt0$.

lemma: the closure of a subgroup is a subgroup

If $G\le SU(2)$, its closure $\bar G$ is a closed subgroup. Proof: if $g_k\to g$ and $h_k\to h$ then $g_kh_k^{-1}\to gh^{-1}$, as multiplication and $U\mapsto U^\dagger$ are continuous.

Work in $SU(2)$. $\det H = -1$ and $\det T = e^{i\pi/4}$, so take the representatives

$$\tilde H = -iH,\qquad\tilde T = e^{-i\pi/8}T = R_z(\tfrac\pi4),$$

both of determinant $1$. A word in $\tilde H, \tilde T$ is the same word in $H, T$ times a phase. Let $G = \langle\tilde H, \tilde T\rangle$.

the plan

1. $G$ contains a rotation $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$ with $\theta/\pi$ irrational. 2. Its powers are dense in all rotations about $\mathbf n$. 3. Conjugating by $\tilde H$ gives a second axis $\mathbf m$. 4. Rotations about two non-parallel axes generate $SU(2)$. So $\bar G\supseteq$ all of $SU(2)$.

The determinants. $\det H = \frac12(1\cdot(-1) - 1\cdot1) = -1$, so $\det(-iH) = (-i)^2(-1) = 1$ (a $2\times2$ scalar factor enters squared). $\det T = e^{i\pi/4}$, so $\det(e^{-i\pi/8}T) = e^{-i\pi/4}e^{i\pi/4} = 1$, and $e^{-i\pi/8}T = \mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\pi/8}, e^{i\pi/8}) = R_z(\frac\pi4)$. Unit 02 wrote $H = iR_{\mathbf h}(\pi)$ with $\mathbf h = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2$, so $\tilde H = -iH = R_{\mathbf h}(\pi)$: a half-turn about $\mathbf h$.

Two useful elements of $G$. $\tilde H^2 = R_{\mathbf h}(2\pi) = -I$, so $-I\in G$ and signs are free. And $\tilde T^8 = R_z(2\pi) = -I$, so $\tilde T^{-1} = -\tilde T^7 = \tilde H^2\tilde T^7$: inverses are words too. Section 4 needs that.

Why up to phase is enough. Every $U\in U(2)$ is $e^{i\alpha}V$ with $V\in SU(2)$. If a word $\tilde W$ in $\tilde H, \tilde T$ has $\lVert V - \tilde W\rVert\lt\varepsilon$, and $\tilde W = e^{i\beta}W$ with $W$ the same word in $H, T$, then $\lVert U - e^{i(\alpha + \beta)}W\rVert\lt\varepsilon$, so $d(U, W)\lt\varepsilon$. On wires that is all a circuit needs (the global-phase slide).

Where the plan comes from. Boykin, Mor, Pulver, Roychowdhury and Vatan, Inf. Proc. Lett. 75, 101 (2000), proved that $\{H, T, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$ is universal along these lines; Nielsen and Chuang §4.5.3 follows them. The proof of the irrationality step given here is short and complete.

Worked: T·HTH as a rotation

worked example 2 · the product T·HTH

Step 1, $HTH$. Conjugation by $H$ swaps the $x$ and $z$ axes (unit 02), so $HR_z(\varphi)H = R_x(\varphi)$, and

$$HTH = e^{i\pi/8}HR_z(\tfrac\pi4)H = e^{i\pi/8}R_x(\tfrac\pi4).$$

Step 2, the product. $T\cdot HTH = e^{i\pi/4}R_z(\frac\pi4)R_x(\frac\pi4)$. With $c = \cos\frac\pi8$, $s = \sin\frac\pi8$:

Algebraic integers

an algebraic integer

A complex number that is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients, $x^d + a_{d-1}x^{d-1} + \dots + a_0$, $a_i\in\mathbb Z$.

Examples: every integer; $\sqrt2$ (root of $x^2 - 2$); $i$; every root of unity $\zeta$, $\zeta^N = 1$ (root of $x^N - 1$).

fact (cited): they form a ring

Sums and products of algebraic integers are algebraic integers. Proved in any first course in algebraic number theory, e.g. Stewart and Tall, Algebraic Number Theory and Fermat's Last Theorem, chapter 2.

lemma: a rational algebraic integer is an integer

If $p/q$ in lowest terms is a root of $x^d + \dots + a_0$, then $q = \pm1$.

proof

Multiply $(p/q)^d + a_{d-1}(p/q)^{d-1} + \dots + a_0 = 0$ by $q^d$:

$$p^d = -q\big(a_{d-1}p^{d-1} + \dots + a_0q^{d-1}\big).$$

So $q\mid p^d$; with $\gcd(p, q) = 1$, $q = \pm1$. $\square$ This is the rational root theorem with leading coefficient 1.

So $\tfrac12$ is not an algebraic integer. That is all the next slide needs.

Why a citation is acceptable here, and how to avoid it. The ring property is genuinely a theorem: the standard proof shows that $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer iff $\mathbb Z[\alpha]$ is a finitely generated abelian group, and then $\mathbb Z[\alpha, \beta]$ is finitely generated whenever $\mathbb Z[\alpha]$ and $\mathbb Z[\beta]$ are. That is about a page, and belongs to number theory, not to this course. The worked example on the next slide uses it three times; its notes give a second proof that uses nothing but the rational root theorem's idea, so the result is fully proved either way.

The name. Inside the rationals, the algebraic integers are exactly the integers (the lemma); inside $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ they are $\{a + b\sqrt2 : a, b\in\mathbb Z\}$. So they are the right generalisation of "integer" to algebraic numbers, which is where the name comes from (Dedekind, 1871).

Where this reappears. Section 5's ring $\mathbb Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}, i]$, the ring of entries of Clifford + $T$ circuits, is built from the same numbers; there, the question will be which algebraic numbers a circuit can produce exactly.

Worked: the angle is not a rational multiple of π

worked example 3 · θ/π is irrational

Claim. For $\theta$ with $\cos\frac\theta2 = \cos^2\frac\pi8$, the ratio $\theta/\pi$ is irrational.

Step 1, suppose not. $\theta/\pi = p/q$ with integers $p, q\gt0$. Put $\zeta = e^{i\theta/2}$. Then $\zeta^{4q} = e^{2\pi ip} = 1$: $\zeta$ is a root of unity, so an algebraic integer, and so is $\bar\zeta = \zeta^{-1} = \zeta^{4q-1}$.

Step 2, the ring. So $\zeta + \bar\zeta = 2\cos\frac\theta2$ is an algebraic integer. But

$$2\cos\tfrac\theta2 = 2\cos^2\tfrac\pi8 = 1 + \cos\tfrac\pi4 = 1 + \tfrac{\sqrt2}2 .$$

Step 3, subtract and square. $-1$ is an algebraic integer, so $\frac{\sqrt2}{2} = (1 + \frac{\sqrt2}2) - 1$ is one, and so is its square, $\frac{\sqrt2}2\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}2 = \frac12$.

Step 4, contradiction. $\frac12$ is rational and not an integer, so by the lemma it is not an algebraic integer. Hence $\theta/\pi$ is irrational. $\square$

the same, in one line

$x = 1 + \frac{\sqrt2}2$ has minimal polynomial $x^2 - 2x + \frac12$, which is not in $\mathbb Z[x]$; a root of unity's $\zeta + \bar\zeta$ always has an integral one.

Check, a rational case: for $\theta/\pi = \frac25$, $2\cos\frac\theta2 = 2\cos\frac\pi5 = \frac{1 + \sqrt5}2 = 1.618$, a root of $x^2 - x - 1$: monic and integral, as the argument requires.

Full proof without the cited ring theorem

Each use of the ring property can be replaced by an explicit polynomial.

(i) $y = 2\cos\varphi$ is a root of a monic integer polynomial when $\varphi = \pi p/q$. Let $c_n = 2\cos n\varphi$. From $\cos(n + 1)\varphi + \cos(n - 1)\varphi = 2\cos\varphi\cos n\varphi$ we get $c_{n+1} = y\,c_n - c_{n-1}$, with $c_0 = 2$, $c_1 = y$. By induction $c_n = P_n(y)$ with $P_n$ monic of degree $n$ with integer coefficients. With $\varphi = \theta/2$ and $\theta/\pi = p/q$, $2q\varphi = p\pi$, so $P_{2q}(y) = 2\cos(p\pi) = \pm2$, and $y$ is a root of the monic integer polynomial $P_{2q}(x)\mp2$.

(ii) Shifting. If $y$ is a root of monic integer $P$, then $w = y - 1$ is a root of $P(x + 1)$, still monic with integer coefficients.

(iii) $w = \frac1{\sqrt2}$ is not. Suppose $R(w) = 0$, $R(x) = x^d + r_{d-1}x^{d-1} + \dots + r_0$. Split $R(w)$ into the even powers, which are rational ($w^{2j} = 2^{-j}$), and $w$ times the odd ones, also rational times $w$. Since $\sqrt2$ is irrational, both parts vanish. If $d$ is even, the even part is $2^{-d/2} + \sum_{k\lt d,\ k\text{ even}}r_k2^{-k/2} = 0$; multiply by $2^{d/2}$: $1 + \sum r_k2^{(d-k)/2} = 0$, and every term of the sum is even — so an odd number is zero, a contradiction. If $d$ is odd, do the same with the odd part multiplied by $2^{(d-1)/2}$. So $\theta/\pi$ is irrational, with nothing cited.

Why this matters. If $\theta/\pi$ were rational, $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)$ would have finite order and its powers would be finitely many points. Irrationality is exactly what makes them fill the circle (next slide). The card for this unit planned to cite the irrationality; this argument, the standard one in algebraic number theory, is short enough to give in full.

Powers of an irrational rotation are dense

theorem (pigeonhole)

If $\alpha/\pi$ is irrational, the multiples $k\alpha$, $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots$, are dense in the circle $\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z$.

proof

Distinct: $j\alpha\equiv k\alpha$ with $j\ne k$ would make $\alpha/\pi$ rational. Close: split the circle into $N$ arcs of length $2\pi/N$; among the $N + 1$ points $0, \alpha, \dots, N\alpha$ two share an arc, say $j\alpha$ and $k\alpha$, $j\lt k$. So $\beta = (k - j)\alpha$ is within $2\pi/N$ of $0$, and nonzero. March: the multiples of $\beta$ step round the circle by less than $2\pi/N$, so every arc of length $2\pi/N$ contains one. $N$ is arbitrary. $\square$

Applied: $R_{\mathbf n}(\theta)^k = R_{\mathbf n}(k\theta)$ depends on $k\theta/2$ mod $2\pi$, and $\frac{\theta/2}{\pi}$ is irrational. So the powers are dense among all $R_{\mathbf n}(\varphi)$, since $\lVert R_{\mathbf n}(\varphi) - R_{\mathbf n}(\varphi')\rVert = 2|\sin\frac{\varphi - \varphi'}4|$.

$k\alpha$ for $\alpha = \theta/2 = 31.40^\circ$. Point 11 falls $14.6^\circ$ short of 0, point 12 lands $16.8^\circ$ past it, point 23 only $2.2^\circ$ past.

The march, carefully. Let $\beta$ be the representative of $(k - j)\alpha$ in $(-\pi, \pi]$, so $0\lt|\beta|\lt2\pi/N$. The points $0, \beta, 2\beta, \dots, M\beta$ with $M = \lfloor2\pi/|\beta|\rfloor$ go once round the circle in steps shorter than $2\pi/N$, so every arc of that length contains one of them. Each $m\beta = m(k - j)\alpha$ is a multiple of $\alpha$ — a non-negative one if $\beta$ came from $(k - j)\gt0$, which it did. So every point of the circle is within $2\pi/N$ of some $k\alpha$.

Why the half-angle. In $SU(2)$, $R_{\mathbf n}(\varphi) = \cos\frac\varphi2 I - i\sin\frac\varphi2\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$ has period $4\pi$ in $\varphi$, so the natural circle is $\varphi/2$ mod $2\pi$. Apply the theorem with $\alpha = \theta/2$: $\alpha/\pi = \frac12\cdot\theta/\pi$, irrational. Then for any target $R_{\mathbf n}(\varphi)$ pick $k$ with $k\theta/2$ within $\delta$ of $\varphi/2$ mod $2\pi$; the distance is $2|\sin\frac{k\theta - \varphi}{4}|\le|\frac{k\theta - \varphi}2|\le\delta$.

The figure. $\alpha = 31.3997^\circ$; $11\alpha = 345.40^\circ$, i.e. $-14.60^\circ$; $12\alpha = 376.80^\circ$, i.e. $+16.80^\circ$; $23\alpha = 722.19^\circ$, i.e. $+2.19^\circ$. The pigeonhole proof would find these: they are the best returns so far.

Density is not efficiency. The proof gives no useful bound on $k$. To land within $10^{-11}$ the returns must be that close, and by the pigeonhole count that takes $k$ of order $10^{11}$ powers, each costing two $T$ gates. Powers of one rotation are a proof of reachability, not a way to compute. Section 4 is the efficient way.

History. This is the simplest case of Weyl's equidistribution theorem (1916): the multiples are not only dense but spread evenly. Kronecker (1884) proved the density for several angles at once.

Two axes generate every rotation

lemma

If $\mathbf n, \mathbf m$ are not parallel, every $U\in SU(2)$ is a finite product of rotations about $\mathbf n$ and about $\mathbf m$.

proof, in three moves

1. Axes spread. If all rotations about $\mathbf k$ are products, so are all about $\Phi(R)\mathbf k$ for any product $R$: conjugate.

2. Make a right angle. $R_{\mathbf m}(\pi)$ moves $\mathbf n$ to an axis at twice the angle $a$ from it. Double until $a\in[\frac\pi4, \frac\pi2]$; then as $\gamma$ runs from $0$ to $\pi$, $\Phi(R_{\mathbf m}(\gamma))\mathbf n$ moves from angle $0$ to $2a\ge\frac\pi2$ from $\mathbf n$, so some $\mathbf p\perp\mathbf n$ is an axis.

3. Euler. With $\mathbf n\perp\mathbf p$, unit 02's Z-Y-Z in the frame $(\mathbf p\times\mathbf n, \mathbf p, \mathbf n)$ writes $U = \pm R_{\mathbf n}(\beta)R_{\mathbf p}(\gamma)R_{\mathbf n}(\delta)$, and $-I = R_{\mathbf n}(2\pi)$. $\square$

Turning $\mathbf n$ about $\mathbf m$ sweeps a circle; half-way, at $R_{\mathbf m}(\pi)\mathbf n$, it is twice as far from $\mathbf n$ as $\mathbf m$ is. Here $a = 24^\circ$: double again.

Full proof

Let $K$ be the group of finite products of rotations about $\mathbf n$ and $\mathbf m$. Call $\mathbf k$ an axis of $K$ if every $R_{\mathbf k}(\varphi)$ lies in $K$.

Move 1. For $R\in K$ and an axis $\mathbf k$: $RR_{\mathbf k}(\varphi)R^\dagger = R_{\Phi(R)\mathbf k}(\varphi)$ (the conjugation rule in the notes of A second axis, from conjugating by H), a product of elements of $K$. So $\Phi(R)\mathbf k$ is an axis.

Move 2. Since $R_{-\mathbf m}(\varphi) = R_{\mathbf m}(-\varphi)$, $-\mathbf m$ is an axis too; replace $\mathbf m$ by $-\mathbf m$ if needed so that the angle $a$ between $\mathbf n$ and $\mathbf m$ lies in $(0, \frac\pi2]$ ($a\ne0$ as they are not parallel). The half-turn about $\mathbf m$ sends $\mathbf n$ to $\mathbf n' = 2(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf m)\mathbf m - \mathbf n$, and $\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf n' = 2\cos^2a - 1 = \cos2a$: the angle is $2a$. If $a\lt\frac\pi4$, replace $\mathbf m$ by $\mathbf n'$, an axis by move 1, at angle $2a\in(0, \frac\pi2)$ from $\mathbf n$. After finitely many doublings $a\in[\frac\pi4, \frac\pi2]$. Now $f(\gamma) = \mathbf n\cdot\Phi(R_{\mathbf m}(\gamma))\mathbf n$ is continuous, $f(0) = 1$, $f(\pi) = \cos2a\le0$; by the intermediate value theorem $f(\gamma_0) = 0$ for some $\gamma_0$, and $\mathbf p = \Phi(R_{\mathbf m}(\gamma_0))\mathbf n$ is an axis perpendicular to $\mathbf n$.

Move 3. $(\mathbf p\times\mathbf n, \mathbf p, \mathbf n)$ is a right-handed orthonormal frame, so there is $Q\in SO(3)$ with $Q\mathbf e_x = \mathbf p\times\mathbf n$, $Q\mathbf e_y = \mathbf p$, $Q\mathbf e_z = \mathbf n$; $\Phi$ is onto (unit 02), so $Q = \Phi(V)$ for some $V\in SU(2)$. Then $VR_z(\varphi)V^\dagger = R_{\mathbf n}(\varphi)$ and $VR_y(\varphi)V^\dagger = R_{\mathbf p}(\varphi)$. Given $U\in SU(2)$, apply unit 02's Z-Y-Z to $V^\dagger UV$: it is $e^{i\alpha}R_z(\beta)R_y(\gamma)R_z(\delta)$, and taking determinants $e^{2i\alpha} = 1$, so $e^{i\alpha} = \pm1$. Conjugating back, $U = \pm R_{\mathbf n}(\beta)R_{\mathbf p}(\gamma)R_{\mathbf n}(\delta)\in K$. $\square$

Citation, for comparison. Nielsen and Chuang, Exercise 4.11, states that a bounded-length product $R_{\mathbf n}(\beta_1)R_{\mathbf m}(\gamma_1)R_{\mathbf n}(\beta_2)\cdots$ suffices; the proof above gives that too, since each doubling and the final perpendicular axis use a fixed number of factors.

H and T are approximately universal

theorem (Boykin et al. 2000)

(i) $\langle\tilde H, \tilde T\rangle$ is dense in $SU(2)$; so words in $H$ and $T$ approximate every single-qubit gate up to phase.

(ii) $\{H, T, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$ is approximately universal.

proof

(i) $\bar G$ is a closed subgroup containing every rotation about $\mathbf n$ and about $\mathbf m$ (previous slides), hence by the lemma every product of them: all of $SU(2)$.

(ii) Unit 10: any $U$ on $n$ qubits is exactly a circuit of $g$ CNOTs and single-qubit gates. Approximate each single-qubit gate within $\varepsilon/g$ up to phase, by (i). Errors add: total within $\varepsilon$. $\square$

the running example, reachable

So the rotation $R_z(\theta)$ can be met to $10^{-11}$ by some word in $H$ and $T$. Nothing so far says how long the word is.

two cautions

Up to phase, gate by gate — fine on wires, as the global-phase slide showed, because unit 10's exact construction has already turned every controlled gate into CNOTs and single-qubit gates.

Density says nothing about length. The powers of one rotation need $\sim10^{11}$ steps for $10^{-11}$. Section 4 asks how short a word can be.

$S = T^2$, so the menu $\{H, S, T, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$ is universal too.

(i) in detail. $\bar G$ contains every $R_{\mathbf n}(\varphi)$ and $R_{\mathbf m}(\varphi)$ and is a group, so it contains $K$ of the lemma, which is $SU(2)$. A closed subset of $SU(2)$ containing $G$ is all of $SU(2)$ exactly when $G$ is dense: that is the definition of the closure. Up to phase for $U(2)$ by the notes of Dense subgroups, and the plan.

(ii) in detail. Unit 10's circuit is $U = W_g\cdots W_1$ with each $W_k$ a CNOT or a single-qubit gate on some wire. Replace each single-qubit $W_k$ by a word $W_k'$ in $H, T$ with $d(W_k, W_k')\le\varepsilon/g$, and keep each CNOT. By worked example 1's argument, $d(U, W_g'\cdots W_1')\le g\cdot\varepsilon/g = \varepsilon$.

Other universal pairs. Nothing was special about $H$ and $T$ except that they produce two non-parallel irrational rotations. Any single-qubit gate whose angle is an irrational multiple of $\pi$, together with one that moves its axis, would do; so would almost any pair (problem P5 finds a third irrational rotation in $T\cdot HT^\dagger H$). What makes $H$ and $T$ the standard choice is section 3 and unit 24: they are the gates an error-corrected machine can provide.

What unit 12 takes from this. BQP will be defined with some gate set. By (ii) and section 4's efficiency, changing the gate set changes circuit size only by a polylogarithmic factor, so the class does not depend on the choice.

11 · Clifford

Paulis, and the gates that permute them

the Pauli group on n qubits $$\mathcal P_n = \{\,i^kP_1\otimes\cdots\otimes P_n\,\},$$

with $k\in\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ and each $P_j\in\{I, X, Y, Z\}$. A group under multiplication, of order $4\cdot4^n$: Pauli products multiply to Paulis times $\pm1$ or $\pm i$ (unit 02's $XY = iZ$ and cyclic).

the Clifford group

The unitaries $U$ on $n$ qubits with $U\mathcal P_nU^\dagger = \mathcal P_n$ — the normaliser of $\mathcal P_n$ in the unitary group — taken up to global phase.

lemma: check the generators

$U$ is Clifford iff $UX_jU^\dagger$ and $UZ_jU^\dagger$ are in $\mathcal P_n$ for each qubit $j$ ($X_j$: $X$ on qubit $j$, $I$ elsewhere).

proof

$P\mapsto UPU^\dagger$ is multiplicative: $UPQU^\dagger = (UPU^\dagger)(UQU^\dagger)$. The $X_j$, $Z_j$ and $iI$ generate $\mathcal P_n$ ($Y = iXZ$), and $iI$ is fixed. So the image of $\mathcal P_n$ lies in $\mathcal P_n$, and a conjugation is injective on a finite set, hence onto. $\square$

The menu splits. $H$, $S$, CNOT are Clifford (next two slides). $T$ is not. Gottesman (1998), cited: $H$, $S$, CNOT generate the whole Clifford group on $n$ qubits.

The order of $\mathcal P_n$. Each element is uniquely a phase $i^k$ ($4$ choices) times a tensor product of $n$ letters ($4^n$ choices): uniqueness because the $4^n$ tensor products are a basis of all $2^n\times2^n$ matrices (unit 02's basis $\{I, X, Y, Z\}$ tensored up, unit 03). Closure: $(P_1\otimes\cdots)(Q_1\otimes\cdots) = P_1Q_1\otimes\cdots$ and each $P_jQ_j$ is a Pauli times a power of $i$.

Why "up to phase". $e^{i\alpha}U$ conjugates exactly as $U$ does, so the condition cannot see phases, and the Clifford group proper (with all phases) is infinite. Modulo phases it is finite: a Clifford is determined up to phase by where it sends the $2n$ generators — if $U$ and $U'$ agree there, $U'^\dagger U$ commutes with every Pauli, and the Paulis span all matrices, so it is a scalar — and there are finitely many choices (problem P8 counts $n = 1$). For $n = 1$ the count is 24.

Name and history. The connection to Clifford algebras is through the Pauli matrices' anticommutation; the group's role in quantum computing is Gottesman's (The Heisenberg representation of quantum computers, 1998, arXiv:quant-ph/9807006), who proved the generation statement cited here. Its proof, by induction on $n$ using the symplectic linear algebra of unit 23, is not reproduced.

Forward pointers, not arguments. Unit 23 proves that circuits of Clifford gates on basis states, measured in the computational basis, can be simulated efficiently on a classical computer (Gottesman–Knill). Unit 24 shows why Clifford gates are cheap in an error-corrected machine and $T$ is not.

Worked: H and S permute the Paulis; T does not

$\pm x, \pm y, \pm z$: the Bloch vectors of unit 00's three bases. $H$ and $S$ permute them; $T$ sends $+x$ off the set.

worked example 4 · conjugation tables

$H$. $HXH = \frac12(X + Z)X(X + Z) = \frac12(X + Z + ZXX + ZXZ)$. Now $ZXZ = -X$, so this is $\frac12(X + Z + Z - X) = Z$. Likewise $HZH = X$, and $HYH = -Y$ (as $Y = iXZ$).

$S$. $S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i)$: $SXS^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = Y$, $\ SYS^\dagger = -X$, $\ SZS^\dagger = Z$.

$T$. $T = \mathrm{diag}(1, \omega)$, $\omega = e^{i\pi/4}$:

$$TXT^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}0&\bar\omega\\ \omega&0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{X + Y}{\sqrt2},$$

not $\pm$ or $\pm i$ times a Pauli. $T$ is not Clifford.

Check: conjugation preserves squares, and $\big(\frac{X + Y}{\sqrt2}\big)^2 = \frac{2I + XY + YX}{2} = I$ ✓.

$H$ in full. $(X + Z)X(X + Z) = (X + Z)(I + XZ) = X + X^2Z + Z + ZXZ = X + Z + Z + ZXZ$. And $ZXZ = -XZZ = -X$ by anticommutation, so the bracket is $2Z$ and $HXH = Z$. Since $H^2 = I$, $HZH = H(HXH)H = X$. Then $HYH = H(iXZ)H = i(HXH)(HZH) = iZX = i\cdot iY = -Y$.

$S$ in full. $SXS^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = Y$. $SZS^\dagger = Z$ as both are diagonal. $SYS^\dagger = S(iXZ)S^\dagger = i(SXS^\dagger)(SZS^\dagger) = iYZ = i\cdot iX = -X$.

$T$ in full. $TXT^\dagger$ has off-diagonal entries $1\cdot\bar\omega$ (top right) and $\omega\cdot1$ (bottom left). Write $\omega = \frac{1 + i}{\sqrt2}$: the matrix is $\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1 - i\\1 + i&0\end{pmatrix} = \frac1{\sqrt2}(X + Y)$. The Paulis times phases are $\pm I, \pm iI, \pm X, \dots$, and $\frac1{\sqrt2}(X + Y)$ is none of them: in the basis $\{I, X, Y, Z\}$ its coordinates are $(0, \frac1{\sqrt2}, \frac1{\sqrt2}, 0)$, two nonzero.

On the sphere. By unit 02, $U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger = (\Phi(U)\mathbf r)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, so these tables are the rotations themselves: $\Phi(H)$ swaps $\pm x$ with $\pm z$ and reverses $y$ (the half-turn about $(x + z)/\sqrt2$); $\Phi(S)$ is the quarter-turn about $z$; $\Phi(T)$ is the eighth-turn about $z$, which takes $+x$ to $(x + y)/\sqrt2$ — a point that is not a vertex of the octahedron.

proof, first row

On a basis state, $\mathrm{CNOT}(X\otimes I)\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{a, b}$: CNOT gives $\ket{a, b\oplus a}$; $X\otimes I$ gives $\ket{a\oplus1, b\oplus a}$; CNOT gives $\ket{a\oplus1, b\oplus a\oplus a\oplus1} = \ket{a\oplus1, b\oplus1} = (X\otimes X)\ket{a, b}$ ✓. CNOT is its own inverse.

Rows 1 and 4 as circuits, qubit 0 (the control) on top: a Pauli pushed through a CNOT comes out a Pauli, possibly on both wires.

All four images are Paulis, so CNOT is Clifford by the lemma. Check: $X\otimes X$ and $Z\otimes Z$ commute, as $X\otimes I$ and $I\otimes Z$ do — conjugation preserves commutation ✓.

Full proof of the table

CNOT acts as $\ket{a, b}\mapsto\ket{a, a\oplus b}$ (unit 10, control first). Row 2: $X$ on the target commutes with "add $a$ to $b$", since both are additions mod 2 on $b$; so $\mathrm{CNOT}(I\otimes X)\mathrm{CNOT} = I\otimes X$. Row 3: $Z\otimes I$ is diagonal, $(-1)^a$, and CNOT does not change $a$, so it commutes. Row 4: $(I\otimes Z)\ket{a, b} = (-1)^b\ket{a, b}$. Conjugating: CNOT sends $\ket{a, b}$ to $\ket{a, a\oplus b}$, the phase there is $(-1)^{a\oplus b} = (-1)^a(-1)^b$, and CNOT returns $\ket{a, b}$. So the result is $(-1)^a(-1)^b$, which is $Z\otimes Z$. Row 1 is on the slide.

Why this matters later. The table is the whole of how errors spread through a CNOT: a bit flip on the control before the gate becomes bit flips on both after; a phase flip on the target becomes phase flips on both. Unit 22 builds codes against exactly these, and unit 24's fault-tolerant circuits are designed so that one such spreading never turns one error into two in the same code block.

The commutation check. If $PQ = QP$ then $UPU^\dagger UQU^\dagger = UPQU^\dagger = UQPU^\dagger$, so images of commuting Paulis commute; and anticommuting pairs stay anticommuting. $X\otimes I$ and $I\otimes Z$ commute (different qubits); their images $X\otimes X$ and $Z\otimes Z$ commute because $XZ = -ZX$ twice gives $+$. Twenty-four gates: the rotations of a cube

theorem

Up to phase, the one-qubit Clifford group has exactly 24 elements, and $\Phi$ maps it onto the rotation group of the cube with faces centred on $\pm x, \pm y, \pm z$.

proof

At most 24. A Clifford $U$ sends $X$ to a Hermitian Pauli of square $I$ other than $\pm I$: one of $\pm X, \pm Y, \pm Z$, so $\Phi(U)$ sends $e_x$ to one of six vertices. $e_z$ must go to one of the four perpendicular to that. Then $e_y = e_z\times e_x$ is fixed: $6\times4 = 24$, and $\Phi$ is injective up to sign (unit 02).

At least 24. Under $\langle H, S\rangle$ the orbit of $+z$ is all six vertices ($H$: $z\to x$; $S$: $x\to y$; $S^2$, then $H$, give the negatives), and the stabiliser of $+z$ contains $S^0, S, S^2, S^3$. Orbit–stabiliser: $\ge6\times4$. $\square$

The 24, by axis: identity $1$; face axes $3\times3 = 9$ ($S$ is one); vertex axes $4\times2 = 8$; edge axes $6\times1 = 6$ ($H$ is one). $1 + 9 + 8 + 6 = 24$.

Full proof

The images of $X$. $UXU^\dagger$ is Hermitian, squares to $I$, and has trace $0$ (trace is invariant under conjugation). The elements of $\mathcal P_1$ with these properties are exactly $\pm X, \pm Y, \pm Z$: $\pm iP$ is not Hermitian and $\pm I$ has trace $\pm2$. Same for $Z$. By unit 02, $U(\mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)U^\dagger = (\Phi(U)\mathbf r)\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$, so $\Phi(U)$ maps $\{\pm e_x, \pm e_y, \pm e_z\}$ to itself. $\Phi(U)$ is a rotation, so $\Phi(U)e_z\perp\Phi(U)e_x$, and $\Phi(U)e_y = \Phi(U)(e_z\times e_x) = \Phi(U)e_z\times\Phi(U)e_x$. Hence at most $6\cdot4$ rotations, and since $\Phi(U) = \Phi(U')$ forces $U' = \pm U$, at most 24 Cliffords up to phase. Conversely any rotation permuting the six vertices is a symmetry of the octahedron they span, equivalently of the dual cube.

The lower bound. $\Phi(H)$: $e_z\mapsto e_x$, $e_x\mapsto e_z$, $e_y\mapsto-e_y$. $\Phi(S)$: $e_x\mapsto e_y\mapsto-e_x$, $e_z$ fixed. The orbit of $e_z$: $e_z\xrightarrow{H}e_x\xrightarrow{S}e_y$, $e_x\xrightarrow{S^2}-e_x\xrightarrow{H}-e_z$, $e_y\xrightarrow{S^2}-e_y$. Six points. The rotations fixing $e_z$ within the group include the four powers of $\Phi(S)$, distinct. The orbit–stabiliser theorem gives order $=$ orbit $\times$ stabiliser $\ge24$: $g\,\mathrm{Stab}\mapsto g\,e_z$ is a well-defined bijection from the cosets of the stabiliser to the orbit, so by Lagrange the group's order is the orbit's size times the stabiliser's. With the upper bound, exactly 24, and $\langle H, S\rangle$ is the whole one-qubit Clifford group up to phase.

The cube's census. Face axes: 3 (through $\pm x$, $\pm y$, $\pm z$), turns of $90^\circ, 180^\circ, 270^\circ$: $S$, $Z$, $S^\dagger$ about $z$. Vertex axes: 4 (the diagonals), turns of $120^\circ, 240^\circ$: $SH$ is one, as unit 02's checkpoint notes found (a third of a turn about $-(1, 1, 1)/\sqrt3$). Edge axes: 6 (through midpoints of opposite edges, along $(x \pm z)/\sqrt2$ and the like), half-turns: $H$ is the one along $(x + z)/\sqrt2$.

The running example. $R_z(\theta)$ permutes the six vertices only if $\theta$ is a multiple of $\frac\pi2$. For any other $\theta$ — the running example's — it is not Clifford, and no Clifford circuit, of any length, comes near it: the Cliffords are 24 isolated points.

Clifford + T: the standard universal set

Clifford + T

The gate set $\{H, S, \mathrm{CNOT}, T\}$ — Clifford generators plus one non-Clifford gate. Approximately universal, since $\{H, T, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$ already is.

why the cost is counted in T gates

Without $T$, circuits on one qubit give 24 operations. So every approximation of a non-Clifford rotation must use $T$, and the more accurate it is, the more $T$s it needs (section 5).

the T-count

The number of $T$ and $T^\dagger$ gates in a Clifford + $T$ circuit. Unit 10's Toffoli has T-count 7.

forward pointers, not arguments

Unit 23: Clifford circuits on basis states can be simulated classically in polynomial time (Gottesman–Knill). So the quantum power is in the $T$ gates.

Unit 24: in an error-corrected machine, Clifford gates are cheap and each $T$ is made by consuming a specially prepared state, at a large cost in qubits and time.

the running example's question, sharpened

One rotation, accuracy $10^{-11}$: what is the least T-count of a Clifford + $T$ circuit that meets it? Cliffords are free; $T$s are what we count.

Why $T$ and not another non-Clifford gate. $T$ is the simplest: $T^2 = S$ is Clifford, so $T$ sits just one step outside the group, and in unit 24's scheme the state needed to perform it is easy to prepare and to purify. Section 2 showed directly that it suffices.

T-count versus gate count. Clifford gates are not literally free in hardware, but in the error-corrected setting each $T$ costs far more than a Clifford (unit 24 gives the numbers), so the T-count is the figure of merit used in the literature, and Cliffords are counted as free.

Unit 10's Toffoli, revisited. With an extra qubit and a measurement the Toffoli's T-count can be lowered to four (Jones, Phys. Rev. A 87, 022328, 2013, cited). Either way, exact constructions like this one cost a fixed number of $T$s. The running example's rotation cannot be made exactly, and its cost will depend on $\varepsilon$.

Solovay–Kitaev

Density says a word exists. Solovay and Kitaev say a short one exists and can be found quickly: a word of length polylogarithmic in $1/\varepsilon$, built by a recursion that squares the error away.

11 · Solovay–Kitaev

How long must the word be?

At most $2^L$ words of length $L$: the count doubles per letter.

A lower bound, by counting. $SU(2)$ is three-dimensional (unit 02: a 3-sphere), so covering it with balls of radius $\varepsilon$ takes about $\varepsilon^{-3}$ of them. With at most $2^{L+1}$ words of length $\le L$: $\ L\ge3\log_2(1/\varepsilon) - O(1)$. Problem P12.

the Solovay–Kitaev theorem (cited analysis)

Let $\mathcal G\subset SU(2)$ be finite, closed under inverses, generating a dense subgroup. Then every $U\in SU(2)$ is within $\varepsilon$ of a word of length

$$O\big(\log^c(1/\varepsilon)\big),\qquad c = \frac{\log5}{\log(3/2)}\approx3.97,$$

found in time $O(\log^{2.71}(1/\varepsilon))$ (Dawson and Nielsen's version).

Brute force would search $2^L$ words — exponential in $L$. Solovay–Kitaev builds the word by a recursion instead. Its engine, the commutator step, is proved on the next four slides; the recursion's analysis is cited.

Sources. Kitaev, Russian Math. Surveys 52, 1191 (1997); Solovay, unpublished manuscript (1995), announced on a mailing list; Dawson and Nielsen, "The Solovay–Kitaev algorithm", Quantum Inf. Comput. 6, 81 (2006), arXiv:quant-ph/0505030, which is the clearest account, gives the algorithm in a few lines of pseudocode and proves the length $O(\log^{3.97}(1/\varepsilon))$ and running time $O(\log^{2.71}(1/\varepsilon))$. Nielsen and Chuang, Appendix 3, proves a slightly weaker version.

The two exponents. The recursion makes five recursive calls' worth of length per level and three recursive calls per level (two of the five are inverses of words already found), while the accuracy goes from $\varepsilon$ to $\varepsilon^{3/2}$. So length $\propto5^k$ and time $\propto3^k$ with $(3/2)^k\propto\log(1/\varepsilon)$: $c = \log5/\log\frac32 = 3.969$ and $\log3/\log\frac32 = 2.710$. Derived on The recursion.

Closed under inverses is needed because the commutator $VWV^\dagger W^\dagger$ uses $V^\dagger$ and $W^\dagger$. For Clifford + $T$ this is automatic: $H^\dagger = H$, $S^\dagger = S^3$, $T^\dagger = T^7$.

Multi-qubit gates. The theorem holds for $SU(d)$ for any fixed $d$, with the same exponent. Since unit 10 reduces everything to CNOTs and single-qubit gates, $d = 2$ is all this course needs.

Group commutators: a loop that fails to close

Seen from above the north pole: a point moved by $R_y(-\varphi)$, $R_x(-\varphi)$, $R_y(\varphi)$, $R_x(\varphi)$, with $\varphi = 0.6$. The loop misses by a turn of $20.0^\circ$ about an axis near $z$.

the group commutator $$[\![V, W]\!] = VWV^\dagger W^\dagger .$$

"Do $W^{-1}$, then $V^{-1}$, then $W$, then $V$." It is $I$ exactly when $V$ and $W$ commute.

near the identity

For $V = e^{A}$, $W = e^{B}$ with $A, B$ small, $\ VWV^\dagger W^\dagger = I + [A, B] + O(\lVert A\rVert^3 + \lVert B\rVert^3)$.

For rotations: $A = -\frac{i\varphi}2X$, $B = -\frac{i\varphi}2Y$ give $[A, B] = -\frac{\varphi^2}4[X, Y] = -\frac{i\varphi^2}2Z$: a rotation by $\approx\varphi^2$ about $z$.

The point: two rotations by $\varphi$ make a rotation by $\varphi^2$. To make a small rotation, use two much less small ones.

The expansion

Expand to second order: $V = I + A + \frac12A^2 + O(\lVert A\rVert^3)$, $V^\dagger = e^{-A} = I - A + \frac12A^2 + \dots$ (for anti-Hermitian $A$, $V^\dagger = e^{A^\dagger} = e^{-A}$), and likewise for $W$. Multiply the four and keep terms of total degree at most two. Degree one: $A + B - A - B = 0$. Degree two: the squares give $\frac12A^2 + \frac12B^2 + \frac12A^2 + \frac12B^2$; the cross terms, taking factors in order $V, W, V^\dagger, W^\dagger$, give $AB - A^2 - AB - BA - B^2 + AB$. The sum is $AB - BA = [A, B]$. So $VWV^\dagger W^\dagger = I + [A, B] + O(\text{cubic})$.

The exact answer, for comparison. Problem P11 multiplies it out exactly: for $V = R_x(\varphi)$, $W = R_y(\varphi)$ the commutator is a rotation by $\vartheta$ with $\cos\frac\vartheta2 = \cos\varphi + \frac12\sin^2\varphi$, about the axis $(s, -s, c)/\sqrt{1 + s^2}$ where $s = \sin\frac\varphi2$, $c = \cos\frac\varphi2$. At $\varphi = 0.6$: $\vartheta = 0.3498$ rad $= 20.04^\circ$, against $\varphi^2 = 0.36$ rad $= 20.63^\circ$; the axis is $23.6^\circ$ from $z$.

The figure. The starting point is $(-0.339, -0.339, 0.878)$; the legs are computed exactly, each an arc of a small circle about $y$ or $x$. Read in time order, $W^\dagger$ acts first. It lands at $(-0.283, -0.524, 0.803)$. The miss is the commutator's rotation acting on the start: what "the loop fails to close" means.

Why this is the engine. Approximating $V$ and $W$ each to accuracy $\delta$ disturbs the commutator by only about $\delta\cdot\varphi$, not $\delta$: the errors in $V$ and in $V^\dagger$ nearly cancel, because they enter with opposite signs. With $\varphi\sim\sqrt\varepsilon$ that is $\delta\sqrt\varepsilon$. Error squaring makes this exact.

The balanced-commutator lemma

lemma (balanced commutator)

If $U\in SU(2)$ and $\varepsilon = \lVert U - I\rVert\le1$, then $U = VWV^\dagger W^\dagger$ for some $V, W\in SU(2)$ with

$$\lVert V - I\rVert = \lVert W - I\rVert\le0.92\sqrt\varepsilon .$$
proof

$U = R_{\mathbf n}(\vartheta)$ with $2\sin\frac\vartheta4 = \varepsilon\le1$, so $\vartheta\le\frac{2\pi}3$. The commutator angle $g(\varphi)$, $\cos\frac{g}{2} = \cos\varphi + \frac12\sin^2\varphi$, is continuous with $g(0) = 0$ and $g(\frac\pi2) = \frac{2\pi}3$; pick $\varphi\in[0, \frac\pi2]$ with $g(\varphi) = \vartheta$ (intermediate values). Rotate the commutator's axis onto $\mathbf n$, as in the worked example. The bound on $2\sin\frac\varphi4$ is in the notes.

what the lemma says

Anything within $\varepsilon$ of $I$ is a commutator of two things within about $\sqrt\varepsilon$ of $I$. Going from $U$ to $V, W$ loses a square root of accuracy — which looks like the wrong direction. The next slide shows it is the right one.

Check against the worked example: $\varepsilon = 0.0100$, bound $0.92\sqrt{0.01} = 0.092$; actual $0.0708$ ✓.

Check, $\varepsilon = 0$: $\varphi = 0$, $V = W = I$ ✓.

Full proof of the bound

Let $s = \sin\frac\varphi2$, $c = \cos\frac\varphi2\ge\cos\frac\pi4 = \frac1{\sqrt2}$ for $\varphi\le\frac\pi2$. By problem P11, $\sin\frac\vartheta2 = 2s^2c\sqrt{1 + s^2}\ge2s^2c\ge\sqrt2s^2$. On the other side $\sin\frac\vartheta2 = 2\sin\frac\vartheta4\cos\frac\vartheta4\le2\sin\frac\vartheta4 = \varepsilon$. So $s^2\le\varepsilon/\sqrt2$. Finally $s = 2\sin\frac\varphi4\cos\frac\varphi4\ge2\sin\frac\varphi4\cos\frac\pi8$, so

$$\lVert V - I\rVert = 2\sin\tfrac\varphi4\le\frac{s}{\cos(\pi/8)}\le\frac{2^{-1/4}}{\cos(\pi/8)}\sqrt\varepsilon = 0.910\sqrt\varepsilon .$$

The endpoint values. $g(0)$: $\cos0 + 0 = 1$, so $g = 0$. $g(\frac\pi2)$: $\cos\frac\pi2 + \frac12 = \frac12$, so $\frac g2 = \frac\pi3$. And $\varepsilon = 2\sin\frac\vartheta4\le1$ iff $\vartheta\le\frac{2\pi}3$: the hypothesis $\varepsilon\le1$ was chosen to fit.

Aligning the axis, in general. The commutator is $R_{\mathbf k}(\vartheta)$; choose $Q\in SU(2)$ with $\Phi(Q)\mathbf k = \mathbf n$ ($\Phi$ is onto, and some rotation carries any unit vector to any other). Then $V' = QVQ^\dagger$, $W' = QWQ^\dagger$ have commutator $QR_{\mathbf k}(\vartheta)Q^\dagger = R_{\mathbf n}(\vartheta) = U$, and $\lVert V' - I\rVert = \lVert Q(V - I)Q^\dagger\rVert = \lVert V - I\rVert$.

Every $U$ with $\lVert U - I\rVert\le1$ is some $R_{\mathbf n}(\vartheta)$ with $0\le\vartheta\le2\pi$: unit 02 showed every element of $SU(2)$ is a rotation operator, and replacing $(\mathbf n, \vartheta)$ by $(-\mathbf n, 4\pi - \vartheta)$ if needed puts $\vartheta$ in $[0, 2\pi]$, where the slide on a rotation's distance gives $\lVert U - I\rVert = 2\sin\frac\vartheta4$.

Error squaring

lemma (error squaring)

Let $V, W, \tilde V, \tilde W$ be unitary with $\lVert V - I\rVert, \lVert W - I\rVert\le r$ and $\lVert\tilde V - V\rVert, \lVert\tilde W - W\rVert\le\delta$. Write $f = VWV^\dagger W^\dagger$ and $\tilde f = \tilde V\tilde W\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger$. Then

$$\lVert\tilde f - f\rVert\le4r\delta + 4r^2\delta + 2\delta^2 .$$

With $r = \sqrt\varepsilon$ and $\delta = \varepsilon$ (both $\le1$): $\ \le4\varepsilon^{3/2} + 6\varepsilon^2\le10\,\varepsilon^{3/2}$.

Approximating $V$ and $W$ to $\varepsilon$ gives their commutator to $10\varepsilon^{3/2}$ — better than $\varepsilon$ once $\varepsilon\lt0.01$.

proof

$f - I = (VW - WV)V^\dagger W^\dagger$, and $VW - WV = [a, b]$ with $a = V - I$, $b = W - I$. With $\tilde a = a + D$, $\tilde b = b + E$:

$$[\tilde a, \tilde b] - [a, b] = [D, b] + [a, E] + [D, E],$$

of norm $\le2\delta r + 2r\delta + 2\delta^2$. So

$$\begin{aligned}\tilde f - f &= \big([\tilde a, \tilde b] - [a, b]\big)\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger\\ &\quad + [a, b]\big(\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger - V^\dagger W^\dagger\big),\end{aligned}$$

and $\lVert[a, b]\rVert\le2r^2$, $\ \lVert\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger - V^\dagger W^\dagger\rVert\le2\delta$ (errors add). $\square$

Full proof, every step

$VWV^\dagger W^\dagger - I = (VW - WV)V^\dagger W^\dagger$ because $WVV^\dagger W^\dagger = I$. And $VW - WV = (I + a)(I + b) - (I + b)(I + a) = ab - ba$. The commutator is bilinear, so $[a + D, b + E] = [a, b] + [D, b] + [a, E] + [D, E]$, and $\lVert[P, Q]\rVert\le\lVert PQ\rVert + \lVert QP\rVert\le2\lVert P\rVert\lVert Q\rVert$. Subtract $f = I + [a, b]V^\dagger W^\dagger$ from $\tilde f = I + [\tilde a, \tilde b]\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger$ and add and subtract $[a, b]\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger$: that is the displayed identity. The first term has norm $\le4r\delta + 2\delta^2$ (the unitary $\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger$ does not change norms); the second $\le2r^2\cdot2\delta$, using the theorem that errors add for $\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger$ against $V^\dagger W^\dagger$ and $\lVert\tilde V^\dagger - V^\dagger\rVert = \lVert\tilde V - V\rVert$.

The $\varepsilon$ form. $4\sqrt\varepsilon\cdot\varepsilon + 4\varepsilon\cdot\varepsilon + 2\varepsilon^2 = 4\varepsilon^{3/2} + 6\varepsilon^2$, and $\varepsilon^2\le\varepsilon^{3/2}$ for $\varepsilon\le1$. The constant 10 is crude; Dawson and Nielsen get a smaller one by a sharper expansion. Any constant gives the same exponent $c$.

Why "squaring". In terms of $\eta = 100\varepsilon$ the step reads $\eta' \le \eta^{3/2}$: if $\eta\lt1$ the accuracy improves super-exponentially in the number of steps, like Newton's method. The exponent is $\frac32$ rather than $2$; the name is traditional.

The sanity check. At $\varepsilon = 0.01$ the bound is $4\times10^{-3} + 6\times10^{-4} = 0.0046$, under $10\varepsilon^{3/2} = 0.01$: the step breaks even at $\varepsilon = 0.01$ with this constant, and gains below it. Hence the base case's requirement $\varepsilon_0\lt0.01$ on the next slide.

The recursion

One level of the recursion: $U_k = \tilde V\tilde W\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger U_{k-1}$.

the algorithm (Dawson–Nielsen)

Level 0: the nearest word from a table good to $\varepsilon_0\lt0.01$. Level $k$: find $U_{k-1}$; the error $\Delta = UU_{k-1}^\dagger$ is within $\varepsilon_{k-1}$ of $I$; write $\Delta = VWV^\dagger W^\dagger$ balanced; find $\tilde V, \tilde W$ at level $k - 1$; return $\tilde V\tilde W\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger U_{k-1}$.

$$\varepsilon_k\le10\,\varepsilon_{k-1}^{3/2},\qquad l_k = 5\,l_{k-1}.$$

The exponent. With $\eta_k = 100\varepsilon_k$: $\eta_k\le\eta_0^{(3/2)^k}$. Reaching $\varepsilon$ needs $(3/2)^k\approx\log(1/\varepsilon)$, so length $5^k = \big((3/2)^k\big)^{\log5/\log(3/2)}\approx\log^{3.97}(1/\varepsilon)$. Problem P10.

Full proof of the error recursion

Suppose level $k - 1$ approximates every element of $SU(2)$ to within $\varepsilon_{k-1}\le\varepsilon_0\lt0.01$. Then $\lVert\Delta - I\rVert = \lVert U - U_{k-1}\rVert\le\varepsilon_{k-1}$ by unitary invariance, so by the balanced-commutator lemma $\Delta = VWV^\dagger W^\dagger$ with $\lVert V - I\rVert, \lVert W - I\rVert\le\sqrt{\varepsilon_{k-1}}$. Level $k - 1$ gives $\tilde V, \tilde W$ within $\varepsilon_{k-1}$ of $V, W$. Then $\lVert U_k - U\rVert = \lVert\tilde V\tilde W\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger U_{k-1} - \Delta U_{k-1}\rVert = \lVert\tilde V\tilde W\tilde V^\dagger\tilde W^\dagger - \Delta\rVert\le10\varepsilon_{k-1}^{3/2}$ by error squaring. $U_k$ is a product of five words of length $l_{k-1}$, two of them inverses (reversed words of inverse gates), so $l_k = 5l_{k-1}$.

The table exists. The open $\varepsilon_0$-balls around all words cover $SU(2)$, by density; $SU(2)$ is compact (a closed bounded subset of $\mathbb C^4$), so finitely many suffice; let $l_0$ be the longest. This is the only place density is used.

Solving the recursion. Multiply $\varepsilon_k\le10\varepsilon_{k-1}^{3/2}$ by 100: $100\varepsilon_k\le1000\varepsilon_{k-1}^{3/2} = (100\varepsilon_{k-1})^{3/2}$. So $\eta_k\le\eta_{k-1}^{3/2}$ and $\eta_k\le\eta_0^{(3/2)^k}$. To get $\varepsilon_k\le\varepsilon$ it suffices that $(3/2)^k\ge\frac{\ln(1/(100\varepsilon))}{\ln(1/\eta_0)}$. Then $l_k = l_05^k = l_0\big((3/2)^k\big)^{c}$ with $c = \frac{\ln5}{\ln1.5} = 3.969$, and taking the least such $k$ makes this $O(\log^c(1/\varepsilon))$, the constants depending on $\eta_0$ and $l_0$. That is the theorem's length bound. The running-time bound and Dawson and Nielsen's sharper constants are cited.

section 5

Exact synthesis, and the price

Clifford + $T$ circuits have entries in one ring. That arithmetic says which gates are exact, and turns approximation into number theory — at the optimal cost of $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ $T$ gates.

11 · exact synthesis

Which unitaries Clifford + T makes exactly

the ring Z[1/√2, i]

All $\sum_{j,k}a_{jk}\,2^{-j/2}i^k$ with $a_{jk}\in\mathbb Z$: the smallest ring containing $\frac1{\sqrt2}$ and $i$. It contains $\omega = e^{i\pi/4} = \frac{1 + i}{\sqrt2}$.

easy half: entries stay in the ring

Every entry of $H$, $S$, $T$ and CNOT lies in $\mathbb Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}, i]$, and matrix products only add and multiply entries. So every Clifford + $T$ circuit has its entries there. $\square$

hard half (Kliuchnikov, Maslov, Mosca 2013, cited)

Conversely, every single-qubit unitary with entries in $\mathbb Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}, i]$ is exactly a Clifford + $T$ circuit — with the fewest $T$s, found efficiently. This is exact synthesis.

Example, in the ring: $HT = \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&\omega\\1&-\omega\end{pmatrix}$ ✓. And $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\omega = \frac{1 + i}{2}$.

Example, not in the ring: $R_z(\frac\pi{16})$, even up to phase. If $e^{i\alpha}R_z(\frac\pi{16})$ had entries in the ring, their ratio $e^{i\pi/16}$ would lie in its field of fractions $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2, i)$; then so would $\cos\frac\pi{16}$, and $\cos\frac\pi8 = 2\cos^2\frac\pi{16} - 1$ would lie in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$. But $\cos\frac\pi8 = \frac12\sqrt{2 + \sqrt2}\notin\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$ (problem P9).

so the running example must be approximated

A generic $R_z(\theta)$ has entries outside the ring: no Clifford + $T$ circuit is exactly it. Exact synthesis turns the question into: find the cheapest unitary in the ring within $\varepsilon$ of $R_z(\theta)$.

The entries. $H$: $\pm\frac1{\sqrt2}$. $S$: $0, 1, i$. $T$: $0, 1, \omega = \frac1{\sqrt2} + i\frac1{\sqrt2}$. CNOT: $0, 1$. $HT$: first row $\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, \omega)$, second $\frac1{\sqrt2}(1, -\omega)$, from $H\,\mathrm{diag}(1, \omega)$ — each entry a product of ring elements.

The source. Kliuchnikov, Maslov and Mosca, "Fast and efficient exact synthesis of single-qubit unitaries generated by Clifford and T gates", Quantum Inf. Comput. 13, 607 (2013). Their algorithm peels off one $HT$ or $HT^\dagger$ at a time, each step lowering the power of $\sqrt2$ in the denominators, so it terminates with T-count equal to that power (up to a constant). For $n$ qubits the analogous statement needs one extra ancilla qubit (Giles and Selinger, Phys. Rev. A 87, 032332, 2013), cited.

Field of fractions. Every element of $\mathbb Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}, i]$ is $a + b\sqrt2 + ci + di\sqrt2$ with $a, b, c, d\in\mathbb Q$ (use $2^{-j/2} = 2^{-j}(\sqrt2)^j$ and $\sqrt2^2 = 2$), and these numbers form a field, $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2, i)$: inverses by multiplying by conjugates. Problem P9 does the rest, including why $\cos\frac\pi{16}$ would have to lie in its real part $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$.

Countability, again. The ring is countable, so the exactly-synthesisable unitaries are a countable set; almost every $\theta$ gives an $R_z(\theta)$ outside it. The running example's rotation is typical.

11 · exact synthesis

Three T gates per bit of accuracy: optimal rotations

Ross and Selinger (2016, cited)

There is an efficient algorithm which, given $\theta$ and $\varepsilon$, finds an ancilla-free Clifford + $T$ circuit within $\varepsilon$ of $R_z(\theta)$ with T-count

$$3\log_2(1/\varepsilon) + O\big(\log\log(1/\varepsilon)\big),$$

and the leading term is optimal: for most $\theta$, no ancilla-free circuit has T-count below $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon) - O(1)$.

How: exact synthesis reduces the search to number theory — find $u, t$ in the ring with $|u|^2 + |t|^2 = 1$ and $u$ near $e^{-i\theta/2}$ — solved by a lattice search and a norm equation, not by recursion.

Why 3: the counting lower bound. Up to phase, single-qubit unitaries are a three-dimensional space, and there are at most a constant times $2^n$ Clifford + $T$ operators of T-count at most $n$ (notes), so $2^{n}\gtrsim\varepsilon^{-3}$.

Covering a $D$-dimensional space with $\varepsilon$-balls takes $\sim\varepsilon^{-D}$ of them; drawn for $D = 2$. $SU(2)$ has $D = 3$, so $\log_2(\#\text{words})\ge3\log_2(1/\varepsilon) - O(1)$.

The source. Ross and Selinger, "Optimal ancilla-free Clifford+T approximation of z-rotations", Quantum Inf. Comput. 16, 901 (2016), arXiv:1403.2975. "Optimal" means: the T-count they find is the least possible for the given $\theta$ and $\varepsilon$, given the ability to factor certain integers (which is fast at these sizes); and the leading term $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ matches the counting lower bound for typical $\theta$.

The counting, sketched (problem P12 in full). The Matsumoto–Amano normal form (2008, cited) writes each single-qubit Clifford + $T$ operator uniquely as an optional $T$, then a string of syllables $HT$ or $SHT$, then a Clifford; so the number of distinct operators with T-count at most $n$ is at most a constant times $2^n$. For every unitary to lie within $\varepsilon$ of one of them, their $\varepsilon$-balls must cover $SU(2)$, a three-sphere of volume $2\pi^2$ whose balls of radius $\varepsilon$ have volume about $\frac43\pi\varepsilon^3$. Each operator gives two points $\pm W$ of $SU(2)$, so $2C2^n\ge\frac{3\pi}{2}\varepsilon^{-3}$, i.e. $n\ge3\log_2(1/\varepsilon) - O(1)$.

The reduction, a little more. A unitary in the ring with determinant one has the form $\begin{pmatrix}u&-\bar t\\t&\bar u\end{pmatrix}$ with $u, t\in\mathbb Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}, i]$ and $|u|^2 + |t|^2 = 1$. Approximating $R_z(\theta)$ means $u$ close to $e^{-i\theta/2}$; then $t$ must solve $|t|^2 = 1 - |u|^2$, a norm equation in the ring, solvable when a certain integer can be factored. The T-count is governed by the power of $\sqrt2$ in $u$'s denominator, which the number of candidate $u$'s in an $\varepsilon$-region forces up to about $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$.

The figure. In two dimensions, halving $\varepsilon$ quadruples the discs needed; in three, it multiplies them by eight, which costs three more doublings of the word count — three more $T$ gates per halving of $\varepsilon$, i.e. three $T$ gates per bit of accuracy.

11 · summary

What this unit bought, and where it stops

the toolkit
  • Distance: the operator norm; outcome probabilities move by at most $2\lVert U - V\rVert$; global phase may be dropped gate by gate, never inside a controlled gate.
  • Errors add: $\lVert U_n\cdots U_1 - V_n\cdots V_1\rVert\le\sum\lVert U_i - V_i\rVert$, so a budget splits.
  • Density: $T\cdot HTH$ turns by an irrational multiple of $\pi$; two axes generate $SU(2)$; $\{H, T, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$ is universal.
  • The Clifford group: maps Paulis to Paulis; 24 cube rotations on one qubit; $T$ is outside.
  • Solovay–Kitaev: balanced commutators and error squaring; length $O(\log^{3.97}(1/\varepsilon))$.
  • Exact synthesis: entries in $\mathbb Z[\frac1{\sqrt2}, i]$; optimal $z$-rotations at $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ $T$s.

Where it stops

Why Clifford gates are cheap and $T$ is dear is unit 24's; that Clifford circuits are classically simulable is unit 23's. Synthesis with ancillas and measurements, which lowers the expected T-count below $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$, is not covered.

checkpoint
