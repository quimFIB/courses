BQP, and where it sits among the classes you already know

One class, defined by uniform families of quantum circuits with bounded error, and placed exactly: BPP ⊆ BQP ⊆ PP ⊆ PSPACE, every inclusion proved — and the honest news that nobody can show BQP is larger than BPP, because that would separate P from PSPACE.

needs 11~2½ h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 12

You know P, NP and BPP, and you have proved Cook–Levin. Units 10 and 11 built the machine: circuits of gates on qubits, exactly universal with CNOT and single-qubit gates, approximately universal with finitely many gates at a polylogarithmic price. This unit asks the complexity theorist's question about that machine — which languages does it decide efficiently? — and answers with one new class, BQP, placed between two classical ones with every inclusion proved.

The answer has a second half that matters as much as the first. Nobody knows whether BQP is larger than BPP, and a proof that it is would prove P ≠ PSPACE, which is far beyond present technique. "Quantum computers are faster" is a hypothesis, supported by evidence that Part D supplies; it is not a theorem, and this unit says exactly why.

the running example: one small circuit

12 · the class

What you already know, in the form used here

classes by certificates (assumed background)

$R(x, y)$ is a predicate computable in polynomial time, $p$ a polynomial, and $y$ ranges over $\{0,1\}^{p(|x|)}$.

NP: $\ x\in L \iff \exists y:\ R(x, y)$.

BPP: $\ x\in L \Rightarrow \Pr_y[R(x, y)] \ge \tfrac23$, $\ x\notin L \Rightarrow \Pr_y[R(x, y)] \le \tfrac13$, with $y$ uniform: the string of coin flips.

Hoeffding's inequality (assumed background, cited)

For independent $X_1, \dots, X_k \in [0, 1]$ with mean $\mu = \mathbb E\bar X$ and any $t \gt 0$,

$$\Pr\big[\bar X \le \mu - t\big] \le e^{-2kt^2}.$$

What the background proves. Whether NP ⊆ BPP or BPP ⊆ NP is open.

A deterministic machine is the case $p = 0$. This unit adds a class beside BPP, then proves where it sits.

Why certificates. Every class in this unit will be defined by what is done with the set of strings $y$ for which $R(x, y)$ holds: NP asks whether it is non-empty, BPP whether it is a large or a small fraction, #P counts it, PP asks whether it is more than half. A nondeterministic machine running in time $p(n)$ with binary choices is the same thing: a path through its computation tree is the string $y$ of its choices, and "the path accepts" is $R(x, y)$, a polynomial-time check. Padding with dummy choices makes all paths the same length $p(n)$. The certificate form avoids the bookkeeping of paths of different lengths.

Hoeffding's inequality (Hoeffding, J. Amer. Statist. Assoc. 58, 13, 1963) is in the assumed background as a statement; its proof bounds $\mathbb E e^{\lambda(X_i - \mathbb EX_i)} \le e^{\lambda^2/8}$ for a variable in $[0, 1]$ and applies Markov's inequality to $e^{\lambda\sum X_i}$. It is used twice here: for amplification, and to count how many runs a small gap needs.

The known facts on the figure. P ⊆ NP takes $R$ to ignore $y$; P ⊆ BPP likewise, with probability 0 or 1. BPP ⊆ NP is not known, and nor is the reverse; it is widely believed that BPP = P (because of pseudorandom generators — Impagliazzo and Wigderson, STOC 1997, cited, derive it from a circuit lower bound that nobody has proved).

12 · the class

A circuit family, and who builds it

a uniform circuit family

A sequence $(C_n)_{n\ge0}$, where $C_n$ is a circuit of gates from a fixed finite set $\mathcal G$ acting on $n + a(n)$ qubits, such that a deterministic Turing machine, given $1^n$, prints a description of $C_n$ in time polynomial in $n$.

Run on $x\in\{0,1\}^n$: apply $C_n$ to $\ket x\ket{0^{a(n)}}$ and measure qubit 0. The acceptance probability is

$$p(x) = \big\lVert(\ket1\!\bra1\otimes I)\,C_n\ket{x, 0^{a(n)}}\big\rVert^2 .$$

The gate set, fixed for this unit: $\mathcal G = \{H, T, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$ — approximately universal (unit 11), and it builds Toffoli exactly (unit 10's seven-T circuit, with $T^\dagger = T^7$).

The generator prints $C_n$; the circuit runs on $x$; qubit 0 is read.

The running circuit accepts on the whole outcome $000$, not on one qubit. That is no extra power: compute the predicate "outcome $= 000$" reversibly into a fresh qubit (unit 10), and read that.

Descriptions. A description of $C_n$ is a list of gates, each a name from $\mathcal G$ and the indices of the qubits it acts on. A machine running in time $q(n)$ prints at most $q(n)$ symbols, so $C_n$ has at most $q(n)$ gates and touches at most $q(n)$ qubits: uniformity forces polynomial size.

Reading one qubit is enough. Suppose a circuit is to accept when the full outcome $z$ lies in a set $A$ decided in polynomial time. Unit 10 gives a reversible circuit $\ket z\ket b \mapsto \ket z\ket{b\oplus[z\in A]}$ with its garbage uncomputed. Apply it to the output register and a fresh qubit, swap the fresh qubit into qubit 0 with three CNOTs (the definition reads qubit 0), then measure qubit 0 only. On each basis state $\ket z$ it writes $[z\in A]$, so by linearity the state becomes $\sum_z\alpha_z\ket z\ket{[z\in A]}$ and the probability of reading 1 is $\sum_{z\in A}|\alpha_z|^2$ — the same as measuring everything and checking $z \in A$. For the running example, $[z = 000]$ is $\bar z_0\bar z_1\bar z_2$: three $X$ gates, a Toffoli-built three-controlled NOT, and three $X$ gates again.

Why $\{H, T, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$. Any finite approximately universal set would do — The gate set does not matter proves that the choice does not change the class. This one is convenient: $X = HT^4H$ (since $T^4 = Z$ and $HZH = X$), $T^\dagger = T^7$, and Toffoli is exact (unit 10), so every classical reversible circuit is available with no approximation at all.

Why the builder must be efficient

non-uniform families decide undecidable languages

Let $M_1, M_2, \dots$ enumerate Turing machines and

$$U = \{1^n : M_n \text{ halts on the empty input}\}.$$

$U$ is undecidable, yet some family $(C_n)$ of polynomial-size circuits decides it with error $0$.

proof

Let $C_n$ compute $\mathrm{AND}(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ into a fresh output qubit with Toffolis (unit 10), follow it by nothing if $1^n\in U$ and by a CNOT that erases the answer if $1^n\notin U$, then swap the output into qubit 0. One bit of advice per length, and it is the uncomputable bit. $\blacksquare$

Each circuit is tiny; the sequence of them is uncomputable. (The pattern drawn is illustrative.)

Uniformity forbids it: if a machine printed $C_n$ from $1^n$, looking for the erasing CNOT would decide $U$.

Full proof

$U$ is undecidable: a decider for $U$ would decide the halting problem on empty input, by computing the index $n$ of a given machine and asking whether $1^n\in U$; that problem is undecidable (assumed background).

The circuit. On input $x\in\{0,1\}^n$, a chain of $n - 1$ Toffolis with fresh ancillas computes $x_1\wedge\dots\wedge x_n$ into the last ancilla (unit 10); a CNOT copies it to a fresh output qubit $o$, which starts in $\ket0$. If $1^n\notin U$, a second CNOT from the same ancilla onto $o$ sets it back to $0$. Finally three CNOTs swap $o$ with qubit 0 — which, by the definition, holds the input bit $x_1$, not a fresh $\ket0$. So qubit 0 reads $1$ exactly when $x = 1^n$ and $1^n \in U$, with probability $1$ or $0$: error $0$, size $O(n)$.

If $(C_n)$ were uniform, a machine could print $C_n$ from $1^n$ and check whether the erasing CNOT is there, deciding $U$. So no uniform family does this, and some non-uniform one does.

The moral. A circuit family is a different program for every input length, and without uniformity the programs may encode anything — including answers no algorithm could find. The classical version is the class P/poly, which contains undecidable unary languages for the same reason. Uniformity says the family is one finite algorithm after all. The Uniformity matters problem asks for this construction with every gate written down.

12 · the class

BQP

BQP, bounded-error quantum polynomial time

$L \in \mathsf{BQP}$ iff some uniform family $(C_n)$ over $\mathcal G$ has, for every $x$,

$$\begin{aligned} x\in L &\;\Rightarrow\; p(x) \ge \tfrac23,\\ x\notin L &\;\Rightarrow\; p(x) \le \tfrac13 .\end{aligned}$$

Error at most $\tfrac13$ on every input — not on average over inputs.

P ⊆ BQP, in one line: a polynomial-time decider becomes a reversible circuit (unit 10) mapping $\ket{x, 0}$ to $\ket{x, L(x), 0}$ — a basis state, so $p(x) \in \{0, 1\}$.

the running circuit decides nothing

Put it in a family as $C_n$ for some $n$, ignoring the input. It accepts with $\tfrac9{16}$, inside $(\tfrac13, \tfrac23)$: at that length the family neither accepts nor rejects. BQP's definition demands a gap.

Where $p(x)$ may lie. The running circuit's $\tfrac9{16}$ falls in the band a BQP family never visits.

Three questions about this definition, each a slide: does it depend on $\mathcal G$? on the constant $\tfrac13$? Is it closed under complement and reductions? No, no, yes.

Where the name comes from. Bernstein and Vazirani (SIAM J. Comput. 26, 1411, 1997; conference version 1993) defined BQP with quantum Turing machines; Yao (FOCS 1993) proved that uniform circuit families give the same class, and the circuit definition is the one in use today. "Bounded error" means the error is bounded away from $\tfrac12$ by a constant — the $\tfrac16$ between $\tfrac13$ and $\tfrac12$.

The running circuit, again. A family has one circuit per length, and the definition must hold at every length. If $C_5$, say, were the running circuit with five idle input wires, then for every $x$ of length 5 the acceptance probability would be $\tfrac9{16}$, which is neither $\ge\tfrac23$ nor $\le\tfrac13$, so the family would decide no language at all. Two things could repair it. Amplification (The constant does not matter) repeats it and takes a majority; that works because $\tfrac9{16}$ is $\tfrac1{16}$ above $\tfrac12$, and turns "$\ge\tfrac9{16}$" into "$\ge\tfrac23$" with 141 runs. Or one can drop the gap altogether, which is PP — section 4.

Promise problems. Many natural quantum problems come with a promise (the input has some property), and BQP is often defined for promise problems, with no condition on inputs outside the promise. Factoring is a function problem, turned into a language by asking for bits of the answer; unit 16 does that. Nothing here depends on the choice.

12 · the class

The gate set does not matter

gate-set independence

Let $\mathcal G'$ contain CNOT and a finite set of single-qubit gates, closed under inverses, generating a dense subgroup of $SU(2)$ up to phase (unit 11). Then BQP defined over $\mathcal G'$ equals BQP over $\mathcal G$.

proof

Take $C_n$ over $\mathcal G$ with $s$ gates. Each is a product of at most $c_0$ CNOTs and single-qubit gates (unit 10; $c_0$ depends only on $\mathcal G$). Approximate each single-qubit gate to $\delta = 1/(24c_0s)$ by Solovay–Kitaev: $O(\log^{3.97}(24c_0s))$ gates of $\mathcal G'$ each. Errors add (unit 11):

$$\lVert U - V\rVert \le c_0s\cdot\delta = \tfrac1{24},$$

so $p(x)$ moves by at most $2\cdot\tfrac1{24} = \tfrac1{12}$. Error $\le \tfrac13 + \tfrac1{12} = \tfrac5{12} \lt \tfrac12$; amplification (next slide) restores $\tfrac13$. The other direction is symmetric. $\blacksquare$

Each gate is swapped for a short string of the other set's gates; the errors add, and stay small.

Uniformity survives: the Solovay–Kitaev construction is itself an algorithm, running in time polylogarithmic in $1/\delta$ (Dawson–Nielsen, cited in unit 11).

Full proof

Let $C_n = G_s\cdots G_1$ over $\mathcal G$. By unit 10 each $G_i$ is exactly a product of CNOTs and single-qubit unitaries; since $\mathcal G$ is finite, one decomposition per gate type fixes a constant $c_0$, and $C_n$ becomes a product $U = U_{N}\cdots U_1$ of $N \le c_0 s$ gates, each a CNOT or a single-qubit unitary. Keep the CNOTs. For each single-qubit $U_j$, Solovay–Kitaev (unit 11) gives a product $V_j$ of $O(\log^{c}(1/\delta))$ gates from $\mathcal G'$, $c \approx 3.97$, with $\lVert U_j - e^{i\phi_j}V_j\rVert \le \delta$ for some phase $\phi_j$. The phases commute with everything and multiply to one global phase $e^{i\Phi}$ of the whole circuit, which changes no probability (unit 01).

Errors add (unit 11): $\lVert U - e^{i\Phi}V\rVert \le \sum_j\lVert U_j - e^{i\phi_j}V_j\rVert \le N\delta \le c_0s\delta$. With $\delta = 1/(24c_0s)$ this is $\le\tfrac1{24}$. Unit 11 proved that every outcome probability moves by at most $2\lVert U - V\rVert$ for any input state, so $|p_V(x) - p_U(x)| \le \tfrac1{12}$. The new family has $O(s\log^{c}(24c_0s))$ gates: polynomial, since $s$ is.

Correctness after the move: if $x\in L$, $p_V \ge \tfrac23 - \tfrac1{12} = \tfrac7{12}$; if $x\notin L$, $p_V \le \tfrac13 + \tfrac1{12} = \tfrac5{12}$. The gap is now $\tfrac1{12}$ on each side of $\tfrac12$, and the amplification theorem, with $\mu - \tfrac12 \ge \tfrac1{12}$, gives error $\le e^{-2k/144}$, below $\tfrac13$ once $k \ge 72\ln 3 \approx 79.1$, so $k = 80$ runs. The reverse inclusion is the same argument with $\mathcal G$ and $\mathcal G'$ exchanged ($\mathcal G$ satisfies the same hypothesis: $T^\dagger = T^7$ is in the group it generates, and $\langle H, T\rangle$ is dense, unit 11).

Uniformity. The new generator prints the old circuit, decomposes each gate, and replaces each single-qubit gate by its Solovay–Kitaev string. Only the finitely many gate types of $\mathcal G$ (after decomposition) need approximating, each to accuracy $\delta$, and Dawson and Nielsen's algorithm does that in time $O(\log^{2.71}(1/\delta))$ (QIC 6, 81, 2006, cited). A problem on the sheet, Gate-set independence, runs the numbers.

12 · the class

The constant does not matter: majority of $k$ runs

amplification

If a uniform family decides $L$ with error $\le\tfrac13$, then for every polynomial $k(n)$ another uniform family decides $L$ with error $\le e^{-k/18}$, at size $k$ times the old plus $O(k\log k)$.

proof

Run $C_n$ on $k$ disjoint registers. The state is the product $\big(C_n\ket{x,0}\big)^{\otimes k}$, so the $k$ output bits are independent (unit 03), each correct with probability $\mu \ge \tfrac23$. The majority is wrong only if the fraction correct is $\le\tfrac12 \le \mu - \tfrac16$; Hoeffding with $t = \tfrac16$:

$$\Pr[\text{majority wrong}] \le e^{-2k/36} = e^{-k/18}. \;\blacksquare$$

The majority is computed reversibly into one output qubit (deferred measurement, unit 10), so the result is again a family that reads one qubit.

$k$ independent runs and a reversible majority, read on one fresh qubit.

Full proof

Independence. Copy $j$ acts only on register $j$, which starts in $\ket{x, 0^{a}}$. So the joint state after all copies is $\ket{\psi}^{\otimes k}$ with $\ket\psi = C_n\ket{x, 0^a}$ — each copy is $C_n$ tensored with the identity on the other registers, and these commute. Measuring the output qubit of every register, the probability of the bit string $b_1\cdots b_k$ is $\prod_j\Pr[b_j]$ (unit 03: for a product state and a product of projectors, $\lVert\otimes_jP_j\ket\psi\rVert^2 = \prod_j\lVert P_j\ket\psi\rVert^2$). So the bits are independent and identically distributed.

Hoeffding. Let $X_j = 1$ if bit $j$ is correct. Then $\mathbb E\bar X = \mu \ge \tfrac23$. The majority is wrong only if $\bar X \le \tfrac12$ (for even $k$, count a tie as wrong), and $\tfrac12 \le \mu - \tfrac16$, so $\Pr[\bar X \le \tfrac12] \le \Pr[\bar X \le \mu - \tfrac16] \le e^{-2k(1/6)^2} = e^{-k/18}$.

The circuit for the majority. A counter of $\lceil\log(k+1)\rceil$ qubits starts at $0$; for each $j$, add $b_j$ into it with a reversible incrementer controlled on $b_j$ (unit 10's reversible adders), then compare the counter with $k/2$ into the fresh output qubit, then run the incrementers backwards to return the counter to $0$ (Bennett's uncomputation, unit 10). That is $O(k\log k)$ Toffolis. Measuring only the output qubit gives the same distribution as measuring the $k$ bits and taking the majority classically: the majority circuit permutes basis states, so the argument of A circuit family, and who builds it applies.

Uniform. The generator prints $k(n)$ copies of $C_n$ and the majority circuit; both are polynomial.

12 · the class · example

Worked: from one third to $2^{-100}$

how many runs push the error from $\tfrac13$ to $2^{-100}$?

1. The condition. Amplification gives error $\le e^{-k/18}$; ask for $e^{-k/18} \le 2^{-100}$.

2. Take logs. $-k/18 \le -100\ln2$, so

$$k \ge 1800\ln 2 = 1247.66\ldots,\qquad k = 1248 .$$

3. Check. $e^{-1248/18} = 7.74\times10^{-31} \le 2^{-100} = 7.89\times10^{-31}$ ✓, while $e^{-1247/18} = 8.19\times10^{-31}$ is not.

4. The price. A factor 1248 in size: a constant. The bound is loose — at $k = 101$ the exact binomial error is $2.72\times10^{-4}$ against the bound's $3.66\times10^{-3}$ — but loose is fine for a theorem.

5. On the running circuit, $\mu = \tfrac9{16}$ is $t = \tfrac1{16}$ above $\tfrac12$: $e^{-2k/256} \le \tfrac13$ needs $k \ge 128\ln3 = 140.6$, so $141$ runs turn "$\ge\tfrac9{16}$" into "$\ge\tfrac23$".

Majority error against the number of runs (odd $k$, computed exactly from the binomial distribution). The bound is a straight line on this scale; the truth falls faster.

The arithmetic. $\ln 2 = 0.693147$, so $1800\ln2 = 1247.665$, and the least integer above it is $1248$. With $k = 1248$ and ties counted as failures the bound applies as stated; if you prefer odd $k$ so that there are no ties, take $1249$.

The exact errors. For $k$ odd, the majority is wrong iff at most $(k-1)/2$ runs are correct, so the error is $\sum_{j=0}^{(k-1)/2}\binom kj\mu^j(1-\mu)^{k-j}$. With $\mu = \tfrac23$: $0.2593$ at $k = 3$, $0.1221$ at $k = 11$, $2.72\times10^{-4}$ at $k = 101$, $5.53\times10^{-7}$ at $k = 201$. With $\mu = \tfrac9{16}$: $0.0677$ at $k = 141$ (the bound promised $\le\tfrac13$) and $0.0374$ at $k = 201$. The gap $\mu - \tfrac12$ sets the slope: $\tfrac16$ against $\tfrac1{16}$ is a factor $(16/6)^2 \approx 7.1$ in the exponent.

Why the constant was arbitrary. Any error bound $\tfrac12 - \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \ge 1/q(n)$ for a polynomial $q$ can be pushed to $2^{-r(n)}$ for any polynomial $r$ with $k = O(q^2r)$ runs: Hoeffding with $t = \varepsilon$ gives $e^{-2k\varepsilon^2} \le 2^{-r}$ once $k \ge r\ln2/(2\varepsilon^2)$. So "$\tfrac13$" in the definition could be any constant below $\tfrac12$, or even $\tfrac12 - 1/\mathrm{poly}$. What cannot be allowed is an exponentially small gap: that is section 4, and PP.

12 · the class

Using an answer without measuring it

A BQP subroutine inside a larger quantum computation must not be measured and must leave no garbage (unit 10). Compute, copy, uncompute:

lemma: a clean answer, nearly

If $C\ket{x, 0} = \alpha\ket{f(x)}\ket a + \beta\ket{\overline{f(x)}}\ket b$ with $|\beta|^2 \le \varepsilon$, then

$$\begin{aligned}\big\lVert C^\dagger\,\mathrm{CNOT}\,C\ket{x,0}\ket y &- \ket{x,0}\ket{y\oplus f(x)}\big\rVert\\ &\le \sqrt{2\varepsilon}.\end{aligned}$$

With $\varepsilon = 2^{-100}$ (1248 runs) it is within $2^{-49.5}$ of unit 10's clean $U_f$.

BQP is low for itself (cited)

$\mathsf{BQP}^{\mathsf{BQP}} = \mathsf{BQP}$: calling BQP languages as gates gains nothing (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, Vazirani, SIAM J. Comput. 26, 1510, 1997).

Bennett's pattern from unit 10, now with a slightly wrong answer: the error enters as $\sqrt{2\varepsilon}$.

proof of the lemma

After the CNOT the state differs from $(C\ket{x,0})\ket{y\oplus f(x)}$ only in the $\beta$ term, by $\beta\ket{\bar f}\ket b\big(\ket{y\oplus\bar f} - \ket{y\oplus f}\big)$, of norm $|\beta|\sqrt2$. $C^\dagger$ is unitary, so the distance survives it unchanged. $\blacksquare$

Full proof of the lemma

Write $\ket\psi = C\ket{x, 0} = \alpha\ket{f}\ket a + \beta\ket{\bar f}\ket b$, where the first factor is the answer qubit and $\ket a, \ket b$ are unit vectors on the rest (the input register included), with $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$. The CNOT from the answer qubit onto $\ket y$ gives

$\ket{\psi'} = \alpha\ket f\ket a\ket{y\oplus f} + \beta\ket{\bar f}\ket b\ket{y\oplus\bar f}$.

The ideal state $\ket\psi\ket{y\oplus f} = \alpha\ket f\ket a\ket{y\oplus f} + \beta\ket{\bar f}\ket b\ket{y\oplus f}$ differs from it by $\beta\ket{\bar f}\ket b\big(\ket{y\oplus\bar f} - \ket{y\oplus f}\big)$; the two kets in the bracket are distinct basis states, so the bracket has norm $\sqrt2$, and the difference has norm $|\beta|\sqrt2 \le \sqrt{2\varepsilon}$. Applying $C^\dagger\otimes I$ preserves norms and maps the ideal state to $\ket{x, 0}\ket{y\oplus f}$. $\blacksquare$

Many calls. A circuit that calls such a subroutine $q$ times, each within $\sqrt{2\varepsilon}$ of the clean $U_f$, is within $q\sqrt{2\varepsilon}$ of the circuit with clean calls — unit 11's errors-add bound, applied to these subroutines as the "gates" (each subroutine is exactly unitary, and only its action on the relevant inputs is close to $U_f$; the telescoping argument goes through on states). With $\varepsilon = 2^{-r}$ and $r$ a large enough polynomial, polynomially many calls cost a negligible error. This is the heart of $\mathsf{BQP}^{\mathsf{BQP}} = \mathsf{BQP}$; the cited paper handles the remaining bookkeeping (oracle calls on superpositions of inputs of different lengths).

Oracles, briefly. $\mathsf{BQP}^A$ is BQP with circuits allowed the gate $U_A\ket{y, b} = \ket{y, b\oplus A(y)}$ for a fixed language $A$ — unit 10's $U_f$ with $f$ the membership function of $A$. Section 5 uses the notion again.

Closed under complement and under reductions

closure

(i) $L\in\mathsf{BQP} \Rightarrow \bar L \in \mathsf{BQP}$.

(ii) If $L \le_p L'$ (a polynomial-time map $f$ with $x\in L \iff f(x)\in L'$) and $L'\in\mathsf{BQP}$, then $L\in\mathsf{BQP}$.

proof

(i) Append $X$ on qubit 0: $p(x) \mapsto 1 - p(x)$, so $\ge\tfrac23$ and $\le\tfrac13$ trade places.

So BQP is a class one can build with: closed under complement, under reductions, and — by the last slide — under subroutine calls.

(i) in full. $X$ on qubit 0 maps $\ket1\!\bra1\otimes I$ to $\ket0\!\bra0\otimes I$ by conjugation, so the new acceptance probability is $\lVert(\ket0\!\bra0\otimes I)C_n\ket{x,0}\rVert^2 = 1 - p(x)$, the two projectors summing to $I$. The family stays uniform: one gate more, and $X = HT^4H$ in $\mathcal G$. The sheet's Complement problem asks for this with every step.

(ii) in full, with the one wrinkle. The output $f(x)$ may have any length $m \le q(n)$, and $L'$'s family has a different circuit $C'_m$ for each length. Compute $f(x)$ and its length $m$ reversibly (unit 10). Then run every $C'_m$, $m = 0, \dots, q(n)$, each on its own fresh register, fed with the first $m$ bits of the $f(x)$-register; and finally copy into the output qubit, reversibly, the output bit of the register whose index equals the computed $m$. The registers act on disjoint qubits, so their outputs are independent, and the copied bit has exactly the distribution of $C'_m$'s output on $f(x)$. The others are junk that is never read. Total size: $\sum_{m\le q(n)}|C'_m|$ plus the classical parts, polynomial. Uniform: the generator prints all of it.

Also closed under union and intersection (run both, amplify each to error $\tfrac16$, combine the two bits reversibly: error $\le\tfrac16 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac13$ by the union bound). None of this is surprising; it says that BQP behaves like a class of problems with efficient algorithms should.

12 · simulation

Pushing a vector through the gates

state-vector simulation

Store all $2^m$ amplitudes $a_z$, $z\in\{0,1\}^m$. A gate on qubit $q$ mixes each pair $(a_{z}, a_{z'})$ with $z, z'$ differing only in bit $q$; for $H$,

$$\begin{aligned}a_z' &= \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(a_z + a_{z'}),\\ a_{z'}' &= \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(a_z - a_{z'})\qquad(z_q = 0).\end{aligned}$$

A permutation gate (CNOT, Toffoli) only moves amplitudes. Each gate costs $O(2^m)$ operations.

consequence

$\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{EXP}$, where EXP is deterministic time $2^{q(n)}$ for some polynomial $q$: $T$ gates on $m$ qubits take $O(T\,2^m)$ steps, and the acceptance probability is $\sum_{z_0 = 1}|a_z|^2$.

One gate, as a butterfly on the $2^m$ amplitudes: qubit 0 is the most significant bit, so $H_0$ pairs index $i$ with $i + 4$ when $m = 3$.

Why the pair rule. A single-qubit gate $U$ on qubit $q$ is $I\otimes\cdots\otimes U\otimes\cdots\otimes I$ (unit 03). On a basis state $\ket z$ it changes only bit $q$, so the new amplitude of $\ket z$ depends only on the old amplitudes of $\ket z$ and of $\ket{z'}$, the string with bit $q$ flipped: $a'_z = U_{z_q z_q}a_z + U_{z_q z'_q}a_{z'}$. For $H$ the entries are $\bra aH\ket b = (-1)^{ab}/\sqrt2$ (unit 02), which gives the formula on the slide. For a gate on $k$ qubits the same argument mixes groups of $2^k$ amplitudes.

EXP. The class of languages decided by a deterministic machine in time $2^{q(n)}$, $q$ a polynomial. The simulation needs the amplitudes to finite precision; with $\mathcal G = \{H, T, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$ every amplitude lies in the ring $\mathbb Z[\tfrac1{\sqrt2}, i]$ (unit 11's exact-synthesis ring; it contains $e^{i\pi/4} = (1 + i)/\sqrt2$), so the arithmetic can even be exact, with numbers of polynomially many bits. Either way the time is $2^{O(m)}\cdot\mathrm{poly}$, and $m$ is polynomial in $n$.

The text in the figure writes the rule with index arithmetic: with three qubits, the strings $0bc$ and $1bc$ are indices $i$ and $i + 4$. With Qiskit's opposite qubit order the partner of index $i$ under a gate on qubit 0 would be $i + 1$ for even $i$, which is why the course fixes its order once.

Where the 9/16 comes from: amplitudes add before they square

The running circuit's eight amplitudes after each layer (positive up, negative down; the accepting one in the unit colour). Every snapshot has $\sum_z a_z^2 = 1$.

Three routes lead to $000$, from $\ket{000}$, $\ket{010}$ and $\ket{100}$ of the middle snapshot, each with amplitude $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac14$ and the same sign. They add: $\tfrac34$, squared $\tfrac9{16}$.

Measure qubits 0 and 1 after the Toffoli instead, and the routes become exclusive events: $\Pr = 3\cdot\big(\tfrac14\big)^2 = \tfrac3{16}$. Adding before squaring tripled it.

The comparison, in full. Measuring qubits 0 and 1 after the Toffoli gives each of $00, 01, 10, 11$ with probability $\tfrac14$ and leaves a basis state; qubit 2 then holds their AND. Applying $H\otimes H$ to a basis state $\ket{ab}$ and measuring gives $00$ with probability $|\tfrac12|^2 = \tfrac14$ whatever $ab$ is. So the outcome $000$ needs $ab \ne 11$ (probability $\tfrac34$) and then $00$ (probability $\tfrac14$): $\tfrac3{16} = 0.1875$. The quantum circuit gives $\tfrac9{16}$ because the three contributions are amplitudes, which add before the Born rule squares the total.

The other direction. Interference can also cancel: the amplitude of $110$ is $\tfrac14(1 - 1 - 1) = -\tfrac14$, three routes of which two have a minus sign. Section 3 makes "routes" precise as paths, and section 4 counts them with their signs.

Why this matters for complexity. A classical randomised algorithm also has many routes to each outcome, but its probabilities are non-negative and add. A quantum algorithm's amplitudes carry signs (and in general phases), so routes can cancel. Every speedup in Part D is an arrangement in which the routes to wrong answers cancel and the routes to right ones reinforce.

Feynman's other way of computing an amplitude: a sum over histories. Add the histories up one at a time, and the memory needed is only as long as one history.

PSPACE: polynomial memory, any amount of time

PSPACE

The languages decided by a deterministic Turing machine that, on inputs of length $n$, uses at most $q(n)$ tape cells for some polynomial $q$ — running for as long as it likes, but halting.

$\mathsf P \subseteq \mathsf{NP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE} \subseteq \mathsf{EXP}$

P: in $t$ steps a machine visits at most $t + 1$ cells.

NP: try $y = 0\cdots0, 0\cdots01, \dots$ in turn, erasing the work for each $y$ before the next; memory: $y$ plus one run of $R$.

EXP: a halting machine never repeats a configuration, and with $q(n)$ cells there are only $2^{O(q(n))}$ of them.

Space can be reused; time cannot. Enumerating $2^{p}$ certificates takes exponential time but only the cells of one.

Whether P = PSPACE is open. That will matter at the end.

P ⊆ PSPACE. The head moves at most one cell per step, so a machine halting within $t(n)$ steps writes on at most $t(n) + 1$ cells.

NP ⊆ PSPACE, in full. Given $R$ and $p$, keep a $p(n)$-bit counter $y$, starting at $0$. For each value, run the polynomial-time machine for $R(x, y)$ on a separate work area of polynomial size, accept if it accepts, and otherwise erase the work area and increment $y$; reject when $y$ overflows. The space is $p(n)$ plus the space of one run, which is polynomial; the time is $2^{p(n)}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)$. The same idea — enumerate, reuse — will count accepting certificates (PP ⊆ PSPACE, section 4) and sum amplitudes over paths (this section).

PSPACE ⊆ EXP. A configuration is the state ($|Q|$ choices), the head position ($q(n)$ choices) and the tape contents ($|\Gamma|^{q(n)}$ choices), so there are at most $|Q|\,q(n)\,|\Gamma|^{q(n)} = 2^{O(q(n))}$. A deterministic machine that repeated a configuration would loop forever; a halting one therefore runs for fewer steps than there are configurations.

What is known. P ≠ EXP, by the time hierarchy theorem (Hartmanis and Stearns 1965, cited), so at least one of the inclusions $\mathsf P \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE} \subseteq \mathsf{EXP}$ is strict — but nobody knows which. A complete problem for PSPACE is deciding the truth of fully quantified Boolean formulas (Stockmeyer and Meyer 1973, cited).

First, make every gate a coin or a permutation

lemma: the real-amplitude encoding

Write a gate as $U = A + iB$ with $A, B$ real. On one extra qubit, put

$$\tilde U = A\otimes I + B\otimes J,\qquad J = \begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

Then $\tilde U$ is real orthogonal, and encoding $a + ib \mapsto a\otimes\ket0 + b\otimes\ket1$ turns $U$'s action into $\tilde U$'s, with the same outcome probabilities.

One line: $\tilde U(a\otimes\ket0 + b\otimes\ket1) = (Aa - Bb)\otimes\ket0 + (Ba + Ab)\otimes\ket1$ encodes $(A + iB)(a + ib)$, and $|a_z + ib_z|^2 = a_z^2 + b_z^2$.

$H$ and Toffoli suffice (cited)

Every BQP language is decided by a uniform family of $H$ and Toffoli gates (Shi 2003; Aharonov 2003); $X$ and CNOT may be added freely.

$J$ is multiplication by $i$, drawn: a quarter-turn of the plane of real and imaginary parts.

After this, every gate is either $H$ — two branches, entries $\pm\tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ — or a permutation of basis states — one branch, entry $1$. The running circuit is already of this form.

Full proof of the lemma

Let $U$ be unitary on $\mathbb C^N$ and $U = A + iB$ with $A, B$ real $N\times N$. Then $U^\dagger = A^{\mathsf T} - iB^{\mathsf T}$ and $U^\dagger U = (A^{\mathsf T}A + B^{\mathsf T}B) + i(A^{\mathsf T}B - B^{\mathsf T}A) = I$; real and imaginary parts give $A^{\mathsf T}A + B^{\mathsf T}B = I$ and $A^{\mathsf T}B = B^{\mathsf T}A$. Now $J^{\mathsf T} = -J$ and $J^{\mathsf T}J = I$, so $\tilde U^{\mathsf T}\tilde U = A^{\mathsf T}A\otimes I + A^{\mathsf T}B\otimes J + B^{\mathsf T}A\otimes J^{\mathsf T} + B^{\mathsf T}B\otimes J^{\mathsf T}J = (A^{\mathsf T}A + B^{\mathsf T}B)\otimes I + (A^{\mathsf T}B - B^{\mathsf T}A)\otimes J = I$.

The encoding $E(a + ib) = a\otimes\ket0 + b\otimes\ket1$ is $\mathbb R$-linear and preserves norms: $\lVert a\rVert^2 + \lVert b\rVert^2 = \lVert a + ib\rVert^2$. The computation on the slide shows $\tilde UE = EU$, so a whole circuit $U_T\cdots U_1$ is simulated by $\tilde U_T\cdots\tilde U_1$, each $\tilde U_t$ acting on the gate's qubits and the one shared extra qubit. The probability of outcome $z$ on the original qubits, summed over the extra qubit, is $a_z^2 + b_z^2 = |a_z + ib_z|^2$. The start state $\ket{x, 0}$ is real, so $E$ maps it to $\ket{x, 0}\ket0$. A path through a circuit

a path, and its amplitude

For $C = G_T\cdots G_1$ on $m$ qubits started at $\ket s$: a sequence of basis strings $s = z_0, z_1, \dots, z_T$, with amplitude $\prod_{t=1}^T\bra{z_t}G_t\ket{z_{t-1}}$.

the path sum $$\bra zC\ket s = \sum_{\text{paths ending at } z}\ \prod_{t=1}^T\bra{z_t}G_t\ket{z_{t-1}}.$$

Why: insert $I = \sum_y\ket y\!\bra y$ (unit 00) between each pair of gates and expand.

For $H$ and permutations: at an $H$ on qubit $q$, $z_t$ copies $z_{t-1}$ except bit $q$, which is free, with entry $(-1)^{(z_{t-1})_q(z_t)_q}/\sqrt2$; at a permutation $\pi$, $z_t = \pi(z_{t-1})$. So the non-zero paths are labelled by $w\in\{0,1\}^h$, the free bits, and

$$\text{amplitude}(w) = \sigma(w)\,2^{-h/2},\quad \sigma(w) = \pm1 .$$

One path through the running circuit: basis strings (rows) against time (columns). It branches only at the Hadamards.

Both $\sigma(w)$ and the endpoint of $w$ are computed in polynomial time by following one basis string through the gates — no amplitude vector is ever stored.

Full proof of the path sum

$\bra zC\ket s = \bra zG_T\,I\,G_{T-1}\,I\cdots I\,G_1\ket s$, and each $I = \sum_{y}\ket y\!\bra y$ over the $2^m$ basis strings (unit 00). Expanding the $T - 1$ sums, with $y$ in the $t$-th slot named $z_t$, gives $\sum_{z_1,\dots,z_{T-1}}\bra zG_T\ket{z_{T-1}}\cdots\bra{z_1}G_1\ket s$ — a sum over all sequences, of which only those with every factor non-zero contribute.

The entries. For $H$ on qubit $q$, $\bra{z_t}(I\otimes H\otimes I)\ket{z_{t-1}}$ is zero unless $z_t$ and $z_{t-1}$ agree off bit $q$, and then equals $\bra{(z_t)_q}H\ket{(z_{t-1})_q} = (-1)^{(z_t)_q(z_{t-1})_q}/\sqrt2$ (unit 02). For a permutation gate the entry is $1$ if $z_t = \pi(z_{t-1})$ and $0$ otherwise. So a non-zero path is determined by its free bits, one per $H$, in order: $w = w_1\cdots w_h$, and its amplitude is $2^{-h/2}$ times the product of the $h$ signs.

Computing $\sigma(w)$ and the endpoint. Start with the string $s$ and the sign $+1$. Go through the gates in order: at a permutation, apply it to the string; at the $j$-th Hadamard, on qubit $q$, multiply the sign by $(-1)^{(\text{current bit } q)\cdot w_j}$ and set bit $q$ to $w_j$. This takes $O(T)$ operations on an $m$-bit string, and the circuit itself comes from the uniform generator in polynomial time.

History. Feynman's sum over histories is his formulation of quantum mechanics (Rev. Mod. Phys. 20, 367, 1948, cited), in which a particle's amplitude to go from one place to another is a sum, over every path between them, of a phase. A circuit's path sum is the same idea with finitely many paths, and no analysis is needed to make it rigorous.

theorem (Bernstein and Vazirani 1997)

$\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}$.

proof

Take an $H$-and-permutation family (cited above). Let $h_t$ count the Hadamards among $G_1, \dots, G_t$ and put $N(t, z) = 2^{h_t/2}\bra zG_t\cdots G_1\ket s$, an integer. Then $N(0, z) = [z = s]$ and

if $G_t = H$ on qubit $q$, $\ N(t, z) = \sum_{b}(-1)^{b z_q}N(t-1, z^{q\leftarrow b})$;
if $G_t$ is a permutation $\pi$, $\ N(t, z) = N(t-1, \pi^{-1}z)$.

Evaluate $N(T, z)$ by this recursion, depth-first. Then $S = \sum_{z:\,z_0 = 1}N(T, z)^2$ gives $p(x) = S/2^h$; accept iff $2S \gt 2^h$. Space: next slide. $\blacksquare$

The recursion for one amplitude. Its call tree is the path tree of the last slide; the stack is one root-to-leaf chain.

Full proof

The recursion is right. $\bra zG_tV\ket s = \sum_y\bra zG_t\ket y\bra yV\ket s$ with $V = G_{t-1}\cdots G_1$. If $G_t = H$ on qubit $q$, only $y = z^{q\leftarrow b}$ ($z$ with bit $q$ set to $b$) gives a non-zero entry, namely $(-1)^{bz_q}/\sqrt2$; multiplying by $2^{h_t/2} = \sqrt2\cdot2^{h_{t-1}/2}$ cancels the $\sqrt2$. If $G_t$ is a permutation $\pi$ of basis strings, only $y = \pi^{-1}z$ contributes, with entry $1$, and $h_t = h_{t-1}$. At $t = 0$ the operator is $I$. By induction every $N(t, z)$ is an integer with $|N(t, z)| \le 2^{h_t}$ (it is a signed count of at most $2^{h_t}$ paths).

The acceptance probability is exact. $p(x) = \sum_{z_0 = 1}|\bra zC\ket s|^2 = 2^{-h}\sum_{z_0 = 1}N(T, z)^2 = S/2^h$, where $h = h_T$. $S$ is an integer between $0$ and $2^h$ (the full sum over all $z$ is exactly $2^h$, by normalisation). For $x\in L$, $p \ge \tfrac23$; for $x\notin L$, $p \le \tfrac13$; so "accept iff $2S \gt 2^h$" decides $L$ with no rounding anywhere.

The algorithm. For each $z\in\{0,1\}^m$ with $z_0 = 1$, in order: compute $N(T, z)$ recursively, square it, add it to $S$, erase everything but $S$ and $z$. The circuit's gates are read from the uniform generator on demand: to know $G_t$, rerun the generator and keep only the $t$-th gate, which costs time but no more than polynomial space. Space is counted on the next slide; time is $2^{m}\cdot O(2^hT)$ plus the generator's reruns, exponential, which PSPACE permits.

Relativisation. The proof works unchanged if the circuits may call an oracle $A$: an oracle gate is a permutation of basis strings, and the simulator answers it by asking its own oracle. So $\mathsf{BQP}^A \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}^A$ for every $A$ — a fact section 5 uses.

#P and GapP

#P and GapP

#P: functions $f(x) = \#\{y\in\{0,1\}^{p(|x|)} : R(x, y)\}$, with $R$ polynomial-time. Example: the number of satisfying assignments of a formula.

GapP: differences $f - g$ of two #P functions. Values in $\mathbb Z$.

lemma: the signed form

$F \in \mathrm{GapP}$ iff $F(x) = \sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{p(|x|)}}s(x, y)$ for some polynomial-time $s$ with values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$.

Why: ($\Leftarrow$) $F = \#\{s = 1\} - \#\{s = -1\}$. ($\Rightarrow$) Let a first bit of $y$ choose: $s(x, 0y') = R_f(x, y')$, $s(x, 1y') = -R_g(x, y')$. Full proof in the notes.

A GapP value is a signed count over certificates. For the running circuit, $2^{h/2}\bra{000}C\ket{000} = 3$ is one.

Full proof of the signed form

($\Leftarrow$) If $F = \sum_ys(x, y)$, put $R_+(x, y) = [s(x, y) = 1]$ and $R_-(x, y) = [s(x, y) = -1]$, both polynomial-time; then $F = \#R_+ - \#R_-$, a difference of #P functions.

($\Rightarrow$) Let $f = \#R_f$ with certificates of length $p_f$ and $g = \#R_g$ with length $p_g$, and put $p = 1 + \max(p_f, p_g)$. For $y = b\,y'$ with $b$ a bit and $y'$ of length $p - 1$, set $s(x, 0y') = R_f(x, y'_{1..p_f})$ if the remaining bits of $y'$ are all $0$, and $0$ otherwise; $s(x, 1y') = -R_g(x, y'_{1..p_g})$ under the same padding rule. The padding rule makes each certificate of $f$ (and of $g$) appear exactly once, so $\sum_ys = f - g$. $\blacksquare$

History. Valiant defined #P in 1979 (Theoret. Comput. Sci. 8, 189) and proved the permanent #P-complete. Fenner, Fortnow and Kurtz named GapP (J. Comput. Syst. Sci. 48, 116, 1994) to make exactly this unit's kind of argument clean: subtraction is free.

The running circuit's number. Ordering the 16 paths by $w = x_0x_1y_0y_1$ as a binary number, the three paths ending at $000$ are $w = 0000, 0100, 1000$ — cells 0, 4 and 8 in the figure — each with sign $+$. The function $s(x, w) = \sigma(w)[\text{end}(w) = 000]$ is computed by following one string through the circuit.

GapP is closed under what a path sum needs

closure

If $F, F' \in \mathrm{GapP}$ and $q$ is a polynomial, these are in GapP:

  • $-F$ and $F + F'$ (so $F - F'$);
  • $F\cdot F'$;
  • $x \mapsto \sum_{u\in\{0,1\}^{q(|x|)}}F(x, u)$, for $F$ in GapP as a function of the pair $(x, u)$;
  • $x\mapsto 2^{q(|x|)}$.
proof, in signed form

Negate $s$. For a sum, a first bit chooses which of $s, s'$ to use. For a product, concatenate certificates: $s''(x, yy') = s(x, y)\,s'(x, y')$. For the big sum, put $u$ into the certificate: $s''(x, uy) = s((x, u), y)$. For $2^q$: $s \equiv 1$ on $\{0,1\}^q$. $\blacksquare$

The product rule: concatenate certificates, multiply signs. The sum over pairs factors.

The proof of BQP ⊆ PP uses exactly this: an acceptance probability is a sum over outputs of squares of signed counts — products of GapP functions, summed.

Full proof

Let $F = \sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^{p}}s(x, y)$ and $F' = \sum_{y'\in\{0,1\}^{p'}}s'(x, y')$, signed forms.

Negation: $-F = \sum_y(-s)$. Sum: with lengths padded to $P = \max(p, p')$ by the rule "extra bits must be zero, else $0$", set $s''(x, 0y) = s(x, y)$ and $s''(x, 1y) = s'(x, y)$; then $\sum s'' = F + F'$. Product: $\sum_{y, y'}s(x, y)s'(x, y') = \big(\sum_ys\big)\big(\sum_{y'}s'\big)$ by distributivity, and $s\cdot s'$ takes values in $\{-1, 0, 1\}$ and is polynomial-time. Exponential sum: if $F(x, u) = \sum_ys((x, u), y)$ with certificate length $p(|x| + q(|x|))$, then $\sum_uF(x, u) = \sum_{u, y}s((x, u), y)$, a signed sum over certificates $uy$ of polynomial length. Powers of two: $\sum_{u\in\{0,1\}^q}1 = 2^q$. In each case the new $s$ is computed by one or two calls of the old ones. $\blacksquare$

What is not closed. #P alone is not closed under subtraction (its values are non-negative), which is why the signed version is the natural home for amplitudes. GapP is also not known to be closed under division or under taking the maximum; nothing here needs them.

PP: more than half, with no gap required

PP, probabilistic polynomial time

$L\in\mathsf{PP}$ iff for some polynomial-time $R$ and polynomial $p$,

$$x\in L \iff \#\{y : R(x, y)\} \gt \tfrac12\cdot2^{p(|x|)} .$$

A coin-flipping machine that is right with probability more than $\tfrac12$ — perhaps $\tfrac12 + 2^{-p}$.

theorem: PP is "GapP is positive"

$L\in\mathsf{PP}$ iff for some $F\in\mathrm{GapP}$: $\ x\in L \iff F(x) \gt 0$.

proof

($\Rightarrow$) $F = \#\{R\} - \#\{\neg R\}$. ($\Leftarrow$) Signed form, with $P, M, Z$ the numbers of $y$ with $s = 1, -1, 0$. Accept $(y, b)$ if $s = 1$, or if $s = 0$ and $b = 1$: then $2P + Z$ of the $2(P + M + Z)$ pairs accept, which is more than half iff $P \gt M$. $\blacksquare$

BPP and BQP need a constant gap; PP needs only the right side of $\tfrac12$. The running circuit's $\tfrac9{16}$ is a PP "accept".

PP ⊆ PSPACE: count the accepting $y$ with a $(p + 1)$-bit counter, reusing space as for NP.

Full proof

($\Rightarrow$) Given $R$ and $p$, let $F(x) = \#\{y : R\} - \#\{y : \neg R\}$, a difference of #P functions. Since the two counts add to $2^p$, $F \gt 0$ iff $\#\{R\} \gt 2^{p-1}$.

($\Leftarrow$) Write $F = \sum_{y\in\{0,1\}^p}s(x, y)$ and let $P, M, Z$ count the $y$ with $s = 1, -1, 0$; so $F = P - M$ and $P + M + Z = 2^p$. Define $R'(x, (y, b))$ on certificates of length $p + 1$: true if $s(x, y) = 1$; false if $s(x, y) = -1$; equal to $b$ if $s(x, y) = 0$. Then $\#R' = 2P + Z$, out of $2^{p+1} = 2P + 2M + 2Z$, and $2P + Z \gt P + M + Z$ iff $P \gt M$ iff $F(x) \gt 0$. $\blacksquare$

Two corollaries, one line each. PP is closed under complement: $x\notin L$ iff $F(x) \le 0$ iff $1 - 2F(x) \gt 0$ (the values are integers), and $1 - 2F$ is in GapP. NP ⊆ PP: the sheet's NP sits inside PP asks for it.

Names and history. Gill defined PP and BPP together (SIAM J. Comput. 6, 675, 1977). The nondeterministic-machine form — accept iff more than half the computation paths accept — is the certificate form with $y$ the string of choices, all paths of length $p(n)$.

The running circuit in PP's terms. $\tfrac9{16} \gt \tfrac12$, so if a family's acceptance probabilities were all either $\gt\tfrac12$ or $\le\tfrac12$ with no promise of a gap, $\tfrac9{16}$ would count as accept. That is exactly what PP allows and BQP does not; the next two slides show it is all BQP ⊆ PP needs.

BQP ⊆ PP: an acceptance probability is a GapP number over $2^h$

theorem (Adleman, DeMarrais and Huang 1997)

$\mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PP}$.

proof, part 1: the number

Take an $H$-and-permutation family, $h$ Hadamards. By the path sum, $\bra zC\ket s = 2^{-h/2}N(z)$, $N(z) = \sum_{\mathrm{end}(w) = z}\sigma(w)$. Square, and sum over accepting $z$:

$$\begin{aligned}p(x) &= 2^{-h}\!\sum_{z:\,z_0 = 1}N(z)^2\\ &= 2^{-h}\!\!\sum_{(w, w')\in\{0,1\}^{2h}}\!\! s(x, w, w'),\end{aligned}$$

$s = \sigma(w)\sigma(w')$ if $w, w'$ end at the same $z$ with $z_0 = 1$, else $0$ — computed by following two strings through $C_n$. So $p(x) = g(x)/2^h$ with $g\in\mathrm{GapP}$.

One pair $(w, w')$ in the running circuit: both end at $010$, with opposite signs, so the pair contributes $-1$ to the count for that output.

A pair of paths is a certificate, signed by the product of its paths' signs.

Full proof of part 1

The square. $N(z)^2 = \big(\sum_{w}\sigma(w)[\mathrm{end}(w) = z]\big)\big(\sum_{w'}\sigma(w')[\mathrm{end}(w') = z]\big) = \sum_{w, w'}\sigma(w)\sigma(w')[\mathrm{end}(w) = \mathrm{end}(w') = z]$. Summing over $z$ with $z_0 = 1$, each pair $(w, w')$ is counted once if its two endpoints coincide and that common endpoint has $z_0 = 1$, and not at all otherwise. That is the formula with $s$. (This is the product and exponential-sum closure of GapP is closed under what a path sum needs, done by hand.)

Polynomial time. On input $x$ of length $n$, run the uniform generator to get $C_n$ (polynomial time), and then, for each of $w$ and $w'$, follow one $m$-bit string through the gates as on A path through a circuit, recording the sign. Compare the endpoints and look at bit 0. The certificate $ww'$ has length $2h(n)$, a polynomial in $n$ that the generator also determines; if it varies with $n$ in a way that is awkward, pad to a fixed polynomial with the "extra bits zero" rule.

Cross terms. Pairs ending at different outputs contribute $0$: that is how the cross terms of the square vanish, and why the certificate is a pair of paths rather than one.

Where the start string went. The circuit starts at $\ket s = \ket{x, 0^a}$, so $x$ enters as the initial string of every path. If one also uses the real-amplitude encoding, $s$ has the extra qubit in $\ket0$, and "accept" means $z_0 = 1$ with the extra qubit either value — still a polynomial-time test on $z$.

BQP ⊆ PP, finished

proof, part 2: the threshold

Bounded error puts $p(x)$ strictly on the correct side of $\tfrac12$:

$$\begin{aligned}x\in L &\Rightarrow p \ge \tfrac23 \gt \tfrac12,\\ x\notin L &\Rightarrow p \le \tfrac13 \lt \tfrac12 .\end{aligned}$$

So $x\in L \iff g(x)/2^h \gt \tfrac12 \iff$

$$F(x) := 2g(x) - 2^{h} \gt 0 .$$

$F\in\mathrm{GapP}$ by closure ($g + g$, minus $2^h$), and "GapP is positive" is PP. $\blacksquare$

what the proof used

The path sum (unit 00's resolution of the identity); $H$ and permutations, so every path weighs $\pm2^{-h/2}$ (cited: Shi, Aharonov); GapP's closure. The bounded error was used only to stay off $\tfrac12$.

The whole proof, as a chain.

The closure step in full. $g\in\mathrm{GapP}$; so is $g + g = 2g$ (sum); so is $x\mapsto2^{h(|x|)}$ (powers of two; $h(n)$ is computable in polynomial time from $1^n$ by running the generator and counting Hadamards); so is the difference. Then the theorem PP is "GapP is positive" turns $F$ into a polynomial-time predicate $R'$ whose acceptance count exceeds half exactly when $F \gt 0$.

Approximation and amplification. The cited $H$-and-Toffoli theorem delivers error $\le\tfrac13$. BPP ⊆ BQP: coins from Hadamards

theorem

$\mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP}$.

proof

Let $R(x, r)$, $r\in\{0,1\}^{q}$, decide $L$ with error $\le\tfrac13$. Unit 10 gives a uniform reversible circuit $U_R\ket{x, r, 0}\ket{0\cdots0} = \ket{x, r, R(x, r)}\ket{0\cdots0}$, garbage uncomputed, from Toffolis (exact in $\mathcal G$). Apply $H^{\otimes q}$ to $\ket{0^q}$, then $U_R$:

$$2^{-q/2}\sum_{r}\ket{x, r, R(x, r)}\ket{0\cdots0}.$$

The terms are orthonormal (norm $2^q\cdot2^{-q} = 1$), so the output bit reads 1 with probability $\#\{r : R(x, r)\}/2^q = \Pr_r[R(x, r)]$ — the same as the coins'. $\blacksquare$

A randomised algorithm, run quantumly: Hadamards make the coins, Toffolis run $R$.

Measuring the coins first, or not at all, changes nothing: deferred measurement (unit 10).

Full proof

The circuit. $R$ is computed by a uniform family of classical Boolean circuits (assumed background: polynomial-time computation is uniform circuits). Unit 10 converts each AND, NOT and fan-out into Toffolis with ancillas, and Bennett's trick — compute, copy the answer with a CNOT, compute backwards — leaves $\ket{x, r}\ket{R(x, r)}\ket{0\cdots0}$. In $\mathcal G$ the Toffoli is exact (unit 10's seven-T circuit) and $X = HT^4H$.

The probability. $H^{\otimes q}\ket{0^q} = 2^{-q/2}\sum_r\ket r$ (unit 02, $q$ times), normalised: $2^q$ terms of weight $2^{-q}$. $U_R$ is linear and maps each $\ket{x, r, 0, 0\cdots0}$ to a basis state, distinct for distinct $r$. So the final state is a sum of orthonormal terms with amplitudes $2^{-q/2}$, and the output bit reads 1 with probability $\sum_{r:\,R(x, r) = 1}2^{-q}$.

Why garbage removal matters here only mildly. For this proof, garbage would do no harm: it is measured or ignored, and the outcome probabilities of the output bit are the same. It matters when the BPP computation is used inside a larger quantum algorithm, where garbage entangled with $r$ would spoil later interference (unit 10). The proof keeps the clean version so that it can be used either way.

What the example does and does not show. It shows the mechanism BQP adds to BPP — sums of signed amplitudes. Every algorithm in Part D is this mechanism arranged so that a classical simulation would need exponentially many routes.

The chain

Upward means "contained in". Thick: proved in this unit. Dashed curve: BQP ⊆ PSPACE directly, by paths. "?": neither inclusion between NP and BQP is known.

The chain, with every link proved here or in the background:

$$\begin{aligned}\mathsf P &\subseteq \mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP}\\ &\subseteq \mathsf{PP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE} \subseteq \mathsf{EXP}.\end{aligned}$$

Known strict: $\mathsf P \ne \mathsf{EXP}$ (time hierarchy, cited). So some link is strict.

Unknown: which. No single one of the five inclusions is known to be strict — including $\mathsf{BPP} \subsetneq \mathsf{BQP}$.

Where each link is proved. P ⊆ BPP: background. BPP ⊆ BQP: BPP ⊆ BQP: coins from Hadamards. BQP ⊆ PP: the two slides of section 4. PP ⊆ PSPACE: count accepting certificates with a counter (PP: more than half, with no gap required). PSPACE ⊆ EXP: count configurations (PSPACE: polynomial memory, any amount of time). NP ⊆ PP: the sheet. NP ⊆ PH and PH ⊆ PSPACE: next slide. BQP ⊆ PSPACE also directly, by the depth-first path sum.

The time hierarchy theorem (Hartmanis and Stearns, Trans. AMS 117, 285, 1965, cited): for nice $f$, there are languages decidable in time $f(n)^2$ but not in time $f(n)$. It gives $\mathsf P \subsetneq \mathsf{EXP}$, and that is essentially the only separation among these classes anyone can prove. (The space hierarchy theorem gives $\mathsf{L}\subsetneq\mathsf{PSPACE}$ for logarithmic space, which is below this picture.)

What is believed. Most complexity theorists believe P = BPP (derandomisation), BPP ≠ BQP (factoring), BQP ≠ PP, and NP ⊄ BQP. None is proved. The next three slides say what is known about the last two, and why the second is out of reach.

The polynomial hierarchy, and what an oracle is

the polynomial hierarchy

$L\in\Sigma_k^{\mathsf p}$ iff for a polynomial-time $R$ and certificates of polynomial length,

$$\begin{aligned}x\in L \iff{} &\exists y_1\,\forall y_2\,\exists y_3\cdots Q_ky_k:\\ &R(x, y_1, \dots, y_k).\end{aligned}$$

$\Sigma_1^{\mathsf p} = \mathsf{NP}$; $\ \mathsf{PH} = \bigcup_k\Sigma_k^{\mathsf p}$. PH ⊆ PSPACE: evaluate the quantifiers by depth-first enumeration, $k$ counters deep.

computation relative to an oracle

Fix a language $A$. A classical machine may ask "$y\in A$?" in one step; a quantum circuit may use the gate

$$U_A\ket{y, b} = \ket{y, b\oplus A(y)},$$

unit 10's $U_f$ with $f$ the indicator of $A$. The classes become $\mathsf{BQP}^A$, $\mathsf{NP}^A$, $\mathsf{PH}^A$, ….

An oracle gate inside a circuit. It may be queried on a superposition — the source of every query speedup in Part D.

Why oracles. A proof that relativises — works unchanged for every $A$ — cannot prove a statement that is false relative to some $A$. The path-sum proof relativises: $\mathsf{BQP}^A \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}^A$ for all $A$.

PH ⊆ PSPACE, in full. Evaluate $\exists y_1\forall y_2\cdots R$ by a recursive procedure that, for the outermost quantifier, loops over all $y_1$ and calls itself on the rest, returning true if some (for $\exists$) or every (for $\forall$) call does. The recursion depth is $k$, a constant for each $\Sigma_k^{\mathsf p}$; each level stores one certificate, polynomial space. It is the NP-in-PSPACE argument, nested.

Levels. $\Pi_k^{\mathsf p}$ is the same with $\forall$ first ($\Pi_1^{\mathsf p} = \mathsf{coNP}$). $\Sigma_2^{\mathsf p}$ contains, for example, "is there a circuit of size $\le s$ equivalent to this one?". BPP ⊆ $\Sigma_2^{\mathsf p}$ (Sipser–Gács–Lautemann 1983, cited), so BPP is inside PH; whether BQP is, is the question of the next slide.

Relativisation. Baker, Gill and Solovay (SIAM J. Comput. 4, 431, 1975, cited) found oracles $A$ and $B$ with $\mathsf P^A = \mathsf{NP}^A$ and $\mathsf P^B \ne \mathsf{NP}^B$, so no relativising argument settles P versus NP. The simulation arguments of this unit (enumeration, path sums) all relativise, which is why oracle results bound what such arguments can ever show.

What is known relative to oracles

two oracle separations

(a) There is an oracle $A$ with $\mathsf{NP}^A \not\subseteq \mathsf{BQP}^A$ (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani 1997). Proved in unit 18: searching $2^n$ strings for a marked one needs $\Omega(2^{n/2})$ quantum queries.

(b) There is an oracle $B$ with $\mathsf{BQP}^B \not\subseteq \mathsf{PH}^B$ (Raz and Tal, STOC 2019; cited).

What they mean. Any proof that NP ⊆ BQP — that quantum computers solve NP-complete problems — or that BQP ⊆ PH must fail relative to some oracle, so it must use techniques that look inside the machines. None of this unit's techniques do.

And the other way: relative to a PSPACE-complete oracle, P = PSPACE, so BQP = BPP (Baker–Gill–Solovay, cited). So $\mathsf{BQP} \ne \mathsf{BPP}$ cannot be proved by relativising arguments either.

Two worlds, each with a language (dot) on the far side of a boundary. In the real world, without oracles, nobody knows.

(a) C. H. Bennett, E. Bernstein, G. Brassard and U. Vazirani, "Strengths and weaknesses of quantum computing", SIAM J. Comput. 26, 1510 (1997). The oracle hides one marked string per input length, or none, and the language asks which; an NP machine guesses the string and checks it with one query, and their lower bound says a quantum circuit needs about $2^{n/2}$ queries. Grover (unit 17) shows $2^{n/2}$ queries suffice, so the bound is tight, and a quadratic speedup is the most a quantum computer gets on unstructured search.

(b) R. Raz and A. Tal, "Oracle separation of BQP and PH", STOC 2019 (journal version J. ACM 69, 2022). The oracle encodes Aaronson's "Forrelation" problem: decide whether one Boolean function is correlated with the Fourier transform of another. A quantum circuit does it with one query; Raz and Tal proved that constant-depth classical circuits — which is what PH relative to an oracle becomes — cannot. It says there are problems quantum computers solve that are outside the whole hierarchy, in at least one world.

Forward. Unit 14's Simon problem gives an oracle with $\mathsf{BPP}^O \ne \mathsf{BQP}^O$, the first exponential separation. Oracle separations are the kind of evidence one can actually prove; they are evidence, not proof, about the unrelativised classes.

What nobody knows, and why a proof is out of reach

theorem: BQP ≠ BPP would separate P from PSPACE

If $\mathsf{BQP} \ne \mathsf{BPP}$, then $\mathsf P \ne \mathsf{PSPACE}$.

proof

Contrapositive. Suppose $\mathsf P = \mathsf{PSPACE}$. The chain $\mathsf P \subseteq \mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}$ starts and ends at the same class, so every class in it equals $\mathsf P$; in particular $\mathsf{BQP} = \mathsf{BPP}$. $\blacksquare$

Nobody can prove $\mathsf P \ne \mathsf{PSPACE}$ — it is open, like P versus NP, and believed far harder to settle than to state. So an unconditional proof that quantum computers beat classical ones, in this sense, is not in sight.

"quantum computers are faster" is a hypothesis

It is the conjecture $\mathsf{BQP} \ne \mathsf{BPP}$. The evidence:

  • oracle separations (Part D: Simon, unit 14);
  • factoring and discrete logarithm are in BQP (unit 16) and, despite long effort, not known to be in BPP;
  • the apparent exponential cost of simulating quantum systems (this unit, sections 2–3).
open, in both directions

BQP vs NP. Neither NP ⊆ BQP nor BQP ⊆ NP is known. Factoring is in NP ∩ coNP (as a decision problem), so it is not evidence that BQP contains anything NP-hard.

The one-paragraph argument the checkpoint asks for, written out: Every language in P is in BPP (ignore the coins), every language in BPP is in BQP (Hadamard coins and a reversible circuit), and every language in BQP is in PSPACE (depth-first path sum). If someone proved P = PSPACE, the two ends of $\mathsf P \subseteq \mathsf{BPP} \subseteq \mathsf{BQP} \subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}$ would be equal, so every inclusion would be an equality and BQP would equal BPP. Hence a proof that BQP ≠ BPP is, among other things, a proof that P ≠ PSPACE, which would be a major separation that current techniques — which mostly relativise, and P = PSPACE holds relative to a PSPACE-complete oracle — cannot deliver.

Factoring as a decision problem: "does $N$ have a prime factor $\le k$?". A certificate for yes is the factor; for no, the full prime factorisation (with primality certificates, or since 2002 the AKS test, cited), so the language is in NP ∩ coNP. If an NP ∩ coNP language were NP-complete, NP would equal coNP, which is not believed. So Shor's algorithm, impressive as it is, is not believed to touch NP-complete problems.

What would change the picture. A classical polynomial-time factoring algorithm would remove the main piece of non-oracle evidence. It has happened before, in a smaller way: unit 26 tells the story of a claimed exponential quantum speedup that turned out to have a classical counterpart (dequantisation).

The circuit decided nothing hard — and could not, being three qubits. But every BQP computation's acceptance probability is a number of exactly its form, $g(x)/2^{h}$ with $g$ a signed count of pairs of paths. A quantum computer is a machine for estimating such numbers, and the question of this whole course is when that is hard to do any other way.

One number, three readings.

What "estimating" means. Running the circuit $k$ times and counting acceptances estimates $p(x)$ to within about $1/\sqrt k$ (Hoeffding again). So a quantum computer estimates $g(x)/2^h$ to additive precision $1/\mathrm{poly}$ efficiently. Computing $g(x)$ exactly is #P-hard in general, by the path sum run backwards: for a Boolean formula $f$ on $n$ variables, apply $H^{\otimes n}$, then the phase $(-1)^{f(x)}$ (unit 10's $U_f$ on an ancilla in $\ket- = HX\ket0$: since $\ket{0\oplus f} - \ket{1\oplus f} = (-1)^f(\ket0 - \ket1)$, it maps $\ket x\ket-$ to $(-1)^{f(x)}\ket x\ket-$, unit 10's Toffoli-on-$\ket-$ computation), then $H^{\otimes n}$; the amplitude of $\ket{0^n}$ is $2^{-n}\sum_x(-1)^{f(x)} = 2^{-n}\big(2^n - 2\,\#\{x : f(x) = 1\}\big)$, so its exact value counts satisfying assignments. That gap — additive estimate versus exact value — is where the power and the limits of BQP both live.
