Simon's problem: the first exponential separation, and the algorithm Shor read

A two-to-one function hides an $n$-bit string. A quantum computer finds it with about $n$ queries and some linear algebra over $\mathbb F_2$; every randomised classical algorithm needs about $2^{n/2}$. Both are proved here.

needs 13~2 h slides + 4½ h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 14

Unit 13 ended on an honest scorecard. Deutsch–Jozsa's exponential advantage disappears as soon as the classical side may flip coins; Bernstein–Vazirani's survives coin flips but is only $n$ queries against $1$. Neither is an exponential separation against randomised algorithms, which is the only kind worth having: BPP, not P, is the classical class a quantum computer has to beat. This unit supplies the first one, Daniel Simon's (1994), and proves both sides of it — the quantum algorithm's $O(n)$ queries and every randomised algorithm's $\Omega(2^{n/2})$.

The history is part of the argument. Simon's paper is the one Peter Shor read before finding the factoring algorithm; replace the group $\mathbb Z_2^n$ by $\mathbb Z_N$ and the Hadamard transform by the Fourier transform over $\mathbb Z_N$, and you are most of the way to unit 16. Units 15 and 16 cash that remark.

What this unit is not: the hidden subgroup problem in general (unit 16 frames it), and not a course in linear algebra over finite fields — only the three facts about $\mathbb F_2$ that Simon's algorithm uses.

14 · the problem

Simon's problem

Simon's problem

Given oracle access to $f:\{0,1\}^n\to\{0,1\}^n$, with the promise that for some $s\ne 0^n$,

$$f(x) = f(x') \iff x' \in \{x,\ x\oplus s\},$$

find $s$, the hidden XOR-period.

What the promise says. $f$ is two-to-one: the inputs fall into $2^{n-1}$ pairs $\{x, x\oplus s\}$, $f$ is constant on each pair and takes different values on different pairs. The pairs are the cosets of the subgroup $\{0, s\}$ of the group $(\{0,1\}^n, \oplus)$.

Check on the running example: $x\mapsto x\oplus110$ has no fixed point and squares to the identity, so it splits the eight inputs into four pairs; the table gives each pair one value and the four values $101, 010, 000, 110$ are distinct ✓.

Bit 0 runs left to right, bit 1 into the page, bit 2 upwards. Flipping bits 0 and 1 together is a diagonal of a horizontal face: the four pairs, one colour per value of $f$.

Why "XOR-period". A function on the integers with period $r$ satisfies $f(x) = f(x + r)$. Here the group operation is $\oplus$, bitwise addition mod 2, so the analogue is $f(x) = f(x\oplus s)$; the promise adds that this is the only coincidence of values. Unit 16's period finding is the same problem with $\oplus$ replaced by $+$ — the subject of section 6.

The coset statement, proved. $K = \{0, s\}$ is a subgroup of $(\{0,1\}^n, \oplus)$: it contains $0$, and $s\oplus s = 0$. Its cosets are $x\oplus K = \{x, x\oplus s\}$, each of size 2 because $s\ne0$, and they partition the $2^n$ strings into $2^n/2 = 2^{n-1}$ classes (Lagrange). The promise says exactly: $f$ is constant on each coset and injective on the set of cosets.

Simon's own formulation (D. R. Simon, "On the power of quantum computation", Proc. 35th FOCS, 1994, 116–123; SIAM J. Comput. 26, 1474, 1997) is a decision problem: decide whether $f$ is one-to-one or two-to-one with such an $s$. The search version here is at least as hard for a classical algorithm (a found $s$ is checked with two queries, $f(0) = f(s)$) and no harder for the quantum one; problem P10 on the sheet does the decision version. The codomain can be any set with at least $2^{n-1}$ elements; $n$ bits is the standard choice.

Reading the cube. In the running example $s = 110$ flips bits 0 and 1, which on the cube is a move one step right and one step back: a diagonal of a horizontal face. A different $s$ would pair the corners along a different family of parallel segments — for $s = 111$ the four long diagonals of the cube.

14 · the problem

The query model, recalled

Classical. A deterministic algorithm is a decision tree: each node names an input $x$, learns $f(x)$ and branches; each leaf outputs. A randomised one is a probability distribution over trees, correct if on every valid $f$ it is right with probability $\ge\tfrac23$ (unit 13).

Quantum. Oracle-independent unitaries alternate with $U_f\ket x\ket w = \ket x\ket{w\oplus f(x)}$, then a measurement. Unit 10 builds $U_f$ from any circuit for $f$, garbage-free.

Cost. Only oracle calls count; section 4 checks the rest is cheap too.

why queries

Proving that some problem needs more classical gates than quantum ones would separate P from PSPACE (unit 12), and nobody can. Query lower bounds can be proved, so separations live here.

Both models pay only for looking at $f$. A randomised algorithm is a coin-weighted choice of trees.

What is being recalled. All of this is unit 13's set-up, repeated so that the two bounds of this unit have a precise target. The query complexity of a problem in a model is the least worst-case number of queries of a correct algorithm in that model. Unit 13 wrote $D$ for the deterministic version, $R_\varepsilon$ and $Q_\varepsilon$ for the randomised and quantum versions with error at most $\varepsilon$, and $Q_E$ for exact quantum algorithms; bounded error means $\varepsilon = \tfrac13$, so $R_{1/3}$ is the randomised column of the next slide.

Why $\tfrac23$. Any constant above $\tfrac12$ would do for a problem with a checkable answer, and for Simon's problem the answer is checkable with two queries ($f(0) = f(s)$; problem P7). Amplification by repetition — the background fact behind BPP — turns $\tfrac23$ into $1 - 2^{-k}$ at a constant factor $O(k)$ in queries, so the choice does not affect any bound up to constants.

What the oracle costs in practice. If $f$ is given by a classical circuit of size $g$, unit 10's construction (compute, copy, uncompute) gives $U_f$ with $O(g)$ gates and some ancillas returned to $\ket0$. So a quantum algorithm with $q$ queries and $t$ other gates runs in time $O(qg + t)$. That is what makes a query-efficient algorithm an efficient one whenever $f$ is efficiently computable.

an exponential separation

A family of problems, one for each $n$, whose quantum query complexity is polynomial in $n$ and whose randomised query complexity is at least $2^{cn}$ for some constant $c > 0$.

Deutsch–Jozsa beats only deterministic algorithms; Bernstein–Vazirani beats randomised ones by a factor $n$. Simon's problem is the first row with an exponential gap in the randomised column, with both entries proved: the quantum one in section 4, the classical one in section 5.

why randomised

A quantum computer's output is random anyway. The fair classical rival may flip coins too and err with probability $\tfrac13$; beating only deterministic algorithms is beating a straw man.

what the promise buys

Simon's problem is a promise problem: on functions that are neither one-to-one nor two-to-one with a single $s$, nothing is asked. Some promise is necessary for any exponential gap — a theorem unit 18 states, with its proof cited.

The entries, and where each is proved. Deutsch–Jozsa's deterministic $2^{n-1} + 1$ and randomised $O(1)$, and Bernstein–Vazirani's $n$ in both classical columns, are unit 13's. Simon's quantum $O(n)$ is the theorem The quantum upper bound; its randomised $\Omega(2^{n/2})$ is The collision bound with its corollary, and the deterministic lower bound follows from it (a deterministic algorithm is a randomised one that does not flip coins). Problem P9 sharpens the deterministic bound to $\binom q2 \ge 2^n - 2$.

A remark for later. In unit 12's language (not needed here, and not on this unit's path), Simon's problem gives an oracle $O$ relative to which $\mathrm{BQP}^O \ne \mathrm{BPP}^O$: a language decided by a polynomial-time quantum machine with access to $O$ that no polynomial-time randomised machine with access to $O$ decides. Unit 12 states that reading; this unit proves the query bounds it rests on.

Unit 18's theorem, in one line (cited there: Aaronson, Ben-David, Kothari, Rao and Tal, 2021). For total Boolean functions — no promise — deterministic query complexity is at most a polynomial (the fourth power) of quantum query complexity. So every exponential quantum speed-up in the query model needs a promise, as Simon's and Shor's have and unstructured search does not.

14 · one run

The circuit

One run, drawn for $n = 3$: qubits 0–2 hold $x$, qubits 3–5 receive $f(x)$. One query.

Simon's algorithm, one run
  1. Prepare $\ket{0^n}\ket{0^n}$.
  2. Apply $H^{\otimes n}$ to register 1.
  3. Query: apply $U_f$.
  4. Apply $H^{\otimes n}$ to register 1.
  5. Measure register 1: a string $y$.

Repeat about $n$ times, then solve the linear equations $y\cdot s = 0$ for $s$ (section 3).

No $\ket-$ on the target this time: unit 13's phase kickback is not used. The interference comes from the second register's entanglement with the first.

Gate count of one run. $2n$ Hadamards, one oracle call, $n$ meters. With $U_f$ built from a classical circuit of size $g$, one run costs $O(n + g)$ gates; all runs together, $O(n(n + g))$.

Why no phase kickback. In Deutsch–Jozsa and Bernstein–Vazirani the target was $\ket-$ and the oracle wrote $(-1)^{f(x)}$ into a phase; that works for one output bit. Here $f$ has $n$ output bits and the second register starts in $\ket{0^n}$: the oracle writes $f(x)$ into the second register itself, which entangles the two registers. The useful interference is then among the inputs that share a value of $f$ — the pairs. The dashed meters show that whether register 2 is read changes nothing (Measure the second register first: the coset state).

Order of qubits. As everywhere in the course, qubit 0 is the top wire and the leftmost symbol of a ket; bit $x_0$ of $x$ is on qubit 0. The circuit is symmetric in the qubits of each register, so the order matters only when strings are written down.

The state, layer by layer

Unit 13's Hadamard transform: $H^{\otimes n}\ket x = 2^{-n/2}\sum_y(-1)^{x\cdot y}\ket y$, with $x\cdot y = x_0y_0\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{n-1}y_{n-1}$.

$$\begin{aligned} \ket{0^n}\ket{0^n} &\xrightarrow{\,H\,} 2^{-n/2}\textstyle\sum_x\ket x\ket{0^n}\\ &\xrightarrow{U_f} 2^{-n/2}\textstyle\sum_x\ket x\ket{f(x)}\\ &\xrightarrow{\,H\,} 2^{-n}\textstyle\sum_{y}\sum_x(-1)^{x\cdot y}\ket y\ket{f(x)}. \end{aligned}$$

Here $H$ means $H^{\otimes n}\otimes I$: Hadamards on register 1 only.

Normalised after the query: the $2^n$ vectors $\ket x\ket{f(x)}$ are orthonormal (distinct $x$), so the squared norm is $2^n\cdot2^{-n} = 1$ ✓. The last step is unitary, so the norm stays $1$.

Group by value. Write $v$ for a value of $f$ and $\{x_v, x_v\oplus s\}$ for the pair mapping to it. There are $2^{n-1}$ values, and

$$\ket{\Psi} = \sum_y\sum_v a(y, v)\,\ket y\ket v,$$ $$a(y, v) = 2^{-n}\big[(-1)^{x_v\cdot y} + (-1)^{(x_v\oplus s)\cdot y}\big].$$

The $\ket y\ket v$ are orthonormal, so by the Born rule for register 1 (unit 03),

$$P(y) = \sum_v |a(y, v)|^2 .$$

Everything now rests on the bracket: two signs, one from each member of a pair.

Each step. $H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = 2^{-n/2}\sum_x(-1)^{0\cdot x}\ket x = 2^{-n/2}\sum_x\ket x$. $U_f$ is linear and $U_f\ket x\ket{0^n} = \ket x\ket{0^n\oplus f(x)} = \ket x\ket{f(x)}$. The last Hadamard acts on register 1 only: $(H^{\otimes n}\otimes I)\ket x\ket{f(x)} = 2^{-n/2}\sum_y(-1)^{x\cdot y}\ket y\ket{f(x)}$, and the two factors $2^{-n/2}$ make $2^{-n}$.

The regrouping. The sum over $x$ is a sum over pairs: each $x$ lies in exactly one pair $\{x_v, x_v\oplus s\}$, and both members contribute to the same $\ket{f(x)} = \ket v$. So the coefficient of $\ket y\ket v$ collects exactly two terms. Which member is called $x_v$ does not matter: swapping them swaps the two terms.

The Born rule for one register. Measuring register 1 of a state $\sum_{y,v}a(y,v)\ket y\ket v$ with $\{\ket y\}$ and $\{\ket v\}$ orthonormal gives $y$ with probability $\lVert(\ket y\!\bra y\otimes I)\Psi\rVert^2 = \sum_v|a(y,v)|^2$ (unit 03, measuring one subsystem with $P\otimes I$).

14 · one run · example

Worked: the amplitude of $y$, for any $s$

compute $a(y, v) = 2^{-n}\big[(-1)^{x_v\cdot y} + (-1)^{(x_v\oplus s)\cdot y}\big]$

Step 1: the dot product is linear in each slot, mod 2. Since $x_i\oplus s_i \equiv x_i + s_i \pmod 2$,

$$(x\oplus s)\cdot y = \textstyle\sum_i(x_i + s_i)y_i = x\cdot y\ \oplus\ s\cdot y .$$

Step 2: factor out the first sign. As $(-1)^{a\oplus b} = (-1)^a(-1)^b$ for bits,

$$a(y, v) = 2^{-n}(-1)^{x_v\cdot y}\big[1 + (-1)^{s\cdot y}\big].$$

Step 3: two cases.

$$a(y, v) = \begin{cases}2^{1-n}(-1)^{x_v\cdot y} & s\cdot y = 0,\\[2pt] 0 & s\cdot y = 1.\end{cases}$$

The two members of a pair contribute the same sign or opposite signs, according to $s\cdot y$ alone.

Step 4: the probability. For $s\cdot y = 0$, $P(y) = \sum_v 2^{2-2n} = 2^{n-1}\cdot2^{2-2n} = 2^{1-n}$; for $s\cdot y = 1$, $P(y) = 0$.

Check, $n = 1$: $s = 1$, $f$ constant, and $\{y : y\cdot1 = 0\} = \{0\}$, so $P(0) = 2^0 = 1$. Directly, with $c = f(0) = f(1)$: $\ket0\ket0 \to \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)\ket0 \to \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)\ket c \to \ket0\ket c$ ✓.

The point of step 3. The sign $(-1)^{x_v\cdot y}$ depends on which pair you are in; the bracket does not. So whether a given $y$ can ever be seen is decided by $s$ alone, uniformly across all pairs. That is the whole mechanism: the random, useless $x_v$ goes into a sign, and the useful $s$ goes into an on/off switch.

Step 1 in detail. For bits, $a\oplus b = a + b - 2ab \equiv a + b \pmod 2$. So $(x\oplus s)\cdot y \equiv \sum_i(x_i + s_i)y_i = \sum_ix_iy_i + \sum_is_iy_i \equiv x\cdot y + s\cdot y \pmod 2$. The dot product is a bilinear form over the field $\mathbb F_2$ — the language of section 3.

Step 4 in detail. There are $2^{n-1}$ values $v$, one per pair, and each contributes $|2^{1-n}|^2 = 2^{2-2n}$. The check at $n = 1$: $s$ must be $1$ (the only nonzero string), the promise makes $f(0) = f(1)$, and the circuit is $H$, a query that writes the same $f$ on both branches (so the registers stay unentangled), $H$: since $H^2 = I$ it returns $\ket0$ with certainty, as the formula says.

14 · one run

Each run samples $s^\perp$ uniformly

theorem (Simon, 1994)

One run of the circuit returns $y$ with probability

$$P(y) = \begin{cases}2^{1-n} & y\cdot s = 0,\\ 0 & y\cdot s = 1.\end{cases}$$

So $y$ is uniform on $s^\perp = \{y : y\cdot s = 0\}$, a set of exactly $2^{n-1}$ strings.

proof

The distribution is Worked: the amplitude of y, for any s. For the count: pick $i$ with $s_i = 1$. Flipping bit $i$ of $y$ flips $y\cdot s$, so it is a bijection from $\{y\cdot s = 0\}$ to $\{y\cdot s = 1\}$; the two halves have $2^{n-1}$ elements each. ∎

normalised

$\sum_y P(y) = 2^{n-1}\cdot 2^{1-n} = 1$ ✓.

What one run tells you. One linear equation in the unknown bits of $s$: $y_0s_0\oplus\cdots\oplus y_{n-1}s_{n-1} = 0$. One query, one equation; $s$ has $n$ bits, and section 3 shows that $n - 1$ independent equations fix it.

Check on $s = 110$: $y\cdot s = y_0\oplus y_1$, which vanishes on $\{000, 001, 110, 111\}$ — four strings, $= 2^{3-1}$ ✓.

Why each run is independent of the last: every run starts afresh from $\ket{0^n}\ket{0^n}$.

The notation $s^\perp$. Section 3 justifies the symbol: $s^\perp$ is the orthogonal complement of $s$ for the bilinear form $x\cdot y$ over $\mathbb F_2$, and it is a subspace of dimension $n - 1$. Here only its size is needed, and the flipping argument gives that without any linear algebra.

Independence of runs. Each run is a fresh preparation, the same circuit and a fresh measurement. By the Born rule applied to the product of the runs' state spaces — or simply because nothing carries over — the outcomes of different runs are independent random variables, each with the distribution of the theorem. Section 4 uses that independence.

An honest remark about the classical analogue. A classical algorithm can also produce a uniformly random $x$ and learn $f(x)$; what it gets is a random point of a random pair, which carries no information about $s$ until a second point of the same pair turns up. The quantum run gets a different kind of sample — a random element of $s^\perp$ — and every such sample carries information about $s$.

Measure the second register first: the coset state

Read register 2 straight after the query. Outcome $v$ has probability $2\cdot2^{-n} = 2^{1-n}$, and register 1 is left in a coset state:

$$\ket{C_v} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{x_v} + \ket{x_v\oplus s}\big).$$

Now apply $H^{\otimes n}$, using Step 3 of the worked example:

$$H^{\otimes n}\ket{C_v} = 2^{-(n-1)/2}\!\!\sum_{y\,:\,y\cdot s = 0}\!\!(-1)^{x_v\cdot y}\ket y .$$

Normalised: $2^{n-1}$ terms of squared size $2^{-(n-1)}$ ✓. Every $v$ gives the same distribution, uniform on $s^\perp$: the unknown $x_v$ survives only as a sign.

nothing changes

Unit 10's implicit measurement: register 2 is never touched again, so reading it or not leaves the statistics of register 1 unchanged.

The running example's four pairs. Each outcome $v$ has probability $\tfrac14$ (sum $1$ ✓) and leaves its pair in equal superposition.

Full proof that both routes agree. Route A (measure only register 1 at the end) gives $P_A(y) = \sum_v|a(y,v)|^2$, computed in the worked example. Route B: after the query the state is $\sum_v 2^{-n/2}\sqrt2\,\ket{C_v}\ket v$, since each $\ket v$ is attached to $2^{-n/2}(\ket{x_v} + \ket{x_v\oplus s})$. Measuring register 2 gives $v$ with probability $(2^{-n/2}\sqrt2)^2 = 2^{1-n}$ and leaves $\ket{C_v}$. Then $H^{\otimes n}$ and a measurement give $y$ with probability $|\braket{y|H^{\otimes n}|C_v}|^2 = 2^{-(n-1)}[y\cdot s = 0]$. So $P_B(y) = \sum_v 2^{1-n}\cdot2^{1-n}[y\cdot s = 0] = 2^{n-1}\cdot2^{2-2n}[y\cdot s = 0] = 2^{1-n}[y\cdot s = 0] = P_A(y)$. The general reason is unit 10's: a measurement on a register that no later gate touches commutes with everything after it.

The amplitude of $H^{\otimes n}\ket{C_v}$. By the Hadamard formula, $\braket{y|H^{\otimes n}|C_v} = 2^{-n/2}\cdot2^{-1/2}[(-1)^{x_v\cdot y} + (-1)^{(x_v\oplus s)\cdot y}] = 2^{-(n+1)/2}(-1)^{x_v\cdot y}[1 + (-1)^{s\cdot y}]$, which is $2^{-(n-1)/2}(-1)^{x_v\cdot y}$ when $s\cdot y = 0$ and $0$ otherwise.

Why this picture is worth having. It is the one Shor generalised. Measuring the value register leaves a uniform superposition over a coset of the hidden subgroup — here $x_v\oplus\{0, s\}$; in unit 16, $x_0 + r\mathbb Z$. A Fourier transform then turns "which coset" into an unobservable phase and "which subgroup" into the support of the output distribution.

The classical temptation. Measuring register 1 of $\ket{C_v}$ directly would give $x_v$ or $x_v\oplus s$, each with probability $\tfrac12$ — one random point of a random pair, exactly what a classical query gives. The Hadamards are what turn the pair into information about $s$.

The distribution of $y$: uniform on $s^\perp = \{000, 001, 110, 111\}$.

From the grid, $P(y)$ is the sum of the squares along row $y$:

and the other four are $0$. Sum: $4\times\tfrac14 = 1$ ✓, matching the theorem's $2^{1-n} = \tfrac14$.

$001$$s_2 = 0$

Each run gives an equation $y\cdot s = 0$. Three facts about bits as a field say when $n - 1$ of them pin $s$ down, and how to solve them.

The field with two elements

the field with two elements, $\mathbb F_2$

$\{0, 1\}$ with addition $\oplus$ (XOR) and multiplication $\wedge$ (AND):

$\oplus$01$\cdot$01
001000
110101

It is $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$, a field because 2 is prime: $1$ is its own inverse, and $-1 = 1$.

$\mathbb F_2^n = \{0,1\}^n$ is an $n$-dimensional vector space over it: vectors add by XOR, and the only scalars are $0$ and $1$.

what carries over from $\mathbb R$

Every theorem of linear algebra whose proof uses only the field axioms holds over $\mathbb F_2$ unchanged: bases and dimension, rank–nullity $\dim\ker A + \operatorname{rank}A = n$, and Gaussian elimination. A $k$-dimensional subspace has exactly $2^k$ elements.

what does not

Anything using order or positivity. Over $\mathbb R$, $y\cdot y = \sum y_i^2 > 0$ for $y\ne 0$. Over $\mathbb F_2$, $y\cdot y$ is the parity of the number of 1s in $y$: $110\cdot110 = 1\oplus1 = 0$. There is no Gram–Schmidt, and a nonzero vector can be orthogonal to itself.

Check: the subspace $\{000, 001, 110, 111\}$ has basis $\{001, 110\}$, dimension 2, and $2^2 = 4$ elements ✓.

Why the theorems carry over. The proofs of the exchange lemma (any two bases have the same size), of rank–nullity, and of the correctness of row reduction use addition, multiplication, additive and multiplicative inverses, and the distributive law — the field axioms — and nothing else. They never compare sizes of scalars or take square roots. So they are theorems about vector spaces over any field, and the background course's proofs over $\mathbb R$ are proofs over $\mathbb F_2$ word for word.

Counting a subspace. If $b_1, \dots, b_k$ is a basis of $W$, the map $(c_1, \dots, c_k)\mapsto\sum c_ib_i$ from $\mathbb F_2^k$ to $W$ is a bijection (onto by spanning, one-to-one by independence), so $|W| = 2^k$.

What fails, and where. The spectral theorem, Gram–Schmidt and orthogonal projection all rest on $y\cdot y > 0$ for $y\ne0$, which is false here. Of section 3's three facts only one could have been affected — the dimension of an orthogonal complement — and the next slide proves it without positivity.

Notation. The course writes $\oplus$ for addition in $\mathbb F_2^n$ (as in the oracle) and $x\cdot y$ for $\bigoplus_ix_iy_i$; in $\mathbb F_2$ the symbols $+$ and $\oplus$ mean the same thing, and $-$ means $+$.

Orthogonal complements over $\mathbb F_2$

the orthogonal complement

For a subspace $W\subseteq\mathbb F_2^n$, a subspace again:

$$W^\perp = \{y : y\cdot w = 0 \text{ for all } w\in W\}.$$

For one vector, $s^\perp = \{0, s\}^\perp$.

theorem

(a) $\dim W^\perp = n - \dim W$.  (b) $(W^\perp)^\perp = W$.

So, for $s\ne0$, $\ \dim s^\perp = n - 1$ and $(s^\perp)^\perp = \{0, s\}$.

proof

(a) Put a basis $w_1, \dots, w_k$ of $W$ as the rows of a $k\times n$ matrix $A$. Then $W^\perp = \ker A$ and $\operatorname{rank}A = k$; rank–nullity gives $\dim\ker A = n - k$. (b) $W\subseteq(W^\perp)^\perp$ by definition; by (a) twice both have dimension $k$. ∎

$s = 110$: $s^\perp$ is the shaded plane of four corners, and $s$ lies in it, since $s\cdot s = 0$.

a self-orthogonal vector

Here $W\cap W^\perp\ne\{0\}$ can happen, so $W^\perp$ need not be a complement in the sense of $W\oplus W^\perp = \mathbb F_2^n$. Nothing below uses that; only (a) and (b).

Full proof of (a), with the step rank–nullity hides. $y\in W^\perp$ iff $y\cdot w_i = 0$ for each basis vector $w_i$ (then $y\cdot w = 0$ for every combination, by linearity), iff $Ay = 0$ for the matrix $A$ with rows $w_i$. So $W^\perp = \ker A$. The row space of $A$ is $W$, of dimension $k$, so $\operatorname{rank}A = k$ (row rank equals column rank — another field-axiom theorem). Rank–nullity: $\dim\ker A = n - k$.

Full proof of (b). If $w\in W$ and $y\in W^\perp$ then $w\cdot y = y\cdot w = 0$, so every $w$ is in $(W^\perp)^\perp$. By (a) applied to $W^\perp$, $\dim(W^\perp)^\perp = n - (n - k) = k = \dim W$. A subspace of a finite-dimensional space containing another of the same dimension equals it.

For Simon. $W = \{0, s\}$ has dimension 1, so $s^\perp$ has dimension $n - 1$ and $2^{n-1}$ elements — the count of the theorem in section 2, now by linear algebra — and $(s^\perp)^\perp = \{0, s\}$. That last equation is the one that recovers $s$.

Self-orthogonality, and why it is harmless. In the running example $s = 110$ has two 1s, so $s\cdot s = 0$ and $s\in s^\perp$: the run can return $y = s$ itself. Nothing breaks: the argument above never needed $W\cap W^\perp = \{0\}$. Over $\mathbb R$ that intersection is always $\{0\}$ because $w\cdot w > 0$; here $W\cap W^\perp$ is the set of self-orthogonal vectors of $W$ — for $W = \{0, s\}$, it is $\{0, s\}$ if $s$ has even weight and $\{0\}$ if odd. Coding theorists call a subspace with $W\subseteq W^\perp$ self-orthogonal; unit 23 meets them as the source of stabiliser codes.

$n - 1$ independent samples pin down $s$

theorem

If $y_1, \dots, y_{n-1}\in s^\perp$ are linearly independent, then the system

$$y_j\cdot t = 0\qquad (j = 1, \dots, n-1)$$

has exactly two solutions $t$: $t = 0$ and $t = s$.

proof

$n - 1$ independent vectors in the $(n-1)$-dimensional $s^\perp$ span it. The solutions are $\operatorname{span}(y_j)^\perp = (s^\perp)^\perp = \{0, s\}$ by (b). ∎

Check, $n = 2$, $s = 11$: $s^\perp = \{00, 11\}$; one independent sample is $y_1 = 11$, and $t_0\oplus t_1 = 0$ has solutions $\{00, 11\}$ ✓.

The rank is the thing to watch. Fewer than $n - 1$ independent samples leave $(\operatorname{span} y_j)^\perp$ of dimension at least 2: at least three nonzero candidates, and the samples cannot tell them apart.

Dependent samples are wasted, not harmful. A sample in the span of the others adds no new equation. Samples are never wrong: every one satisfies $y\cdot s = 0$.

the samples never reach rank $n$

All samples lie in $s^\perp$, of dimension $n - 1$. Rank $n - 1$ is the most one can get, and it is exactly enough because $t = 0$ is excluded by $s\ne0$.

Full proof. Let $Y = \operatorname{span}(y_1, \dots, y_{n-1})$. Since the $y_j$ are independent, $\dim Y = n - 1$; since they lie in $s^\perp$, $Y\subseteq s^\perp$, which also has dimension $n - 1$ (previous slide, (a)); so $Y = s^\perp$. A vector $t$ solves every equation iff $t\cdot y_j = 0$ for all $j$, iff $t\in Y^\perp$ (linearity again). And $Y^\perp = (s^\perp)^\perp = \{0, s\}$ by (b).

Fewer samples, more candidates. If $\dim Y = k < n - 1$ then $\dim Y^\perp = n - k \ge 2$, so $Y^\perp$ has at least $4$ elements, at least $3$ of them nonzero. Each is consistent with every sample seen, and since the samples' distribution would be uniform on a different $(n-1)$-space for each candidate, no rule can pick the right one with certainty.

The decision version. If $f$ were one-to-one ($s = 0$ allowed, the case Simon actually posed), the samples would be uniform on all of $\mathbb F_2^n$ and could reach rank $n$, whose only solution is $t = 0$. That is how problem P10 tells the two cases apart.

Gaussian elimination over $\mathbb F_2$

Gaussian elimination, as samples arrive

Keep a list of rows in echelon form, each with a pivot: its leftmost 1. For each new sample $y$:

  1. For each row in order of pivot column: if $y$ has a 1 there, replace $y$ by $y\oplus\text{row}$.
  2. If $y$ is now $0$, it was dependent: discard it.
  3. Otherwise keep it; its leftmost 1 is a new pivot.

At rank $n - 1$ exactly one column has no pivot. Set that bit of $t$ to $1$ and back-substitute, bottom row first.

Cost. Each sample is XORed with at most $n - 1$ rows of $n$ bits: $O(n^2)$ bit operations; $O(n)$ samples make $O(n^3)$.

Echelon form at rank $n - 1 = 4$ (the worked example two slides on). Pivots shaded; the one pivot-free column is set to $1$.

Why step 1 keeps the echelon form. A row with pivot $p$ has zeros in every column left of $p$, so XORing it into $y$ changes no column left of $p$. Processing rows in increasing pivot order therefore clears the pivot columns one by one without disturbing those already cleared. A surviving $y$ has zeros in every existing pivot column; its leftmost 1 is in a new column, which becomes its pivot. The kept rows are independent (each has a 1 where all the others with smaller pivots have 0s — an echelon matrix has full row rank) and span the same space as the samples kept so far.

Why back-substitution works. At rank $n - 1$ there are $n - 1$ pivot columns among $n$, so exactly one free column $j$. The solution space $\{0, s\}$ has dimension 1, so a nonzero solution must have $t_j = 1$ (if $t_j = 0$, back- substitution forces every pivot variable to $0$). With $t_j = 1$, the bottom row's equation determines its pivot variable from the variables to its right, which are already known; then the row above; and so on to the top.

The count. At most $n - 1$ rows, each XOR is $n$ bit operations, so reducing one sample is at most $(n - 1)n$ operations; at $n = 100$ with about $n$ samples that is about $99\cdot99\cdot100 \approx 10^6$. A computer packs 64 bits into a word and does a row XOR in two instructions, so it is less in practice.

The chance that $n - 1$ samples are independent

theorem

$n - 1$ independent uniform samples from $s^\perp$ are linearly independent with probability

$$p_{n-1} = \prod_{k=1}^{n-1}\big(1 - 2^{-k}\big).$$
proof

Write $m = n - 1$. If the first $j$ samples are independent, they span $2^j$ of the $2^m$ elements, so the next one is outside their span with probability $1 - 2^{j-m}$, whatever they were. Multiply over $j = 0, \dots, m - 1$ and put $k = m - j$. ∎

Check, $n = 2$: one sample from $\{0, s\}$ is independent iff it is $s$: probability $\tfrac12 = 1 - 2^{-1}$ ✓. And at $n = 3$: $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac34 = \tfrac38$, as on Running example: two samples, one answer ✓.

$n = 5$: the span doubles with each new independent sample. Product $\tfrac{15\cdot14\cdot12\cdot8}{16^4} = \tfrac{20160}{65536} = 0.3076$.

Full proof. Let $y_1, \dots, y_m$ be independent and uniform on $s^\perp$, an $m$-dimensional space with $2^m$ elements. Let $E_j$ be the event that $y_1, \dots, y_j$ are linearly independent ($E_0$ is certain). On $E_j$ their span $S_j$ has exactly $2^j$ elements, and $E_{j+1} = E_j\cap\{y_{j+1}\notin S_j\}$. Conditioning on the values of $y_1, \dots, y_j$, the sample $y_{j+1}$ is still uniform (independence of runs), so $P(y_{j+1}\notin S_j \mid y_1, \dots, y_j) = 1 - 2^j/2^m$ on $E_j$, a number that does not depend on which values occurred. Hence $P(E_{j+1}) = (1 - 2^{j-m})P(E_j)$, and by induction $P(E_m) = \prod_{j=0}^{m-1}(1 - 2^{j-m}) = \prod_{k=1}^{m}(1 - 2^{-k})$.

The factor that hurts. The last factor, $j = m - 1$, is $1 - \tfrac12$: when the span is already half the space, a new sample is a coin flip. Most of the loss is there, at the end; the early samples are almost always new.

The figure's number. $\tfrac{15}{16}\cdot\tfrac{14}{16}\cdot\tfrac{12}{16}\cdot\tfrac8{16} = \tfrac{20160}{65536} = 0.30762$, and $\prod_{k=1}^4(1 - 2^{-k}) = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac78\cdot\tfrac{15}{16} = \tfrac{315}{1024} = 0.30762$ — the same product, factors in the other order.

The tail, and why the limit is 0.2888

the Weierstrass product inequality

For $a_1, \dots, a_r\in[0, 1]$: $\ \prod_{k=1}^{r}(1 - a_k) \ \ge\ 1 - \sum_{k=1}^{r}a_k$.

proof, by induction on $r$

$r = 1$ is equality. If it holds for $r$, write $A = \sum_{k\le r}a_k$ and multiply both sides by $1 - a_{r+1}\ge0$:

$$\begin{aligned}\textstyle\prod_{k\le r+1}(1 - a_k) &\ge (1 - A)(1 - a_{r+1})\\ &= 1 - A - a_{r+1} + a_{r+1}A,\end{aligned}$$

and $a_{r+1}A\ge 0$. ∎

Check, $a_k = 2^{-k}$, $r = 2$: $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac34 = \tfrac38 \ge 1 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac14$ ✓.

The infinite tail. The inequality is for finite products; apply it to $\prod_{k=m+1}^{R}(1 - 2^{-k}) \ge 1 - \sum_{k=m+1}^R2^{-k} \ge 1 - 2^{-m}$ for every $R$, then let $R\to\infty$ (the partial products decrease to the infinite product, and a limit of numbers $\ge c$ is $\ge c$). The history of the inequality. It is elementary and goes back to Weierstrass's work on infinite products; the form with $\ge 1 - \sum a_k$ is sometimes called the Weierstrass inequality or the union bound in disguise — indeed, for independent events $A_k$ of probabilities $a_k$, it says $P(\text{none of the }A_k)\ge 1 - \sum P(A_k)$, which is the union bound.

Expected runs: fewer than $n + 1$

theorem

Run until the samples have rank $n - 1$. The expected number of runs $T$ is

$$\mathbb E[T] = \sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac1{1 - 2^{-k}} \ <\ n + 1 .$$
proof

Split $T$ into stages: $T_j$ runs to go from rank $j$ to $j + 1$. Each run in stage $j$ succeeds independently with probability $q_j = 1 - 2^{j-(n-1)}$, so $T_j$ is a geometric waiting time with $\mathbb E[T_j] = 1/q_j$. Sum by linearity; put $k = n - 1 - j$. Then $\frac1{1 - 2^{-k}} = 1 + \frac1{2^k - 1} \le 1 + 2^{1-k}$, and $\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}2^{1-k} < 2$. ∎

$n = 5$: $\tfrac{16}{15} + \tfrac{16}{14} + \tfrac{16}{12} + \tfrac{16}8 = 5.543$, between $n - 1 = 4$ and $n + 1 = 6$.

Check, $n = 3$ (running example): $\tfrac1{1/2} + \tfrac1{3/4} = 2 + \tfrac43 = \tfrac{10}3 = 3.33 < 4$ ✓. And $n = 100$: $\mathbb E[T] = 100.61$.

Full proof. Let $T_j$ be the number of runs made while the rank is $j$, for $j = 0, \dots, n-2$. While the rank is $j$ the held rows span $2^j$ of the $2^{n-1}$ elements of $s^\perp$, and each new run, independent of everything before it, is outside that span with probability $q_j = 1 - 2^{j-(n-1)}$. So $P(T_j = t) = (1 - q_j)^{t-1}q_j$ for $t\ge1$, whose mean is $\sum_tt(1 - q_j)^{t-1}q_j = 1/q_j$ (differentiate the geometric series). $T = \sum_jT_j$, and expectation is linear — no independence between the $T_j$ is needed for that.

The bound. $2^k - 1\ge 2^{k-1}$ for $k\ge1$, so $\frac1{2^k - 1}\le 2^{1-k}$. Then $\mathbb E[T]\le\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}(1 + 2^{1-k}) = (n - 1) + 2(1 - 2^{-(n-1)}) < n + 1$. The exact excess over $n - 1$ tends to $\sum_{k\ge1}\frac1{2^k - 1} = 1.6067$, so $\mathbb E[T]\approx n + 0.607$ for large $n$; at $n = 100$ it is $100.607$.

Why the bound is not $n - 1$. The last stage is the expensive one: with the span already half of $s^\perp$, each run is a coin flip, and costs $2$ on average. The early stages cost barely more than 1 each.

The quantum upper bound

theorem

Simon's problem has quantum query complexity $O(n)$:

  • zero error, a Las Vegas algorithm: running until rank $n - 1$ returns $s$ with certainty, after fewer than $n + 1$ queries on average;
  • bounded error: stopping after $3(n + 1)$ runs fails with probability below $\tfrac13$.

With a polynomial-size circuit for $f$, the whole algorithm runs in polynomial time: $O(n)$ circuits of $O(n)$ gates plus the oracle, and $O(n^3)$ bit operations of elimination.

proof

Correctness: at rank $n - 1$ the solution is $s$ (section 3). Mean: previous slide. Markov's inequality: $P(T\ge3(n + 1))\le\mathbb E[T]/3(n + 1) < \tfrac13$. ∎

The whole algorithm. The loop runs fewer than $n + 1$ times on average.

Check on the running example: rank 2 is reached after $\tfrac{10}3$ runs on average, and then the output is $110$ ✓.

Zero error, but a random running time. Under the promise the loop always ends (rank $n - 1$ is reached with probability 1, since each stage ends with probability 1), and when it ends the answer is right. Such an algorithm — always right, random cost — is a Las Vegas algorithm. Cutting it off at a fixed budget and accepting a small failure probability makes it a Monte Carlo algorithm, the bounded-error kind the query model of unit 13 counts.

Markov's inequality (background): for a nonnegative random variable, $P(T\ge a)\le\mathbb E[T]/a$. With $a = 3(n + 1)$ and $\mathbb E[T] < n + 1$ the bound is below $\tfrac13$. A sharper budget: in blocks of $n - 1$ runs, each block independently gives a basis with probability above $0.2888$, so $k$ blocks all fail with probability below $0.7112^k$; four blocks already fail with probability below $0.26$.

Without the promise. If $f$ might violate the promise, a candidate $s$ can be checked with two classical queries, $f(0^n) = f(s)$; problem P7 builds the zero-error version that also survives a broken promise, and P10 the one-to-one versus two-to-one test.

An exact algorithm exists. Brassard and Høyer (1997, cited on the sheet) modify each run so that every sample is guaranteed new: $O(n)$ queries, zero error, worst-case bound. Not needed here.

The classical lower bound

A classical algorithm learns nothing about $s$ until two of its queries collide, and $q$ queries make a collision with probability about $q^2/2^{n+1}$.

14 · the classical lower bound

What a classical algorithm sees

Each query returns a value. Until two queries return the same value, every answer is a value not seen before — exactly what a one-to-one function would give.

a collision

Two queries $x\ne x'$ with $f(x) = f(x')$. Under the promise it reveals everything: $s = x\oplus x'$.

Without a collision, $q$ distinct queries rule out only the $\binom q2$ differences $x_i\oplus x_j$ as candidates for $s$: had $s$ been one of them, that pair would have collided. Every other nonzero string is still possible.

The algorithm's whole record — queries and answers, in order — is its transcript. The proof shows that, without a collision, the transcript carries almost no information about $s$.

The running example, played classically: queries $000, 001, 010, 100$. The fourth answer repeats the third, so $s = 010\oplus100 = 110$.

Reading the figure. After $000$ and $001$, the candidate $000\oplus001 = 001$ is ruled out: if $s$ were $001$, those two queries would have returned the same value, and they did not. After $010$, two more: $000\oplus010 = 010$ and $001\oplus010 = 011$. The fourth query $100$ returns $000$, the value already returned by $010$: a collision, and the promise says the colliding inputs differ by $s$.

Here the classical algorithm wins quickly. At $n = 3$ there are only seven candidates, and problem P9 shows that these four queries find $s$ for every $s$: if no collision occurs, the six differences of the four queries are $001, 010, 011, 100, 101, 110$, so $s = 111$. The separation is asymptotic; at $n = 3$ there is none worth the name.

Why "almost no information". Without a collision the answers are distinct values, and which distinct values appear is (by the lazy-sampling lemma two slides on) a uniformly random choice made independently of $s$. The only thing the transcript says about $s$ is "not one of these $\binom q2$ differences".

From randomised to deterministic: averaging

Yao's principle, the easy direction

If a randomised algorithm with $q$ queries succeeds with probability $\ge\tfrac23$ on every valid input, then for any probability distribution $\mu$ on inputs, some deterministic algorithm with $q$ queries succeeds with probability $\ge\tfrac23$ when the input is drawn from $\mu$.

proof

The randomised algorithm picks a tree $D$ with probability $p_D$. Then $\sum_\mu\mu(f)\sum_Dp_D[D\text{ right on }f]\ge\tfrac23$. Swap the sums: $\sum_Dp_D\,\Pr_\mu[D\text{ right}]\ge\tfrac23$, so some $D$ has $\Pr_\mu[D\text{ right}]\ge\tfrac23$. ∎

So: to show every randomised algorithm needs many queries, pick one hard distribution $\mu$ and show every deterministic algorithm fails on it.

Equal weights, for the picture: averaging the grid by columns or by rows gives the same total, $12$ of $18$.

Full proof. A randomised query algorithm with $q$ queries is a probability distribution $(p_D)$ over deterministic trees $D$ of depth at most $q$ (unit 13). Write $c(D, f) = 1$ if $D$ outputs the right answer on $f$ and $0$ otherwise. Correctness says $\sum_Dp_Dc(D, f)\ge\tfrac23$ for every valid $f$. Multiply by $\mu(f)$ and sum over $f$ (the weights sum to 1): $\sum_f\mu(f)\sum_Dp_Dc(D,f)\ge\tfrac23$. Both sums are finite, so they can be exchanged: $\sum_Dp_D\big(\sum_f\mu(f)c(D, f)\big)\ge\tfrac23$. A weighted average of the numbers $\sum_f\mu(f)c(D,f)$ that is at least $\tfrac23$ has at least one term at least $\tfrac23$.

The name. A. C.-C. Yao, "Probabilistic computations: toward a unified measure of complexity", Proc. 18th FOCS, 1977, 222–227, proved that the best a randomised algorithm can guarantee equals the best a deterministic one can do against the worst distribution — a minimax theorem. Only the inequality proved here is needed, and it is just an exchange of two finite sums.

14 · the classical lower bound

A random Simon function, sampled lazily

lemma (lazy sampling)

Fix $s$; let $f$ be uniform among functions with XOR-period $s$. Against any deterministic algorithm, the answer to a new query $x$, given the answers so far, is

  • $f(x')$, if an earlier query $x'$ has $x = x'\oplus s$;
  • otherwise uniform over the values not yet returned.
proof

$f$ is a uniform injective assignment of values to the $2^{n-1}$ pairs. Given its values on some pairs, each unused value is equally likely on a new pair: each leaves the same number of ways to finish. ∎

The free oracle answers every new query with a fresh uniform unused value, ignoring $s$: a random one-to-one function.

Drive both oracles with the same fresh values: the transcripts agree up to the first collision, and the free one never sees $s$.

Full proof of the lemma. A function with XOR-period $s$ is the same thing as an injective map $g$ from the set $\mathcal P$ of $2^{n-1}$ pairs into $\{0,1\}^n$ ($f(x) = g(\text{pair of }x)$); "uniform" means each of the $(2^n)!/(2^n - 2^{n-1})!$ injections is equally likely. Suppose the values on $c$ pairs are known to be $v_1, \dots, v_c$ (distinct), and $P$ is another pair. For an unused value $u$, the injections with those $c$ values and $g(P) = u$ number $(2^n - c - 1)!/(2^n - 2^{n-1})!$ — the same for every $u$. So conditionally $g(P)$ is uniform on the $2^n - c$ unused values. A deterministic algorithm's next query is a function of the answers so far, so conditioning on the transcript is conditioning on $g$'s values at the queried pairs: the lemma follows. A repeated query, or one in a pair already queried, is answered by the first bullet.

The coupling, precisely. Fix the algorithm; assume it never repeats a query (a repeat is answered from memory at no cost). Draw the fresh values $u_1, u_2, \dots$ once: $u_t$ uniform among the values not among the first $t - 1$ answers. The free oracle answers query $t$ with $u_t$. The real oracle answers with $u_t$ unless query $t$ is $x_i\oplus s$ for an earlier $x_i$, in which case it repeats $x_i$'s answer. (Up to the first such repeat the two runs have returned the same answers, so "values not yet returned" means the same set for both; after it, the real run draws its own fresh values, and nothing below looks past that point.) By the lemma the real oracle so defined has exactly the distribution of a uniform Simon function with period $s$. The two runs make identical queries and receive identical answers up to the first such $t$, since each query is determined by the earlier answers.

The name. "Lazy sampling" is the cryptographers' term: instead of choosing the whole random function in advance, choose each value only when it is first asked for. It is standard in the analysis of hash functions and block ciphers.

The collision bound

theorem (the collision bound)

Draw $s$ uniformly from the $2^n - 1$ nonzero strings and $f$ uniformly with period $s$. A deterministic algorithm making $q$ queries finds a collision with probability at most $\binom q2\big/(2^n - 1)$, and outputs $s$ with probability at most

$$\frac{\binom q2 + 1}{2^n - 1}.$$

Check, $q = 1$: no collision is possible, and the bound $\tfrac1{2^n - 1}$ is a blind guess among the nonzero strings ✓.

proof

1. Run the algorithm against the free oracle. Its queries $X_1, \dots, X_q$ and its output $\hat s$ depend only on the fresh values, so they are independent of $s$.

2. The real run collides iff some $X_j = X_i\oplus s$, i.e. iff $s\in D = \{X_i\oplus X_j : i < j\}$. Since $|D|\le\binom q2$ and $s$ is uniform on $2^n - 1$ strings independently of $D$: $P(\text{collision})\le\binom q2/(2^n - 1)$.

3. With no collision the real output is $\hat s$, and $P(\hat s = s)\le\frac1{2^n - 1}$ by independence.

4. $P(\text{output } s)\le P(\text{collision}) + P(\hat s = s)$. ∎

Full proof, step 2. By the coupling, the real and free runs agree up to the first index $t$ at which $X_t = X_i\oplus s$ for some $i < t$; if there is no such $t\le q$, they agree throughout, and the real run sees $q$ distinct values — no collision. If there is such a $t$, the real run's $t$-th answer repeats the $i$-th: a collision. So the event "the real run collides" equals the event "$s\in D$", with $D$ computed from the free run. The free queries are distinct, so every element of $D$ is nonzero; $D$ is a random set, but independent of $s$, so conditioning on $D$: $P(s\in D\mid D) = |D|/(2^n - 1)\le\binom q2/(2^n - 1)$. Average over $D$.

Step 3. On the event "no collision" the two runs agree throughout, so the real output equals $\hat s$. Hence $\{\text{real output} = s\}\subseteq\{\text{collision}\}\cup\{\hat s = s\}$. Since $\hat s$ is independent of $s$ and $s$ is uniform on the nonzero strings, $P(\hat s = s) = \sum_tP(\hat s = t)P(s = t)\le\frac1{2^n - 1}$.

The decision version. The same argument bounds how well $q$ queries can tell a random two-to-one $f$ from a random one-to-one $f$: the one-to-one function is the free oracle, so the two transcripts differ only on the collision event, and the advantage is at most $\binom q2/(2^n - 1)$. This is the form in Simon's paper.

A slightly sharper count. Conditioning on the absence of a collision, $s$ is uniform on the nonzero strings outside $D$, of which there are at least $2^n - 1 - \binom q2$; using that instead of step 4's union gives the same order. The constant does not matter for $\Omega(2^{n/2})$.

$\Omega(2^{n/2})$, and a matching upper bound

corollary (Simon, 1994)

Every randomised algorithm that finds $s$ with probability $\ge\tfrac23$ on every valid $f$ makes

$$q \ \ge\ \sqrt{\tfrac43(2^n - 1) - 2}\ \approx\ 1.15\cdot2^{n/2}$$

queries. So the randomised query complexity is $\Omega(2^{n/2})$.

proof

Averaging gives a deterministic algorithm with success $\ge\tfrac23$ on the hard distribution; the collision bound gives $\big(\binom q2 + 1\big)/(2^n - 1)\ge\tfrac23$, i.e. $q^2 > q(q - 1)\ge\tfrac43(2^n - 1) - 2$. ∎

Tight. Classically, $O(2^{n/2})$ queries do suffice: for even $n$, query every $(a, 0)$ and $(0, b)$ with $a, b$ of length $n/2$. Two of them differ by $s$, so a collision is certain. The square root is the birthday bound.

On a log scale the classical bound is a straight line of slope $\tfrac12\log_{10}2$ per bit; the quantum count barely leaves the floor.

The algebra. $\frac{\binom q2 + 1}{2^n - 1}\ge\frac23 \iff \binom q2\ge\frac23(2^n - 1) - 1 \iff q(q-1)\ge\frac43(2^n - 1) - 2$, and $q^2 > q(q - 1)$. For large $n$, $\sqrt{4/3} = 1.1547$. Exactly computed minima: $q\ge4$ at $n = 3$; $q\ge1\,300\,077\,228\,592\,328\approx1.30\times10^{15}$ at $n = 100$. For comparison, $2^{50} = 1.13\times10^{15}$.

The matching upper bound, for even $n$. Write strings as $(a, b)$ with halves of length $n/2$. Query all $(a, 0)$ and all $(0, b)$: $2\cdot2^{n/2} - 1$ distinct strings (the zero string is in both families). For $s = (s_L, s_R)$ the two queries $(s_L, 0)$ and $(0, s_R)$ differ by $s$ — when one half of $s$ is zero they are $(0,0)$ and $s$ itself — so they collide. This is deterministic and always succeeds: $D(\text{Simon}) = O(2^{n/2})$, so the randomised bound is tight up to a constant. A randomised alternative is the birthday paradox: about $2^{n/2}$ uniformly random queries collide with constant probability.

The birthday bound. The collision bound is the birthday paradox seen from the other side: with $q$ queries there are $\binom q2\approx q^2/2$ pairs, each colliding with probability about $2^{-n}$, so collisions become likely only near $q\approx2^{n/2}$ — the same square root that makes 23 people enough for a shared birthday among 365 days.

What has been separated, and what has not

proved

In the query model: quantum $O(n)$, randomised $\Omega(2^{n/2})$, deterministic $\Omega(2^{n/2})$. Both sides are theorems, with no unproved assumption.

an oracle separation

A statement that two kinds of machine differ when both are given the same black box. Simon's problem gives one between polynomial-time quantum and randomised computation, relative to the oracle $f$.

not proved

That any explicit function — one given as a circuit — is hard classically. With the circuit in hand, a classical algorithm may read it. If $f(x) = Mx$ for a matrix $M$ with kernel $\{0, s\}$, Gaussian elimination on $M$ finds $s$ in $O(n^3)$ time — and a structured $f$ can be easy even as a black box.

not proved either

That quantum computers beat classical ones on unrestricted computation. That would separate complexity classes, which nobody can do; an oracle separation is evidence, not proof.

In unit 12's language (unit 12 is not on this unit's path; this is a forward pointer). The classes BPP and BQP can be given access to an oracle. Turning Simon's problem into a language — say, "the first bit of $s$ is 1", with a family of Simon functions built into the oracle by diagonalisation — gives an oracle $O$ with $\mathrm{BQP}^O\not\subseteq\mathrm{BPP}^O$ (stated here, not proved: the diagonalisation is standard, and the query bounds of this section are the whole of its content). Bernstein and Vazirani had already found a superpolynomial oracle separation (recursive Fourier sampling, unit 13); Simon's was the first exponential one.

Why oracle separations are not proofs. There are oracles relative to which P = NP and others relative to which P ≠ NP (T. Baker, J. Gill and R. Solovay, SIAM J. Comput. 4, 431, 1975, cited): a black box can tilt a comparison either way, so a relativised statement says nothing definite about the unrelativised one. What Simon's problem does say is that any proof of BPP = BQP would have to use the inside of circuits, not just their input–output behaviour.

The linear example. If $M$ is an $n\times n$ matrix over $\mathbb F_2$ with kernel exactly $\{0, s\}$, then $f(x) = Mx$ satisfies the promise: $Mx = Mx'$ iff $x\oplus x'\in\ker M$. Given $M$, elimination finds $\ker M$ directly. It is easy even as a black box: the $n$ queries $f(e_0), \dots, f(e_{n-1})$ on the standard basis vectors return the columns of $M$, and elimination does the rest — $n$ classical queries, no collision needed. That is no contradiction. The lower bound is about the worst Simon function, and its proof used a uniformly random one, whose values on different pairs are unrelated; linearity is extra structure, and a classical algorithm exploits it. Every hard instance must lack such structure, which is exactly why the separation says nothing about explicit circuits.

The paper Shor read

1994. Daniel Simon wrote down this problem and both bounds. Peter Shor, at Bell Labs, read the paper and saw the template: a function constant on the cosets of a hidden subgroup, a Hadamard-like transform, and classical post-processing of the samples.

He asked what happens over the integers, where the hidden structure is a period $r$ with $f(x) = f(x + r)$. That spring he found an algorithm for discrete logarithms, and then one for factoring.

The two papers appeared consecutively: FOCS 1994, pages 116–123 and 124–134; and again in SIAM J. Comput. 26(5), 1997, pages 1474–1483 and 1484–1509.
