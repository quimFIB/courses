Fourier on finite abelian groups, the quantum Fourier transform, and phase estimation

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. On paper and without notes, you derive the QFT circuit on \(\mathbb Z_{2^n}\) from the product formula — including where the swaps go in the course's qubit order — and count its gates; you prove the \(4/\pi^2\) bound for the nearest \(t\)-bit fraction; and you compute the full outcome distribution of phase estimation for \(\varphi = \tfrac13\) with three counting qubits: \(0.688\) on \(3/8\), \(0.175\) on \(2/8\), all eight summing to 1.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about five hours. Work them on paper first. P9 is the checkpoint's computation; P4, P10 and P11 are its proofs.

Conventions, as in the deck: \(\braket{u|v}\) is conjugate-linear in the first slot. Qubit 0 is the leftmost symbol of a ket, the most significant bit of an index and the top wire of a circuit, so \(\ket{j_1j_2j_3}\) is basis vector \(4j_1 + 2j_2 + j_3\). A circuit drawn \(G_1\) then \(G_2\) is the operator \(G_2G_1\). \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/N}\) and \(F_N\ket j = N^{-1/2}\sum_k\omega^{jk}\ket k\) — the plus sign is the course's. \(R_k = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{2\pi i/2^k})\), \(C(U) = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes U\), and \(C_{cq}(R_k)\) has control \(c\) and target \(q\) (it is symmetric, so the distinction is only in the drawing). In phase estimation counting qubit \(l\) controls \(U^{2^{t-1-l}}\), and outcome \(y\) has probability \(p(y) = \sin^2(\pi\delta_y)/(2^{2t}\sin^2(\pi\delta_y/2^t))\) with \(\delta_y = 2^t\varphi - y\). Logarithms are base 2.

Characters and the transform

P1 Characters of \(\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_4\) routine

exercises: characters of a product group, the Fourier matrix as a Kronecker product, why groups of the same order can have different transforms

Let \(G = \mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_4\), and label \((x, y)\in G\) by the integer \(4x + y\), so that \(x\) sits on qubit 0 and \(y\) on qubits 1–2.

  1. List the eight characters of \(G\), and tabulate their values at \((1, 1)\).
  2. Show that in this labelling \(F_G = F_2\otimes F_4\), and write \(F_G\ket5\) as a vector and as a product.
  3. Show that no relabelling of rows and columns makes \(F_G\) equal to \(F_8\). What property of the two groups is behind this?
  4. Check in one line that \(F_G\) is unitary.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The characters are eight functions on \(G\), each a product of a character of \(\mathbb Z_2\) and one of \(\mathbb Z_4\) — the theorem on Products of groups, and \(\hat G\cong G\). Part 3 needs an invariant of a matrix that relabelling cannot change.

Rung 2

The characters of \(\mathbb Z_2\) are \(x\mapsto(-1)^{ax}\), those of \(\mathbb Z_4\) are \(y\mapsto i^{by}\). For part 3, permuting rows and columns permutes the entries: compare the sets of values the entries take.

Rung 3

First line: \(\chi_{(a, b)}(x, y) = (-1)^{ax}\,i^{by}\), \(a\in\{0, 1\}\), \(b\in\{0, 1, 2, 3\}\), so \((F_G)_{4a+b,\,4x+y} = \tfrac1{\sqrt8}(-1)^{ax}i^{by} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-1)^{ax}\cdot\tfrac12i^{by}\).

Worked solution

Idea. A character of a product is a product of characters, so the Fourier matrix of a product is a Kronecker product. Its entries are then fourth roots of unity, which \(F_8\)'s are not.

1. By the product theorem every character is \(\chi_{(a, b)}(x, y) = (-1)^{ax}i^{by}\) with \(a\in\mathbb Z_2\), \(b\in\mathbb Z_4\): eight of them. At \((x, y) = (1, 1)\) the value is \((-1)^ai^b\); in the order \(4a + b = 0, \dots, 7\):

\[ 1,\ i,\ -1,\ -i,\ -1,\ -i,\ 1,\ i . \]

2. \((F_G)_{4a+b,\,4x+y} = \tfrac1{\sqrt8}(-1)^{ax}i^{by} = (F_2)_{ax}(F_4)_{by}\), since \(F_2 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big((-1)^{ax}\big)\) and \(F_4 = \tfrac12\big(i^{by}\big)\). That is the entry of \(F_2\otimes F_4\) in row \(4a + b\), column \(4x + y\) (unit 03's Kronecker product, with qubit 0 the outer index). Column \(5 = (1, 1)\) is the list of part 1 over \(\sqrt8\):

\[ F_G\ket5 = \tfrac1{\sqrt8}(1, i, -1, -i, -1, -i, 1, i)^{\mathsf T} = \ket-\otimes\tfrac12\big(\ket0 + i\ket1 - \ket2 - i\ket3\big) = (F_2\ket1)\otimes(F_4\ket1). \]

3. Every entry of \(\sqrt8F_G\) is \((-1)^{ax}i^{by}\), a fourth root of unity. But \(\sqrt8F_8\) has the entry \(\omega = (1 + i)/\sqrt2\) in row 1, column 1, and \(\omega^4 = -1\ne1\). Permuting rows and columns only rearranges entries, so it cannot turn one matrix into the other. Behind it: \(\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_4\not\cong\mathbb Z_8\). Every element of \(\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_4\) has order dividing 4, so every character value \(z\) satisfies \(z^4 = \chi(4g) = 1\); \(\mathbb Z_8\) has an element of order 8, and a character sending it to \(\omega\).

4. \((F_2\otimes F_4)^\dagger(F_2\otimes F_4) = F_2^\dagger F_2\otimes F_4^\dagger F_4 = I\otimes I\).

Check. Column 5 has two each of \(1, i, -1, -i\), so its entries sum to \(0\): it is orthogonal to column 0, the all-ones column, as orthogonality demands. Its norm is \(8\cdot\tfrac18 = 1\) ✓.

P2 Proof: orthogonality, both ways medium

exercises: counting homomorphisms, translation invariance on \(G\) and on \(\hat G\), rows versus columns of a square unitary

\(G\) is a finite abelian group.

  1. Prove from scratch that \(\mathbb Z_N\) has exactly \(N\) characters, and that \(|\hat G| = |G|\) for \(G = \mathbb Z_{N_1}\times\mathbb Z_{N_2}\).
  2. Assuming \(|\hat G| = |G|\), prove the dual orthogonality \(\sum_{\chi\in\hat G}\overline{\chi(g)}\chi(g') = |G|\,\delta_{gg'}\).
  3. Prove it again for \(G = \mathbb Z_N\) without matrices: translation invariance, this time on \(\hat G\).
  4. For \(\mathbb Z_8\) and \(g = 4\), \(g' = 0\), evaluate the sum term by term.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a count of homomorphisms out of a cyclic group; part 2 is a statement about the columns of a square matrix whose rows you already know are orthonormal; part 3 is the lemma of Orthogonality of characters with the roles of \(G\) and \(\hat G\) exchanged.

Rung 2

A homomorphism from \(\mathbb Z_N\) is fixed by where it sends \(1\), and that image must satisfy \(z^N = 1\). For part 3, multiplication by a fixed character is a bijection of \(\hat G\), just as translation by a fixed element is a bijection of \(G\).

Rung 3

First line of part 3: for \(g\ne0\), \(\chi_1(g) = \omega^g\ne1\), and \(S = \sum_\chi\chi(g) = \sum_\chi(\chi_1\chi)(g) = \chi_1(g)\,S\).

Worked solution

Idea. Characters and group elements play symmetric roles: a sum over either, of something that a non-trivial "translation" multiplies by a number other than 1, vanishes.

1. A character of \(\mathbb Z_N\) satisfies \(\chi(j) = \chi(1)^j\) (induction on \(j\)), so it is determined by \(\chi(1)\), and \(\chi(1)^N = \chi(N\cdot1) = \chi(0) = 1\): \(\chi(1)\) is one of the \(N\) roots \(\omega^k\). Each root occurs: \(j\mapsto\omega^{jk}\) is well defined modulo \(N\) and multiplicative. So exactly \(N\). For \(\mathbb Z_{N_1}\times\mathbb Z_{N_2}\), the product theorem gives a bijection \(\hat{\mathbb Z}_{N_1}\times\hat{\mathbb Z}_{N_2}\to\hat G\), so \(|\hat G| = N_1N_2 = |G|\).

2. The matrix \(M_{\chi g} = \chi(g)/\sqrt{|G|}\) has orthonormal rows (orthogonality of characters) and is square (\(|\hat G| = |G|\)), so \(MM^\dagger = I\) implies \(M^\dagger M = I\). The \((g, g')\) entry of \(M^\dagger M\) is \(\frac1{|G|}\sum_\chi\overline{\chi(g)}\chi(g')\), so the sum is \(|G|\delta_{gg'}\).

3. \(\overline{\chi(g)}\chi(g') = \chi(g' - g)\), so it suffices to show \(S(h) = \sum_\chi\chi(h)\) is \(N\) for \(h = 0\) (clear: every term is 1) and \(0\) for \(h\ne0\). For \(h\ne0\) in \(\mathbb Z_N\), \(\chi_1(h) = \omega^h\ne1\) since \(0 \lt h \lt N\). The map \(\chi\mapsto\chi_1\chi\) is a bijection of \(\hat G\) (inverse: multiply by \(\bar\chi_1\)), so

\[ S(h) = \sum_\chi(\chi_1\chi)(h) = \chi_1(h)\,S(h)\ \Longrightarrow\ S(h) = 0 . \]

For a general finite abelian \(G\) the same proof works once some character has \(\chi(h)\ne1\) for each \(h\ne0\); for products of cyclic groups take \(\chi_1\) on a factor where \(h\) has a non-zero coordinate, and the structure theorem (cited in the deck) covers the rest.

4. \(\sum_{k=0}^7\omega^{4k} = \sum_k(-1)^k = 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + 1 - 1 = 0\) ✓, as \(g = 4\ne0 = g'\).

Check. At \(g = g' = 0\) the sum is \(\sum_\chi1 = |\hat G| = |G|\) ✓. In matrix terms part 2 says the columns of \(F_8\), entry by entry are orthonormal: column 4 is \((1, -1, 1, -1, \dots)/\sqrt8\) and column 0 is \((1, 1, \dots)/\sqrt8\), with inner product \(\tfrac18\cdot0 = 0\), which is part 4.

P3 Shifts become phases, on a coset medium

exercises: the translation theorem \(F_GT_a = D_aF_G\), the Fourier transform of a subgroup state, what a measurement after \(F\) reveals and what it hides

In \(\mathbb Z_8\), let \(H = \{0, 2, 4, 6\}\) and, for \(c\in\mathbb Z_8\), \(\ket{c + H} = \tfrac12\sum_{h\in H}\ket{c + h}\).

  1. Compute \(F_8\ket{0 + H}\) and \(F_8\ket{1 + H}\), and their outcome distributions.
  2. For \(r\mid N\), let \(H_r = \{0, r, 2r, \dots\}\subset\mathbb Z_N\) (it has \(N/r\) elements). Show that \(F_N\ket{c + H_r}\) is supported on the multiples of \(N/r\), uniformly, with phases depending on \(c\).
  3. Someone prepares \(\ket{c + H_r}\) with \(c\) uniformly random and unknown to you, applies \(F_N\) and measures. What do you learn about \(c\), and what about \(r\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answers are states supported on a few basis vectors. Part 1 is a direct computation; part 2 the same computation with letters; part 3 uses Shifts become phases.

Rung 2

\(\ket{c + H} = T_c\ket{0 + H}\), so you only need \(F\ket{0 + H}\); then \(F T_c = D_cF\) multiplies the amplitude of \(\ket k\) by \(\omega^{ck}\). For \(\ket{0 + H}\) the amplitude of \(\ket k\) is a geometric sum in \(\omega^{2k} = i^k\).

Rung 3

First line: \(F_8\ket{0 + H} = \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt8}\sum_k\Big(\sum_{m=0}^3i^{km}\Big)\ket k\), and \(\sum_{m=0}^3i^{km}\) is \(4\) if \(4\mid k\), else \(0\).

Worked solution

Idea. A subgroup state is periodic, and the transform of a periodic state lives on the characters that are trivial on the subgroup; shifting the state only changes phases.

1. With \(H = \{2m\}\), \(F_8\ket{0 + H} = \frac1{2\sqrt8}\sum_k\sum_{m=0}^3\omega^{2mk}\ket k\), and \(\omega^{2k} = i^k\). The inner sum is \(4\) when \(i^k = 1\), i.e. \(k\in\{0, 4\}\), and \(0\) otherwise (a geometric sum of a fourth root of unity \(\ne1\)). So

\[ F_8\ket{0 + H} = \frac4{2\sqrt8}(\ket0 + \ket4) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket4),\qquad F_8\ket{1 + H} = D_1F_8\ket{0 + H} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \omega^4\ket4) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - \ket4). \]

Both give outcome 0 or 4 with probability \(\tfrac12\) each.

2. \(\ket{c + H_r} = \sqrt{r/N}\sum_{m=0}^{N/r-1}\ket{c + mr}\). The amplitude of \(\ket k\) in its transform is

\[ \sqrt{\frac rN}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt N}\,\omega^{ck}\sum_{m=0}^{N/r-1}\big(\omega^{rk}\big)^m . \]

\(\omega^{rk} = e^{2\pi ik/(N/r)}\) is 1 exactly when \((N/r)\mid k\), and then the sum is \(N/r\); otherwise it is a full geometric sum of an \((N/r)\)-th root of unity \(\ne1\), so 0. For \(k = lN/r\) the amplitude is \(\frac{\sqrt r}{N}\cdot\frac Nr\,\omega^{ck} = \frac1{\sqrt r}\omega^{clN/r}\):

\[ F_N\ket{c + H_r} = \frac1{\sqrt r}\sum_{l=0}^{r-1}e^{2\pi i\,cl/r}\,\ket{lN/r} . \]

3. The outcome is a uniformly random multiple \(lN/r\) of \(N/r\), whatever \(c\) is: the shift is invisible, so you learn nothing about \(c\). You do learn about \(r\): the outcome \(k\) satisfies \(k/N = l/r\), a fraction with denominator \(r\) (in lowest terms, a divisor of \(r\)). Two or three samples typically pin \(r\) down through the denominators of \(k/N\). This is unit 16's order finding in the special case \(r\mid N\); unit 16 handles \(r\nmid N\) with phase estimation and continued fractions.

Check. Norms: \(r\cdot\frac1r = 1\) ✓. Part 2 with \(N = 8\), \(r = 2\), \(c = 1\): \(\frac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{i\pi}\ket4)\), which is part 1 ✓. With \(r = 4\), \(c = 1\) (the deck's \(\{1, 5\}\)), it gives \(\tfrac12\) times \(\ket0, i\ket2, -\ket4, -i\ket6\) — probability \(\tfrac14\) on each even \(k\), as in the figure on Shifts become phases ✓.

P4 Proof: the product formula medium

exercises: binary expansions, factorising a sum over bit strings into a tensor product, reading the formula in another qubit order

  1. Prove, for every \(n\) and every \(j = j_12^{n-1} + \cdots + j_n\), that \(F_{2^n}\ket j = \bigotimes_{l=1}^n\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i\,0.j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n}\ket1\big)\), factor \(l\) on qubit \(l - 1\).
  2. Deduce \(F\ket1\) and \(F\ket{2^{n-1}}\) as products.
  3. Show that \(F\ket j\) has Schmidt rank 1 across every cut of the \(n\) qubits, but that \(F\) applied to a superposition of two basis states can be entangled: compute \(F_4\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)\) and decide.
  4. How does the formula read in Qiskit's order, where qubit 0 is the least significant bit?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is an identity between two vectors in \((\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes n}\); expand both in the computational basis, or start from the left side and factorise it. Part 3 needs unit 03's criterion for a two-qubit state \(\sum a_{xy}\ket{xy}\) to be a product.

Rung 2

Write \(k = \sum_lk_l2^{n-l}\) with \(k_l\) on qubit \(l - 1\). Then \(e^{2\pi ijk/2^n} = \prod_le^{2\pi ijk_l/2^l}\), and each factor depends on one bit of \(k\). Finally, \(j/2^l\) is an integer plus \(0.j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n\).

Rung 3

First line: \(F\ket j = 2^{-n/2}\sum_{k_1, \dots, k_n}\bigotimes_{l=1}^ne^{2\pi ijk_l/2^l}\ket{k_l} = 2^{-n/2}\bigotimes_{l=1}^n\big(\ket0 + e^{2\pi ij/2^l}\ket1\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. The exponent \(jk\) is linear in the bits of \(k\), so the exponential is a product over those bits and the sum factorises; \(e^{2\pi ix}\) then forgets the integer part of each \(j/2^l\).

1. With \(k = \sum_{l=1}^nk_l2^{n-l}\), \(\frac{jk}{2^n} = \sum_l\frac{jk_l}{2^l}\), and \(\ket k = \ket{k_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{k_n}\) in the course's order. So

\[ F\ket j = \frac1{2^{n/2}}\sum_{k_1, \dots, k_n\in\{0, 1\}}\ \bigotimes_{l=1}^ne^{2\pi ijk_l/2^l}\ket{k_l} = \frac1{2^{n/2}}\bigotimes_{l=1}^n\Big(\sum_{k_l\in\{0, 1\}}e^{2\pi ijk_l/2^l}\ket{k_l}\Big), \]

by multilinearity of \(\otimes\). The \(l\)-th factor is \(\ket0 + e^{2\pi ij/2^l}\ket1\). Now \(j/2^l = j_12^{n-1-l} + \cdots + j_{n-l} + \big(j_{n-l+1}2^{-1} + \cdots + j_n2^{-l}\big)\): an integer plus \(0.j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n\). Since \(e^{2\pi im} = 1\) for integer \(m\), the factor is \(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i\,0.j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n}\ket1\). Distributing \(2^{-n/2}\) as \(2^{-1/2}\) per factor gives the formula. ∎

2. \(j = 1\): \(j_n = 1\), others 0, so factor \(l\) has \(0.0\cdots01\) (\(l\) digits) \(= 2^{-l}\): \(F\ket1 = \bigotimes_l\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i/2^l}\ket1)\) — qubit 0 carries \(-1\), qubit 1 carries \(i\), …, the last carries \(\omega = e^{2\pi i/2^n}\). \(j = 2^{n-1}\): only \(j_1 = 1\), which appears only in the last factor, \(0.10\cdots0 = \tfrac12\): \(F\ket{2^{n-1}} = \ket+^{\otimes(n-1)}\otimes\ket-\).

3. A product of one-qubit states has Schmidt rank 1 across every cut. But \(F_4\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1) = \tfrac1{2\sqrt2}\sum_k(1 + i^k)\ket k = \tfrac1{2\sqrt2}\big(2\ket{00} + (1 + i)\ket{01} + 0\ket{10} + (1 - i)\ket{11}\big)\). Arrange the coefficients as the matrix \(\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1 + i\\ 0 & 1 - i\end{pmatrix}\) (rows: qubit 0); its determinant is \(2(1 - i)\ne0\), so its rank is 2 and the state is entangled (unit 03). Linearity does not preserve "being a product".

4. In Qiskit's order the bit of weight \(2^{n-l}\) sits on qubit \(n - l\), so the same factors appear in reverse: qubit 0 carries \(0.j_1j_2\cdots j_n\) (with \(j_1\) now meaning the most significant bit, stored on the last qubit), and the last qubit carries \(0.j_n\). The mathematics is unchanged; only the qubit on which each factor is stored moves. That is why a Qiskit circuit is drawn as the mirror image of the course's: its first \(H\) acts on the last wire, which holds \(j_1\), and its rotations are controlled from the wires above. The swaps are still needed at the end, for the same reason as here.

Check. \(n = 2\), \(j = 1\): the formula gives \(\tfrac12(\ket0 - \ket1)(\ket0 + i\ket1) = \tfrac12(\ket{00} + i\ket{01} - \ket{10} - i\ket{11})\), and directly \(F_4\ket1 = \tfrac12\sum_ki^k\ket k = \tfrac12(\ket0 + i\ket1 - \ket2 - i\ket3)\) ✓. The norm of part 3's state: \(\tfrac18(4 + 2 + 0 + 2) = 1\) ✓.

P5 The four-qubit circuit routine

exercises: building the QFT circuit from the product formula, the gate count, placing the swaps in the course's order

  1. Write the QFT circuit on four qubits as a list of gates in time order, in the course's order, and count the Hadamards, controlled phases and swaps.
  2. Track the input \(\ket{11} = \ket{1011}\) through it: give each qubit's phase (as a binary fraction) just before the swaps, and after them.
  3. Compare with the product formula, and check the amplitude of \(\ket{0001}\) against \(F_{16}\)'s matrix entry.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 1 is a list of twelve gates; to part 2, four binary fractions twice. The pattern is the three-qubit circuit of The three-qubit QFT with one more qubit.

Rung 2

Qubit \(q\) gets \(H\) and then \(R_2, R_3, \dots\) controlled by qubits \(q + 1, q + 2, \dots\); then qubit \(q\) and qubit \(3 - q\) are swapped for \(q = 0, 1\). Before the swaps qubit \(q\) holds \(0.j_{q+1}\cdots j_4\).

Rung 3

First lines: \(H_0\), \(C_{10}(R_2)\), \(C_{20}(R_3)\), \(C_{30}(R_4)\); for \(j = 1011\) qubit 0 then holds \(0.1011 = \tfrac{11}{16}\).

Worked solution

Idea. Each qubit needs one \(H\) for its first digit and one controlled rotation for each further digit; the digits come out in reverse order, so the qubits are reversed at the end.

1. In time order:

\[ H_0,\ C_{10}(R_2),\ C_{20}(R_3),\ C_{30}(R_4),\ \ H_1,\ C_{21}(R_2),\ C_{31}(R_3),\ \ H_2,\ C_{32}(R_2),\ \ H_3,\ \ \mathrm{SWAP}_{03},\ \mathrm{SWAP}_{12} . \]

4 Hadamards, \(3 + 2 + 1 = 6 = \tfrac{4\cdot3}2\) controlled phases, \(2 = \lfloor4/2\rfloor\) swaps.

2. \(j = 1011\): \(j_1 = 1, j_2 = 0, j_3 = 1, j_4 = 1\). Before the swaps qubit \(q\) holds \(0.j_{q+1}\cdots j_4\):

qubit 0: \(0.1011 = \tfrac{11}{16}\); qubit 1: \(0.011 = \tfrac38\); qubit 2: \(0.11 = \tfrac34\); qubit 3: \(0.1 = \tfrac12\).

After \(\mathrm{SWAP}_{03}\) and \(\mathrm{SWAP}_{12}\): qubit 0 \(\tfrac12\), qubit 1 \(\tfrac34\), qubit 2 \(\tfrac38\), qubit 3 \(\tfrac{11}{16}\) — i.e. the phases \(-1\), \(-i\), \(e^{3\pi i/4}\), \(e^{11\pi i/8}\).

3. The product formula wants qubit \(l - 1\) to carry \(0.j_{5-l}\cdots j_4\): \(0.j_4 = 0.1\), \(0.j_3j_4 = 0.11\), \(0.j_2j_3j_4 = 0.011\), \(0.j_1j_2j_3j_4 = 0.1011\) ✓. The amplitude of \(\ket{0001}\) is \(\tfrac14\cdot1\cdot1\cdot1\cdot e^{2\pi i\cdot11/16}\), and \(F_{16}\)'s entry is \(\tfrac14\omega_{16}^{11\cdot1} = \tfrac14e^{2\pi i\cdot11/16}\) ✓.

Check. Gate count against the theorem of The QFT on \(n\) qubits: \(n = 4\) gives \(4\), \(6\), \(2\) ✓. One more amplitude: \(\ket{1000}\) has \(\tfrac14(-1) = \tfrac14\omega_{16}^{11\cdot8} = \tfrac14\omega_{16}^{88}\), and \(88\bmod16 = 8\), \(\omega_{16}^8 = -1\) ✓.

import numpy as np
from qclib import Circuit, gates, library

def qft(n):
    c = Circuit(n)
    for q in range(n):
        c.h(q)
        for k in range(2, n - q + 1):              # R_k on qubit q, controlled by qubit q + k - 1
            c.controlled(gates.phase(2 * np.pi / 2**k), [q + k - 1], [q], name=f"R{k}")
    for q in range(n // 2):
        c.swap(q, n - 1 - q)
    return c

print(np.allclose(qft(4).unitary(), library.qft_matrix(4)))   # True
print(sorted(qft(4).counts().items()))
# [('R2', 3), ('R3', 2), ('R4', 1), ('h', 4), ('swap', 2)]

P6 The inverse QFT, and \(F\) to the fourth routine

exercises: inverting a circuit, the identity \(F_N^2\ket j = \ket{-j}\), eigenvalues of a unitary of finite order

Idea. Each dropped rotation is nearly the identity, errors in a product add, and there are few large rotations and many tiny ones — so the total is dominated by the first dropped column.

2. 3. Write \(F\) as its gate list and \(\tilde F\) as the same list with \(V_i = I\) for each dropped \(C(R_k)\) and \(V_i = U_i\) otherwise. By part 2 the error is at most the sum over dropped gates of \(\lVert C(R_k) - I\rVert\). \[ \lVert F - \tilde F\rVert\le\sum_{k=m+1}^n(n - k + 1)\frac{2\pi}{2^k}\le(n - m)\,2\pi\sum_{k=m+1}^\infty2^{-k} = \frac{2\pi(n - m)}{2^m} .\ \ ∎ \]

4. Let \(a = F\ket\psi\), \(b = \tilde F\ket\psi\), and \(P\) the projector of the outcome. Check. \(n = 2\), \(m = 1\): the only dropped gate is \(C(S)\), and \(\lVert F_4 - \tilde F\rVert = \lVert C(S) - I\rVert = |i - 1| = \sqrt2 = 1.414\) exactly (multiplying by unitaries preserves the operator norm), within the bound \(2\pi\cdot1/2 = \pi\) ✓. 4. \(\varepsilon = 0.01\): \(2 + 50 = 52\), \(\log52 = 5.70\), so \(t = 10 + 6 = 16\), and the guaranteed failure is \(\frac1{2(64 - 2)} = \frac1{124} = 0.0081\). \(\varepsilon = 0.001\): \(2 + 500 = 502\), \(\log502 = 8.97\), \(t = 19\). P12 A superposition input, and a global phase that is not global medium

exercises: phase estimation on a superposition of eigenvectors, the post-measurement state, why a controlled gate sees the phase of \(U\)

Let \(U = \mathrm{diag}(i, -i)\): eigenvectors \(\ket{u_1} = \ket0\) with phase \(\tfrac14\), \(\ket{u_2} = \ket1\) with phase \(\tfrac34\). Use \(t = 2\) counting qubits.

  1. With input \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{u_1} + \ket{u_2}) = \ket+\), find the outcome distribution and, for each outcome, the state of the target afterwards.
  2. Now use \(U' = Z\), which equals \(U\) up to the global phase \(-i\). Repeat part 1. Why is the answer different, when \(U\) and \(U'\) are the "same gate"?
  3. With input \(\cos\theta\ket0 + \sin\theta\ket1\) and the original \(U\), what is the distribution?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Use A superposition of eigenvectors: a sample of one phase: each eigenvector contributes its own distribution, weighted by \(|c_s|^2\). With \(t = 2\) both phases here are exact two-bit fractions.

Rung 2

Write \(\tfrac14\) and \(\tfrac34\) as two-bit binary fractions: which case of What the register holds before the inverse QFT is this? For \(U' = Z\), find its eigenphases first. A controlled gate \(C(e^{i\alpha}V)\) equals \((P(\alpha)\otimes I)\,C(V)\), not \(C(V)\) (unit 10).

Rung 3

First line: the final state is \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{01}\ket{u_1} + \ket{11}\ket{u_2}\big)\).

Worked solution

Idea. Phase estimation acts on each eigenvector separately and the eigenvectors are orthogonal, so the outcome is a sample of one eigenphase — and the eigenphase includes any "global" phase of \(U\), because the controlled gate turns it into a relative one.

1. \(2^2\cdot\tfrac14 = 1\) and \(2^2\cdot\tfrac34 = 3\), both integers: the exact case. So the final state is \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{01}\ket0 + \ket{11}\ket1)\): outcome \(01\) (estimate \(\tfrac14\)) with probability \(\tfrac12\), leaving the target in \(\ket0\); outcome \(11\) (estimate \(\tfrac34\)) with probability \(\tfrac12\), leaving \(\ket1\). The measurement has projected the target onto an eigenvector.

2. \(Z\) has phases \(0\) on \(\ket0\) and \(\tfrac12\) on \(\ket1\): outcomes \(00\) and \(10\), probability \(\tfrac12\) each, targets \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\). The difference is real: \(C(U) = C(iZ) = (S\otimes I)\,C(Z)\), an extra \(S\) on the control. Controlled, the factor \(i\) multiplies only the branch where the control is \(\ket1\), which is a relative phase. Here even \(C(U^2) = C(-I) = Z\otimes I\) acts — on the control alone — though \(U^2 = -I\) does nothing to any state of the target. Phase estimation measures the eigenvalues of the operator you control, phase included.

3. Weights \(\cos^2\theta\) and \(\sin^2\theta\): outcome \(01\) with probability \(\cos^2\theta\), \(11\) with \(\sin^2\theta\); total \(1\) ✓.

Check. The distributions in parts 1 and 2 sum to \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1\) ✓. Part 2 is part 1 shifted by \(-\tfrac14\) in phase, i.e. \(-1\) in outcome: \(1\to0\), \(3\to2\) ✓, as \(U' = e^{-2\pi i/4}U\).
