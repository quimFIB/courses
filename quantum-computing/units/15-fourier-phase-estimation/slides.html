Fourier on finite abelian groups, the quantum Fourier transform, and phase estimation

Units 13 and 14 used a Fourier transform without the name. Here it is in general, with a circuit of $O(n^2)$ gates on $\mathbb Z_{2^n}$ — and phase estimation, which turns that circuit into the engine of Shor, quantum counting and energy readout.

needs 14~3 h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 15

Unit 13 wrote $H^{\otimes n}\ket x = 2^{-n/2}\sum_y(-1)^{x\cdot y}\ket y$ and remarked that the signs $(-1)^{x\cdot y}$ are the characters of the group $\mathbb Z_2^n$. Unit 14 ran Simon's algorithm on that transform, and its problem sheet showed that replacing $\mathbb Z_2^n$ by $\mathbb Z_4$ breaks it: two signs are not enough. This unit builds the object both units were using — characters of a finite abelian group, and the Fourier transform as the unitary change of basis to them — and then does two things with it.

  • On $\mathbb Z_{2^n}$ the transform of a basis state is a product state, and that product formula is a circuit of $n$ Hadamards and $n(n-1)/2$ controlled phases. Classically the fast Fourier transform needs about $n2^n$ operations on the same vector. The comparison is real and also misleading, and the deck says why early and loudly: the output is a state, not a list of numbers.
  • Phase estimation uses the inverse transform to read an eigenvalue $e^{2\pi i\varphi}$ of a unitary into a register, to $t$ bits, with probability at least $4/\pi^2$ for the nearest $t$-bit fraction. Unit 16 (Shor), unit 17 (quantum counting) and unit 19 (energy readout) are all this one circuit.

What this unit is not: harmonic analysis on non-abelian groups (unit 16 names it, for the hidden subgroup problem), or signal processing.

Unit 13's signs were homomorphisms

Unit 13: $H^{\otimes n}\ket x = 2^{-n/2}\sum_y(-1)^{x\cdot y}\ket y$. Fix $y$; then $x\mapsto(-1)^{x\cdot y}$ turns addition into multiplication:

$$(-1)^{(x\oplus x')\cdot y} = (-1)^{x\cdot y}\,(-1)^{x'\cdot y}.$$

It is a group homomorphism $(\mathbb Z_2^n, \oplus)\to(\{\pm1\}, \times)$, and the $2^n$ of them, one per $y$, are the columns of $H^{\otimes n}$ up to $2^{-n/2}$.

why signs are not enough

A homomorphism $\mathbb Z_4\to\{\pm1\}$ is fixed by the image of $1$, so there are only two. But $\mathbb C^4$ needs four orthogonal vectors. Allow values on the whole unit circle and $\chi(1)\in\{1, i, -1, -i\}$ gives four (unit 14, problem sheet).

The values a homomorphism into the circle can take: $\pm1$ for $\mathbb Z_2$, the fourth roots of unity for $\mathbb Z_4$.

So the target is $U(1) = \{z\in\mathbb C : |z| = 1\}$, a group under multiplication.

The homomorphism property, checked. $(x\oplus x')\cdot y = \sum_i(x_i + x'_i)y_i$ modulo 2, and $(-1)^{a}$ only depends on $a$ modulo 2, so $(-1)^{(x\oplus x')\cdot y} = (-1)^{x\cdot y + x'\cdot y}$. That is the whole content: the bitwise XOR is addition in $\mathbb Z_2^n$, and the exponential turns sums into products.

Why $\mathbb Z_4$ needs $i$. A homomorphism $\chi$ from the cyclic group $\mathbb Z_4$ is determined by $\chi(1)$, since $\chi(j) = \chi(1)^j$; and $\chi(1)^4 = \chi(4) = \chi(0) = 1$. Into $\{\pm1\}$ that leaves $\chi(1) = \pm1$: two homomorphisms, the vectors $(1,1,1,1)$ and $(1,-1,1,-1)$ in $\mathbb C^4$. Two vectors cannot be a basis of a four-dimensional space. Into $U(1)$ the equation $z^4 = 1$ has four solutions, and the four vectors $(1, z, z^2, z^3)$ turn out to be orthogonal (the next slides prove it in general). This is the gap unit 14's problem P11, on $\mathbb Z_4\times\mathbb Z_4$, pointed at.

Characters, and the characters of $\mathbb Z_N$

a character, the dual group

For a finite abelian group $(G, +)$, a character is a homomorphism $\chi: G\to U(1)$: $\ \chi(g + h) = \chi(g)\chi(h)$. The characters form a group $\hat G$ under pointwise product, with identity the trivial character $\chi_0\equiv1$.

lemma

$\chi(g)^{|G|} = 1$, so $|\chi(g)| = 1$ and $\chi(-g) = \overline{\chi(g)}$.

Proof. $|G|g = 0$ (Lagrange), so $\chi(g)^{|G|} = \chi(|G|g) = \chi(0) = 1$; then $\chi(-g) = \chi(g)^{-1} = \overline{\chi(g)}$. ∎

theorem: the characters of $\mathbb Z_N$

With $\omega = e^{2\pi i/N}$: $\ \chi_k(j) = \omega^{jk}$, $k = 0, \dots, N-1$, and $k\mapsto\chi_k$ is an isomorphism $\mathbb Z_N\cong\hat{\mathbb Z}_N$.

$\mathbb Z_8$: $\chi_k$ sends $1$ to $\omega^k$, so the eight characters are the eight points. $\chi_3$ steps round by $\omega^3$.

Full proof of the theorem. A character of $\mathbb Z_N$ satisfies $\chi(j) = \chi(1)^j$ by induction on $j$, and $\chi(1)^N = \chi(N\cdot1) = \chi(0) = 1$, so $\chi(1) = \omega^k$ for exactly one $k\in\{0, \dots, N-1\}$: $\chi = \chi_k$. Conversely each $\chi_k(j) = \omega^{jk}$ is well defined on residues (changing $j$ by $N$ changes $\omega^{jk}$ by $\omega^{Nk} = 1$) and is a homomorphism, $\omega^{(j+j')k} = \omega^{jk}\omega^{j'k}$. Finally $\chi_k\chi_l = \chi_{k+l}$, so $k\mapsto\chi_k$ is a homomorphism, and it is a bijection by the first part. ∎

Why $\hat G$ is a group. If $\chi, \psi$ are characters then $(\chi\psi)(g+h) = \chi(g)\chi(h)\psi(g)\psi(h) = (\chi\psi)(g)(\chi\psi)(h)$ because $U(1)$ is commutative; and $\bar\chi = \chi^{-1}$ is a character. Commutativity of the target is what makes this work, and it is why the theory here is for abelian groups: for a non-abelian group one-dimensional characters do not separate enough elements, and matrices replace numbers (representation theory; named in unit 16, not used).

The running example. The three counting qubits will hold a label $x\in\{0, \dots, 7\}$, and the group is $\mathbb Z_8$ with $\omega = e^{2\pi i/8} = e^{i\pi/4} = (1 + i)/\sqrt2$. The eight characters are drawn as the eight values $\chi_k(1) = \omega^k$; the star is the path $j\mapsto\omega^{3j}$, which visits every root because $\gcd(3, 8) = 1$. Unit 00's problem sheet met the case $N = 3$ and fixed the sign: $\omega = e^{+2\pi i/N}$, kept for the whole course.

Products of groups, and $\hat G\cong G$

theorem: characters of a product

Every character of $G\times H$ is $(g, h)\mapsto\chi(g)\psi(h)$ for unique $\chi\in\hat G$, $\psi\in\hat H$; so $\widehat{G\times H}\cong\hat G\times\hat H$.

Proof. Given $\rho\in\widehat{G\times H}$, set $\chi(g) = \rho(g, 0)$, $\psi(h) = \rho(0, h)$; then $\rho(g, h) = \rho(g, 0)\rho(0, h)$. Uniqueness: put $h = 0$, then $g = 0$. ∎

$\mathbb Z_2^n$ recovered

$\hat{\mathbb Z}_2 = \{\chi_0, \chi_1\}$ with $\chi_y(x) = (-1)^{xy}$. So the characters of $\mathbb Z_2^n$ are $x\mapsto\prod_i(-1)^{x_iy_i} = (-1)^{x\cdot y}$ — unit 13's signs, and there are no others.

the structure theorem for finite abelian groups (cited)

Every finite abelian group is isomorphic to $\mathbb Z_{N_1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb Z_{N_r}$.

With the two theorems before it: $\hat G\cong\hat{\mathbb Z}_{N_1}\times\cdots\cong\mathbb Z_{N_1}\times\cdots\cong G$. In particular

$$|\hat G| = |G| .$$
two traps

The isomorphism $\hat G\cong G$ depends on the chosen decomposition: it is not canonical. And groups of the same order need not match: $\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_4\not\cong\mathbb Z_8$ (problem P1), and their Fourier matrices differ.

Full proof of the product theorem. Existence: $\rho(g, h) = \rho((g, 0) + (0, h)) = \rho(g, 0)\rho(0, h)$, and $g\mapsto\rho(g, 0)$ is a homomorphism because $g\mapsto(g, 0)$ is. Uniqueness: if $\chi(g)\psi(h) = \chi'(g)\psi'(h)$ for all $g, h$, then $h = 0$ gives $\chi = \chi'$ and $g = 0$ gives $\psi = \psi'$. Conversely $(g, h)\mapsto\chi(g)\psi(h)$ is a character, and the bijection $(\chi, \psi)\mapsto\chi\psi$ respects pointwise products. ∎

Cited. The structure theorem: Dummit and Foote, Abstract Algebra, §5.2 (the fundamental theorem of finitely generated abelian groups). This course uses it only to know that $|\hat G| = |G|$ for every finite abelian $G$; for the groups the course actually meets — $\mathbb Z_N$, $\mathbb Z_2^n$, and products of a few cyclic groups — the two theorems on these slides prove it directly, and nothing is cited.

Why "not canonical". For $\mathbb Z_N$ the isomorphism $k\mapsto\chi_k$ needed a choice of generator ($1$) and of a primitive root ($\omega$); a different choice gives a different isomorphism. There is a canonical isomorphism $G\to\hat{\hat G}$, $g\mapsto(\chi\mapsto\chi(g))$, but not one $G\to\hat G$. In practice this is why one must say which convention a Fourier matrix uses.

Why $\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_4\not\cong\mathbb Z_8$. Every element of $\mathbb Z_2\times\mathbb Z_4$ has order dividing 4, while $1\in\mathbb Z_8$ has order 8.

Orthogonality of characters

orthogonality of characters

On $\mathbb C^G$ with $\braket{\chi|\psi} = \sum_{g}\overline{\chi(g)}\,\psi(g)$:

$$\braket{\chi|\psi} = |G|\,\delta_{\chi\psi}.$$
proof: translation invariance

Lemma. If $\chi\ne\chi_0$ then $\sum_g\chi(g) = 0$. Pick $h$ with $\chi(h)\ne1$. As $g$ runs over $G$ so does $g + h$, so

$$S = \sum_g\chi(g + h) = \chi(h)\,S\ \Rightarrow\ S = 0 .$$

Theorem. $\overline{\chi}\psi = \chi^{-1}\psi$ is a character, trivial iff $\chi = \psi$. Apply the lemma to it. ∎

So the $|G|$ vectors $|G|^{-1/2}\chi$ are orthonormal in $\mathbb C^G$, and there are $|\hat G| = |G|$ of them: an orthonormal basis.

$\chi_3$ on $\mathbb Z_8$: the eight values $\omega^{3j}$, laid head to tail, close up. Shifting every $j$ by $h$ rotates the star by $\chi_3(h)$ and gives the same sum.

The inner product. It is unit 00's, conjugate-linear in the first slot, on the space $\mathbb C^G$ of functions $G\to\mathbb C$ with the basis of indicator functions $\ket g$. A character is a vector in it: $\ket\chi = \sum_g\chi(g)\ket g$, of squared norm $\sum_g|\chi(g)|^2 = |G|$ by the lemma on the previous slide.

The step $\overline\chi\psi$. $\overline{\chi(g)}\psi(g) = \chi(g)^{-1}\psi(g) = (\chi^{-1}\psi)(g)$, using $|\chi(g)| = 1$. So $\braket{\chi|\psi} = \sum_g(\chi^{-1}\psi)(g)$, which is $|G|$ if $\chi^{-1}\psi = \chi_0$ (every term 1) and $0$ otherwise by the lemma.

The trick is the whole theory. "The sum over the group is invariant under translation, and a non-trivial character is not" is the only idea in this section. It is the same idea that made unit 13's $\sum_x(-1)^{x\cdot y}$ vanish for $y\ne0$, and unit 14's amplitudes cancel unless $y\cdot s = 0$.

Worked: orthogonality on $\mathbb Z_N$, and $F_N$ unitary

$\chi_k(j) = \omega^{jk}$, $\ \omega = e^{2\pi i/N}$; the matrix $(F_N)_{kj} = \omega^{jk}/\sqrt N$

1. Directly, by a geometric series. With $r = \omega^{l-k}$:

$$\braket{\chi_k|\chi_l} = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}\omega^{-jk}\omega^{jl} = \sum_{j=0}^{N-1}r^j .$$

If $l = k$, $r = 1$ and the sum is $N$. If $0 \lt |l - k| \lt N$ then $r\ne1$ and $r^N = 1$, so the sum is $\dfrac{r^N - 1}{r - 1} = 0$.

2. Unitarity. The rows of $F_N$ are the $\chi_k/\sqrt N$, orthonormal by step 1, so $F_NF_N^\dagger = I$; a square matrix with a right inverse has it as a left inverse, so $F_N^\dagger F_N = I$.

3. Check at $N = 8$, $k = 0$, $l = 3$: $\sum_j\omega^{3j} = 1 + \omega^3 + \omega^6 + \omega + \omega^4 + \omega^7 + \omega^2 + \omega^5$, every eighth root once, since $3j$ runs over all residues mod 8. The roots pair off as $\omega^m + \omega^{m+4} = \omega^m(1 + \omega^4) = 0$. Sum $0$ ✓.

4. Check at $N = 2$: $\omega = -1$ and

$$F_2 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix} = H ,$$

the Hadamard gate, unitary ✓ — the one-qubit case of unit 13.

5. Columns too. $F_N^\dagger F_N = I$ says $\sum_k\omega^{k(j'-j)} = N\delta_{jj'}$: the sum over characters at fixed group elements.

Two proofs of the same fact. Step 1 is the translation-invariance proof with the translation made explicit: multiplying the geometric sum by $r$ shifts it by one term, which is translating $j$ by 1. The pairing in step 3 is translation by $4$, where $\chi_3(4) = \omega^{12} = \omega^4 = -1$.

The linear-algebra step in 2. If $AB = I$ for square $A, B$ then $B$ is injective ($Bv = 0\Rightarrow v = ABv = 0$), so invertible, and $B^{-1} = AB B^{-1} = A$. Hence $BA = I$. Here $A = F_N$, $B = F_N^\dagger$.

Step 5, in general. For any finite abelian $G$, the same argument gives the dual orthogonality $\sum_{\chi\in\hat G}\overline{\chi(g)}\chi(g') = |G|\delta_{gg'}$: rows orthonormal implies columns orthonormal, once the matrix is square — which is where $|\hat G| = |G|$ is used.

The Fourier transform of a finite abelian group

the Fourier transform $F_G$

From $\mathbb C^G$ (basis $\ket g$) to $\mathbb C^{\hat G}$ (basis $\ket\chi$):

$$F_G\ket g = \frac1{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{\chi\in\hat G}\chi(g)\,\ket\chi .$$
theorem

$F_G$ is unitary.

Proof. Its matrix $M_{\chi g} = \chi(g)/\sqrt{|G|}$ is square ($|\hat G| = |G|$) with orthonormal rows (orthogonality of characters), so $MM^\dagger = I = M^\dagger M$, as in the worked example. ∎

$G$characters$F_G$
$\mathbb Z_N$$\chi_k(j) = \omega^{jk}$$F_N\ket j = \frac1{\sqrt N}\sum_k\omega^{jk}\ket k$
$\mathbb Z_2^n$$\chi_y(x) = (-1)^{x\cdot y}$$H^{\otimes n}$ (unit 13)

Labelling $\chi_k$ by $k$ makes $F_N$ a map $\mathbb C^N\to\mathbb C^N$: the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) on $\mathbb Z_N$. On qubits, $N = 2^n$.

the sign, fixed for the course

$e^{+2\pi ijk/N}$ and $1/\sqrt N$. Numerical libraries' DFT uses $e^{-2\pi ijk/N}$ and no normalisation: their DFT is $\sqrt N\,F_N^\dagger$.

Two instances, one theorem. Unit 13 proved $H^{\otimes n}$ unitary by computing $H^{\otimes n}H^{\otimes n} = I$; unit 00's sheet checked $F_3$ by hand. Both are this theorem, and the proof is the one line of orthogonality.

Why "Fourier". For $G = \mathbb Z_N$ the coefficients $\braket{\chi_k|f}/\sqrt N$ of a function $f$ are the discrete Fourier coefficients of $f$, and the inverse transform writes $f$ as a sum of the "pure frequencies" $\chi_k$. On the circle group (not finite, not used here) the same construction gives Fourier series. The dual-orthogonality relation of the worked example is the statement that $F_G^{-1} = F_G^\dagger$, the inverse transform.

Basis states of $\mathbb C^{\hat G}$. Writing $\ket\chi$ as a basis vector is a labelling choice. For the computation the labels must be bit strings, and the isomorphism $\hat G\cong G$ supplies them: for $\mathbb Z_N$, $\chi_k\mapsto k$. That is the non-canonical step, made once and for all by the course's convention.

Shifts become phases

theorem

Let $T_a\ket g = \ket{g + a}$ (a permutation of the basis, so unitary) and $D_a = \sum_\chi\chi(a)\ket\chi\!\bra\chi$ (diagonal, entries of modulus 1, so unitary). Then

$$F_G\,T_a = D_a\,F_G .$$

Proof. $F_GT_a\ket g = |G|^{-1/2}\sum_\chi\chi(g + a)\ket\chi = |G|^{-1/2}\sum_\chi\chi(a)\chi(g)\ket\chi = D_aF_G\ket g$. ∎

Consequence. Measuring $F_GT_a\ket\psi$ in the basis $\ket\chi$ gives the same distribution as $F_G\ket\psi$: each amplitude only gains a factor $\chi(a)$ of modulus 1. An unknown shift is invisible after the transform.

$\mathbb Z_8$: a state on the coset $\{1, 5\}$ of the subgroup $\{0, 4\}$, and its shift by 2. After $F_8$ both give $\tfrac14$ on each even $k$; total $4\cdot\tfrac14 = 1$ ✓.

The example in the figure, computed. $F_8\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket1 + \ket5) = \tfrac14\sum_k(\omega^k + \omega^{5k})\ket k = \tfrac14\sum_k\omega^k(1 + (-1)^k)\ket k$, since $\omega^{4k} = (-1)^k$. Odd $k$ cancel; even $k$ have amplitude $\omega^k/2$ and probability $\tfrac14$. The shifted state gives $\tfrac14\sum_k\omega^{3k}(1 + (-1)^k)\ket k$: the same moduli, different phases, exactly as the theorem says with $D_2 = \mathrm{diag}(\omega^{2k})$.

Why this is the engine. Unit 14's Simon algorithm produced, after the query and a measurement of the second register, a state $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket x + \ket{x\oplus s})$ with $x$ uniformly random and unknown. That is a translate of the subgroup state $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket s)$. By this theorem the random $x$ disappears from the outcome distribution after $H^{\otimes n}$, and what is left depends only on the subgroup $\{0, s\}$: it is supported on the characters trivial on it, $y\cdot s = 0$. Unit 16 replaces $\mathbb Z_2^n$ by $\mathbb Z_N$ and $\{0, s\}$ by the multiples of a period $r$, and the same theorem puts the outcomes near multiples of $N/r$. The figure is the smallest instance: subgroup $\{0, 4\}$, period 4, outcomes the multiples of $8/4 = 2$.

The converse, for completeness. $F_G$ also turns multiplication by a character into a shift of the label: $F_G\,(\psi\cdot) = T\,F_G$, the dual statement. It is not needed in this unit.

On $\mathbb Z_{2^n}$, a basis state goes to a product state

binary fraction

For bits $j_l, \dots, j_n$: $\ 0.j_lj_{l+1}\cdots j_n = \dfrac{j_l}2 + \dfrac{j_{l+1}}4 + \cdots + \dfrac{j_n}{2^{n-l+1}}$.

Write $j\in\{0, \dots, 2^n - 1\}$ as $j = j_12^{n-1} + \cdots + j_n$: $j_1$ the most significant bit, on qubit 0.

the product formula $$F_{2^n}\ket j = \bigotimes_{l=1}^{n}\frac{\ket0 + e^{2\pi i\,0.j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n}\ket1}{\sqrt2},$$

factor $l$ on qubit $l - 1$: qubit 0 carries $0.j_n$, the last qubit carries $0.j_1j_2\cdots j_n$.

A product state: Schmidt rank 1 across every cut (unit 03). A Fourier transform of $2^n$ numbers, and the result is $n$ unentangled qubits, each on the Bloch equator. Each factor has norm $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sqrt{1 + 1} = 1$ ✓.

checks

$j = 0$: every phase is $0$, so $F\ket0 = \ket+^{\otimes n}$, the uniform superposition — column 0 of the table ✓.

$n = 1$: $F_2\ket{j_1} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{i\pi j_1}\ket1) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + (-1)^{j_1}\ket1) = H\ket{j_1}$ ✓.

$j = 2^{n-1}$ ($j_1 = 1$, rest 0): only the last factor has a phase, $e^{i\pi} = -1$; so $F\ket{10\cdots0} = \ket+^{\otimes(n-1)}\ket-$ ✓, the state $\sum_k(-1)^k\ket k/\sqrt{2^n}$.

Why it matters. A circuit acts on qubits; a product state is $n$ separate one-qubit states, so it can be built one qubit at a time. The next section turns this formula, read factor by factor, into gates. Nothing like it holds for a general unitary on $2^n$ dimensions: unit 10's count says most need $\Omega(4^n)$ gates. The QFT is one of the rare exceptions, and the product formula is the reason.

The pattern of the phases. Qubit 0 (the most significant output bit) sees only $j_n$, the parity of $j$; qubit 1 sees the last two bits, and so on. The least significant output qubit sees all of $j$. Read on the Bloch sphere, each factor is a point on the equator at longitude $2\pi\cdot0.j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n$.

Proof of the product formula

expand $k$ in binary

Write $k = \sum_{l=1}^nk_l2^{n-l}$, $k_l\in\{0, 1\}$, with $k_l$ on qubit $l - 1$, so $\ket k = \ket{k_1}\cdots\ket{k_n}$. Then $\dfrac{jk}{2^n} = \sum_l\dfrac{jk_l}{2^l}$ and

$$F\ket j = \frac1{2^{n/2}}\sum_{k_1, \dots, k_n}\ \bigotimes_{l=1}^n e^{2\pi i\,jk_l/2^l}\ket{k_l} .$$

The sum over $k$ is a sum over each $k_l$ independently, so it factorises:

$$F\ket j = \frac1{2^{n/2}}\bigotimes_{l=1}^n\big(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i\,j/2^l}\ket1\big) .$$

The last step. Dividing $j$ by $2^l$ moves the binary point $l$ places left:

$$\frac{j}{2^l} = \underbrace{j_1\cdots j_{n-l}}_{\text{an integer}}.\,j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n ,$$

and $e^{2\pi i\cdot\text{integer}} = 1$. So $e^{2\pi ij/2^l} = e^{2\pi i\,0.j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n}$, which is the formula. ∎

Every step is an identity of finite sums; the only idea is that $e^{2\pi i x}$ forgets the integer part of $x$.

The factorisation, in detail. For any vectors, $\sum_{k_1, \dots, k_n}a_1(k_1)\ket{k_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes a_n(k_n)\ket{k_n} = \big(\sum_{k_1}a_1(k_1)\ket{k_1}\big)\otimes\cdots\otimes\big(\sum_{k_n}a_n(k_n)\ket{k_n}\big)$ by multilinearity of $\otimes$ (unit 03). Here $a_l(k_l) = e^{2\pi ijk_l/2^l}$, which depends on $k_l$ alone — that is what the binary expansion of $k$ bought. The analogous expansion of $j$ is what reduces each phase to a binary fraction.

Where the course's conventions enter. Qubit 0 is the most significant bit of both $j$ and $k$, so $\ket k = \ket{k_1}\cdots\ket{k_n}$ with $k_1$ the coefficient of $2^{n-1}$, and $k_l$ meets $2^{-l}$. With the opposite order (Qiskit's) the factors appear in the opposite order. The sign $+2\pi i$ is the course's; with $-2\pi i$ every phase is conjugated.

History. The product formula is implicit in Coppersmith's 1994 report and in Shor's paper; the form here, with the binary-fraction notation, is from Cleve, Ekert, Macchiavello and Mosca (1998) and Nielsen and Chuang §5.1. The classical fast Fourier transform rests on the same factorisation, applied to vectors instead of states.

Two gates build every factor

the controlled phase rotation $R_k$ $$R_k = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & e^{2\pi i/2^k}\end{pmatrix} = P(2\pi/2^k),$$

unit 10's phase gate: diagonal with entries of modulus 1, so unitary ✓. $R_1 = Z$, $R_2 = S$, $R_3 = T$. Controlled, $C(R_k) = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, 1, e^{2\pi i/2^k})$: it puts a phase on $\ket{11}$ only, so it is symmetric in its two qubits.

(a) $H\ket{j_l} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i\,0.j_l}\ket1)$, since $e^{2\pi i\,j_l/2} = (-1)^{j_l}$.

(b) If the target is $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i\theta}\ket1)$ and the control holds the bit $b$, $C(R_k)$ makes it $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i(\theta + b/2^k)}\ket1)$.

$H$ writes the first digit; each $C(R_k)$ from the qubit $k - 1$ places below appends the next.

So $0.j_l$, then $+\tfrac{j_{l+1}}4$, then $+\tfrac{j_{l+2}}8$: after $H$ and $C(R_2), \dots, C(R_{n-l+1})$, the qubit holding $j_l$ carries $0.j_lj_{l+1}\cdots j_n$, and the controls are untouched.

Proof of (b). $C(R_k)$ is diagonal in the computational basis of both qubits; on $\ket a\ket b$ it multiplies by $e^{2\pi i\,ab/2^k}$. With the control in $\ket b$ and the target $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i\theta}\ket1)$, the $\ket0$ term is unchanged and the $\ket1$ term gains $e^{2\pi ib/2^k}$. The control is untouched, because the gate is diagonal. This is phase kickback (unit 13) in the case where the "eigenvector" is a basis state and the "eigenvalue" depends on its bit.

Why symmetric matters. Drawing $R_k$ on the upper wire with the dot on the lower one, or the other way round, is the same gate. Circuits in the literature draw it both ways; nothing changes.

Order of operations. The rotations on the qubit holding $j_l$ use the bits $j_{l+1}, \dots, j_n$ as controls, so those qubits must still hold their input bits. That is why the circuit treats qubit 0 first, then qubit 1, and so on: by the time a qubit is transformed, every qubit that still needs it as a control has finished.

Where the swaps go

Before the swaps, qubit $l - 1$ holds $0.j_l\cdots j_n$; the formula wants it on qubit $n - l$.

Derived in the course's order (qubit 0 the most significant bit of input and output): the gates finish qubit $l - 1$ with $0.j_l\cdots j_n$, but factor $l$ of the product formula, $0.j_{n-l+1}\cdots j_n$, belongs on qubit $l - 1$. The two orders are reverses, so the circuit ends with swaps of qubits $q$ and $n - 1 - q$ for $q \lt n/2$: $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ swaps.

sources differ

Nielsen and Chuang (Fig. 5.1) number qubits as this course does and also end with swaps — left undrawn, named in the caption.

Qiskit makes qubit 0 the least significant bit, so its drawn circuit is the mirror image, rotations aimed the other way.

Many implementations omit the swaps and read the output register backwards: a relabelling costs no gates. Phase estimation can do the same.

Before reusing a circuit, check its qubit order and its sign against $F\ket1 = 2^{-n/2}\sum_k\omega^k\ket k$.

The check suggested in the box. $F\ket1$, $j = 0\cdots01$: by the product formula qubit $l - 1$ carries $0.0\cdots01$ with $l$ digits, i.e. phase $e^{2\pi i/2^l}$. So qubit 0 carries $-1$, qubit 1 carries $i$, and the last qubit carries $\omega$. A circuit with the swaps missing puts $\omega$ on qubit 0 instead; a circuit with the opposite sign puts $\bar\omega$ on the last qubit. One input distinguishes all four possibilities.

Swaps first instead? Reversing the qubits before the gates is a different circuit: it would need the rotations aimed the other way (from the top qubits down) to produce the same matrix. Either arrangement is correct if derived; mixing them is the common error.

Cost. A swap is three CNOTs (unit 10, problem P1), so the reversal costs $3\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ CNOTs, linear in $n$, negligible beside the $n(n-1)/2$ rotations. In phase estimation and in Shor's algorithm the swaps are usually not built at all.

The QFT on $n$ qubits

theorem: gate count

$F_{2^n}$ is computed exactly by $n$ Hadamards, $\tfrac{n(n-1)}2$ controlled phases and $\lfloor n/2\rfloor$ swaps: $O(n^2)$ gates.

Proof. Qubit $l - 1$ needs the phase $0.j_l\cdots j_n$, of $n - l + 1$ digits: one $H$ and $n - l$ rotations $R_2, \dots, R_{n-l+1}$. Summing, $\sum_{l=1}^n(n - l) = \tfrac{n(n-1)}2$; the swaps are the previous slide's. ∎ Check, $n = 3$: $3$, $3$, $1$ — the running circuit ✓.

Correctness for general $n$ is the worked example's table with $n$ columns: qubit $q$ is untouched until its turn, then receives $H$ and its rotations, whose controls (qubits $q + 1, \dots, n - 1$) still hold their input bits; afterwards it holds $0.j_{q+1}\cdots j_n$ and is never touched again except by the final swaps. The reversal then produces the product formula.

Uniformity. The circuit for $n$ qubits is written down by a simple loop over $q$ and $k$, so the family is uniform in the sense of the assumed background: a Turing machine prints it in time polynomial in $n$. The gates are not from a finite set, since $R_k$'s angle shrinks with $k$; unit 11 says how to approximate them, and the next slides show that most of them can simply be dropped.

Depth. As drawn the depth is $O(n^2)$. Gates on disjoint qubits can run in the same time step, and pipelining the rotations brings the depth to $O(n)$; an approximate version reaches $O(\log n)$ (cited: R. Cleve and J. Watrous, "Fast parallel circuits for the quantum Fourier transform", FOCS 2000). Not needed here.

Exponentially faster at a task nobody wants

The fast Fourier transform on a $2^n$-vector against the QFT's gate count, log scale.

$n = 30$: the QFT is $30$ $H$ + $435$ controlled phases $= 465$ gates (+ 15 swaps). The FFT (Cooley–Tukey 1965, cited) takes about $n2^n = 30\cdot2^{30}\approx3.2\times10^{10}$ operations.

why the comparison misleads

1. You cannot read the output. $F\ket\psi$ is a state. A measurement returns one $k$, with probability $|\braket{k|F|\psi}|^2$, and destroys the rest. Estimating one amplitude to $\pm\varepsilon$ takes about $1/\varepsilon^2$ fresh runs; there are $2^n$ amplitudes.

2. You cannot load the input. An arbitrary $2^n$-vector needs exponentially many gates to prepare, by unit 10's parameter count.

3. So the QFT pays only when the computation itself prepares the input, and the answer is concentrated on a few outcomes that one sample reveals. Phase estimation is the case where both hold.

The numbers. $n(n-1)/2 = 30\cdot29/2 = 435$ controlled phases, plus $30$ Hadamards, is $465$ gates; the $\lfloor30/2\rfloor = 15$ swaps add 45 CNOTs if built. $30\cdot2^{30} = 32\,212\,254\,720\approx3.2\times10^{10}$. The FFT count is up to a constant: Cooley and Tukey's radix-2 recursion does $N/2$ butterflies at each of $\log N$ levels, about $\tfrac12N\log N$ complex multiplications.

Cited: the FFT. J. W. Cooley and J. W. Tukey, "An algorithm for the machine calculation of complex Fourier series", Math. Comp. 19, 297, 1965. The idea: $F_N$ on a vector splits into $F_{N/2}$ on the even-indexed entries and $F_{N/2}$ on the odd ones, recombined with $N/2$ multiplications by powers of $\omega$, so $T(N) = 2T(N/2) + O(N) = O(N\log N)$. The product formula is the same splitting, one bit at a time.

Item 1, made precise. By Chernoff–Hoeffding (assumed background), estimating a probability $p$ to $\pm\varepsilon$ with confidence $1 - \delta$ needs $O(\log(1/\delta)/\varepsilon^2)$ independent samples, and each sample costs a fresh preparation of $\ket\psi$ and a fresh QFT. Amplitude phases are harder still: they need interference with a reference.

Item 2. Unit 10 counted parameters for unitaries; the same count for states — a normalised state up to phase has $2^{n+1} - 2$ real parameters, and a circuit of $g$ two-qubit gates has at most $16g$ — shows most states need $\Omega(2^n)$ gates to prepare from $\ket{0\cdots0}$.

The comparison is still a theorem worth having: no classical algorithm could even write down the output of a $2^n$-point transform in fewer than $2^n$ steps, and the QFT produces it, in superposition, in $O(n^2)$. The art of quantum algorithms is arranging for that superposition to be worth one measurement.

The approximate QFT: most rotations can be dropped

The operator norm: $\lVert A\rVert = \max_{\lVert v\rVert = 1}\lVert Av\rVert$. For diagonal $D$, $\lVert D\rVert = \max_i|d_i|$.

lemma: errors add

For unitaries, $\lVert U_m\cdots U_1 - V_m\cdots V_1\rVert\le\sum_i\lVert U_i - V_i\rVert$.

Proof. Telescope: the difference is $\sum_iU_m\cdots U_{i+1}(U_i - V_i)V_{i-1}\cdots V_1$, and unitaries have norm 1. ∎

theorem: the approximate QFT (Coppersmith 1994)

Drop every $C(R_k)$ with $k \gt m$. The result $\tilde F$ satisfies

$$\lVert F - \tilde F\rVert\le\frac{2\pi(n - m)}{2^m} .$$

Proof. $\lVert C(R_k) - I\rVert = |e^{2\pi i/2^k} - 1| = 2\sin\tfrac\pi{2^k}\le\tfrac{2\pi}{2^k}$. There are $n - k + 1$ gates $C(R_k)$, and $n - k + 1\le n - m$ when $k \gt m$; so the lemma gives at most $(n - m)\sum_{k\gt m}2\pi/2^k = 2\pi(n - m)/2^m$. ∎

$n = 8$, $m = 3$: keep $13$ of the $28$ rotations.

The diagonal norm. $\lVert Dv\rVert^2 = \sum_i|d_i|^2|v_i|^2\le\max_i|d_i|^2\lVert v\rVert^2$, with equality at the basis vector where the max is attained. Here $C(R_k) - I = \mathrm{diag}(0, 0, 0, e^{2\pi i/2^k} - 1)$.

The telescoping, in full. $U_m\cdots U_1 - V_m\cdots V_1 = \sum_{i=1}^m\big(U_m\cdots U_iV_{i-1}\cdots V_1 - U_m\cdots U_{i+1}V_i\cdots V_1\big)$ — consecutive terms cancel, leaving the first minus the last. The $i$-th term is $U_m\cdots U_{i+1}(U_i - V_i)V_{i-1}\cdots V_1$, and $\lVert AB\rVert\le\lVert A\rVert\lVert B\rVert$ with $\lVert U\rVert = 1$ for unitary $U$. Apply it with $V_i = I$ for the dropped gates and $V_i = U_i$ for the rest.

The sine bound. $|e^{i\theta} - 1| = |e^{i\theta/2} - e^{-i\theta/2}| = 2|\sin(\theta/2)|\le|\theta|$.

What the norm means for outcomes. For any state $\ket\psi$ and projector $P$, with $a = F\ket\psi$ and $b = \tilde F\ket\psi$: $\big|\lVert Pa\rVert^2 - \lVert Pb\rVert^2\big| = \big|\lVert Pa\rVert - \lVert Pb\rVert\big|\,(\lVert Pa\rVert + \lVert Pb\rVert)\le\lVert a - b\rVert\cdot2\le2\lVert F - \tilde F\rVert$. So every outcome probability moves by at most twice the norm error.

Cited, with the proof given here. D. Coppersmith, "An approximate Fourier transform useful in quantum factoring", IBM Research Report RC 19642, 1994 (arXiv:quant-ph/0201067). Barenco, Ekert, Suominen and Törmä (Phys. Rev. A 54, 139, 1996) showed that with decoherence the approximate transform can do better than the exact one.

The check in detail. The experiment: an interferometer measures a phase

Unit 01's Mach–Zehnder interferometer: one photon, two paths, a slab adding phase $\varphi$ to one, two detectors.

The apparatus, from nothing. A half-silvered mirror (beam splitter $B$) sends a single photon into a superposition of two paths. Mirrors bring the paths together at a second splitter, and a detector on each exit clicks. A glass slab on path 1 delays it: in unit 01's model, $P(\varphi) = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\varphi})$ on the path qubit.

What it measures. The apparatus is the unitary $BP(\varphi)B$, and

$$\Pr(D_0) = \sin^2\tfrac\varphi2,\qquad\Pr(D_1) = \cos^2\tfrac\varphi2 ,$$

summing to 1 ✓. One photon is one biased coin. To learn $\varphi$ to $\pm\varepsilon$ by counting clicks takes about $1/\varepsilon^2$ photons (Chernoff–Hoeffding).

the question phase estimation answers

If the slab could be made $2, 4, 8, \dots$ times thicker — the phase doubled each time — could $t$ photons give $t$ binary digits of $\varphi$, instead of $4^t$ photons for $t$ digits?

Atomic clocks are interferometers of this kind with atoms in place of photons (Ramsey, 1950).

The calculation. With unit 01's $B = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & i\\ i & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and the photon entering path 0: $P(\varphi)B\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + ie^{i\varphi}\ket1)$, then $B$ gives $\tfrac12\big[(1 - e^{i\varphi})\ket0 + i(1 + e^{i\varphi})\ket1\big]$. So $\Pr(D_0) = \tfrac14|1 - e^{i\varphi}|^2 = \sin^2(\varphi/2)$ and $\Pr(D_1) = \cos^2(\varphi/2)$; with balanced arms, $\varphi = 0$, the photon always reaches $D_1$, and $D_0$ is unit 01's dark detector. The figure places them as unit 01 does: $D_0$ above the second splitter, $D_1$ to its right.

Why $1/\varepsilon^2$. Each photon is an independent Bernoulli trial with success probability $p = \sin^2(\varphi/2)$. With $M$ photons the empirical frequency is within $\pm\varepsilon'$ of $p$ with probability at least $1 - 2e^{-2M\varepsilon'^2}$ (Hoeffding), and away from $p\in\{0, 1\}$ an error $\varepsilon'$ in $p$ is an error of order $\varepsilon'$ in $\varphi$. So $M\sim1/\varepsilon^2$: $t$ binary digits, $\varepsilon = 2^{-t}$, cost about $4^t$ photons. Phase estimation will use $2^t - 1$ applications of the phase.

Ramsey's method (N. F. Ramsey, Phys. Rev. 78, 695, 1950; Nobel Prize 1989): an atom with two energy levels is a qubit. A short pulse of radiation acts on it as a beam splitter; the atom then evolves freely for a time $\tau$, during which its two levels acquire a relative phase proportional to $\tau$ times the mismatch between the atom's frequency and the radiation's; a second pulse recombines. Doubling $\tau$ doubles the phase — the "thicker slab". How a pulse acts on an atom is derived in unit 25 for a different two-level system and is taken as given here. Since 1967 the SI second has been defined by the caesium transition used in such clocks: $9\,192\,631\,770$ periods.

Kickback of an eigenphase

theorem: phase kickback of an eigenphase

If $U\ket u = e^{2\pi i\varphi}\ket u$, then for any $\alpha, \beta$

$$\begin{aligned}&C(U)\,(\alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1)\ket u\\ &\quad= (\alpha\ket0 + \beta e^{2\pi i\varphi}\ket1)\ket u .\end{aligned}$$

Proof. $\ket0\ket u\mapsto\ket0\ket u$ and $\ket1\ket u\mapsto\ket1U\ket u = e^{2\pi i\varphi}\ket1\ket u$; extend linearly. ∎ The eigenvector comes out unchanged, and the phase has moved to the control. Unit 13's kickback is $U = X$, $\ket u = \ket-$, $\varphi = \tfrac12$.

The Hadamard test: $H$, $C(U)$, $H$, measure. The control ends in $\tfrac12[(1 + e^{2\pi i\varphi})\ket0 + (1 - e^{2\pi i\varphi})\ket1]$, so

$$\Pr(0) = \cos^2\pi\varphi,\quad\Pr(1) = \sin^2\pi\varphi .$$

The interferometer again, with $H$ for $B$ and the eigenphase for the slab. With $C(U^2)$ the fringes are twice as fine.

Running example: $\varphi = \tfrac13$ gives $\Pr(0) = \cos^2\tfrac\pi3 = \tfrac14$, $\Pr(1) = \tfrac34$; sum 1 ✓.

Unitary eigenvalues. Every eigenvalue of a unitary has modulus 1 (unit 00), so it can always be written $e^{2\pi i\varphi}$ with $\varphi\in[0, 1)$: the eigenphase is well defined modulo 1, which is why phase estimation lives on the circle and why the estimate $0.999$ is close to $0$.

The Hadamard test, in full. $H\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)$; kickback gives $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{2\pi i\varphi}\ket1)$; $H$ maps this to $\tfrac12[(1 + e^{2\pi i\varphi})\ket0 + (1 - e^{2\pi i\varphi})\ket1]$. And $|1 + e^{i\theta}|^2 = 2 + 2\cos\theta = 4\cos^2(\theta/2)$, so $\Pr(0) = \cos^2(\pi\varphi)$.

What one test cannot tell. $\cos^2(\pi\varphi) = \cos^2(\pi(1 - \varphi))$: the test cannot separate $\varphi$ from $1 - \varphi$. A second test with $S^\dagger$ inserted before the last $H$ measures $\sin(2\pi\varphi)$ and resolves it. Phase estimation needs neither trick: the inverse Fourier transform combines all the doublings coherently.

Why "Hadamard test". The name is standard (it estimates $\mathrm{Re}\braket{u|U|u}$ when $\ket u$ is not an eigenvector). It is the $t = 1$ case of phase estimation, since $F_2^{-1} = H$.

The phase-estimation circuit

phase estimation, $t$ counting qubits

The counting register: $t$ qubits in $\ket+$. Counting qubit $l$ (qubit 0 on top) controls $U^{2^{t-1-l}}$ on $\ket u$. Then $F_{2^t}^{-1}$, then measure: outcome $y$, estimate $\tilde\varphi = y/2^t$.

Running example, $t = 3$: $U^2 = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{4\pi i/3})$, $U^4 = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{8\pi i/3}) = U$. Applications of $U$: $1 + 2 + 4 = 7 = 2^t - 1$ in all.

Each controlled power is unitary, as a controlled unitary (unit 10).

The circuit, gate by gate. $H$ on each counting qubit; $C(U)$ from qubit 2, $C(U^2)$ from qubit 1, $C(U^4)$ from qubit 0, all acting on the target $\ket u$; the inverse QFT on qubits 0–2; three meters. Unit 10's deferred-measurement principle says the meters can be moved anywhere after the last gate on their qubit, and also that the inverse QFT's controlled rotations may be replaced by classically controlled ones after measuring the controls first (Griffiths and Niu, Phys. The powers. For the running example $U^{2^j}$ is just another phase gate, so building it is free. In general it is not: implementing $U^{2^j}$ by repeating $U$ costs $2^j$ applications, $2^t - 1$ in all — the subject of What it costs, and what it is for.

History. Kitaev introduced the method ("Quantum measurements and the Abelian stabilizer problem", 1995, arXiv:quant-ph/9511026) with one counting qubit at a time; Cleve, Ekert, Macchiavello and Mosca (Proc. The general case: the outcome distribution

theorem

With $N = 2^t$ and $\delta_y = N\varphi - y$, outcome $y$ has probability

$$p(y) = \frac{\sin^2(\pi\delta_y)}{N^2\sin^2(\pi\delta_y/N)}\qquad(\delta_y\notin N\mathbb Z),$$

and $p(y) = 1$ if $\delta_y = 0$.

Proof. The amplitude of $\ket y$ after $F^{-1}$ is $\frac1N\sum_x\omega^{-xy}e^{2\pi i\varphi x} = \frac1N\sum_{x=0}^{N-1}r^x$ with $r = e^{2\pi i\delta_y/N}\ne1$. That is $\frac1N\frac{r^N - 1}{r - 1}$, and $|e^{i\theta} - 1| = 2|\sin\tfrac\theta2|$. ∎

checks

Sums to 1: $\sum_yp(y) = \lVert F^{-1}\ket\psi\rVert^2 = 1$, since $F^{-1}$ is unitary (Parseval).

Exact case: if $N\varphi$ is an integer, every other $\delta_y$ is a non-zero integer and $\sin\pi\delta_y = 0$: $p(y) = 0$ ✓.

$t = 1$, $\varphi = \tfrac13$: $p(0) = \dfrac{\sin^2(2\pi/3)}{4\sin^2(\pi/3)} = \tfrac14$, the Hadamard test's $\cos^2\tfrac\pi3$ ✓.

The distribution depends on $\varphi$ only through $N\varphi$: it peaks at the integers nearest $2^t\varphi$, and falls off like $1/\delta_y^2$.

The amplitude, step by step. $F^{-1}\ket x = N^{-1/2}\sum_y\omega^{-xy}\ket y$, so $\braket{y|F^{-1}|\psi} = N^{-1/2}\sum_x\omega^{-xy}\,N^{-1/2}e^{2\pi i\varphi x}$, and $\omega^{-xy}e^{2\pi i\varphi x} = e^{2\pi ix(\varphi - y/N)} = e^{2\pi ix\delta_y/N}$. The geometric series has ratio $r = e^{2\pi i\delta_y/N}$, which is 1 exactly when $\delta_y\in N\mathbb Z$. Then $|r^N - 1| = |e^{2\pi i\delta_y} - 1| = 2|\sin\pi\delta_y|$ and $|r - 1| = 2|\sin(\pi\delta_y/N)|$.

Parseval proves a trigonometric identity. Since the probabilities sum to 1 for every $\varphi$, setting $x = \pi\varphi$ gives $\sum_{y=0}^{N-1}\csc^2(x - \pi y/N) = N^2\csc^2(Nx)$ for $Nx\notin\pi\mathbb Z$. (It can be proved directly by partial fractions; here it falls out of unitarity.)

Wrap-around. $\delta_y$ and $\delta_y + N$ give the same $p$: the outcomes live on a circle of $N$ points, and $y = 0$ is adjacent to $y = N - 1$. An eigenphase near $1$ produces outcomes near $0$ as well as near $N - 1$; both are good estimates of a phase modulo 1.

theorem

Let $\ket\psi = \sum_sc_s\ket{u_s}$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $U\ket{u_s} = e^{2\pi i\varphi_s}\ket{u_s}$ and $\sum_s|c_s|^2 = 1$. Phase estimation on $\ket\psi$ gives

$$\Pr(y) = \sum_s|c_s|^2\,p_{\varphi_s}(y) ,$$

exactly as if $s$ were drawn with probability $|c_s|^2$ first and then $\varphi_s$ estimated.

Proof. By linearity the final state is $\sum_sc_s\ket{\Phi_s}\ket{u_s}$, $\ket{\Phi_s}$ the register state for $\varphi_s$. Then $\Pr(y) = \lVert\sum_sc_s\braket{y|\Phi_s}\ket{u_s}\rVert^2 = \sum_s|c_s|^2|\braket{y|\Phi_s}|^2$, cross terms vanishing by orthonormality. ∎

The running $U$ with $\ket+ = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)$ as input: $\ket0$ has phase $0$, $\ket1$ has phase $\tfrac13$.

$\Pr(000) = \tfrac12\cdot1 + \tfrac12\cdot0.0156 = 0.5078$; the rest is half the familiar distribution. Total $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ ✓.

The fact Shor uses. In order finding (unit 16), $U$ multiplies by $a$ modulo $N$, and its eigenvectors $\ket{u_s}$ have phases $s/r$, $s = 0, \dots, r - 1$, where $r$ is the unknown order. theorem: the $4/\pi^2$ bound

Let $y^*$ be the integer nearest $2^t\varphi$ (mod $2^t$), so $|\delta| \le\tfrac12$ with $\delta = 2^t\varphi - y^*$. Then

$$p(y^*)\ge\frac4{\pi^2}\approx0.405 .$$
proof

If $\delta = 0$, $p = 1$. Otherwise use two inequalities:

$|\sin(\pi\delta/2^t)|\le\pi|\delta|/2^t$, since $|\sin x|\le|x|$;
$|\sin\pi\delta|\ge2|\delta|$ for $|\delta|\le\tfrac12$, since $\sin$ is concave on $[0, \tfrac\pi2]$ and so lies above its chord: $\sin x\ge\tfrac{2}{\pi}x$.

$$p(y^*)\ge\frac{4\delta^2}{2^{2t}\,\pi^2\delta^2/2^{2t}} = \frac4{\pi^2}.\ \ ∎$$

$p(y^*)$ against $\delta$. As $t\to\infty$ it tends to $\big(\frac{\sin\pi\delta}{\pi\delta}\big)^2$, which is $4/\pi^2$ at $\delta = \pm\tfrac12$.

Running example: $\delta = -\tfrac13$, $p = 0.688\ge0.405$ ✓.

Concavity, in full. On $[0, \pi/2]$, $\sin'' = -\sin\le0$, so $\sin$ lies above the chord from $(0, 0)$ to $(\pi/2, 1)$, which is $x\mapsto2x/\pi$. With $x = \pi|\delta|\le\pi/2$ this gives $|\sin\pi\delta|\ge2|\delta|$ (Jordan's inequality). And $|\sin x|\le|x|$ for all real $x$ because $|\sin x| = |\int_0^x\cos|\le|x|$.

The two nearest together. At $\delta = \tfrac12$ both neighbours have the same probability, so by the theorem the two together have at least $8/\pi^2\approx0.81$. $n$ bits with confidence $1 - \varepsilon$

theorem (Cleve et al. 1998; Nielsen and Chuang §5.2.1)

With $t = n + \big\lceil\log\big(2 + \tfrac1{2\varepsilon}\big)\big\rceil$ counting qubits, the estimate satisfies $|\tilde\varphi - \varphi| \lt 2^{-n}$ (distance on the circle) with probability at least $1 - \varepsilon$.

Proof, in outline (full in the notes). Let $b$ be the largest integer with $b\le2^t\varphi$. With $\Delta = 2^t\varphi - b\in[0, 1)$, the amplitude of outcome $b + l$ is at most $\dfrac1{2|l - \Delta|}$ in modulus. Summing the squares over $|l| \gt e$ gives at most $\dfrac1{2(e - 1)}$. Accept $|l|\le e = 2^{t-n} - 1$; then $\dfrac1{2(2^{t-n} - 2)}\le\varepsilon$ is the condition on $t$.

ten bits at 99 per cent

$n = 10$, $\varepsilon = 0.01$: $2 + \tfrac1{0.02} = 52$, $\log52 = 5.70$, so $t = 10 + 6 = 16$.

Guaranteed failure $\le\tfrac1{2(2^6 - 2)} = \tfrac1{124} = 0.0081$. Computed worst case over $\varphi$: about $0.0032$. The bound is safe, and about $2.5$ times pessimistic.

"n correct bits"

means $|\tilde\varphi - \varphi| \lt 2^{-n}$. The binary digits can still differ through a carry: $\varphi = 0.0111\cdots1$ just below $\tfrac12$, estimate $0.1000\cdots$.

Full proof. Let $N = 2^t$, $b = \lfloor N\varphi\rfloor$, $\Delta = N\varphi - b\in[0, 1)$. For $-N/2 \lt l\le N/2$ the amplitude of outcome $(b + l)\bmod N$ is $\alpha_l = \frac1N\sum_xe^{2\pi ix(\Delta - l)/N} = \frac1N\frac{1 - e^{2\pi i(\Delta - l)}}{1 - e^{2\pi i(\Delta - l)/N}}$. The numerator has modulus at most 2. In the denominator $\theta = 2\pi(\Delta - l)/N$ satisfies $|\theta|\le\pi$ (from the range of $l$ and $0\le\Delta \lt 1$), and $|1 - e^{i\theta}| = 2|\sin(\theta/2)|\ge2|\theta|/\pi$ by the chord bound of the previous slide. So $|\alpha_l|\le\frac1N\cdot\frac{2}{4|\Delta - l|/N} = \frac1{2|l - \Delta|}$.

Now sum over $|l| \gt e$ for an integer $e\ge2$. For $l\ge e + 1$, $l - \Delta \gt l - 1\ge e$; for $l\le-(e + 1)$, $|l - \Delta|\ge|l|\ge e + 1$. Hence $\Pr(|l| \gt e)\le\frac14\big[\sum_{l\ge e+1}\frac1{(l-1)^2} + \sum_{l\ge e+1}\frac1{l^2}\big]\le\frac12\sum_{m\ge e}\frac1{m^2}\le\frac12\int_{e-1}^\infty\frac{\mathrm dm}{m^2} = \frac1{2(e - 1)}$.

Finally, if $|l|\le e = 2^{t-n} - 1$ then $|\tilde\varphi - \varphi| = |l - \Delta|/N \lt (e + 1)/N = 2^{-n}$: for $l\ge0$, $l - \Delta \gt -1$ and $l - \Delta\le e$; for $l \lt 0$, $|l - \Delta| = |l| + \Delta \lt e + 1$. So the failure probability is at most $\frac1{2(2^{t-n} - 2)}$, which is $\le\varepsilon$ exactly when $2^{t-n}\ge2 + \frac1{2\varepsilon}$, i.e. $t - n\ge\log(2 + \frac1{2\varepsilon})$. ∎

What it costs, and what it is for

The cost in uses of $U$. The controlled powers are $C(U^{2^j})$, $j = 0, \dots, t - 1$. Built by repeating $C(U)$, that is $2^t - 1$ uses for precision $2^{-t}$: cost $O(1/\varepsilon)$, against $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ for repeating the Hadamard test. A quadratic saving, no more.

The exponential saving needs fast powers: a circuit for $U^{2^j}$ of size polynomial in $j$. That is a property of $U$, not of phase estimation.

One circuit, three subjects: the engine of the next four units.

Shor's fast powers. $U_a^{2^j}$ multiplies by $a^{2^j}\bmod N$, which is again a single modular multiplication by a number a classical computer finds by $j$ squarings. So each controlled power costs one controlled modular multiplier, $O(L^2)$ gates for an $L$-bit $N$, and the whole of phase estimation is polynomial (unit 16).

Quantum counting (unit 17) has no fast powers: the Grover rotation's powers cost one oracle query each, so $t$ bits cost about $2^t$ queries — which is exactly Grover's quadratic scaling, and optimal by unit 18.

Energy readout (unit 19): an eigenvector of a Hamiltonian $H$ with energy $E$ is an eigenvector of $e^{-iH\tau}$ with eigenphase $-E\tau/2\pi$ modulo 1 (with $\hbar = 1$); simulating the evolution for time $2^j\tau$ is the controlled power, at cost growing with the time. Phase estimation of an approximate ground state is the standard proposal for computing molecular energies.

The Heisenberg limit. In metrology the $1/\varepsilon$ scaling with the number of uses is called the Heisenberg limit and $1/\varepsilon^2$ the standard quantum limit; phase estimation attains the former. No strategy does better than $\Omega(1/\varepsilon)$ uses (cited: e.g. Giovannetti, Lloyd and Maccone, Phys. Rev. Lett. 96, 010401, 2006).

the running example, answered

Three counting qubits, $U$ applied $7$ times, inverse $F_8$ (7 gates). Outcome $3/8 = 0.375$ with probability $0.688\ge4/\pi^2 = 0.405$ ✓; one of $2/8, 3/8, 4/8$ with $0.910$. For ten bits at 99 per cent: 16 counting qubits.
