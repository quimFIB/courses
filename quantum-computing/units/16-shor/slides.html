Why factoring: RSA in one slide

the RSA cryptosystem (1977)

Primes $p \ne q$, $N = pq$, $\varphi(N) = (p - 1)(q - 1)$. Public: $N$ and an $e$ coprime to $\varphi(N)$. Secret: $d = e^{-1} \bmod \varphi(N)$.

Encrypt $m \mapsto c = m^e \bmod N$; decrypt $c \mapsto c^d \bmod N$.

Why it decrypts. $ed = 1 + k\varphi(N)$, so $c^d = m\,(m^{\varphi(N)})^k \equiv m$ by Euler's theorem, for $\gcd(m, N) = 1$.

Why it is believed secure. From $N$ alone, $d$ seems to need $\varphi(N)$ — and $\varphi(N)$ gives the factors: $p$ and $q$ are the roots of $X^2 - (N - \varphi(N) + 1)X + N$.

Everything Eve sees is public; factoring $N$ is all she lacks.

The best classical factoring algorithm known, the number field sieve, takes time $\exp\!\big(O(n^{1/3}(\log n)^{2/3})\big)$ for an $n$-bit $N$ (cited): faster than exponential, slower than any polynomial. This unit's algorithm uses $O(n^3)$ gates.

The computational question is the motivation. This unit has no experiment and no physics: what forces its definitions is a question about computation — can a quantum computer factor in polynomial time? — and RSA is why the answer matters.

Decryption for every $m$. If $p \mid m$, then $m^{ed} \equiv 0 \equiv m \pmod p$; otherwise $m^{ed} = m\,(m^{p-1})^{k(q-1)} \equiv m \pmod p$ by Fermat. The same holds mod $q$, and the CRT gives $m^{ed} \equiv m \pmod N$.

Knowing $\varphi(N)$ is knowing the factors. $(X - p)(X - q) = X^2 - (p + q)X + pq$, and $p + q = pq - (p-1)(q-1) + 1 = N - \varphi(N) + 1$. Knowing $d$ is also enough: $ed - 1$ is a multiple of every order modulo $N$, and this section's square-root trick, run with a known multiple of $r$ in place of $r$ itself, splits $N$ with probability at least ½ per random $a$. Problem P4 on the sheet works it through.

The number field sieve (Lenstra, Lenstra, Manasse and Pollard, 1990; analysis in Lenstra and Lenstra, eds., The Development of the Number Field Sieve, 1993) runs in heuristic time $\exp\big((1.923 + o(1))(\ln N)^{1/3}(\ln\ln N)^{2/3}\big)$, cited. With it, the 829-bit RSA-250 challenge was factored in 2020. No polynomial-time classical algorithm is known, and none is known to be impossible.

History. Rivest, Shamir and Adleman, Comm. ACM 21, 120 (1978), circulated in 1977. Shor's paper (FOCS 1994; SIAM J. Comput. 26, 1484, 1997) is what turned quantum computing from a curiosity into a field.

Order, and a square root of 1 that splits N

the order of $a$ modulo $N$

For $\gcd(a, N) = 1$: the least $r \ge 1$ with $a^r \equiv 1 \pmod N$. It exists, because $a^{\varphi(N)} \equiv 1$ (Euler), and $r$ divides every $k$ with $a^k \equiv 1$.

a square root of 1 splits $N$

If $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod N$ but $x \not\equiv \pm1$, then $\gcd(x - 1, N)$ and $\gcd(x + 1, N)$ are proper factors of $N$.

proof

$N \mid (x - 1)(x + 1)$. If $\gcd(x - 1, N) = 1$ then $N \mid x + 1$: excluded. If it is $N$, then $x \equiv 1$: excluded. Likewise for $x + 1$.

Residues mod 21 by their residues mod 3 and mod 7 (the CRT). The square roots of 1 sit where both coordinates are $\pm1$.

An odd $N$ with $m$ distinct prime factors has $2^m$ square roots of 1, so a nontrivial square root of 1 exists exactly when $m \ge 2$.

Check, 21: $x = 8$, $64 = 3\cdot21 + 1$ ✓; $\gcd(7, 21) = 7$ and $\gcd(9, 21) = 3$ ✓.

The order divides every exponent that works. If $a^k \equiv 1$, write $k = qr + s$ with $0 \le s < r$; then $a^s = a^k(a^r)^{-q} \equiv 1$, and minimality forces $s = 0$.

Counting square roots of 1. By the CRT, $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod N$ iff $x^2 \equiv 1$ modulo each prime-power factor $p^k$ of $N$, independently. Modulo an odd prime power there are exactly two roots, $\pm1$ (proved on Prime powers, primes, and the general case). So an odd $N = \prod_{i=1}^m p_i^{k_i}$ has $2^m$ roots, and only two of them, $(1, \dots, 1)$ and $(-1, \dots, -1)$, are $\pm1$. With $m \ge 2$ the other $2^m - 2$ are nontrivial.

The figure. 8 sits at $(8 \bmod 3, 8 \bmod 7) = (2, 1) = (-1, 1)$, and 13 at $(1, 6) = (1, -1)$. The two nontrivial roots are the two mixed-sign corners — and a mixed sign is exactly why $x - 1$ shares a factor with $N$ ($x \equiv 1$ modulo one prime) without being divisible by $N$.

The lemma is old. It is the basis of Fermat's factoring method and of every modern sieve: find $x$ and $y$ with $x^2 \equiv y^2 \pmod N$ and $x \not\equiv \pm y$, and $\gcd(x - y, N)$ is a factor. Here $y = 1$, and the order supplies $x$.

The reduction, as an algorithm

the order-finding reduction

Input: $N$, and a subroutine returning orders mod $N$.

  1. $N$ prime (a primality test, cited): stop. $N$ even: output 2.
  2. $N = p^k$, $k \ge 2$: output $p$ (integer roots).
  3. Pick $a$ uniformly in $\{2, \dots, N - 1\}$. If $\gcd(a, N) > 1$, output it.
  4. $r \leftarrow$ the order of $a$ mod $N$.
  5. If $r$ is even and $a^{r/2} \not\equiv -1$: output $\gcd(a^{r/2} - 1, N)$. Otherwise return to 3.

Why step 5 is right. $x = a^{r/2}$ has $x^2 = a^r \equiv 1$; $x \not\equiv 1$ because $r/2 < r$ and $r$ is the least; $x \not\equiv -1$ by the test. So $x$ is a nontrivial square root of 1.

Cost of one round: one order finding, plus $\gcd$s and one power by repeated squaring — $O(n^3)$ bit operations for $n = \lceil\log_2N\rceil$. The next slides show a round succeeds with probability at least ½.

Steps 1–5. A "no" at the last test costs one more round.

Where it comes from. The argument is the randomised form of one in G. L. Miller's 1976 paper on primality (J. Comput. System Sci. 13, 300), which showed that a multiple of the orders — $\varphi(N)$, say — lets one factor $N$. Shor's contribution was the subroutine in step 4, not the reduction.

Primality is decided in randomised polynomial time by the Miller–Rabin test (Rabin 1980) and in deterministic polynomial time by Agrawal, Kayal and Saxena (2002); both cited. A prime must be caught first, because for prime $N$ the only square roots of 1 are $\pm1$ and step 5 would loop for ever.

Step 3 is not wasted when it "fails". If $\gcd(a, N) > 1$ we have a factor without any order finding. For an RSA modulus this happens with probability $(p + q - 2)/(N - 2)$, negligible; the theorem on When does a random a work? is about $a$ uniform in $\mathbb Z_N^\times$, and conditioning step 3 on $\gcd(a, N) = 1$ gives $a$ uniform in $\mathbb Z_N^\times$ except for $a = 1$. Since $a = 1$ always fails (its order is 1), leaving it out can only raise the success probability.

Cost. A $\gcd$ of two $n$-bit numbers takes $O(n)$ division steps of $O(n^2)$ bit operations. The power $a^{r/2} \bmod N$, with $r < N$, takes at most $2n$ multiplications mod $N$ by repeated squaring, each $O(n^2)$. So $O(n^3)$ per round, plus the order finding. The prime-power test is on Prime powers, primes, and the general case.

Worked: 15 with a = 7, and 21 with a = 2

two runs of the reduction, every step

$N = 15$, $a = 7$. $\gcd(7, 15) = 1$. Powers of 7 mod 15: $7$; $49 = 3\cdot15 + 4$, so $4$; $4\cdot7 = 28 \equiv 13$; $13\cdot7 = 91 = 6\cdot15 + 1$, so $1$. Hence $r = 4$, even.

$x = 7^2 \equiv 4$, and $-1 \equiv 14$: $x \not\equiv \pm1$. Euclid: $\gcd(3, 15) = 3$ since $15 = 5\cdot3$; $\gcd(5, 15) = 5$ since $15 = 3\cdot5$. And $3\times5 = 15$ ✓.

$N = 21$, $a = 2$ (the running example). $\gcd(2, 21) = 1$. Powers of 2 mod 21: $2, 4, 8, 16$; $32 = 21 + 11$, so $11$; $22 = 21 + 1$, so $1$. Hence $r = 6$, even.

$x = 2^3 = 8$, and $-1 \equiv 20$: $x \not\equiv \pm1$. Euclid: $21 = 3\cdot7$, so $\gcd(7, 21) = 7$; $21 = 2\cdot9 + 3$ and $9 = 3\cdot3$, so $\gcd(9, 21) = 3$. And $7\times3 = 21$ ✓.

The powers of $a$ go round a cycle of length $r$; $a^{r/2}$ is the point opposite 1.

Here the order came from listing powers — $r$ steps, which for a 2048-bit $N$ could be about $2^{2048}$. Finding $r$ fast is the whole problem.

Why both factors are proper. For 15: $x - 1 = 3$ and $x + 1 = 5$; $15 \mid 3\cdot5$ but divides neither. For 21: $x - 1 = 7$, $x + 1 = 9$; $21 \mid 63$, and $21$ divides neither 7 nor 9. In both cases each $\gcd$ picks out one prime, because $x \equiv 1$ modulo one prime and $x \equiv -1$ modulo the other: $8 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$ and $8 \equiv -1 \pmod 3$; $4 \equiv 1 \pmod 3$ and $4 \equiv -1 \pmod 5$.

The orders behind the scenes. Mod 3, 2 has order 2; mod 7, 2 has order 3; $\operatorname{lcm}(2, 3) = 6$. The next slide shows that this mismatch — one order even, the other odd — is exactly why $a = 2$ works.

How long the list can be. The order divides $\operatorname{lcm}(p - 1, q - 1)$ and can equal it, and that is at least $\varphi(N)/\gcd(p - 1, q - 1)$ — for an RSA modulus, comparable to $N$. Listing powers is then exponential in $n$. No polynomial-time classical algorithm for the order is known, and by this section one would factor.

When does a random a work?

theorem: the reduction succeeds half the time

Let $N$ be odd with at least two distinct prime factors, $a$ uniform in $\mathbb Z_N^\times$, and $r$ its order. Then, modulo $N$,

$$\Pr\big[\,r \text{ even},\ a^{r/2} \not\equiv -1\,\big] \ge \tfrac12 .$$

Proved here for $N = pq$; the general case is cited on Prime powers, primes, and the general case.

key lemma, $N = pq$

Let $r_p, r_q$ be the orders of $a$ mod $p$ and mod $q$, and $v(m)$ the 2-adic valuation: the exponent of 2 in $m$. Then $a$ succeeds iff $v(r_p) \ne v(r_q)$.

proof of the lemma

$r = \operatorname{lcm}(r_p, r_q)$, since $a^k \equiv 1 \pmod N$ iff it holds mod $p$ and mod $q$.

Both valuations 0: $r$ is odd. Fail.

Both $d \ge 1$: $v(r/2) = d - 1$, so $r_p \nmid r/2$, and $a^{r/2}$ is a square root of 1 mod $p$ other than 1 — so it is $-1$ mod $p$. Likewise mod $q$; so $a^{r/2} \equiv -1 \pmod N$. Fail.

Unequal, say $v(r_p) > v(r_q)$: $r$ is even and $r_q \mid r/2$, so $a^{r/2} \equiv 1 \pmod q$, hence $\not\equiv -1 \pmod N$. Succeed.

Check, 21: $a = 2$ has $r_3 = 2$, $r_7 = 3$, valuations $1 \ne 0$ — succeeds ✓. $a = 5$ has $r_3 = 2$, $r_7 = 6$, both valuation 1, and $5^3 = 125 = 5\cdot21 + 20 \equiv -1$ — fails ✓.

Full proof of the lemma

The order mod $N$. $a^k \equiv 1 \pmod{pq}$ iff $p \mid a^k - 1$ and $q \mid a^k - 1$ (distinct primes), iff $r_p \mid k$ and $r_q \mid k$, iff $\operatorname{lcm}(r_p, r_q) \mid k$. So $r = \operatorname{lcm}(r_p, r_q)$, and $v(r) = \max(v(r_p), v(r_q))$.

Square roots of 1 mod a prime. $\mathbb Z_p$ is a field, so $x^2 - 1 = (x - 1)(x + 1) = 0$ forces $x = \pm1$, and $1 \ne -1$ as $p$ is odd.

Case $v(r_p) = v(r_q) = d \ge 1$. Then $v(r) = d$, so $r$ is even and $v(r/2) = d - 1 < v(r_p)$; hence $r_p \nmid r/2$ and $a^{r/2} \not\equiv 1 \pmod p$. But $(a^{r/2})^2 = a^r \equiv 1$, so $a^{r/2} \equiv -1 \pmod p$. The same argument gives $a^{r/2} \equiv -1 \pmod q$, and by the CRT $a^{r/2} \equiv -1 \pmod N$.

Case $v(r_p) > v(r_q)$. Write $r = 2^{v(r_p)}m$ with $m$ odd, the lcm of the odd parts. Then $r_q = 2^{v(r_q)}m_q$ with $m_q \mid m$ and $v(r_q) \le v(r_p) - 1$, so $r_q \mid 2^{v(r_p) - 1}m = r/2$. Hence $a^{r/2} \equiv 1 \pmod q$. If also $a^{r/2} \equiv -1 \pmod N$, then $1 \equiv -1 \pmod q$, impossible for odd $q$. And $r$ is even since $v(r) = v(r_p) \ge 1$. The case $v(r_q) > v(r_p)$ is symmetric.

Case $v(r_p) = v(r_q) = 0$. $r$ is the lcm of two odd numbers, so odd.

The four cases are exhaustive, so success $\iff v(r_p) \ne v(r_q)$. The theorem then needs only the distribution of $v(r_p)$, which is the next slide.

Half the residues carry all the twos

lemma

$p$ an odd prime, $p - 1 = 2^em$ with $m$ odd, $a$ uniform in $\mathbb Z_p^\times$. Then $v(\operatorname{ord}a) = e$ with probability exactly ½. So $\Pr[v(\operatorname{ord}a) = d] \le \tfrac12$ for every $d$.

proof

Squaring is 2-to-1 on $\mathbb Z_p^\times$ ($x^2 = y^2$ iff $x = \pm y$), so half the residues are squares, the quadratic residues. A square $b^2$ has $(b^2)^{(p-1)/2} = b^{p-1} = 1$: $v(\operatorname{ord}) \le e - 1$. The squares fill all $\tfrac{p-1}2$ roots of $X^{(p-1)/2} - 1$, so a non-square has $a^{(p-1)/2} \ne 1$: its order divides $2^em$ but not $2^{e-1}m$, so $v(\operatorname{ord}) = e$.

Hence, for $N = pq$: $\ \Pr[\text{fail}] = \sum_d \Pr[v(r_p) = d]\,\Pr[v(r_q) = d] \le \tfrac12\sum_d\Pr[v(r_p) = d] = \tfrac12$, with $a \bmod p$ and $a \bmod q$ independent by the CRT.

$\mathbb Z_{21}^\times \cong \mathbb Z_3^\times \times \mathbb Z_7^\times$. In brackets, the valuation of the order modulo 3 and modulo 7.

check: all twelve $a$ for 21

Exactly 6 of 12 fail — 1, 4, 16 with odd order; 5, 17, 20 with $a^{r/2} \equiv -1$. The bound ½ is attained.

Full proof

Exactly half are squares. The map $x \mapsto x^2$ on $\mathbb Z_p^\times$ is a homomorphism whose kernel is $\{x : x^2 = 1\} = \{\pm1\}$, of size 2 because $p$ is odd. So its image has $(p - 1)/2$ elements.

Squares have the smaller valuation. If $a = b^2$, then $a^{(p-1)/2} = b^{p-1} = 1$ by Fermat, so $\operatorname{ord}a \mid (p - 1)/2 = 2^{e-1}m$, and $v(\operatorname{ord}a) \le e - 1$.

Non-squares have valuation exactly $e$. The polynomial $X^{(p-1)/2} - 1$ over the field $\mathbb Z_p$ has at most $(p - 1)/2$ roots; the $(p-1)/2$ squares are all roots, so they are all of them. A non-square $a$ therefore has $a^{(p-1)/2} \ne 1$, so $\operatorname{ord}a \nmid 2^{e-1}m$. But $\operatorname{ord}a \mid p - 1 = 2^em$ (Fermat). Write $\operatorname{ord}a = 2^jm'$ with $m' \mid m$; if $j \le e - 1$ it would divide $2^{e-1}m$. So $j = e$.

The bound. By the CRT, $a \mapsto (a \bmod p, a \bmod q)$ is a bijection $\mathbb Z_N^\times \to \mathbb Z_p^\times \times \mathbb Z_q^\times$, so a uniform $a$ gives independent uniform residues. Failure is the event $v(r_p) = v(r_q)$, whose probability is $\sum_d \Pr[v(r_p) = d]\Pr[v(r_q) = d]$. Each factor $\Pr[v(r_q) = d] \le \tfrac12$ by the lemma, and the $\Pr[v(r_p) = d]$ sum to 1. So $\Pr[\text{fail}] \le \tfrac12$.

The grid, explained. $p - 1 = 2$ for $p = 3$ and $6 = 2\cdot3$ for $p = 7$: both have $e = 1$. Mod 3 the square is 1 (valuation 0) and the non-square 2 (valuation 1). Mod 7 the squares are $1, 2, 4$ (orders 1, 3, 3: valuation 0) and the non-squares $3, 5, 6$ (orders 6, 6, 2: valuation 1). A cell fails when its row and column valuations agree: $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$.

No cyclic-group theorem needed. The usual proof uses that $\mathbb Z_p^\times$ is cyclic. The argument above uses only Fermat and the root count, both background.

Prime powers, primes, and the general case

modulo an odd prime power, 1 has two square roots

$p$ odd: $x^2 \equiv 1 \pmod{p^k}$ implies $x \equiv \pm1$.

Why: $p^k \mid (x - 1)(x + 1)$, and $p$ divides at most one factor, since they differ by 2. So $p^k$ divides that one.

So for $N = p^k$ the reduction always fails: whenever $r$ is even, $a^{r/2}$ is a square root of 1 other than 1, hence $-1$. The same for prime $N$ ($k = 1$). These cases are removed first, classically.

testing for a perfect power

For $k = 2, \dots, \lfloor\log_2N\rfloor$: find $\lfloor N^{1/k}\rfloor$ by bisection and test whether its $k$-th power is $N$. At most $n$ exponents, $n$ bisection steps, $O(n^2\log n)$ per power: $O(n^4\log n)$ in all.

check, 21

$\lfloor\log_221\rfloor = 4$. $4^2 = 16 < 21 < 25$; $2^3 = 8 < 21 < 27$; $2^4 = 16 < 21 < 81$. Not a perfect power ✓.

the general case (cited)

$N$ odd with $m \ge 2$ distinct prime factors, $a$ uniform in $\mathbb Z_N^\times$: the reduction fails with probability at most $2^{1-m}$.

The proof above, with each $\mathbb Z_{p^k}^\times$ cyclic of even order. For $m = 2$ the bound is ½, attained by 21.

a misprint to watch for

Some texts print $1 - 2^{-m}$ for the success probability. $N = 21$ has $m = 2$ and fails exactly half the time, so the exponent must be $1 - m$.

Prime powers in full. If $p \mid x - 1$ and $p \mid x + 1$ then $p \mid 2$, impossible for odd $p$. So $p^k$, which divides the product, divides one factor entirely: $x \equiv 1$ or $x \equiv -1 \pmod{p^k}$. When $r$ is even, $a^{r/2} \not\equiv 1$ by minimality, so $a^{r/2} \equiv -1$ and step 5 rejects $a$ every time.

The perfect-power test in full. If $N = b^k$ with $b \ge 2$, then $k \le \log_2N$. For each such $k$, bisect on $b \in [1, 2^{\lceil n/k\rceil}]$: at most $n$ steps, each computing $b^k$ by repeated squaring and stopping as soon as the partial product exceeds $N$ — $O(\log k)$ multiplications of numbers below $2N$, so $O(n^2\log n)$ bit operations. Total $O(n\cdot n\cdot n^2\log n)$: crude, but polynomial. If the test finds $N = b^k$, recurse on $b$ to reach the prime.

The general case. For $N = \prod_i p_i^{k_i}$ the same lemma holds with $r_i$ the order mod $p_i^{k_i}$: success iff the valuations $v(r_i)$ are not all equal. Each $\mathbb Z_{p^k}^\times$ ($p$ odd) is cyclic of order $p^{k-1}(p - 1)$ (cited: Ireland and Rosen, A Classical Introduction to Modern Number Theory, ch. 4), and in a cyclic group of even order exactly half the elements have the full power of 2 in their order. So $\Pr[\text{all equal}] = \sum_d\prod_i\Pr[v(r_i) = d] \le \sum_d\Pr[v(r_1) = d]\prod_{i\ge2}\tfrac12 = 2^{1-m}$. Nielsen and Chuang, Appendix 4, give this argument.

What the test buys. Without the perfect-power check, $N = 9 = 3^2$ would run for ever: its units 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8 have orders 1, 6, 3, 6, 3, 2, and the even ones give $a^{r/2} \equiv 8 \equiv -1$ every time.

Order finding is phase estimation

Multiplication by $a$ modulo $N$ permutes basis states. Its eigenvalues are $r$-th roots of unity, and $\ket1$ is an equal superposition of its eigenvectors — so unit 15's machine, started on $\ket1$, samples $s/r$.

16 · order finding

Multiplication by a is a permutation, so a unitary

the modular multiplication unitary

For $\gcd(a, N) = 1$, on $n = \lceil\log_2N\rceil$ qubits:

$$U_a\ket y = \begin{cases}\ket{ay \bmod N}, & 0 \le y < N,\\ \ket y, & N \le y < 2^n.\end{cases}$$
unitary

$y \mapsto ay \bmod N$ is a bijection of $\{0, \dots, N - 1\}$, with inverse $y \mapsto a^{-1}y$. So $U_a$ permutes the basis: one 1 in each row and column, and $U_a^\dagger U_a = I$ (unit 10).

Powers. $U_a^k = U_{a^k \bmod N}$: multiplying by $a$ $k$ times is multiplying by $a^k$. So $U_a^r = I$, and on $\ket1$ no smaller power returns.

$U_2$ on five qubits ($21 \le 32$), drawn as its cycles. The orbit of 1 has length 6, and every cycle length divides 6.

Why "identity above $N$". The register has $2^n$ basis states and only $N$ of them are residues. Fixing the rest makes $U_a$ a permutation of all $2^n$ — hence unitary — without changing anything on the states we use. Unit 10's reversible arithmetic builds exactly this map (section 3).

Why every cycle length divides $r$. $a^r \equiv 1$ gives $a^ry \equiv y$ for every $y$, so $U_a^r$ fixes every basis state, and each cycle's length divides $r$. The figure shows lengths 1, 2, 3 and 6. The two 6-cycles are the units: $\mathbb Z_{21}^\times$ has 12 elements, $\langle2\rangle = \{1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 11\}$ is a subgroup of index 2, and $\{5, 10, 20, 19, 17, 13\}$ is its other coset (Lagrange). The short cycles are the non-units, multiples of 3 or 7.

Only the orbit of 1 matters. The algorithm starts the work register in $\ket1$, and $U_a$ never leaves its orbit $\{1, a, a^2, \dots\}$. The other cycles are there; they are never visited.

Its eigenvectors, and their phases s/r

eigenvectors of $U_a$

For $s = 0, 1, \dots, r - 1$ let

$$\ket{u_s} = \frac1{\sqrt r}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}e^{-2\pi isk/r}\,\ket{a^k \bmod N}.$$

The $\ket{u_s}$ are orthonormal, and $U_a\ket{u_s} = e^{2\pi is/r}\ket{u_s}$.

proof

$U_a\ket{a^k} = \ket{a^{k+1}}$. Re-index $k + 1 \to k$: the coefficient of $\ket{a^k}$ becomes $e^{-2\pi is(k-1)/r} = e^{2\pi is/r}\,e^{-2\pi isk/r}$, and $a^r \equiv 1$ closes the cycle. Orthonormal: the $a^k$, $0 \le k < r$, are distinct, and $\frac1r\sum_k e^{2\pi i(s - s')k/r} = \delta_{ss'}$ — orthogonality of the characters of $\mathbb Z_r$ (unit 15).

So the eigenphase $\varphi = s/r$ carries $r$ in its denominator, and phase estimation reads $\varphi$. Normalised: $r$ terms of modulus $1/\sqrt r$ ✓.

$a = 2$, $N = 21$: six eigenvalues on the orbit of 1, the sixth roots of unity; eigenphases $0, \frac16, \dots, \frac56$.

Full proof

Eigenvector. $U_a\ket{u_s} = r^{-1/2}\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}e^{-2\pi isk/r}\ket{a^{k+1}}$. Put $j = k + 1$, running from 1 to $r$, and note $\ket{a^r} = \ket{a^0}$ and $e^{-2\pi is(r-1)/r} = e^{-2\pi is(0-1)/r}$: the sum is $r^{-1/2}\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}e^{-2\pi is(j-1)/r}\ket{a^j} = e^{2\pi is/r}\ket{u_s}$.

Distinct powers. If $a^j \equiv a^k$ with $0 \le j < k < r$, then $a^{k-j} \equiv 1$ with $0 < k - j < r$, contradicting minimality.

Orthonormality. $\braket{u_s|u_{s'}} = \frac1r\sum_{k=0}^{r-1}e^{2\pi isk/r}e^{-2\pi is'k/r}$, the first factor conjugated because the bra is conjugate-linear. This is $\frac1r\sum_k\chi_{s-s'}(k)$ for the character $\chi_j(k) = e^{2\pi ijk/r}$ of $\mathbb Z_r$, which is 1 if $s = s'$ and 0 otherwise (unit 15: a non-trivial character sums to zero).

What they are not. These $r$ vectors span the orbit of 1 and nothing else; the other cycles carry their own eigenvectors, with phases $j/\ell$ for a cycle of length $\ell \mid r$. And no one can prepare $\ket{u_s}$ directly: writing it down needs $r$, which is what we are looking for. The next slide is the way round that.

The sign. The minus in $e^{-2\pi isk/r}$ is what makes the eigenvalue $e^{+2\pi is/r}$, matching unit 15's convention $U\ket u = e^{2\pi i\varphi}\ket u$ with phase estimation returning $\varphi$.

proof

Full proof

Unit 15 showed that phase estimation maps $\ket{0^t}\ket u \mapsto \ket{\tilde\varphi}\ket u$ when $U\ket u = e^{2\pi i\varphi}\ket u$, with $\braket{y|\tilde\varphi} = 2^{-t}\sum_x e^{2\pi ix(\varphi - y/2^t)}$: the controlled powers write $e^{2\pi i\varphi x}$ onto $\ket x$ by phase kickback, and $F^\dagger$ sends $\ket x$ to $2^{-t/2}\sum_ye^{-2\pi ixy/2^t}\ket y$. The whole circuit is one unitary $W$, linear, so $W\ket{0^t}\ket1 = r^{-1/2}\sum_s\ket{\tilde\varphi_s}\ket{u_s}$. Measuring the counting register, the probability of $y$ is $\big\lVert(\bra y\otimes I)W\ket{0^t}\ket1\big\rVert^2 = \big\lVert r^{-1/2}\sum_s\braket{y|\tilde\varphi_s}\ket{u_s}\big\rVert^2 = \frac1r\sum_s|\braket{y|\tilde\varphi_s}|^2$, by Pythagoras in the orthonormal $\ket{u_s}$.

The closed form. $\sum_{x=0}^{M-1}e^{2\pi ix\delta} = \frac{e^{2\pi iM\delta} - 1}{e^{2\pi i\delta} - 1}$ for $\delta \notin \mathbb Z$, and $|e^{i\theta} - 1| = 2|\sin(\theta/2)|$, so its modulus is $|\sin(\pi M\delta)/\sin(\pi\delta)|$. With $M = 2^t$, divide by $2^t$ and square.

The other picture: a period. Unit 10's deferred measurement allows measuring the work register first. It shows some $a^{k_0}$, and the counting register collapses to the $x$ with $a^x \equiv a^{k_0}$, i.e. $x \equiv k_0 \pmod r$: a comb $\sum_m\ket{k_0 + mr}$ with spacing $r$. The Fourier transform of a comb is a comb with spacing $2^t/r$ — the peaks of section 4. This is how Shor described the algorithm in 1994; the eigenvector picture is Kitaev's (1995), and Cleve, Ekert, Macchiavello and Mosca (1998) joined the two. Both give the same $P(y)$.

How many counting qubits

precision

Choose $t$ with $N^2 \le 2^t < 2N^2$. If $y$ is the integer nearest $2^ts/r$, then

$$\Big|\frac y{2^t} - \frac sr\Big| \le \frac1{2^{t+1}} \le \frac1{2N^2},$$

and $s/r$ is the only fraction with denominator below $N$ that close to $y/2^t$.

proof of "only"

Fractions $p/q \ne p'/q'$ with $q, q' < N$ differ by $|pq' - p'q|/qq' \ge 1/qq' > 1/N^2$. Two of them within $1/2N^2$ of one point would be within $1/N^2$ of each other.

By unit 15, $y$ is the nearest integer to $2^ts/r$ with probability at least $4/\pi^2 \approx 0.405$, for each $s$. And $r < N$, since $r \mid \varphi(N) < N$.

Near $5/6$, the fractions with denominator at most 20; the window of half-width $1/882$ around $427/512$ holds one of them.

For 21: $441 \le 512 < 882$, so $t = 9$. With $n = 5$ work qubits ($16 < 21 \le 32$): 14 qubits.

The upper bound $2^t < 2N^2$ is economy, not correctness: more counting qubits only sharpen the peaks. It picks the least $t$ that works, which for 21 is 9. Shor's 1997 paper chose $q = 2^t$ in exactly this range.

Always at most $2n$. $N < 2^n$ (an odd $N$ is not a power of 2), so $N^2 < 2^{2n}$ and $t \le 2n$. Nielsen and Chuang take $t = 2n + 1 + \lceil\log_2(2 + 1/2\varepsilon)\rceil$, which buys success probability $1 - \varepsilon$ on the nearest-fraction step at the price of a few more qubits; both choices give $O(n)$.

The number line. The closest two fractions with denominators at most 20 can be is $1/(19\cdot20) = 1/380$, and the window has width $2/882 = 1/441$. Near $5/6$ the neighbours are $14/17$ and $16/19$, at distances $1/102$ and $1/114$. A window of $\pm1/882$ catches at most one fraction, and the precision theorem says which.

What fails with fewer qubits. With $t = 5$ for 21 the window is $\pm1/64$, wider than the gaps; the peak nearest $32/6$ is $y = 5$, and $5/32$ is nearer $3/19$ than $1/6$. Problem P6 follows this to the end: with $t = 5$ no run ever returns $r = 6$.

Fourteen qubits: the circuit for 21

The running example, gate by gate at the level of controlled multiplications. Counting qubit $k$ carries weight $2^{8-k}$ and controls multiplication by $2^{2^{8-k}} \bmod 21$. The nine multiplications commute — they are all multiplications mod 21 — so their order along the wires does not matter. $F^\dagger$ on nine qubits is 9 Hadamards, 36 controlled phases and 4 swaps (unit 15).

Input and output. The input is $\ket{0}^{\otimes9}\ket{00001}$: counting qubits in $\ket0$, made uniform by the Hadamards, and the work register in $\ket1$. The output is nine bits $y$, read as an integer with qubit 0 most significant; section 4 computes its distribution.

Fourteen, honestly. Fourteen qubits carry the state the algorithm is about. The borrowed qubits end unentangled, so they do not change the distribution.

Multiplying in place

Unit 10's reversible arithmetic and uncomputation build, with no garbage left behind, the multiply-add

$$M_c\ket y\ket w = \ket y\ket{w + cy \bmod N} \quad (y, w < N),$$

but phase estimation needs $U_c$, which overwrites $y$.

in-place multiplication

For $c$ coprime to $N$: $\ M_{c^{-1}}^{-1}\cdot\mathrm{SWAP}\cdot M_c\ $ sends $\ket y\ket0 \mapsto \ket{cy \bmod N}\ket0$.

proof

$\ket y\ket0 \to \ket y\ket{cy} \to \ket{cy}\ket y \to \ket{cy}\ket{y - c^{-1}\cdot cy} = \ket{cy}\ket0$. The ancilla register ends in $\ket0$ for every $y$, so by linearity the work register undergoes exactly $U_c$.

Compute into a fresh register, swap, then uncompute the old value with the inverse multiplier.

For 21: $2^{-1} \equiv 11$, and 4 and 16 are each other's inverses ($64 = 3\cdot21 + 1$).

Controlled-$U_c$: control $M_c$, the swap ($n$ controlled swaps) and $M_{c^{-1}}^{-1}$. With the control off, all three are skipped and nothing moves.

Why the ancilla must come back clean. Unit 10's Garbage destroys interference: if the ancilla ended in a state depending on $y$, it would be a record of which $y$ the work register held, and the phases that phase kickback writes onto the counting register would no longer interfere. Returning it to $\ket0$ for every $y$ is what makes the work register see a unitary, not a channel.

$M_c$ is a permutation. On pairs $(y, w)$ with $y, w < N$ the map $(y, w) \mapsto (y, w + cy)$ has inverse $(y, w) \mapsto (y, w - cy)$; extend by the identity elsewhere. Unit 10's construction builds it (and its inverse) without garbage. $M_{c^{-1}}^{-1}$ is $\ket z\ket w \mapsto \ket z\ket{w - c^{-1}z}$.

The trick in general is Bennett's: to apply a bijection $g$ in place, compute $g(x)$ into a fresh register, then uncompute $x$ from $g(x)$ using a circuit for $g^{-1}$. It needs $g^{-1}$ to be efficiently computable — here multiplication by $c^{-1}$, with $c^{-1}$ found classically by the extended Euclidean algorithm.

What modular exponentiation costs

Adder. Two $n$-bit numbers, carries computed then uncomputed (unit 10's one-bit adder, $n$ times, and back): $O(n)$ gates — about $8n$.

Modular addition of a constant $c < N$: add $c$, subtract $N$, copy the sign bit, add $N$ back if it was negative, then clear the sign bit with one more comparison — about five adders, $O(n)$ (Vedral, Barenco and Ekert 1996, cited for the layout).

Multiply-add $M_c$: $cy = \sum_i y_i\,(2^ic \bmod N)$, the constants precomputed — $n$ controlled modular additions, $O(n^2)$.

In place: two multiply-adds and $n$ swaps, $O(n^2)$. One more control on every gate: still $O(n^2)$.

Exponentiation: $t \le 2n$ controlled multiplications, $O(n^3)$.

the count

Modular exponentiation $\ket x\ket1 \mapsto \ket x\ket{a^x \bmod N}$ takes $O(n^3)$ gates on $O(n)$ qubits; the QFT adds $O(n^2)$. Polynomial in $n = \lceil\log_2N\rceil$.

Each level is a constant number of copies of the level inside it, times the count written there.

Where the constants come from. In the Vedral–Barenco–Ekert adder (Phys. Rev. A 54, 147, 1996), each bit costs a carry block of 3 gates, a sum block of 2 and the carry block reversed, 3 more: about $8n$ Toffolis and CNOTs. Their modular adder is five such adders: add $c$, subtract $N$, add $N$ back under the control of the sign bit, then subtract and re-add $c$ to reset the sign bit. These constants are for orientation only; nothing in this unit depends on them.

Adding a control. A controlled circuit is the circuit with one more control on each gate: $C(G_m\cdots G_1) = C(G_m)\cdots C(G_1)$. A CNOT becomes a Toffoli and a Toffoli a three-control $X$, built from a constant number of Toffolis and one borrowed qubit (unit 10). So adding a control multiplies the count by a constant.

Faster multiplication. Schoolbook multiplication is what makes the $n^3$. Asymptotically faster multiplication lowers it to $O(n^2\log n\log\log n)$, as Shor's paper already noted, at constants that pay off only for very large $n$ (cited). What real factoring costs, with error correction, is unit 24's and the capstone's question; this unit stops at $O(n^3)$.

The classical side is also $O(n^3)$: the squarings for the $b_j$, the continued fraction in section 5, and the $\gcd$s. So the whole algorithm is polynomial in $n$, and the quantum part is not the bottleneck asymptotically.

16 · continued fractions

Continued fractions, with Euclid underneath

the continued fraction of a real $x$ $$x = a_0 + \cfrac{1}{a_1 + \cfrac{1}{a_2 + \cdots}} = [a_0; a_1, a_2, \dots],$$

with $x_0 = x$, $a_k = \lfloor x_k\rfloor$ and, while $x_k \ne a_k$, $x_{k+1} = 1/(x_k - a_k) > 1$. The integers $a_k$ are the partial quotients; all but $a_0$ are $\ge 1$.

For rational $x = u/v$ this is Euclid's algorithm on $(u, v)$: each $a_k$ is a quotient, the remainders fall, and the expansion stops.

Example. $5 = 0\cdot6 + 5$, $\ 6 = 1\cdot5 + 1$, $\ 5 = 5\cdot1$: so $\frac56 = [0; 1, 5] = 0 + \cfrac{1}{1 + \frac15}$. Check: $1 + \frac15 = \frac65$, inverted $\frac56$ ✓.

Euclid as tiling: cut the largest squares off a $6\times5$ rectangle. The counts, 1 then 5, are the partial quotients.

Why it is Euclid. If $x_k = u_k/v_k$ in lowest terms, then $a_k = \lfloor u_k/v_k\rfloor$ is the quotient of $u_k$ by $v_k$, and $x_k - a_k = (u_k \bmod v_k)/v_k$, so $x_{k+1} = v_k/(u_k \bmod v_k)$: the next pair is (divisor, remainder), exactly Euclid's step. The denominators strictly decrease, so the expansion of a rational stops.

Not quite unique. A rational has two expansions, since a last term $a \ge 2$ can be written $(a - 1) + \frac11$: $[0; 1, 5] = [0; 1, 4, 1]$. The canonical one ends in a term $\ge 2$ (or is $[a_0]$). Legendre's proof uses the freedom. An irrational $x$ has exactly one expansion, and it is infinite.

The tiling. A $u\times v$ rectangle with $u > v$: cut off $\lfloor u/v\rfloor$ squares of side $v$, leaving a $(u \bmod v)\times v$ rectangle, and repeat on that turned sideways. The counts are the $a_k$, and the last square's side is $\gcd(u, v)$.

History. Expansions of this kind are as old as Euclid's algorithm; Bombelli (1572) used them for square roots, Huygens in the 1680s to choose the gear ratios of his planetarium, and Euler, Lagrange and Legendre built the theory used here in the eighteenth century.

Convergents, and the identities they obey

convergents

$p_k/q_k = [a_0; a_1, \dots, a_k]$, computed by

$$\begin{aligned}p_k &= a_kp_{k-1} + p_{k-2},\\ q_k &= a_kq_{k-1} + q_{k-2},\end{aligned}$$

from $p_{-2} = 0$, $p_{-1} = 1$, $q_{-2} = 1$, $q_{-1} = 0$.

three identities

(i) $[a_0; \dots, a_k, \omega] = \dfrac{\omega p_k + p_{k-1}}{\omega q_k + q_{k-1}}$ for every real $\omega > 0$.

(ii) $p_kq_{k-1} - p_{k-1}q_k = (-1)^{k+1}$; so $p_k/q_k$ is in lowest terms.

(iii) $q_k \ge F_{k+1}$ (Fibonacci): $x = y/2^t$ has at most about $1.44t + 2$ terms.

proof, in one matrix

With $M(a) = \begin{pmatrix}a&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, induction on the recurrence gives

$$M(a_0)\cdots M(a_k) = \begin{pmatrix}p_k&p_{k-1}\\q_k&q_{k-1}\end{pmatrix}.$$

$M(a)$ applied to $(z, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ gives $(az + 1, z)^{\mathsf T}$, proportional to $(a + \frac1z, 1)^{\mathsf T}$: so the product applied to $(\omega, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ evaluates the continued fraction — (i). $\det M(a) = -1$ — (ii). And $q_k \ge q_{k-1} + q_{k-2}$ as $a_k \ge 1$ — (iii).

A consequence used next: with $\omega = x_{k+1}$ in (i) and (ii),

$$x - \tfrac{p_k}{q_k} = \tfrac{(-1)^k}{q_k(x_{k+1}q_k + q_{k-1})},$$

so convergents alternate around $x$ and close in faster than $1/q_k^2$.

Full proof

The matrix product. For $k = 0$: $M(a_0) = \begin{pmatrix}a_0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}p_0&p_{-1}\\q_0&q_{-1}\end{pmatrix}$ since $p_0 = a_0$, $q_0 = 1$. If it holds for $k - 1$, multiply on the right by $M(a_k)$: the new first column is $a_k(p_{k-1}, q_{k-1})^{\mathsf T} + (p_{k-2}, q_{k-2})^{\mathsf T} = (p_k, q_k)^{\mathsf T}$, and the new second column is the old first.

(i). Write $\mu(a, z) = a + 1/z$, so that $[a_0; \dots, a_k, \omega] = \mu(a_0, \mu(a_1, \dots \mu(a_k, \omega)))$. A vector $(u, v)^{\mathsf T}$ with $v \ne 0$ represents the number $u/v$. $M(a)$ sends a representative of $z$ to $(az + 1, z)^{\mathsf T}$, a representative of $\mu(a, z)$. So the product sends $(\omega, 1)^{\mathsf T}$ to a representative of the whole fraction, namely $(\omega p_k + p_{k-1},\ \omega q_k + q_{k-1})^{\mathsf T}$. Every intermediate value is positive for $\omega > 0$ (all $a_j \ge 1$ for $j \ge 1$), so no denominator vanishes. With $\omega$ replaced by the next term this also shows $p_k/q_k = [a_0; \dots, a_k]$: take $k - 1$ and $\omega = a_k$.

(ii). $\det\begin{pmatrix}p_k&p_{k-1}\\q_k&q_{k-1}\end{pmatrix} = \prod_j\det M(a_j) = (-1)^{k+1}$. A common divisor of $p_k$ and $q_k$ divides $\pm1$.

(iii). $q_0 = 1 = F_1$, $q_1 = a_1 \ge 1 = F_2$, and $q_k = a_kq_{k-1} + q_{k-2} \ge q_{k-1} + q_{k-2}$. Since $F_{k+1} \ge \phi^{k-1}$ with $\phi = 1.618$, $q_k \le 2^t$ forces $k \le 1 + t/\log_2\phi = 1 + 1.44t$.

The consequence. $x = [a_0; \dots, a_k, x_{k+1}]$, so by (i) $x - \frac{p_k}{q_k} = \frac{x_{k+1}p_k + p_{k-1}}{x_{k+1}q_k + q_{k-1}} - \frac{p_k}{q_k} = \frac{p_{k-1}q_k - p_kq_{k-1}}{q_k(x_{k+1}q_k + q_{k-1})}$, and by (ii) the numerator is $(-1)^k$. As $x_{k+1} \ge a_{k+1}$, the denominator is at least $q_kq_{k+1}$.

Cost. Expanding $y/2^t$ is Euclid on $t$-bit numbers: $O(t)$ steps, each a division costing $O(t^2)$, so $O(t^3) = O(n^3)$ bit operations, and the convergents cost no more.

Legendre's theorem

Legendre's theorem (1798)

If $x$ is real, $p/q$ is in lowest terms with $q \ge 1$, and $\ \big|x - \frac pq\big| < \frac1{2q^2}$, then $p/q$ is a convergent of $x$.

proof

Write $x - \frac pq = \frac{\varepsilon\theta}{q^2}$ with $\varepsilon = \pm1$ and $0 \le \theta < \frac12$; if $\theta = 0$, $x = p/q$ and there is nothing to prove. Expand $\frac pq = [a_0; \dots, a_k]$, choosing the length so that $(-1)^k = \varepsilon$ — possible, since a last term can be split, $a = (a - 1) + \frac11$.

Define $\omega$ by $x = \dfrac{\omega p_k + p_{k-1}}{\omega q_k + q_{k-1}}$. By (ii), $x - \frac{p_k}{q_k} = \frac{(-1)^k}{q_k(\omega q_k + q_{k-1})}$, so $\frac{\theta}{q_k^2} = \frac{1}{q_k(\omega q_k + q_{k-1})}$, and

$$\omega = \frac1\theta - \frac{q_{k-1}}{q_k} > 2 - 1 = 1 .$$

Expand $\omega = [b_0; b_1, \dots]$ with $b_0 \ge 1$. By (i), $x = [a_0; \dots, a_k, b_0, b_1, \dots]$: and $p/q = p_k/q_k$ is one of its convergents.

checks

$x = 427/512$, $p/q = 5/6$: $\theta = 36/1536 < \frac12$, and $\frac56$ is the convergent $p_2/q_2$ ✓.

Outside the hypothesis: $|\frac5{32} - \frac3{19}| = \frac1{608}$, but $\frac1{2\cdot19^2} = \frac1{722}$ is smaller; and indeed $\frac3{19}$ is not a convergent of $\frac5{32} = [0; 6, 2, 2]$, whose convergents are $\frac16, \frac2{13}, \frac5{32}$ ✓.

The $\frac12$ in the hypothesis is where $\omega > 1$ comes from; with $\theta$ allowed up to 1, $\omega$ could fall below 1 and $b_0 = 0$ is not a legal term.

Full proof

Choosing the parity. If the canonical expansion $[a_0; \dots, a_k]$ has $(-1)^k \ne \varepsilon$, use the other one: $[a_0; \dots, a_k - 1, 1]$ if $a_k \ge 2$ (or $k = 0$), or $[a_0; \dots, a_{k-1} + 1]$ if $a_k = 1$ and $k \ge 1$. Its length differs by one, its last convergent is still $p/q$, and the recurrence gives the same $p_k, q_k$ for $p/q$ because (ii) makes them coprime with $q_k > 0$.

Solving for $\omega$. The map $\omega \mapsto (\omega p_k + p_{k-1})/(\omega q_k + q_{k-1})$ is a Möbius map with determinant $\pm1$, so some real $\omega$ (or $\infty$, when $x = p/q$) gives $x$. Using (ii) as on the slide, $x - p_k/q_k = (-1)^k/(q_k(\omega q_k + q_{k-1}))$. The left side is $\varepsilon\theta/q_k^2$ with $\varepsilon = (-1)^k$; cancel the sign and solve: $\omega q_k + q_{k-1} = q_k/\theta$, so $\omega = 1/\theta - q_{k-1}/q_k$. Now $1/\theta > 2$ and $0 \le q_{k-1}/q_k \le 1$ (the $q$'s do not decrease, and $q_{-1} = 0$), so $\omega > 1$.

Gluing. $\omega > 1$ has an expansion $[b_0; b_1, \dots]$ with $b_0 = \lfloor\omega\rfloor \ge 1$. Then by (i), applied with this $\omega$, $x = [a_0; \dots, a_k, \omega] = [a_0; \dots, a_k, b_0, b_1, \dots]$ — a valid continued fraction, all terms after $a_0$ at least 1 — whose $k$-th convergent is $p_k/q_k = p/q$.

When $x$ is rational its expansion is not unique, so "a convergent of $x$" should not depend on the choice. The two expansions differ only in the last two terms ($[\dots, c, 1]$ against $[\dots, c + 1]$), which changes only the last convergent and drops the one before it. That dropped convergent sits at index $k + m$ for an $\omega$-expansion of length $m + 1$ with $m \ge 1$; $p/q$ sits at index $k$, earlier. If $m = 0$, $\omega = b_0 \ge 2$ is an integer and nothing is merged. So $p/q$ is a convergent of either expansion.

History. Legendre, Essai sur la théorie des nombres (1798). Hardy and Wright, An Introduction to the Theory of Numbers, Theorem 184, give this proof.

Decoding y: the nearest fraction with denominator below N

the nearest-fraction rule (Shor 1994)

Decode $y$ as the fraction $p/q$ with $q < N$ nearest to $y/2^t$. Then test whether $a^q \equiv 1 \pmod N$.

on the good runs it finds $s/r$

If $y$ is the integer nearest $2^ts/r$, and $s/r = s'/r'$ in lowest terms, the rule returns $s'/r'$; and $s'/r'$ is a convergent of $y/2^t$, found in $O(n^3)$ bit operations.

proof

How many counting qubits gave $|y/2^t - s'/r'| \le 1/2N^2$ and uniqueness, so $s'/r'$ is the nearest. Since $r' < N$, $1/2N^2 < 1/2r'^2$, and Legendre makes it a convergent.

On other runs the nearest fraction is still computable: it is the last convergent with $q < N$ or one semiconvergent $\frac{p_{k-1} + jp_k}{q_{k-1} + jq_k}$, $1 \le j < a_{k+1}$ (cited: Khinchin, Continued Fractions, §6).

what the rule returns

$r' = r/\gcd(s, r)$. If $\gcd(s, r) = 1$, that is $r$. If not, it is a proper divisor of $r$, and $a^{r'} \not\equiv 1$ says so. If $y$ was not the nearest integer, $q$ may be unrelated to $r$, and the test rejects it — unless $q$ happens to be a multiple of $r$, which passes and serves as well (problem P4).

For 21: $427 \to \frac56$, $r = 6$. $\ 171 \to \frac13$: $2^3 = 8 \not\equiv 1$, a divisor. $\ 256 \to \frac12$: $2^2 = 4 \not\equiv 1$, a divisor. $\ 0 \to \frac01$: nothing.

Shor's words (1997): round $c/q$ "to the nearest fraction having a denominator smaller than $n$. This fraction can be found in polynomial time by using a continued fraction expansion of $c/q$, which finds all the best approximations of $c/q$ by fractions." The deck follows him.

A second rule in the textbooks. Many sources decode by the last convergent with denominator below $N$. On every good run the two rules agree — that is the theorem on this slide. Off the peaks they can differ: $5/32$ (the $t = 5$ example) has last convergent $2/13$ but nearest fraction $3/19$. For the 21 distribution, the class "returns 6 directly" has probability $0.308$ under Shor's rule and $0.321$ under the last-convergent rule. This deck and the problem sheet use Shor's rule throughout.

Why test $a^q \equiv 1$. If it holds, $r \mid q$; on a good run $q \mid r$ too, so $q = r$. The test costs one modular power. If it fails, $q$ is kept for the next slide. Off the good runs the test can pass with $q$ a proper multiple of $r$: for 21 the stray denominators 12 and 18 do, with probability $0.0004$ together. Such a $q$ is not the order, but the square-root trick needs only a multiple of it — problem P4 halves the multiple until the power stops being 1, and fails for exactly the same $a$ as the order-based reduction.

The semiconvergent fact (a best approximation of the first kind is a convergent or a semiconvergent) is cited; it is needed only to say that the rule is efficient on bad runs too. The algorithm's correctness rests on the good runs, where the slide's proof is complete.

Divisors of r, and how runs combine

A run that returns a proper divisor $q$ of $r$ is not wasted. Keep every $q$. Any lcm $L$ of some of them with $a^L \equiv 1$ is a multiple of $r$, and the lcm of the good runs is a divisor of $r$ — so output the least such $L$, over the handful of subsets. It is $r$ as soon as the good runs' lcm is.

two good runs usually suffice

For $s_1, s_2$ independent and uniform in $\mathbb Z_r$: $\ \operatorname{lcm}\big(\frac r{\gcd(s_1, r)}, \frac r{\gcd(s_2, r)}\big) = r\ $ with probability $\prod_{p\mid r}\big(1 - \frac1{p^2}\big) \ge \frac6{\pi^2} \approx 0.608$.

proof

The lcm is $r/\gcd(s_1, s_2, r)$. By the CRT the residues of $s$ modulo the primes $p \mid r$ are independent and uniform, and $p$ divides both $s_1$ and $s_2$ with probability $1/p^2$. The product over all primes is $1/\zeta(2) = 6/\pi^2$ (Euler, cited).

Two runs of the 21 circuit, each split into its five classes.

For 21 ($r = 6$): $(1 - \frac14)(1 - \frac19) = \frac23$ for good runs. On the real distribution, two runs give $r = 6$ — one returns 6, or one 3 and the other 2 — with probability $0.629$; three runs $0.813$; four $0.907$.

Full proof

The lcm. Write $g_i = \gcd(s_i, r)$. For each prime $p$, $v_p\big(\operatorname{lcm}(r/g_1, r/g_2)\big) = \max(v_p(r) - v_p(g_1), v_p(r) - v_p(g_2)) = v_p(r) - \min(v_p(g_1), v_p(g_2)) = v_p\big(r/\gcd(g_1, g_2)\big)$, and $\gcd(g_1, g_2) = \gcd(s_1, s_2, r)$. So the lcm is $r$ iff no prime $p \mid r$ divides both $s_1$ and $s_2$.

Independence. $\mathbb Z_r \cong \prod_i\mathbb Z_{p_i^{k_i}}$ (CRT), so a uniform $s$ has independent uniform components, and $s \bmod p_i$ is uniform on $\mathbb Z_{p_i}$: $\Pr[p_i \mid s] = 1/p_i$. For two independent runs, $\Pr[p_i \mid s_1, s_2] = 1/p_i^2$, independently over $i$. So the probability is $\prod_{p\mid r}(1 - p^{-2})$, and each factor is below 1, so the product over $p \mid r$ is at least the product over all primes, $\prod_p(1 - p^{-2}) = 1/\zeta(2) = 6/\pi^2$ (Euler's product and $\zeta(2) = \pi^2/6$, cited).

The real numbers for 21. With the five classes of Worked: every outcome of the 21 circuit, decoded, two runs give $r = 6$ with probability $1 - (1 - 0.308)^2 + 2(0.321)(0.167)$, which is $0.628$ from these three-figure values and $0.629$ from the unrounded ones; the mosaic's green area. For $k$ runs, the failure probability is $(1 - p_6 - p_3)^k + (1 - p_6 - p_2)^k - (1 - p_6 - p_3 - p_2)^k$ (no 6, and not both a 3 and a 2), giving $0.813$ for three and $0.907$ for four.

Why the least $L$, not a running lcm. These numbers need the rule on the slide. The least passing $L$ is 6 exactly when some run returned 6 or two runs returned 3 and 2: a subset with lcm 6 can use only the denominators 1, 2, 3 and 6, and every passing $L$ is a multiple of 6. A running lcm of every $q$ so far, tested after each run, would fold in strays — a 3 and a stray 4 make 12, a stray 19 and a 6 make 114, and both pass — and then two runs return exactly 6 with probability $0.617$, three with $0.778$. The multiple is not useless (problem P4 splits $N$ from it), but it is not $r$.

Without combining, one waits for a run with $\gcd(s, r) = 1$, which has probability $\varphi(r)/r$. That can be as small as about $e^{-\gamma}/\ln\ln r$ (cited: Hardy and Wright, Theorem 328), so $O(\log\log N)$ runs suffice even then. Combining makes it $O(1)$.

Shor's algorithm, whole

One run of order finding, with the reduction around it. Only the shaded box is quantum.

the theorem, assembled

An odd $n$-bit $N$ that is not a prime power is split with constant probability by $O(1)$ runs, each of $O(n^3)$ gates on $O(n)$ qubits and $O(n^3)$ classical bit operations.

The constant, crudely. Two runs each land on the nearest integer with probability $\ge 4/\pi^2$, and then the lcm is $r$ with probability $\ge 6/\pi^2$: at least $(4/\pi^2)^2\cdot6/\pi^2 = 0.0999$. Then the reduction succeeds with probability $\ge \frac12$. So $O(1)$ expected runs.

For 21 the truth is far better than the floor: $0.629$ for two runs, then ½ for the choice of $a$ — and $a = 2$ was a good one.

Putting the pieces together. Section 1: one order finding splits $N$ with probability at least ½ over $a$. Sections 2–4: one run of the circuit samples $y$ from $P(y)$, which is the mixture over uniform $s$ of phase estimation on $s/r$. Section 5: on a run where $y$ is the nearest integer to $2^ts/r$ — probability $\ge 4/\pi^2$ for each $s$, by unit 15 — the nearest-fraction rule returns $r/\gcd(s, r)$, and two such runs give $r$ with probability $\ge 6/\pi^2$. The floor $0.0999$ is crude: it throws away every shoulder outcome and every run beyond two.

Wrong answers cost nothing. Every candidate is checked classically — $a^L \equiv 1$, and then the $\gcd$ either divides $N$ properly or it does not. So the algorithm never outputs a wrong factor; it only sometimes runs again.

With unit 12. For a reader who has taken it: this is a polynomial-size uniform circuit family with bounded error for the decision version of factoring (does $N$ have a factor below $k$?), so factoring is in BQP. It is not known to be NP-hard, and nobody expects it to be — which is why Shor's algorithm is not evidence that quantum computers solve NP-complete problems.

History. By Shor's own account he found the discrete-logarithm algorithm first and factoring a few days later, in the spring of 1994; the paper appeared at FOCS that November. Simon's paper, which he had read, appeared at the same conference.

16 · discrete log and HSP

Discrete logarithms, and the key exchange they protect

the discrete logarithm problem

In a cyclic group $\langle g\rangle$ of known order $r$, given $g$ and $h = g^\ell$, find $\ell \in \mathbb Z_r$.

Diffie–Hellman key exchange (1976)

Public $g$. Alice picks $\alpha$ and sends $g^\alpha$; Bob picks $\beta$ and sends $g^\beta$. Each raises what arrives to their own secret: both hold $g^{\alpha\beta}$. Eve, who sees $g^\alpha$ and $g^\beta$, would need a discrete logarithm.

A toy. In $\mathbb Z_{11}^\times$, $g = 2$ has order 10: its powers are $2, 4, 8, 5, 10, 9, 7, 3, 6, 1$. For $h = 5 = 2^4$, $\ell = 4$.

Computing $g^\alpha$ is fast (repeated squaring). Undoing it is believed hard classically.

The best general classical algorithms take about $\sqrt r$ group operations (Pollard's rho method, cited); in $\mathbb Z_p^\times$ specifically, index calculus is subexponential (cited). Neither is polynomial in $\log r$.

Why both hold the same key. $(g^\beta)^\alpha = g^{\alpha\beta} = (g^\alpha)^\beta$, because exponents multiply in any group. Eve's problem — compute $g^{\alpha\beta}$ from $g^\alpha$ and $g^\beta$ — is at most as hard as the discrete logarithm: find $\alpha$, then raise $g^\beta$ to it.

The toy, checked. $2^4 = 16 = 11 + 5$ ✓. The order of 2 is 10 because $2^5 = 32 = 2\cdot11 + 10 \equiv -1$ and $2^2 = 4 \ne 1$, so the order divides 10 but not 5 or 2.

Knowing $r$. The algorithm on the next slide needs the group order. In $\mathbb Z_p^\times$ the order of $g$ divides $p - 1$, and can be found by order finding itself; for elliptic curves the group order is computed classically in polynomial time (Schoof's algorithm, cited).

Two registers, one hidden line

Let $f(x_1, x_2) = g^{x_1}h^{x_2} = g^{x_1 + \ell x_2}$ on $G = \mathbb Z_r\times\mathbb Z_r$.

what $f$ hides, and what sampling returns

$f(x) = f(x')$ iff $x - x' \in H = \{(-\ell k, k) : k \in \mathbb Z_r\}$. Fourier sampling over $G$ (Abelian: solved. Non-abelian: open) returns $(k_1, k_2)$ uniform on

$$H^\perp = \{(k_1, k_2) : k_2 \equiv \ell k_1 \pmod r\}.$$

So $\ell \equiv k_2k_1^{-1}$ whenever $k_1$ is a unit mod $r$: probability $\varphi(r)/r$ per run.

The circuit. Two counting registers made uniform; a third receives $g^{x_1}h^{x_2}$ by two modular exponentiations; $F_{\mathbb Z_r}$ on each counting register; measure. $O(n^3)$ gates again.

Check, $\ell = 4$, $r = 10$: a sample $(3, 2)$ gives $2\cdot3^{-1} = 2\cdot7 = 14 \equiv 4$ ✓, and $2^4 \equiv 5 = h$ ✓.

$\mathbb Z_{10}\times\mathbb Z_{10}$ for the toy, $\ell = 4$. Left: $H$ wraps round the torus as a line; $f$ is constant along it and along each parallel coset. Right: the samples.

Full proof

The hidden subgroup. $g$ has order $r$, so $g^{x_1 + \ell x_2} = g^{x_1' + \ell x_2'}$ iff $(x_1 - x_1') + \ell(x_2 - x_2') \equiv 0 \pmod r$, iff $(x_1 - x_1', x_2 - x_2') = (-\ell k, k)$ with $k = x_2 - x_2'$. So $f$ is constant on the cosets of $H$ and distinct on distinct cosets.

The annihilator. The characters of $\mathbb Z_r\times\mathbb Z_r$ are $\chi_{(k_1, k_2)}(x_1, x_2) = e^{2\pi i(k_1x_1 + k_2x_2)/r}$ (unit 15: characters of a product are products). Such a character is trivial on $H$ iff $k_1(-\ell k) + k_2k \equiv 0$ for all $k$, iff $k_2 \equiv \ell k_1$. That set has $r$ elements, $= |G|/|H|$, as it must.

Which samples help. If $k_1$ is a unit, $\ell = k_2k_1^{-1}$. If not — say $(2, 8)$ for $\ell = 4$ — the congruence $2\ell \equiv 8 \pmod{10}$ leaves $\ell \in \{4, 9\}$, and one check of $2^\ell$ against $h$ settles it.

Transforms for $r$ not a power of 2. Shor's paper used $2^t$-point transforms and an approximation argument like section 5's. Kitaev (1995) gave the eigenvalue-estimation approach that works for any $r$, and an exact $\mathbb Z_r$ transform can be built too (cited). The post-processing above is the same either way.

The figure's coincidence avoided. For $\ell = 7$ (the sheet's toy) $H$ and $H^\perp$ are the same set, because $7^2 \equiv -1 \pmod{10}$; $\ell = 4$ keeps the two pictures different.

Elliptic curves fall too

the elliptic-curve group

Over $\mathbb F_p$: the points of $y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B$ (with $4A^3 + 27B^2 \ne 0$) and a point at infinity $O$; three collinear points sum to $O$, so $P + Q = -R$. Associativity is a theorem (cited: Silverman, ch. III).

Elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman replaces $g^\alpha$ by $\alpha P$. For Shor it is one more discrete logarithm: the two-register algorithm needs only group operations, done reversibly in polynomially many gates (Proos and Zalka 2003, cited).

so

Shor breaks RSA, finite-field Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography together. Their replacements, post-quantum cryptography (lattice-based ML-KEM and ML-DSA, hash-based SLH-DSA; NIST, 2024), rest on problems with no known efficient quantum attack. Named here, not taught.

Over the reals, to see the rule: the line through $P = (0, 1)$ and $Q = (3, 5)$ meets $y^2 = x^3 - x + 1$ again at $R = (-\frac{11}9, -\frac{17}{27})$; so $P + Q = (-\frac{11}9, \frac{17}{27})$.

The figure's numbers. $P$ and $Q$ are on the curve: $0 - 0 + 1 = 1 = 1^2$ and $27 - 3 + 1 = 25 = 5^2$. The line has slope $\lambda = 4/3$. Substituting $y = 1 + \lambda x$ into the cubic, the three roots sum to $\lambda^2$, so $x_R = \lambda^2 - x_P - x_Q = 16/9 - 3 = -11/9$, and $y_R = 1 + \frac43(-\frac{11}9) = -\frac{17}{27}$. Check: $(-\frac{11}9)^3 + \frac{11}9 + 1 = \frac{-1331 + 891 + 729}{729} = \frac{289}{729} = (\frac{17}{27})^2$ ✓.

Over $\mathbb F_p$ the same formulae, with division done modulo $p$, define the group; the picture is lost but the algebra is identical. Cryptography uses a large prime-order subgroup generated by a point $P$, and the discrete logarithm problem is: given $P$ and $\ell P$, find $\ell$.

The classical attack. On a well-chosen curve the best known classical attack is generic — Pollard's rho, about $\sqrt r$ group operations (cited) — with no analogue of the index calculus that speeds up attacks in $\mathbb Z_p^\times$. That is why elliptic-curve keys are short.

The comparison of key sizes is standard: a 256-bit curve is rated at about the classical security of 3072-bit RSA (NIST SP 800-57, cited). Quantumly the comparison inverts — the curve needs fewer qubits to break, because its numbers are shorter. The resource counts belong to unit 24.

What post-quantum means. The NIST standards FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), 204 (ML-DSA) and 205 (SLH-DSA) were published in August 2024. Their security rests on lattice problems and on hash functions. One link to this unit: the lattice problems are tied to the dihedral hidden subgroup problem (Abelian: solved. Non-abelian: open), which is open.

The hidden subgroup problem

the hidden subgroup problem

A group $G$, a subgroup $H$, and an oracle for $f : G \to S$ that is constant on each coset $g + H$ and distinct on distinct cosets. Find $H$, as a list of generators.

problem$G$$H$$f$
Simon (unit 14)$\mathbb Z_2^n$$\{0, s\}$$f(x) = f(x\oplus s)$
order finding$\mathbb Z$$r\mathbb Z$$x \mapsto a^x \bmod N$
discrete log$\mathbb Z_r\times\mathbb Z_r$$\langle(-\ell, 1)\rangle$$(x_1, x_2) \mapsto g^{x_1}h^{x_2}$

Each algorithm in Part D so far is one recipe: query $f$ on a uniform superposition, Fourier-transform, measure, and post-process with linear algebra — over $\mathbb F_2$ for Simon, continued fractions for $\mathbb Z$, a modular inverse for discrete log.

Order finding for 21 as a hidden subgroup: $f(x) = 2^x \bmod 21$ is constant on the cosets of $6\mathbb Z$ and distinct across them. The hidden $H = 6\mathbb Z$ is generated by $r$.

Each row checked. Simon: $f(x) = f(x')$ iff $x' \in \{x, x\oplus s\}$, i.e. $x - x' \in \{0, s\}$ (subtraction is $\oplus$ in $\mathbb Z_2^n$). Order finding: $a^x \equiv a^{x'}$ iff $a^{x - x'} \equiv 1$ iff $r \mid x - x'$. Discrete log: the slide Two registers, one hidden line.

$\mathbb Z$ is infinite. The circuit sees only $x \in \{0, \dots, 2^t - 1\}$, a window that is not a group, and $6\mathbb Z$ does not tile it exactly (512 is not a multiple of 6). That is why order finding needs continued fractions where Simon needs none: the Fourier transform over $\mathbb Z_{2^t}$ sees the subgroup only approximately. When $r \mid 2^t$, as for 15, the window is a union of whole cosets and the samples are exact.

16 · discrete log and HSP

Abelian: solved. Non-abelian: open

Fourier sampling, finite abelian $G$

Query $f$ on the uniform superposition over $G$ and set the output aside: the input register holds a coset state $\ket{g + H} = |H|^{-1/2}\sum_{h\in H}\ket{g + h}$, $g$ uniform. Apply $F_G$ (unit 15) and measure: the outcome is a character $\chi$ uniform on $H^\perp = \{\chi : \chi(h) = 1 \text{ for all } h \in H\}$.

proof

$\bra\chi F_G\ket{g + H} = |G|^{-1/2}|H|^{-1/2}\sum_{h\in H}\chi(g + h)$, which is $\chi(g)\sqrt{|H|/|G|}$ if $\chi$ is trivial on $H$ and 0 otherwise — orthogonality of characters, on $H$ (unit 15). The modulus depends on neither $\chi$ nor $g$.

Check: Simon, $\chi_y(x) = (-1)^{x\cdot y}$ is trivial on $\{0, s\}$ iff $y\cdot s = 0$ — unit 14's uniform distribution on $s^\perp$ ✓. $O(\log|G|)$ samples generate $H^\perp$, which determines $H$ (Kitaev 1995, cited).

non-abelian groups: open

Dihedral $D_N$: would solve certain lattice problems (Regev 2004, cited). Best known quantum algorithm $2^{O(\sqrt{\log N})}$ (Kuperberg 2005, cited): subexponential, not polynomial.

Symmetric $S_n$: would solve graph isomorphism. Measuring single coset states provably does not suffice (Moore, Russell and Schulman 2005; Hallgren et al. 2006, cited). Classically, graph isomorphism is in quasi-polynomial time (Babai 2016, cited).

No polynomial quantum algorithm is known for either.

The abelian case covers factoring, discrete logarithms in every cyclic group, and Simon. What lattice-based cryptography bets on is that the dihedral case stays hard.

Full proof

The coset state. One query gives $|G|^{-1/2}\sum_x\ket x\ket{f(x)}$. Group the $x$ by coset: $f$ takes a different value on each, so the output register's values label the cosets, and setting it aside (measuring it, or not — unit 10's implicit measurement) leaves $\ket{g + H}$ for a uniformly random coset $g + H$.

The amplitude. $F_G\ket x = |G|^{-1/2}\sum_\chi\chi(x)\ket\chi$ (unit 15), so $\bra\chi F_G\ket{g + H} = |G|^{-1/2}|H|^{-1/2}\sum_{h\in H}\chi(g)\chi(h)$. The restriction of $\chi$ to $H$ is a character of $H$, so $\sum_{h\in H}\chi(h) = |H|$ if it is trivial and 0 otherwise. The probabilities $|H|/|G|$ on $H^\perp$ must sum to 1, which proves $|H^\perp| = |G|/|H|$ on the way.

How many samples. Suppose the samples so far generate $K \subsetneq H^\perp$. By Lagrange $|K| \le |H^\perp|/2$, so a new uniform sample lies outside $K$ with probability at least ½, and each such sample at least doubles $|K|$. Since $|H^\perp| \le |G|$, at most $\log_2|G|$ doublings are needed: expected at most $2\log_2|G|$ samples. Then $H$ is the set of $g$ with $\chi(g) = 1$ for all sampled $\chi$ — linear algebra over the group, efficient when $G$ is given as a product of cyclic groups (Kitaev, "Quantum measurements and the Abelian stabilizer problem", 1995, cited).

The open cases. Regev, "Quantum computation and lattice problems", SIAM J. Comput. 33 (2004): a polynomial algorithm for the dihedral HSP, of a particular kind, would solve the unique shortest vector problem. Kuperberg, SIAM J. Comput. 35 (2005). Babai, STOC 2016. These are named so that the frame is honest: the HSP explains why Shor works, and it also marks exactly where the known quantum speedups stop.

Twenty-one, factored

the running example, closed

One run of 14 qubits returns $y$. With probability $0.308$, $y$ decodes to $\frac16$ or $\frac56$ and gives $r = 6$ at once. With $0.321$ it gives the divisor 3, and with $0.167$ the divisor 2 — and two runs together give $r = 6$ with probability $0.629$.

Then $2^3 = 8 \not\equiv \pm1$, and $\gcd(7, 21) = 7$, $\gcd(9, 21) = 3$.

The failures, and why. $y$ near 0 ($s = 0$, $0.167$): the phase is 0 and carries no $r$. $y$ near 256, 171, 341: $s$ shares a factor with 6, so only a divisor of $r$ shows. $y$ in the tails ($0.037$): a wrong denominator, caught by $2^q \not\equiv 1$ — all but 12 and 18, multiples of 6 that P4 can still use.

What this unit bought

The reduction, with its ½ proved. Order finding as phase estimation on a permutation. Modular exponentiation in $O(n^3)$ gates. Continued fractions and Legendre's theorem, proved. The hidden subgroup problem as the frame for Simon, Shor and discrete logarithms.

What it did not

What factoring a 2048-bit modulus costs on real hardware, with error correction: unit 24 and the capstone. And 21 itself has been "factored" in a 2012 photonic experiment whose circuit was simplified using the known answer — which is why such demonstrations prove little (cited, notes).

Next: unit 17, Grover — the unstructured counterpart, where the speedup is only quadratic.

The whole factorisation, as a reader would run it. 21 is odd, not prime (a primality test), not a perfect power (Prime powers, primes, and the general case). Pick $a = 2$; $\gcd(2, 21) = 1$. Run the 14-qubit circuit; suppose it returns 427. $427/512 = [0; 1, 5, 42, 2]$, nearest fraction with denominator below 21: $5/6$. $2^6 \equiv 1$: $r = 6$. $r$ even, $2^3 = 8 \not\equiv 20$. $\gcd(7, 21) = 7$. Done, and $21/7 = 3$.

Vandersypen et al., Nature 414, 883 (2001), factored 15 with seven NMR qubits; Martín-López et al., Nature Photonics 6, 773 (2012), factored 21 with photons and qubit recycling. Smolin, Smith and Vargo, "Oversimplifying quantum factoring", Nature 499, 163 (2013), showed that circuits "compiled" with knowledge of the answer can factor any number with a coin toss — so a demonstration counts only if its circuit could have been written without knowing the factors.
