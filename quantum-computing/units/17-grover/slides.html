Grover: search as a rotation in a plane

Among $N$ items with $M$ marked and no structure to exploit, about $\frac\pi4\sqrt{N/M}$ queries find a marked one. The whole algorithm lives in a plane, where two reflections make a rotation by $2\theta$ with $\sin\theta = \sqrt{M/N}$ — and that one picture gives the count, the success probability, the overshoot, and amplitude amplification.

Shor's algorithm (unit 16) is fast because its problem has structure: a hidden period, which a Fourier transform finds. This unit asks what is left when there is no structure at all — a box that answers "is this the one?" and nothing else. The answer, found by Lov Grover in 1996, is a quadratic speedup: about $\sqrt N$ queries instead of about $N$. Unit 18 proves that nothing quantum does better, so this is the honest size of a quantum advantage on unstructured problems.

The unit's discipline is a single picture. Everything Grover's algorithm does happens in a two-dimensional real plane, where one iteration is two reflections and therefore a rotation. Every claim on these slides — the iteration count, the success probability, the overshoot, the price of not knowing how many items are marked, amplitude amplification — is a statement about that rotation, and is proved from it.

What this unit does not do. It does not build phase estimation; that is unit 15, which is not among this unit's prerequisites. So quantum counting — phase estimation run on the Grover rotation — is stated with its query count and cited, not derived. It does not use the class BQP (unit 12, also not a prerequisite): "a quadratic speedup for NP search" is said in query terms. And it does not prove that $\sqrt N$ is optimal; unit 18 does.

17 · overview

A million items, one marked

the running example: a million-item search

A box computes $f:\{0, 1\}^{20}\to\{0, 1\}$, and $f(x) = 1$ for exactly one $x$ — the marked item. Nothing else is known: no order, no pattern, no hint. One question to the box is one query.

Classically the only strategy is to open boxes: about $N/2 = 524{,}288$ queries on average, and section 3 proves no classical algorithm does better than order $N$.

Quantumly, $804$ queries find it with probability $0.99999976$. Why 804, why that probability, and why 1608 would be a disaster, is the whole unit.

Where the example appears. Section 1 computes its angle, $\theta = \arcsin 2^{-10}$, and draws the plane (The two vectors that matter). Section 2 turns the state by $2\theta$ per iteration and draws the iterates to scale (Two thousandths of a radian per iteration). Section 3 finds $k = 804$ and the success probability, digit by digit (Worked: a million items), and puts 804 against 524,288. Section 5 runs it without knowing $M$ (Exponential search). Section 6 scales the same arithmetic up to $2^{128}$.

Why this example. A million is large enough that $\sqrt N$ against $N$ is a difference one can feel — 804 against half a million — and small enough that every number in the unit can be computed to many digits by hand with a calculator. It is also a realistic register size: twenty qubits.

Where unstructured search comes from. Not from databases: a quantum computer cannot hold a classical table of a million entries in superposition any more cheaply than a classical machine can read it. The box is a computation. For any problem in NP, a verifier $V(\varphi, x)$ checks a proposed witness $x$ in polynomial time; with the instance $\varphi$ fixed, $f(x) = V(\varphi, x)$ is a function the box can compute, and unit 10 turns it into a polynomial-size oracle. Searching for a witness is unstructured search whenever one ignores everything about $V$ except its answers.

17 · the plane

Unstructured search

unstructured search

Given query access to $f:\{0, 1\}^n\to\{0, 1\}$, with $N = 2^n$ items of which $M\ge1$ are marked ($f = 1$), find a marked item. Nothing is known about $f$ beyond its answers.

Queries go through unit 13's phase oracle,

$$O_f\ket x = (-1)^{f(x)}\ket x,$$

that is,

$$O_f = I - 2\!\!\sum_{f(x) = 1}\!\!\ket x\!\bra x .$$

Checks. $O_f$ is diagonal with entries $\pm1$: Hermitian, unitary, and $O_f^2 = I$ ✓. The second form is the first applied to each basis vector ✓.

Where $f$ comes from. An NP verifier $V(\varphi, x)$ with the instance $\varphi$ fixed: unit 10 makes it a polynomial-size oracle.

Phase kickback (unit 13): one query to the bit oracle with its target in $\ket-$ is one application of $O_f$.

The algorithm will use $O_f$ and one more reflection, alternately — and nothing else that depends on $f$.

The two forms of $O_f$ agree. On a basis vector $\ket y$, $\big(I - 2\sum_{f(x)=1}\ket x\!\bra x\big)\ket y = \ket y - 2\sum_{f(x) = 1}\braket{x|y}\ket x$, and $\braket{x|y} = \delta_{xy}$, so this is $\ket y - 2\ket y = -\ket y$ if $f(y) = 1$ and $\ket y$ otherwise: $(-1)^{f(y)}\ket y$ ✓. The sum $\Pi = \sum_{f(x)=1}\ket x\!\bra x$ is the orthogonal projection onto the span of the marked basis vectors, so $O_f = I - 2\Pi$.

Kickback, recalled. $U_f\ket x\ket- = \ket x\otimes\frac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{f(x)} - \ket{1\oplus f(x)}) = (-1)^{f(x)}\ket x\ket-$, and the ancilla leaves in $\ket-$, unentangled. The oracle must be clean — no garbage — or terms with different garbage stop interfering; unit 10's uncomputation guarantees this for any polynomial-time $f$.

History. L. K. Grover, "A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search", Proc. 28th STOC (1996), 212–219, and "Quantum mechanics helps in searching for a needle in a haystack", Phys. Rev. Lett. 79, 325 (1997). The word "database" in the title has caused much confusion; the overview's notes say why the box is a computation, not a table. The two-reflection picture this deck uses became the standard account soon afterwards; Grover's own was "inversion about the mean", the last slide of this section.

Why $M\ge1$ and $M < N$. With $M = 0$ there is nothing to find, and section 5 says how an algorithm notices. With $M = N$ every item is marked and any guess wins. The deck assumes $0 < M < N$ unless it says otherwise.

17 · the plane

The algorithm, before the explanation

Grover's algorithm
  1. Prepare the uniform superposition $\ket s = H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = N^{-1/2}\sum_x\ket x$ (unit 13).
  2. Apply the Grover iteration $G = D\,O_f$ $k$ times, where $D = 2\ket s\!\bra s - I$ is the diffusion operator.
  3. Measure in the computational basis; check the answer with one more query.

Checks. $\ket s$ has $N$ amplitudes $N^{-1/2}$: norm $N\cdot\frac1N = 1$ ✓. $D^\dagger = D$, and $D^2 = 4\ket s\!\braket{s|s}\!\bra s - 4\ket s\!\bra s + I = I$, so $D$ is Hermitian and unitary ✓; so is $G$, as a product of unitaries ✓.

Cost. $k$ queries, plus one to check. $D$ does not depend on $f$, so it costs gates but no queries.

Hadamards, then $k$ rounds of oracle and diffusion, then measure. Why it works is a rotation, drawn over the next six slides.

The step $D^2 = I$, in full. $(2\ket s\!\bra s - I)^2 = 4\ket s\!\bra s\,\ket s\!\bra s - 2\ket s\!\bra s - 2\ket s\!\bra s + I$, and $\ket s\!\bra s\,\ket s\!\bra s = \ket s\braket{s|s}\bra s = \ket s\!\bra s$ because $\braket{s|s} = 1$. So $D^2 = 4\ket s\!\bra s - 4\ket s\!\bra s + I = I$. A Hermitian operator that squares to $I$ is unitary, since $D^\dagger D = D^2 = I$.

The name. Grover called $D$ the diffusion operator because, written as a matrix, it has $\frac2N - 1$ on the diagonal and $\frac2N$ everywhere else: it spreads amplitude evenly from each basis state to all the others, like a discretised diffusion step. The next slides show it is better thought of as a reflection.

The final check. Measuring gives some $x$; one classical query of $f(x)$ says whether it is marked. If not, run again. This turns any success probability $P$ into an expected $1/P$ runs, and it is why the algorithm never returns a wrong answer — only, occasionally, no answer.

What is not yet clear — and is the point of the rest of the section — is why alternating these two operations should push amplitude onto the marked items at all, and why it should take $\sqrt N$ rounds rather than $N$.

17 · the plane

The two vectors that matter

the marked and unmarked superpositions $$\ket m = \tfrac1{\sqrt M}\!\sum_{f(x) = 1}\!\ket x,\quad \ket{m^\perp} = \tfrac1{\sqrt{N - M}}\!\sum_{f(x) = 0}\!\ket x .$$

Orthonormal: disjoint supports, and norms $M\cdot\frac1M = 1$, $(N - M)\cdot\frac1{N - M} = 1$ ✓. Then

$$\ket s = \sin\theta\,\ket m + \cos\theta\,\ket{m^\perp},$$

with $\sin\theta = \sqrt{M/N} = \braket{m|s}$,

which defines the Grover angle $\theta\in(0, \frac\pi2)$. Normalised: $\frac MN + \frac{N - M}N = 1$ ✓.

Check. Measuring $\ket s$ finds a marked item with probability $\sin^2\theta = M/N$ — a uniformly random guess ✓.

The running example. $\sin\theta = 2^{-10}$: $\theta = 9.7656\times10^{-4}$ rad $= 0.056^\circ$.

The start vector sits at angle $\theta$ above the unmarked direction. For a million items it is almost flat.

The decomposition, in full. $\ket s = N^{-1/2}\sum_{f(x) = 1}\ket x + N^{-1/2}\sum_{f(x) = 0}\ket x = \sqrt{M/N}\,\ket m + \sqrt{(N - M)/N}\,\ket{m^\perp}$, by multiplying and dividing each sum by the normalisation of $\ket m$ and $\ket{m^\perp}$. Since $\frac MN + \frac{N - M}N = 1$ and both coefficients are positive, there is a unique $\theta\in(0, \frac\pi2)$ with $\sin\theta = \sqrt{M/N}$ and $\cos\theta = \sqrt{(N - M)/N}$.

The probability of a marked outcome. By the Born rule (unit 01) each outcome $x$ has probability $|\braket{x|s}|^2 = 1/N$; summing over the $M$ marked $x$ gives $M/N$. Equivalently it is $\|\Pi\ket s\|^2$ with $\Pi$ the projection onto the marked basis vectors, and $\Pi\ket s = \sin\theta\,\ket m$ — the form that generalises to every state in the plane.

The running example's angle. $\arcsin x = x + x^3/6 + \cdots$, so $\theta = 2^{-10} + 2^{-30}/6 + \cdots$, which exceeds $2^{-10} = 9.765625\times10^{-4}$ by $1.55\times10^{-10}$. In degrees, $0.0560^\circ$. When $M = 1$, $\ket m$ is the marked basis vector itself.

17 · the plane

The plane is invariant

theorem: the Grover plane

Let $P = \mathrm{span}\{\ket m, \ket{m^\perp}\}$, which contains $\ket s$. Then $O_f P\subseteq P$ and $D P\subseteq P$. So every state of the algorithm lies in $P$.

proof

Every term of $\ket m$ is marked and none of $\ket{m^\perp}$ is: $O_f\ket m = -\ket m$, $\ O_f\ket{m^\perp} = \ket{m^\perp}$.

For $\ket v\in P$: $D\ket v = 2\braket{s|v}\ket s - \ket v$, a combination of $\ket s$ and $\ket v$, both in $P$. The start $\ket s\in P$, and induction does the rest. $\blacksquare$

And it is a real plane. In the basis $(\ket{m^\perp}, \ket m)$ both operators have real matrices and $\ket s = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ is real, so every state is a real vector: the geometry of $\mathbb R^2$ applies.

Out of $2^{20}$ dimensions, the algorithm moves in two.

Full proof. $O_f$ is linear, so it maps $a\ket m + b\ket{m^\perp}$ to $-a\ket m + b\ket{m^\perp}\in P$. For $D$, the formula $D\ket v = 2\ket s\braket{s|v} - \ket v$ is the definition. The algorithm's state after $j$ steps is a product of $j$ factors $O_f$ or $D$ applied to $\ket s\in P$; each factor maps $P$ into $P$, so by induction on $j$ every intermediate state is in $P$.

The matrices. In the ordered basis $(\ket{m^\perp}, \ket m)$, $$O_f\big|_P = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 0 & -1\end{pmatrix},\qquad D\big|_P = 2\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta\\ \sin\theta\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & \sin\theta\end{pmatrix} - I = \begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta & \sin2\theta\\ \sin2\theta & -\cos2\theta\end{pmatrix}.$$ The second uses $\braket{s|v}$ with $\ket s$'s real coordinates, and $2\cos^2\theta - 1 = \cos2\theta$, $2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \sin2\theta$, $2\sin^2\theta - 1 = -\cos2\theta$. Real matrices applied to a real starting vector give real vectors, so the state's coordinates are real at every step: the inner product on these vectors is the Euclidean dot product, and angles and rotations mean what they mean in $\mathbb R^2$.

What the rest of $\mathbb C^N$ does. Both operators are Hermitian and map $P$ into $P$, so they also map $P^\perp$ into $P^\perp$ (if $\ket w\perp P$ and $\ket v\in P$, then $\braket{v|Aw} = \braket{Av|w} = 0$). The algorithm never touches it, which is why a $2^{20}$-dimensional computation can be followed with a pencil.

Inversion about the mean

inversion about the mean

If $\ket\psi = \sum_x a_x\ket x$ and $\bar a = \frac1N\sum_x a_x$, then $\ D\ket\psi = \sum_x(2\bar a - a_x)\ket x$.

Proof. $\braket{s|\psi} = N^{-1/2}\sum_x a_x = \sqrt N\,\bar a$, so $2\ket s\!\braket{s|\psi} = 2\sqrt N\,\bar a\cdot N^{-1/2}\sum_x\ket x = 2\bar a\sum_x\ket x$. Subtract $\ket\psi$. $\blacksquare$

What it does. $O_f$ flips the marked amplitudes, which lowers the mean slightly; $D$ reflects every amplitude through the mean, lifting the flipped ones by about twice their size.

Check, 16 items, one marked: the mean after $O_f$ is $\frac{15 - 1}{16\cdot4} = 0.21875$; after $D$ the marked amplitude is $0.4375 + 0.25 = 0.6875$ and the others $0.1875$. Norm: $0.6875^2 + 15\cdot0.1875^2 = 0.47265625 + 0.52734375 = 1$ ✓.

One iteration on 16 items, amplitude by amplitude: the marked bar is flipped, then everything is reflected through the dashed mean.

This is Grover's own 1996 picture. It shows what happens to each amplitude; the plane shows why it adds up to a rotation.

Why about $\sqrt N$ iterations — the heuristic. While the marked amplitude $a$ is small, the mean is close to $N^{-1/2}$ and each iteration raises $a$ by about $2N^{-1/2}$. To bring $a$ to order 1 takes order $\sqrt N$ iterations. The heuristic fails near the end, where the mean has dropped; the rotation picture replaces it with an exact formula.

The same numbers from the plane. With $\sin\theta = \frac14$, section 2 will give the marked amplitude after one iteration as $\sin3\theta = 3\sin\theta - 4\sin^3\theta = \frac34 - \frac4{64} = 0.6875$ ✓, and each unmarked amplitude as $\cos3\theta/\sqrt{15}$: $\cos3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta = \cos\theta\,(4\cos^2\theta - 3) = \frac{\sqrt{15}}4\cdot\frac34$, so $\cos3\theta/\sqrt{15} = \frac3{16} = 0.1875$ ✓.

A trap. Inversion about the mean is not "amplify the largest amplitude". If half the items are marked, the mean after $O_f$ is exactly 0 and $D$ merely negates the state: nothing changes (problem P6).

17 · two reflections

Reflections

the reflection about a vector

For a unit vector $\ket u$ in a subspace $V$, $\ R_u = 2\ket u\!\bra u - I_V$ on $V$: it keeps the component along $\ket u$ and negates the component orthogonal to it.

Checks. $R_u^\dagger = R_u$; $R_u^2 = 4\ket u\!\bra u - 4\ket u\!\bra u + I = I$, so unitary ✓. $R_u\ket u = \ket u$ and $R_u\ket v = -\ket v$ for $\ket v\perp\ket u$: eigenvalues $+1$ once and $-1$ on the rest ✓.

both steps are reflections in the plane

$O_f\big|_P = R_{m^\perp}$ and $D\big|_P = R_s$.

Proof. In two dimensions a reflection is fixed by the line it keeps. $O_f$ keeps $\ket{m^\perp}$ and negates $\ket m\perp\ket{m^\perp}$. $D$ keeps $\ket s$ ($D\ket s = 2\ket s - \ket s$) and negates any $\ket v\in P$ with $\ket v\perp\ket s$ ($D\ket v = -\ket v$). $\blacksquare$

A reflection keeps one line and flips everything across it.

Reflections in general. For any orthogonal projection $\Pi$, $I - 2\Pi$ is Hermitian and squares to $I$ ($\Pi^2 = \Pi$): it fixes the range of $I - \Pi$ and negates the range of $\Pi$. On all of $\mathbb C^N$, $O_f = I - 2\Pi_{\rm marked}$ negates the $M$-dimensional marked subspace, and $D = -(I - 2\ket s\!\bra s)$ is minus the reflection in the hyperplane $\ket s^\perp$. Both become line reflections only when restricted to the plane — which is all that matters, by the previous section.

Why "fixed by the line it keeps". In a two-dimensional space with orthonormal basis $\ket u, \ket{u'}$, an operator with $A\ket u = \ket u$ and $A\ket{u'} = -\ket{u'}$ equals $\ket u\!\bra u - \ket{u'}\!\bra{u'} = 2\ket u\!\bra u - I$ on that space, since $\ket u\!\bra u + \ket{u'}\!\bra{u'} = I$ there. For $O_f$ take $\ket u = \ket{m^\perp}$, $\ket{u'} = \ket m$; for $D$ take $\ket u = \ket s$ and $\ket{u'} = \cos\theta\ket m - \sin\theta\ket{m^\perp}$, the unit vector of $P$ orthogonal to $\ket s$.

A trap: sign conventions. Some texts define the diffusion operator as $I - 2\ket s\!\bra s$, which is $-D$, and then write the iteration with a minus sign. The probabilities are the same — a global phase — but the plane picture is cleanest with $D = 2\ket s\!\bra s - I$, a reflection about $\ket s$ itself.

Worked: two reflections make a rotation

the composition, in $\mathbb R^2$

1. The reflection about the line at angle $\alpha$. Write $c_\alpha = \cos2\alpha$, $s_\alpha = \sin2\alpha$: $\ R_\alpha = \begin{pmatrix}c_\alpha & s_\alpha\\ s_\alpha & -c_\alpha\end{pmatrix}$. It fixes $(\cos\alpha, \sin\alpha)$: first entry $\cos2\alpha\cos\alpha + \sin2\alpha\sin\alpha = \cos\alpha$, second $\sin2\alpha\cos\alpha - \cos2\alpha\sin\alpha = \sin\alpha$ ✓. It negates $(-\sin\alpha, \cos\alpha)$ the same way ✓.

2. Multiply.

$$R_\alpha R_\beta = \begin{pmatrix}c_\alpha c_\beta + s_\alpha s_\beta & c_\alpha s_\beta - s_\alpha c_\beta\\ s_\alpha c_\beta - c_\alpha s_\beta & s_\alpha s_\beta + c_\alpha c_\beta\end{pmatrix}$$

3. Addition formulas. $= \begin{pmatrix}\cos2(\alpha - \beta) & -\sin2(\alpha - \beta)\\ \sin2(\alpha - \beta) & \cos2(\alpha - \beta)\end{pmatrix}$: the rotation by $2(\alpha - \beta)$, whatever vector it acts on.

4. Applied. $\beta = 15^\circ$, $\alpha = 45^\circ$, $v$ at $-5^\circ$: $R_\beta$ sends it to $2\cdot15^\circ + 5^\circ = 35^\circ$, then $R_\alpha$ to $90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$. Turned by $60^\circ = 2(45^\circ - 15^\circ)$ ✓.

First reflect in $\ell_\beta$, then in $\ell_\alpha$: the net effect is a rotation by twice the angle from $\ell_\beta$ to $\ell_\alpha$.

Order matters: $R_\beta R_\alpha$ rotates by $-2(\alpha - \beta)$.

Step 1, the second check. On $(-\sin\alpha, \cos\alpha)$: first entry $-\cos2\alpha\sin\alpha + \sin2\alpha\cos\alpha = \sin(2\alpha - \alpha) = \sin\alpha$, second $-\sin2\alpha\sin\alpha - \cos2\alpha\cos\alpha = -\cos(2\alpha - \alpha) = -\cos\alpha$. So the vector goes to $(\sin\alpha, -\cos\alpha)$, its negative ✓. A reflection of a vector at angle $\varphi$ about the line at angle $\alpha$ lands at $2\alpha - \varphi$: apply $R_\alpha$ to $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$ and use the same formulas.

Step 3, the formulas used. $\cos A\cos B + \sin A\sin B = \cos(A - B)$ and $\sin A\cos B - \cos A\sin B = \sin(A - B)$, with $A = 2\alpha$, $B = 2\beta$. The top-right entry is $-(\sin2\alpha\cos2\beta - \cos2\alpha\sin2\beta) = -\sin2(\alpha - \beta)$.

A second proof, by angles. A vector at angle $\varphi$ goes to $2\beta - \varphi$ and then to $2\alpha - (2\beta - \varphi) = \varphi + 2(\alpha - \beta)$. Every vector turns by the same amount, so the composition is that rotation. This is the version to remember; the matrix version is the one to check.

Determinants. $\det R_\alpha = -\cos^22\alpha - \sin^22\alpha = -1$, so the product has determinant $+1$: an orthogonal matrix of determinant one, which in two dimensions is always a rotation. That is a third proof that the composition is a rotation, though not of which one.

17 · two reflections

One iteration is a rotation by $2\theta$

theorem (the rotation picture)

On the plane $P$, with $\ket{m^\perp}$ at angle 0, $G = D\,O_f$ is the rotation by $2\theta$. With $\varphi_k = (2k + 1)\theta$,

$$G^k\ket s = \cos\varphi_k\ket{m^\perp} + \sin\varphi_k\ket m,$$

and after $k$ iterations a marked item is found with probability

$$P_k = \sin^2\big((2k + 1)\theta\big).$$
proof

$O_f\big|_P = R_0$ and $D\big|_P = R_\theta$ (reflections about the lines at angles 0 and $\theta$), so $G = R_\theta R_0$ rotates by $2\theta$. $\ket s$ starts at $\theta$ and reaches $\theta + 2k\theta$. Projecting onto the marked items keeps exactly the $\ket m$-part, so $P_k$ is its square. $\blacksquare$

Checks. $P_0 = \sin^2\theta = M/N$, a random guess ✓; $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$ ✓.

$O_f$ reflects $\ket s$ to angle $-\theta$; $D$ reflects that about $\ket s$, to $3\theta$. Each iteration adds $2\theta$.

Full proof of the probability. Let $\Pi$ project onto the span of the marked basis states. For a state $c\ket{m^\perp} + s\ket m$ in $P$, $\Pi\ket m = \ket m$ (every term of $\ket m$ is marked) and $\Pi\ket{m^\perp} = 0$ (no term is), so $\Pi(c\ket{m^\perp} + s\ket m) = s\ket m$ and the probability of a marked outcome is $\|s\ket m\|^2 = s^2$. With $s = \sin((2k + 1)\theta)$ this is $P_k$.

Item by item. After $k$ iterations every marked item has amplitude $\sin((2k + 1)\theta)/\sqrt M$ and every unmarked one $\cos((2k + 1)\theta)/\sqrt{N - M}$, read off from the definitions of $\ket m$ and $\ket{m^\perp}$. Squared and summed: $M\cdot\frac{\sin^2}{M} + (N - M)\cdot\frac{\cos^2}{N - M} = 1$ ✓. With 16 items and $k = 1$ these are the $0.6875$ and $0.1875$ of the last section ✓.

The same thing on the Bloch sphere. Treat $\ket{m^\perp}, \ket m$ as the $\ket0, \ket1$ of a fictitious qubit. The rotation matrix by $2\theta$ is $\begin{pmatrix}\cos2\theta & -\sin2\theta\\ \sin2\theta & \cos2\theta\end{pmatrix}$, which is unit 02's $R_y(4\theta) = \cos2\theta\,I - i\sin2\theta\,Y$. So one Grover iteration is a rotation of that qubit by $4\theta$ about the $y$ axis: the angle in the plane is half the angle on the sphere, as always.

Why the order matters. $G = D\,O_f$ applies $O_f$ first, and the rotation is by $2(\theta - 0)$, counterclockwise, towards $\ket m$. The other order wastes a query: $D\ket s = \ket s$, so $(O_fD)^k\ket s = O_f\,G^{k-1}\ket s$, at angle $-(2k - 1)\theta$, with success $P_{k-1}$ for $k$ queries.

Worked: four items, one iteration

$N = 4$, $n = 2$, the marked item is $11$

The angle. $\sin\theta = \sqrt{1/4} = \frac12$, so $\theta = \frac\pi6$, and one iteration reaches $3\theta = \frac\pi2$: $\ P_1 = \sin^2\frac\pi2 = 1$.

Every amplitude, in the order $00, 01, 10, 11$:

$\ket s = \frac12(1, 1, 1, 1)$; norm $4\cdot\frac14 = 1$ ✓.

$O_f\ket s = \frac12(1, 1, 1, -1)$.

Mean $\bar a = \frac14\big(\frac12 + \frac12 + \frac12 - \frac12\big) = \frac14$.

$D O_f\ket s$: $\ 2\cdot\frac14 - \frac12 = 0$ for $00, 01, 10$, and $2\cdot\frac14 + \frac12 = 1$ for $11$.

Result: $\ket{11}$ exactly, probability $1$, as the theorem says ✓.

theorem: the optimal count

Let $k^* = \lfloor\pi/4\theta\rfloor$ — the same as the nearest integer to $\pi/4\theta - \frac12$. Then

$$P_{k^*}\ \ge\ 1 - \frac MN,\qquad k^*\le\frac\pi4\sqrt{\frac NM}.$$
proof

$k^*\le\frac\pi{4\theta} < k^* + 1$ gives $\frac\pi2 - \theta < (2k^* + 1)\theta\le\frac\pi2 + \theta$. On that window $\sin^2\ge\sin^2(\frac\pi2\pm\theta) = \cos^2\theta = 1 - \frac MN$. And $\theta\ge\sin\theta = \sqrt{M/N}$ bounds $k^*$. $\blacksquare$

Check, $N = 4$: $k^* = \lfloor\frac{\pi/4}{\pi/6}\rfloor = \lfloor1.5\rfloor = 1$, and $P_1 = 1\ge\frac34$ ✓.

Read it. One marked item in a million: failure at most $2^{-20}$. More than half the items marked: $k^* = 0$, "just guess", which is right.

Full proof, the window. From $k^*\le\pi/4\theta$: $(2k^* + 1)\theta\le\pi/2 + \theta$. From $\pi/4\theta < k^* + 1$: $2k^*\theta > \pi/2 - 2\theta$, so $(2k^* + 1)\theta > \pi/2 - \theta$. The window lies in $[0, \pi]$ because $\theta<\pi/2$.

The two recipes agree. The nearest integer to $y$ is $\lfloor y + \frac12\rfloor$ (ties rounded up), so the nearest integer to $\pi/4\theta - \frac12$ is $\lfloor\pi/4\theta\rfloor$. What one iteration costs in gates

the diffusion operator as a circuit $$D = H^{\otimes n}\big(2\ket{0^n}\!\bra{0^n} - I\big)H^{\otimes n},$$ $$I - 2\ket{0^n}\!\bra{0^n} = X^{\otimes n}\,C^{n-1}(Z)\,X^{\otimes n}.$$

Proof. $H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = \ket s$ and $(H^{\otimes n})^2 = I$, so the first right side is $2\ket s\!\bra s - I$. $C^{n-1}(Z)$ negates $\ket{1^n}$ alone, and $X^{\otimes n}$ swaps $\ket{0^n}$ with $\ket{1^n}$. $\blacksquare$

So the circuit drawn is $-D$: equal to $D$ up to global phase. Unit 10 builds $C^{n-1}(Z)$ from $2(n - 2)$ Toffolis and one $CZ$, with $n - 2$ work qubits: $D$ costs $O(n)$ gates, and no queries.

when the sign matters

Inside a controlled-$G$ the $-1$ becomes a relative phase on the control and must be tracked, or cancelled by a $Z$ on the control. Section 5's quantum counting needs exactly that.

$-D$ on four qubits: Hadamards and $X$'s turn "reflect about $\ket s$" into "negate $\ket{1111}$".

Per iteration: one query, $O(n)$ gates of diffusion. For the running example, 804 of each.

Full proof. $H^{\otimes n}(2\ket{0^n}\!\bra{0^n} - I)H^{\otimes n} = 2(H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n})(\bra{0^n}H^{\otimes n}) - (H^{\otimes n})^2 = 2\ket s\!\bra s - I$, using that $H^{\otimes n}$ is Hermitian, so $\bra{0^n}H^{\otimes n} = (H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n})^\dagger = \bra s$. Next, $C^{n-1}(Z)$ with controls on qubits $0, \dots, n - 2$ and target $n - 1$ applies $Z$ to the last qubit exactly when the others are all 1, and $Z\ket1 = -\ket1$, $Z\ket0 = \ket0$: so it is $I - 2\ket{1^n}\!\bra{1^n}$. Conjugating by $X^{\otimes n}$, which maps $\ket{1^n}$ to $\ket{0^n}$, gives $I - 2\ket{0^n}\!\bra{0^n}$. Finally $2\ket{0^n}\!\bra{0^n} - I = -(I - 2\ket{0^n}\!\bra{0^n})$.

Why the global phase is harmless here. Running the circuit $-D$ in place of $D$ multiplies $G$ by $-1$ and $G^k\ket s$ by $(-1)^k$, which no measurement sees (unit 01). Under a control it is no longer global: controlled-$(-G)$ $= (Z\otimes I)\cdot$controlled-$G$, since both sides act as $I$ when the control reads 0 and as $-G$ when it reads 1; so one $Z$ on the control cancels the sign.

The exact count is problem P7. It is linear in $n$, and almost always much smaller than the cost of the oracle, which must evaluate $f$ reversibly — for key search, a whole block cipher (section 6).

The oracle for a single marked item $w$ is built the same way: $X$ on the qubits where $w$ has a 0, then $C^{n-1}(Z)$, then the $X$'s again. That is how one would test the algorithm; in a real search the oracle is the verifier and $w$ is unknown.

$\sqrt N$ against $N$

theorem: the classical lower bound

Any classical algorithm, deterministic or randomised, that finds the single marked item among $N$ with probability at least $\frac12$ makes at least $\frac N2 - 1$ queries.

Proof. Against a uniformly random marked item, a deterministic algorithm with $q$ queries succeeds with probability at most $(q + 1)/N$ (notes); averaging (unit 13) passes the bound to randomised algorithms. $\blacksquare$

the running example

$804$ quantum queries against about $524{,}288$ classical: a factor of $652$. A quadratic speedup: $\sqrt N$ against $N$.

And no further. Unit 18 proves that every quantum algorithm needs order $\sqrt N$ queries (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani 1997): Grover is optimal.

On a log-log plot the classical line is twice as steep. At a million items the gap is a factor of 652.

Full proof of the classical bound. Fix a deterministic algorithm and let the marked item $w$ be uniform. Run it against $f\equiv0$: it asks a fixed sequence $x_1, x_2, \dots$ and finally outputs some $x$. Against $f$ with marked item $w$, the run is identical until the first query equal to $w$, because all earlier answers are 0 in both. So after $q$ queries the algorithm has found $w$ only if $w\in\{x_1, \dots, x_q\}$, and otherwise outputs the same $x$ as against $f\equiv 0$: it succeeds only if $w$ is one of at most $q + 1$ fixed items, probability at most $(q + 1)/N$. A randomised algorithm is a distribution over deterministic ones, so its success averaged over $w$ is at most $(q + 1)/N$ too, and averaging (unit 13) gives a particular $w$ on which it is at most that. Success $\ge\frac12$ forces $q + 1\ge N/2$.

The expected cost of the obvious algorithm. Open items in a random order, inferring the last: $\sum_{j = 1}^{N - 1}\frac jN + \frac{N - 1}N = \frac{N - 1}2 + \frac{N - 1}N$, which for $N = 2^{20}$ is $524{,}288.499999$ — "about $N/2$". The factor in the box is $524{,}288/804 = 652.1$.

What the theorem is not. It is a query bound. Measured in gates, Grover's advantage is $\sqrt N$ times the ratio of a classical evaluation of $f$ to a reversible quantum one — and section 6 shows that ratio can be large and unfavourable.

The success probability is periodic

theorem: overshoot

The success probability

$$P_k = \tfrac12 - \tfrac12\cos\big((4k + 2)\theta\big)$$

is a sinusoid in $k$ with period $\dfrac\pi{2\theta}$ iterations: it rises to near 1 at $k^*$, falls to near 0 at about $2k^*$, and returns.

Proof. $\sin^2$ has period $\pi$, and the argument grows by $2\theta$ per iteration. $\blacksquare$

The running example. Period $\pi/2\theta = 1608.5$. At $k = 1608$ the state is at angle $3217\theta = \pi + 9.4\times10^{-6}$, almost exactly $-\ket{m^\perp}$:

$$P_{1608} = 8.9\times10^{-11}.$$

A random guess, $P_0 = 2^{-20} = 9.5\times10^{-7}$, is ten thousand times better. At $k = 2412$ it is back to $0.99999978$.

The state keeps turning: up to $\ket m$ at 804, round to $-\ket{m^\perp}$ at 1608, up to $-\ket m$ at 2412.

not like classical search

Classically, more queries never hurt. Here they can, so the count must be right — and the count needs $M$.

The numbers. $3217\theta - \pi = 9.408\times10^{-6}$ rad, so $P_{1608} = \sin^2(9.408\times10^{-6}) = 8.85\times10^{-11}$. Periodic in what sense. As a function of a real variable $k$, $P$ has period exactly $\pi/2\theta$. On the integers it is a sampled sinusoid, which is exactly periodic only when $\pi/2\theta$ is rational — almost never. But the samples nearly repeat: since $|dP/dk| = |2\theta\sin((4k + 2)\theta)|\le2\theta$, shifting $k$ by the integer nearest to $\pi/2\theta$ changes $P$ by at most $2\theta\cdot\frac12 = \theta$. Problem P4 proves this.

A name. Brassard compared it to baking a soufflé: take it out too early or too late and it fails (G. Brassard, "Searching a quantum phone book", Science 275, 627, 1997). "Fixed-point" variants that never overshoot, at the same $O(\sqrt{N/M})$ query cost, exist (Yoder, Low and Chuang, Phys. Rev. The quarter lemma

quarter lemma (Boyer, Brassard, Høyer and Tapp 1998)

Let $0 < M < N$ and pick $k$ uniformly from $\{0, 1, \dots, m - 1\}$. The average success probability is

$$\bar P_m = \frac12 - \frac{\sin4m\theta}{4m\sin2\theta},$$

so $\bar P_m\ge\frac14$ whenever $m\ge m_0 = 1/\sin2\theta$.

proof

$\sin^2x = \frac12(1 - \cos2x)$, so $\bar P_m = \frac12 - \frac1{2m}\sum_{k<m}\cos((2k + 1)\alpha)$ with $\alpha = 2\theta$. Telescope: $2\sin\alpha\cos((2k + 1)\alpha) = \sin((2k + 2)\alpha) - \sin(2k\alpha)$, so the sum is $\frac{\sin2m\alpha}{2\sin\alpha}$. If $m\sin2\theta\ge1$, the correction is at most $\frac1{4m\sin2\theta}\le\frac14$. $\blacksquare$

Running example: $m_0 = 512.0002$; at $m = 513$, $\bar P_m = 0.2735$.

A random count below $m$ succeeds a quarter of the time once $m\ge m_0$ — without knowing where the peak is.

Full proof, the telescoping. The product-to-sum formula $2\sin A\cos B = \sin(B + A) - \sin(B - A)$ with $A = \alpha$, $B = (2k + 1)\alpha$ gives $\sin((2k + 2)\alpha) - \sin(2k\alpha)$. Summing over $k = 0, \dots, m - 1$, everything cancels except $\sin(2m\alpha) - \sin 0$. So $\sum_{k<m}\cos((2k + 1)\alpha) = \sin(2m\alpha)/(2\sin\alpha)$, valid since $\sin\alpha = \sin2\theta > 0$ for $0 < \theta < \pi/2$. Then $\bar P_m = \frac12 - \frac1{2m}\cdot\frac{\sin4m\theta}{2\sin2\theta}$. Since $|\sin4m\theta|\le1$, the correction is at most $1/(4m\sin2\theta)$ in size, and $m\ge1/\sin2\theta$ makes that at most $\frac14$.

How big is $m_0$? $\sin2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = 2\sqrt{M(N - M)}/N$, so $m_0 = N/(2\sqrt{M(N - M)})$. If $M\le\frac34N$ then $N - M\ge N/4$ and $m_0\le N/(2\sqrt{MN/4}) = \sqrt{N/M}$. So a range of about $\sqrt{N/M}$ iterations suffices — the right order, without knowing $M$. For the running example, $1/\sin(2\theta) = 512.0002$, just over $\frac12\sqrt N$.

The check at $m = 513$: $\bar P_{513} = \frac12 - \sin(2052\theta)/(2052\sin2\theta) = 0.27353$ (computed). At $m = 512$, just short of $m_0$, it is $0.2727$: the lemma is a sufficient condition, not a sharp one.

Exponential search

exponential search (BBHT 1998)
  1. Query one random $x$; if marked, stop.
  2. Set $m = 1$. Repeat: pick $k$ uniform in $\{0, \dots, \lceil m\rceil - 1\}$; run $k$ Grover iterations, measure $x$, query $f(x)$; if marked, stop; else $m\leftarrow\min(\frac65m, \sqrt N)$.
theorem

If $1\le M\le\frac34N$, the expected number of Grover iterations is less than $9\sqrt{N/M}$, plus one checking query per stage: $O(\sqrt{N/M})$ queries in all, with $M$ unknown.

Proof in the notes: before $m$ reaches $m_0$ the stages cost a geometric series, under $3m_0$; after, each stage succeeds with probability $\ge\frac14$ while costs grow by $\frac65 < \frac43$, under $6m_0$.

Running example: expected $1454$ iterations $+\ 35$ checks $\approx1489$ queries (computed), against $804$ knowing $M$.

The range grows geometrically; from stage 36 the quarter lemma applies, and the search ends within a few stages on average.

Full proof. Write $\lambda = \frac65$ and $m_s = \min(\lambda^{s-1}, \sqrt N)$ for the range at stage $s$. Stage $s$ runs an expected $(\lceil m_s\rceil - 1)/2 < m_s/2$ iterations. Let $s_0$ be the first stage with $m_{s_0}\ge m_0$; it exists because $m_0\le\sqrt{N/M}\le\sqrt N$ (previous slide's notes), and $m_{s_0}\le\lambda^{s_0 - 1} < \lambda m_0$ (if $s_0 > 1$ then $\lambda^{s_0-2} < m_0$; if $s_0 = 1$ then $m_1 = 1\le m_0$). Before $s_0$: at most $\sum_{s<s_0}\lambda^{s-1}/2 < \lambda^{s_0-1}/(2(\lambda - 1)) < \lambda m_0/(2(\lambda - 1)) = 3m_0$. From $s_0$ on: every range is at least $m_0$, so by the quarter lemma each stage fails with probability at most $\frac34$, independently given that it is reached; stage $s_0 + u$ is reached with probability at most $(\frac34)^u$ and costs at most $\lambda^{s_0 - 1 + u}/2 < \lambda m_0\lambda^u/2$ in expectation. Summing: $\frac{\lambda m_0}2\sum_u(\frac{3\lambda}4)^u = \frac{\lambda m_0}{2(1 - 3\lambda/4)} = 6m_0$, a convergent series because $3\lambda/4 = 0.9 < 1$. Total under $9m_0\le9\sqrt{N/M}$. The checks: at most $s_0 - 1\le\log_\lambda m_0 + 1$ before, and an expected $\sum_u(\frac34)^u = 4$ after: $O(\log N)$, negligible.

Step 1 handles $M > \frac34N$, where the bound on $m_0$ fails: there one random guess succeeds with probability above $\frac34$. If $M = 0$ the loop never stops; in practice one stops after, say, $90\sqrt N$ total iterations and reports "none". For $1\le M\le\frac34N$ the expected count is below $9\sqrt N$, so by Markov's inequality the search runs that long, and wrongly reports "none", with probability at most $\frac1{10}$; independent repetitions drive this down geometrically.

The running example's 1489 was computed by summing over stages: the probability of reaching each stage times its expected cost, with the exact $\bar P_m$ per stage, until the tail is below $10^{-15}$ — $1453.8$ iterations and $34.0$ checks — plus step 1's single query. It is well inside the bound $9m_0 = 4608$. Any $\lambda$ strictly between 1 and $\frac43$ works; $\frac65$ is BBHT's choice.

17 · amplitude amplification

Amplitude amplification

amplitude amplification (Brassard, Høyer, Mosca and Tapp 2002)

Let $A$ be a unitary (an algorithm with no measurement) and $\Pi$ the projection onto a good subspace, with $A\ket0 = \sin\theta\,\ket{\psi_g} + \cos\theta\,\ket{\psi_b}$ and success probability $p = \|\Pi A\ket0\|^2 = \sin^2\theta\in(0, 1)$. With $S_\chi = I - 2\Pi$ and $S_0 = I - 2\ket0\!\bra0$, the round

$$Q = -A\,S_0\,A^{-1}S_\chi$$

rotates $\mathrm{span}\{\ket{\psi_b}, \ket{\psi_g}\}$ by $2\theta$, and $Q^kA\ket0$ is good with probability $\sin^2((2k + 1)\theta)$. About $\frac\pi{4\sqrt p}$ rounds give success $\ge1 - p$; each runs $A$ and $A^{-1}$ once.

Proof. $-AS_0A^{-1} = A(2\ket0\!\bra0 - I)A^{-1} = 2\ket\psi\!\bra\psi - I$ with $\ket\psi = A\ket0$: the reflection about $A\ket0$, in the role of $D$. $S_\chi$ plays $O_f$. The rotation theorem used nothing else. $\blacksquare$

Check: $A = H^{\otimes n}$, $\Pi$ onto the marked items, is Grover's algorithm exactly ✓.

The same plane, relabelled: $A\ket0$ replaces $\ket s$.

Full proof. Define $\ket{\psi_g} = \Pi A\ket0/\|\Pi A\ket0\|$ and $\ket{\psi_b} = (I - \Pi)A\ket0/\|(I - \Pi)A\ket0\|$; they are orthonormal (one in the range of $\Pi$, one in its kernel), and $A\ket0 = \sin\theta\ket{\psi_g} + \cos\theta\ket{\psi_b}$ with $\sin\theta = \|\Pi A\ket0\|$. Now repeat sections 1 and 2 word for word: $S_\chi\ket{\psi_g} = -\ket{\psi_g}$ and $S_\chi\ket{\psi_b} = \ket{\psi_b}$; $2\ket\psi\!\bra\psi - I$ keeps $\ket\psi$ and negates its perpendicular in the plane; both preserve the plane and are real in the basis $(\ket{\psi_b}, \ket{\psi_g})$; so $Q$ is the rotation by $2\theta$ and $Q^kA\ket0$ sits at angle $(2k + 1)\theta$. The identity $A(2\ket0\!\bra0 - I)A^{-1} = 2A\ket0\!\bra0A^\dagger - I$ uses $A^{-1} = A^\dagger$. With $k^* = \lfloor\pi/4\theta\rfloor\le\pi/(4\sqrt p)$, section 3's theorem gives success $\ge\cos^2\theta = 1 - p$.

Where $A$ comes from. A classical randomised algorithm that succeeds with probability $p$ becomes such a unitary: replace its coins by fresh qubits put through $H$, so that each string of coin values has amplitude $2^{-r/2}$, and make its computation reversible (unit 10), keeping its garbage — no uncomputation is needed, because $A^{-1}$ undoes everything. $\Pi$ projects onto "the output register holds a solution", and $S_\chi$ is the phase oracle of the solution-checking predicate. So any classical or quantum search heuristic with a checkable answer gets a square-root speedup in its number of repetitions.

The sign. The $-1$ in $Q$ makes it exactly the rotation; without it, $Q$ changes by a global phase, which is harmless for amplification and not harmless under a control (the warning on What one iteration costs in gates). Unknown $p$ is handled exactly as unknown $M$: exponential search with $A$ in place of $H^{\otimes n}$.

Worked: amplifying a one-in-a-hundred sampler

$p = 1/100$

1. The angle. $\sin\theta = \sqrt p = 0.1$, so $\theta = \arcsin0.1 = 0.100167$ rad.

2. The count. $\pi/4\theta = 7.841$, so $k^* = 7$ rounds (nearest integer to $\pi/4\theta - \frac12 = 7.34$: also 7).

3. The probability. $(2k^* + 1)\theta = 15\theta = 1.50251$ rad, so $\sin^2(15\theta) = 0.99534$. Guarantee $1 - p = 0.99$ ✓.

4. The cost. $A$ once to start, then $A^{-1}$ and $A$ in each of 7 rounds: 15 runs of the sampler, and 7 calls to the checker.

5. Classically. 15 independent runs succeed with probability $1 - 0.99^{15} = 0.140$. Matching $0.9953$ needs $535$ runs; the first success takes $100$ on average.

Check: $k = 0$ gives $\sin^2\theta = 0.01 = p$ ✓.

Fifteen runs of the sampler, used coherently, against fifteen used one at a time: $0.995$ against $0.140$.

The numbers. $\theta = 0.1001674$; $\pi/4\theta = 7.84085$; $15\theta = 1.5025113$ rad, which is $0.06829$ short of $\pi/2$, so the success is $\cos^2(0.06829) = 0.995344$. At $k = 8$, $17\theta$ overshoots by $0.1320$ and the success falls to $0.98266$. Classically, $1 - 0.99^{534} = 0.99533 < 0.99534 < 0.99538 = 1 - 0.99^{535}$, so 535 runs.

The general trade. Classical repetition needs order $1/p$ runs; amplification needs order $1/\sqrt p$ runs of $A$ and $A^{-1}$. For $p = 1/100$: 15 against 535 at this success level. The price is coherence — the 15 runs must happen inside one quantum computation without being observed — and the requirement that $A$ be reversible.

17 · amplitude amplification

Quantum counting, stated

The eigenvalues. On the plane $G$ is a rotation by $2\theta$, so in the basis $(\ket{m^\perp}, \ket m)$ it has eigenvalues $e^{\pm2i\theta}$ with eigenvectors $\ket{g_\pm} = \frac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{m^\perp}\mp i\ket m)$, and

$$\ket s = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(e^{i\theta}\ket{g_+} + e^{-i\theta}\ket{g_-}\big),$$

with weights $\frac12 + \frac12 = 1$ ✓.

So an estimate of the eigenphase $\pm2\theta$ of $G$ is an estimate of $\theta$, hence of $M = N\sin^2\theta$.

quantum counting (Brassard, Høyer and Tapp 1998, cited)

Phase estimation on controlled powers of $G$, started on $\ket s$, estimates $M$ to relative error $\varepsilon$ with constant probability using $O\big(\sqrt{N/M}/\varepsilon\big)$ queries.

Phase estimation is built in unit 15; this unit does not need it.

A rotation by $2\theta$ has eigenvalues $e^{\pm2i\theta}$: reading either angle gives $\theta$.

Proof of the eigenvalues. The rotation matrix $R = \begin{pmatrix}\cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi\\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi\end{pmatrix}$ applied to $(1, -i)$ gives $(\cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi,\ \sin\varphi - i\cos\varphi) = e^{i\varphi}(1, -i)$, since $-ie^{i\varphi} = \sin\varphi - i\cos\varphi$; similarly $(1, i)$ has eigenvalue $e^{-i\varphi}$. With $\varphi = 2\theta$ these are $\ket{g_\pm}$. Both are complex vectors: this is the one place the plane must be complexified. Expansion of $\ket s$: $\braket{g_+|s} = \frac1{\sqrt2}(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta) = e^{i\theta}/\sqrt2$ (the bra conjugates $-i$ to $+i$), and $\braket{g_-|s} = e^{-i\theta}/\sqrt2$. Each eigenvector carries weight $\frac12$, and either eigenphase gives the same $\sin^2\theta$.

The cited result. G. Brassard, P. Høyer and A. Tapp, "Quantum counting", ICALP 1998, LNCS 1443, 820–831; the sharpest form is in Brassard, Høyer, Mosca and Tapp, "Quantum amplitude amplification and estimation", Contemporary Mathematics 305 (2002), 53–74, whose amplitude-estimation theorem gives, from $k$ applications of controlled-$G$, an estimate $\tilde M$ with $|\tilde M - M|\le2\pi\sqrt{M(N - M)}/k + \pi^2N/k^2$ with probability at least $8/\pi^2$. Problem P12 derives the $1/\varepsilon$ scaling from the error-propagation step, taking phase estimation's cost as given.

Forward pointer. Unit 15 builds phase estimation and lists quantum counting among its applications; the reason counting stays at $\sqrt N$ is that powers of $G$ have no shortcut — $G^{2^j}$ costs $2^j$ queries — unlike Shor's modular exponentiation.

17 · what it buys

Search over NP witnesses

Brute force, amplified. For a formula $\varphi$ on $n$ variables, $f(x) = \varphi(x)$ is a polynomial-size oracle (unit 10) on $N = 2^n$ items: Grover finds a satisfying assignment with $O(2^{n/2})$ queries.

still exponential

The square root of an exponential is an exponential. No NP-complete problem becomes polynomial this way, and unit 18 proves no black-box quantum algorithm beats $\sqrt N$.

Classical algorithms are not brute force. Schöning's random walk solves 3-SAT in $(4/3)^n$ up to polynomial factors (cited) — fewer than Grover's $2^{n/2}$ for large $n$, since $\frac43 < \sqrt2$.

The right use is amplitude amplification of the best classical algorithm: Schöning's walk, amplified, takes $(4/3)^{n/2}\approx1.155^n$.

3-SAT on 100 variables, polynomial factors ignored. A clever classical algorithm beats amplified brute force; amplifying the clever one beats both.

The numbers in the figure. $2^{100} = 1.27\times10^{30}$; $\frac\pi4\,2^{50} = 8.8\times10^{14}$; $(4/3)^{100} = 3.1\times10^{12}$; $(4/3)^{50} = 1.8\times10^6$. All ignore the polynomial factors in $n$, which matter at $n = 100$ but not to the comparison of the bases.

Schöning's algorithm, cited. Pick a uniformly random assignment; then $3n$ times, pick an unsatisfied clause and flip one of its three variables at random. For a satisfiable 3-CNF formula one such trial finds a satisfying assignment with probability at least $(3/4)^n$ up to a polynomial factor (U. Schöning, "A probabilistic algorithm for k-SAT and constraint satisfaction problems", FOCS 1999, 410–414). Repeating $(4/3)^n$ times gives the classical bound. Each trial is a sampler with $p\gtrsim(3/4)^n$ and a checkable output, so amplitude amplification needs about $(4/3)^{n/2}$ runs (noted by A. Ambainis, "Quantum search algorithms", SIGACT News 35(2), 2004).

In the language of unit 12, for readers who have taken it: nobody knows whether NP $\subseteq$ BQP, and relative to an oracle NP $\not\subseteq$ BQP (unit 18). Grover's algorithm is consistent with both: it is a black-box speedup, and NP-hardness is about the worst case over all instances, where no black-box method helps by more than a square root.

17 · what it buys

Key search: AES-128

exhaustive key search. Given two known plaintext–ciphertext pairs, $f(k) = 1$ iff $\mathrm{AES}_k(P_i) = C_i$ for both: an oracle on $N = 2^{128}$ keys with, almost surely, $M = 1$ (notes).

The count. $\frac\pi4\cdot2^{64} = 1.45\times10^{19}$ Grover iterations, each computing and uncomputing AES as a reversible circuit.

The time. At a hypothetical $10^9$ iterations per second: $459$ years — one after another, since iteration $j + 1$ acts on the output of iteration $j$.

parallelism buys only $\sqrt P$

Split the keys among $P$ machines: each needs $\frac\pi4\sqrt{N/P}$ iterations, and Zalka (1999, cited) proved no parallel algorithm does better. $10^6$ machines: $0.46$ years, a gain of $1000 = \sqrt{10^6}$, for $1000$ times the total work.

A million machines cut 459 years to 0.46: the depth, not the count, is the obstacle.

The numbers. $\frac\pi4\cdot2^{64} = 1.4488\times10^{19}$; at $10^9$ per second that is $1.4488\times10^{10}$ s $= 459.1$ years (Julian years of $365.25$ days). With $P = 10^6$: $\frac\pi4\sqrt{2^{128}/10^6} = 1.4488\times10^{16}$ iterations per machine, $0.459$ years, about 168 days; total over all machines $1.4488\times10^{22}$, a thousand times the single-machine count. A classical search of the same space, by contrast, parallelises perfectly: $P$ machines take $1/P$ of the time.

Why two pairs. Modelling AES as a random family of permutations, the expected number of wrong keys that also map $P$ to $C$ is $(2^{128} - 1)\cdot2^{-128}\approx1$; with two pairs it is about $2^{-128}$. So $f$ checks two pairs, at twice the cost, to make $M = 1$.

The real cost, cited. Each iteration is far more than one "step": a reversible AES circuit, computed and uncomputed, on thousands of logical qubits with error correction (units 22–24). M. Grassl, B. Langenberg, M. Roetteler and R. Steinwandt, "Applying Grover's algorithm to AES: quantum resource estimates", PQCrypto 2016, LNCS 9606, 29–43, give the gate counts and depths; the $10^9$ iterations per second above is generous by many orders of magnitude.

Zalka's theorem, cited. C. Zalka, "Grover's quantum searching algorithm is optimal", Phys. Rev. A 60, 2746 (1999): $P$ parallel quantum machines, or one machine making $P$ queries at once, need order $\sqrt{N/P}$ rounds. The splitting strategy attains it, so it is the best parallelisation there is.

post-quantum cryptography

NIST's report on post-quantum cryptography (NIST IR 8105, cited) lists AES as needing larger keys, and RSA and elliptic-curve schemes as no longer secure — which is why its post-quantum standards replace the public-key schemes and keep AES.

Shor is different

Factoring and discrete logarithms fall in polynomial time to Shor's algorithm (unit 16, cited here): no key length saves RSA or elliptic curves. Against those, the response is new mathematics; against Grover, a longer key.

What this unit bought, and where it stops

the toolkit
  • The Grover plane: $\ket s = \sin\theta\ket m + \cos\theta\ket{m^\perp}$, $\sin\theta = \sqrt{M/N}$, invariant under $O_f$ and $D$.
  • Two reflections make a rotation by twice the angle between their axes; so $G = D\,O_f$ rotates by $2\theta$ and $P_k = \sin^2((2k + 1)\theta)$.
  • The count $k^* = \lfloor\pi/4\theta\rfloor\le\frac\pi4\sqrt{N/M}$, with success $\ge1 - M/N$.
  • Overshoot: a sinusoid of period $\pi/2\theta$; more iterations can be worse.
  • Unknown $M$: the quarter lemma and exponential search, still $O(\sqrt{N/M})$.
  • Amplitude amplification: any reversible algorithm with success $p$, in $O(1/\sqrt p)$ runs.
