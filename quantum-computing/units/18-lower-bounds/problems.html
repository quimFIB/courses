Lower bounds: what quantum computers provably cannot do

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You prove \(\Omega(\sqrt N)\) for search by the hybrid argument — every line, from the query magnitudes to \(T\ge\sqrt N/6\) — and exactly \(N/2\) for parity by the polynomial method: the lower bound from symmetrisation and the \(N\) sign changes, the upper bound from Deutsch's algorithm on pairs. For \(N = 2^{20}\) that is at least \(171\) queries for search and exactly \(524\,288\) for parity.

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about five and a half hours. Work them on paper first. P3 and P6 are the checkpoint's two computations; P4 is its symmetrisation proof.

Conventions, as in the deck. The input is \(x = x_0x_1\cdots x_{N-1}\in\{0, 1\}^N\), with \(|x|\) its number of ones and \(e_w\) the input whose only \(1\) is at \(w\). It is reached through the bit oracle \(O_x\ket i\ket b = \ket i\ket{b\oplus x_i}\) or the phase oracle \(O_x^\pm\ket i = (-1)^{x_i}\ket i\); each call is one query, and both are \(I\) when \(x = 0^N\). An algorithm computes \(f\) with bounded error if it accepts with probability within \(\tfrac13\) of \(f(x)\) on every allowed \(x\). \(Q(f)\) and \(Q_E(f)\) are the bounded-error and exact quantum query complexities; \(D(f)\) and \(R(f)\) the deterministic and bounded-error randomised ones (unit 13). \(\deg f\) is the degree of \(f\)'s multilinear polynomial and \(\widetilde{\deg}(f)\) the least degree of a real polynomial within \(\tfrac13\) of \(f\) on the whole cube. Grover's success after \(k\) iterations with one of \(N\) marked is \(\sin^2((2k + 1)\theta)\), \(\sin\theta = N^{-1/2}\) (unit 17); for \(N = 2^{20}\), \(\theta = \arcsin2^{-10} = 9.7656\times10^{-4}\).

Two results are cited and may be used: \(\widetilde{\deg}(\mathrm{OR}_N)\ge c\sqrt N\) for an absolute constant \(c \gt 0\) (Nisan and Szegedy 1994; Paturi 1992), and \(D(f) = O(Q(f)^4)\) for total \(f\) (Aaronson, Ben-David, Kothari, Rao and Tal 2021).

Some solutions end with an optional check by the course's referee, qclib, run from the course directory with uv run python. Every solution is complete without it.

Polynomials and degree

P1 Degree of AND, OR and majority routine

exercises: the multilinear representation, its top coefficient, the exact-query corollary \(Q_E\ge\tfrac12\deg\)

Let \(\mathrm{AND}_N(x) = x_0x_1\cdots x_{N-1}\), \(\mathrm{OR}_N\) as in the deck, and \(\mathrm{MAJ}_N(x) = 1\) iff \(|x| \gt N/2\).

  1. Show that the coefficient of \(x_0x_1\cdots x_{N-1}\) in the multilinear polynomial of any \(f\) is \(c_{\rm top} = \sum_{a\in\{0,1\}^N}(-1)^{N-|a|}f(a)\).
  2. Compute \(c_{\rm top}\) for \(\mathrm{AND}_N\) and \(\mathrm{OR}_N\), and show that for \(\mathrm{MAJ}_N\) it is \(\pm\binom{N-1}{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\). Tabulate \(c_{\rm top}(\mathrm{MAJ}_N)\) for \(N = 1, \dots, 6\). Conclude that all three have degree \(N\).
  3. What does the polynomial method then say about exact quantum algorithms for each, and how does it compare with \(D = N\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a sum over the cube; part 2 is three numbers or formulas, each of which must be shown nonzero; part 3 is a lower bound on \(Q_E\). The existence half of the deck's theorem on multilinear polynomials is the only tool part 1 needs.

Rung 2

In \(\sum_af(a)\prod_{a_i = 1}x_i\prod_{a_i = 0}(1 - x_i)\), each product contributes to the top monomial exactly once, and each factor \((1 - x_i)\) contributes \(-x_i\) to it. For majority group the inputs by \(|a| = k\): there are \(\binom Nk\) of them, and you need a partial alternating sum of binomial coefficients.

Rung 3

First line: \(c_{\rm top} = \sum_af(a)(-1)^{\#\{i : a_i = 0\}}\). For majority, with \(m = \lfloor N/2\rfloor + 1\), \(c_{\rm top} = (-1)^N\sum_{k=m}^N(-1)^k\binom Nk\); prove \(\sum_{k=0}^j(-1)^k\binom Nk = (-1)^j\binom{N-1}j\) by induction on \(j\) with Pascal's rule.

Worked solution

Idea. The multilinear polynomial is written down explicitly by the existence formula, so its top coefficient can be read off; showing it is nonzero shows the degree is \(N\).

1. Expanding \(\prod_{a_i = 1}x_i\prod_{a_i = 0}(1 - x_i)\), the monomial \(x_0\cdots x_{N-1}\) arises only by taking \(-x_i\) from every factor \((1 - x_i)\), with coefficient \((-1)^{N - |a|}\). Summing over \(a\) with weights \(f(a)\) gives \(c_{\rm top}\). By uniqueness this is the coefficient in \(f\)'s polynomial.

2. AND: only \(a = 1^N\) has \(f(a) = 1\), and \(|a| = N\): \(c_{\rm top} = 1\). OR: every \(a\) but \(0^N\) contributes, so \(c_{\rm top} = \sum_a(-1)^{N-|a|} - (-1)^N\). The full sum is \(\prod_{i}(1 - 1) = 0\) for \(N\ge1\), so \(c_{\rm top} = (-1)^{N+1}\). MAJ: \(c_{\rm top} = \sum_{k\ge m}(-1)^{N-k}\binom Nk = (-1)^N\sum_{k=m}^N(-1)^k\binom Nk\), \(m = \lfloor N/2\rfloor + 1\). The identity: for \(j = 0\) both sides are \(1\); and \[ (-1)^j\tbinom{N-1}j + (-1)^{j+1}\tbinom N{j+1} = (-1)^{j+1}\Big[\tbinom N{j+1} - \tbinom{N-1}j\Big] = (-1)^{j+1}\tbinom{N-1}{j+1}. \] The full alternating sum is \(0\), so \(\sum_{k=m}^N(-1)^k\binom Nk = -(-1)^{m-1}\binom{N-1}{m-1}\), and \[ c_{\rm top}(\mathrm{MAJ}_N) = (-1)^{N+m}\binom{N-1}{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}\ne0 . \]

P2 Grover on four items, as a polynomial medium

exercises: the degree lemma on a real algorithm, normalisation as a polynomial identity, why OR needs a polynomial right at every layer

Take \(N = 4\) and an arbitrary input \(x\in\{0, 1\}^4\), with any number \(k = |x|\) of ones. The algorithm prepares \(\ket s = \tfrac12\sum_i\ket i\), applies \(O_x^\pm\) once, then the diffusion \(D = 2\ket s\!\bra s - I\), measures the index to get \(j\), queries \(x_j\), and accepts iff \(x_j = 1\).

  1. Show that before the measurement the amplitude of \(\ket j\) is \(\beta_j = \tfrac12(1 - k + 2x_j)\), a polynomial of degree \(1\) in \(x\). Check that \(\sum_j\beta_j^2 = 1\) as a polynomial identity in \(k\).
  2. Show that the acceptance probability is \(p(x) = \tfrac14k(3 - k)^2\), and reconcile its degree with the polynomial method.
  3. Evaluate \(p\) at \(k = 0, \dots, 4\). For which inputs is this a good algorithm for \(\mathrm{OR}_4\)? Check your values against unit 17's \(\sin^2 3\theta\).

Rung 1

Four degree-one polynomials, then a one-variable polynomial in \(k\), then five numbers. Everything depends on \(x\) only through \(x_j\) and \(k\).

Rung 2

Rung 3

First line: \(\bar a = \tfrac14\sum_i\tfrac12(1 - 2x_i) = \tfrac18(4 - 2k) = \tfrac{2 - k}4\), so \(\beta_j = \tfrac{2 - k}2 - \tfrac{1 - 2x_j}2\). For part 2 only the \(k\) marked \(j\) accept, each with \(\beta_j^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. One query, so the amplitudes have degree \(1\); the verification query is a second, so the acceptance probability may have degree up to \(4\). Writing them out shows what they do on inputs Grover was not tuned for.

1. \(\big(2\ket s\!\bra s - I\big)\sum_ia_i\ket i = \sum_i(2\bar a - a_i)\ket i\), since \(\bra s\sum_ia_i\ket i = \tfrac12\sum_ia_i = 2\bar a\), and \(2\ket s\cdot2\bar a\) has every amplitude \(2\cdot2\bar a\cdot\tfrac12 = 2\bar a\). With \(\bar a = (2 - k)/4\): \[ \beta_j = \frac{2 - k}2 - \frac{1 - 2x_j}2 = \frac{1 - k + 2x_j}2 , \] degree \(1\), as \(k = x_0 + x_1 + x_2 + x_3\) is. Marked positions have \(\beta = (3 - k)/2\), unmarked \((1 - k)/2\), so \[ \sum_j\beta_j^2 = \tfrac14\big[k(3 - k)^2 + (4 - k)(1 - k)^2\big] = \tfrac14\big[(9k - 6k^2 + k^3) + (4 - 9k + 6k^2 - k^3)\big] = 1 . \] Normalised for every \(k\) ✓.

2. P3 Parity takes exactly N/2 medium

exercises: Deutsch's algorithm on pairs, symmetrisation with sign changes, the two bounds meeting, a number

  1. Describe Deutsch's algorithm on pairs and prove that it computes \(\mathrm{PARITY}_N\) exactly with \(\lceil N/2\rceil\) queries.
  2. Prove that every algorithm computing \(\mathrm{PARITY}_N\) with error at most \(\tfrac13\) makes at least \(N/2\) queries.
  3. For \(N = 2^{20}\) and for \(N = 5\), give the quantum query count and the classical one, deterministic and randomised.
  4. The hybrid argument proves \(T\ge\sqrt N/6\) for parity too. Why can it not prove more, as used in the deck?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Two inequalities that meet: an algorithm (upper bound) and a degree argument (lower bound). Then two pairs of numbers, and one sentence about which inputs the hybrid argument compares.

Rung 2

Upper: put the index in an equal superposition of the two positions of one pair, the target in \(\ket-\), query once, and apply \(H\) to the low index qubit. Lower: take a polynomial of degree \(\le2T\) within \(\tfrac13\) of parity, symmetrise it, and look at the sign of \(q(k) - \tfrac12\) at each integer.

Rung 3

First line of the lower bound: let \(p\) have degree \(\le2T\) with \(|p - \mathrm{PARITY}_N|\le\tfrac13\); its symmetrisation \(q\) has \(\deg q\le2T\), \(q(k) - \tfrac12\le-\tfrac16\) for even \(k\) and \(\ge\tfrac16\) for odd \(k\), \(k = 0, \dots, N\).

Worked solution

Idea. Deutsch's algorithm reads the parity of two bits with one query; the polynomial method says nothing reads more than two bits' worth of parity per query, because parity's approximate degree is full.

1. Upper bound. For the pair \((x_{2p}, x_{2p+1})\): prepare the index in \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{2p} + \ket{2p+1})\) — the high index qubits hold \(p\), the lowest is put in \(\ket+\) — and the target in \(\ket-\). One query, by kickback, gives \[ \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big((-1)^{x_{2p}}\ket{2p} + (-1)^{x_{2p+1}}\ket{2p+1}\big)\ket- = (-1)^{x_{2p}}\ket p\otimes\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket0 + (-1)^{x_{2p}\oplus x_{2p+1}}\ket1\big)\otimes\ket- , \] and \(H\) on the lowest index qubit leaves \(\pm\ket p\ket{x_{2p}\oplus x_{2p+1}}\ket-\): it reads \(x_{2p}\oplus x_{2p+1}\) with certainty. Doing this for the \(\lfloor N/2\rfloor\) pairs, plus one classical query to the last bit when \(N\) is odd, and XOR-ing the answers gives \(\mathrm{PARITY}_N\) exactly with \(\lceil N/2\rceil\) queries.

2. Lower bound. A \(T\)-query algorithm with error \(\le\tfrac13\) accepts with a polynomial \(p\) of degree \(\le2T\) within \(\tfrac13\) of parity (the polynomial method). Symmetrise it: \(q\) has \(\deg q\le\deg p\le2T\), and \(q(k)\) is the average of \(p\) over the layer \(|x| = k\), on which parity equals \(k\bmod2\). So \(q(k) - \tfrac12\le-\tfrac16\) at even \(k\) and \(\ge\tfrac16\) at odd \(k\). Between each \(k\) and \(k + 1\), \(k = 0, \dots, N - 1\), the continuous \(q - \tfrac12\) changes sign, so it has a root there: \(N\) distinct roots. It is not the zero polynomial (it is \(\le-\tfrac16\) at \(0\)), so \(\deg q\ge N\). Hence \(2T\ge N\), \(T\ge N/2\), and since \(T\) is an integer, \(T\ge\lceil N/2\rceil\). With part 1: \(Q_E(\mathrm{PARITY}_N) = Q(\mathrm{PARITY}_N) = \lceil N/2\rceil\).

P4 Proof: symmetrisation medium

exercises: the Minsky–Papert theorem in full, counting permutations, a check on small cases

Let \(p = \sum_{|S|\le d}c_Sx_S\) be multilinear of degree \(d\) on \(\{0, 1\}^N\), \((\pi x)_i = x_{\pi(i)}\) for a permutation \(\pi\) of the positions, and \(p^{\rm sym}(x) = \frac1{N!}\sum_\pi p(\pi x)\).

  1. Show \(x_S(\pi x) = x_{\pi(S)}\), and deduce that \(p^{\rm sym} = \sum_{j\le d}a_jV_j\) with \(V_j = \sum_{|S| = j}x_S\) and \(a_j = \binom Nj^{-1}\sum_{|S| = j}c_S\).
  2. Show that \(V_j(x) = \binom{|x|}j\) on the cube, so \(p^{\rm sym}(x) = q(|x|)\) with \(q(k) = \sum_ja_j\binom kj\) of degree \(\le d\).
  3. Show that \(q(k)\) is the average of \(p\) over the layer \(\{x : |x| = k\}\).
  4. Check on \(N = 3\): \(p = x_0x_1\), and \(p = x_0 - x_1\). Can symmetrisation lower the degree?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A proof in four counting steps; the only facts needed are what a permutation does to a set of positions and how many permutations send one set to another.

Rung 2

For a fixed \(j\)-element set \(S\) and a fixed \(j\)-element set \(S'\), count the permutations \(\pi\) with \(\pi(S) = S'\): choose the bijection \(S\to S'\) and the bijection between the complements. For part 2, \(x_S(x) = 1\) iff every position in \(S\) holds a one.

Rung 3

First line: \(\#\{\pi : \pi(S) = S'\} = j!\,(N - j)!\), the same for every \(S'\) of size \(j\); so \(\frac1{N!}\sum_\pi x_{\pi(S)} = \frac{j!(N-j)!}{N!}V_j = \binom Nj^{-1}V_j\).

Worked solution

Idea. Averaging over all relabellings makes each coefficient the average of the coefficients of its size, and a symmetric multilinear polynomial is a combination of the elementary symmetric ones, which on the cube are binomial coefficients of \(|x|\).

1. \(x_S(\pi x) = \prod_{i\in S}(\pi x)_i = \prod_{i\in S}x_{\pi(i)} = \prod_{l\in\pi(S)}x_l = x_{\pi(S)}\). So \(p(\pi x) = \sum_Sc_Sx_{\pi(S)}\) and \[ p^{\rm sym} = \sum_Sc_S\cdot\frac1{N!}\sum_\pi x_{\pi(S)} . \] For \(|S| = j\) and any \(S'\) with \(|S'| = j\), the permutations with \(\pi(S) = S'\) are a bijection \(S\to S'\) together with a bijection of the complements: \(j!(N - j)!\) of them, independent of \(S'\). So \(\frac1{N!}\sum_\pi x_{\pi(S)} = \frac{j!(N-j)!}{N!}V_j = \binom Nj^{-1}V_j\), and collecting by size gives \(a_j = \binom Nj^{-1}\sum_{|S| = j}c_S\). Only \(j\le d\) occur.

2. On the cube \(x_S(x) = 1\) iff \(S\) lies inside the set of positions of the ones, which has \(|x|\) elements; so \(V_j(x)\) counts the \(j\)-subsets of a \(|x|\)-set: \(\binom{|x|}j = |x|(|x| - 1)\cdots(|x| - j + 1)/j!\), a polynomial of degree \(j\) in \(|x|\). Hence \(q(k) = \sum_{j\le d}a_j\binom kj\) has degree \(\le d\).

3. Fix \(x\) with \(|x| = k\). As \(\pi\) runs over all permutations, \(\pi x\) runs over the layer, hitting each \(y\) with \(|y| = k\) the same number of times, \(k!(N - k)!\) (the same count as in part 1, applied to the set of ones). So \(\frac1{N!}\sum_\pi p(\pi x)\) is the plain average of \(p\) over the \(\binom Nk\) points of the layer.

4. Checks. \(p = x_0x_1\): \(a_2 = \tfrac13(1 + 0 + 0) = \tfrac13\), so \(q(k) = \tfrac13\binom k2 = k(k - 1)/6\). At \(k = 2\) the layer is \(110, 101, 011\), where \(p = 1, 0, 0\), average \(\tfrac13\); and \(q(2) = \tfrac13\) ✓. At \(k = 3\): \(p = 1\), \(q(3) = 1\) ✓. \(p = x_0 - x_1\): \(a_1 = \tfrac13(1 - 1 + 0) = 0\), so \(q = 0\). Degree \(1\) went to degree \(0\): symmetrisation can lower the degree, never raise it — which is the direction the lower bounds need.

The hybrid argument

P5 Grover against the hybrid bound routine

exercises: query magnitudes of a real algorithm, the hybrid bound against the truth, a trigonometric inequality, numbers

  1. For Grover's algorithm on \(N\) items with \(T\) iterations, show that on the empty oracle every \(\ket{\psi_t}\) equals \(\ket s\), and that \(q_w = T/N\) for every \(w\).
  2. Show that the hybrid bound is \(2T/\sqrt N\) and that the true distance \(\lVert\ket{\psi_T^w} - \ket{\psi_T}\rVert\) is \(2\sin T\theta\). Prove \(|\sin T\theta|\le T\sin\theta\) for every integer \(T\ge0\), so the theorem holds here by trigonometry alone.
  3. For \(N = 2^{20}\) compute both at \(T = 804\) and at \(T = 1608\), and their ratios. Why does the bound get worse?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A state, a number per position, two formulas in \(T\), an inequality, and four numbers. All of it lives in unit 17's plane.

Rung 2

On the empty oracle each iteration is \(D\) alone, and \(D\ket s = \ket s\). On the marked oracle the state after \(T\) iterations is at angle \((2T + 1)\theta\) from \(\ket{w^\perp}\), and \(\ket s\) at angle \(\theta\): two unit vectors at angle \(\phi\) are \(2\sin(\phi/2)\) apart.

Rung 3

First line of the inequality: \(|\sin(T + 1)\theta| = |\sin T\theta\cos\theta + \cos T\theta\sin\theta|\le|\sin T\theta| + \sin\theta\), then induction from \(T = 0\).

Worked solution

Idea. Grover is the algorithm for which every step of the hybrid argument is as tight as it can be except the triangle inequality, so it shows exactly what the argument loses.

1. \(\ket{\psi_0} = H^{\otimes n}\ket{0^n} = \ket s\). With \(x = 0^N\) the oracle is \(I\), so each iteration is \(D = 2\ket s\!\bra s - I\), and \(D\ket s = 2\ket s - \ket s = \ket s\). So \(\ket{\psi_t} = \ket s\) for all \(t\), \(\lVert P_w\ket s\rVert^2 = 1/N\), and \(q_w = T/N\) for every \(w\): the query magnitudes are exactly even.

2. The bound: \(2\sum_t\lVert P_w\ket s\rVert = 2T/\sqrt N\) (and Cauchy–Schwarz is an equality, all terms being equal). The truth: \(\ket{\psi_T} = \ket s\) at angle \(\theta\), \(\ket{\psi_T^w} = G^T\ket s\) at angle \((2T + 1)\theta\), so the angle between them is \(2T\theta\) and the distance \(\sqrt{2 - 2\cos2T\theta} = 2\sin T\theta\) (for \(0\le T\theta\le\pi\)). Rung 1

The improvement comes from looking at amplitudes, of degree \(T\), instead of the acceptance probability, of degree \(2T\). The answer to part 2 is a one-line application of uniqueness.

Rung 2

On \(x\ne0^N\) the algorithm accepts with probability \(1\), so the rejecting part of the final state has norm \(0\). On \(0^N\) it rejects with probability \(1\), so some rejecting amplitude is nonzero there. A function on the cube that is \(0\) except at one point has a multilinear polynomial you can write down.

Rung 3

First line: \(1 - p(x) = \sum_{j\ \rm rejecting}|\alpha_j(x)|^2 = 0\) for \(x\ne0^N\), so each \(\alpha_j(x) = 0\) there; and the function \(c\cdot[x = 0^N]\) has the multilinear polynomial \(c\prod_i(1 - x_i)\).

Idea. Exactness pins every rejecting amplitude down at all but one input, and the only polynomial that vanishes everywhere but at \(0^N\) has full degree.

4. So, whatever happens off length \(n\): if \(M\) is wrong on 'none', it fails with probability \(\ge\Pr[0]\approx0.37\); if right, it fails on '\(\{w\}\)' for at least a fraction \(1 - 6T/\sqrt N\) of the \(w\), so with probability \(\ge\Pr[1](1 - 6T/\sqrt N)\). With \(T\le\mathrm{poly}(n)\) and \(\sqrt N = 2^{n/2}\), this is \(\ge0.3\) for all large \(n\). Choose \(n_1\lt n_2\lt\cdots\) with \(n_{j+1}\) longer than any string \(M\) can query on \(1^{n_j}\) (its running time bounds that). The event 'right at \(1^{n_j}\)' depends only on lengths below \(n_{j+1}\); so, conditioning on all lengths except \(n_k\), the chance of being right at \(1^{n_k}\) is \(\le0.7\) whatever the earlier events, and \(\Pr[\text{right at }1^{n_1}, \dots, 1^{n_k}]\le0.7^k\to0\). So \(M\) fails to decide \(L_A\) with probability \(1\). There are countably many polynomial-time quantum machines (uniform circuit families are described by finite programs), and a countable union of null events is null: with probability \(1\) no \(\mathsf{BQP}^A\) machine decides \(L_A\).

P12 Element distinctness medium

exercises: a query reduction and its cost in queries, the classical bound, cited results in their place, numbers

Element distinctness: given \(y = (y_0, \dots, y_{M-1})\) with entries in \(\{0, \dots, M - 1\}\), queried by \(\ket i\ket b\mapsto\ket i\ket{b\oplus y_i}\) on a register \(b\), decide whether all entries differ. Its quantum query complexity is \(\Theta(M^{2/3})\) (Ambainis 2004; Aaronson and Shi 2004, with the case of range as small as \(M\) completed by Ambainis 2005; cited).

  1. From \(x\in\{0, 1\}^N\) define \(y\in\{0, \dots, N\}^{N+1}\) by \(y_i = i + 1\) if \(x_i = 0\), \(y_i = 0\) if \(x_i = 1\) (\(i\lt N\)), and \(y_N = 0\). Show that \(y\) has distinct entries iff \(\mathrm{OR}_N(x) = 0\), and that one query to \(y\) costs two queries to \(x\).
  2. Deduce \(Q(\mathrm{ED}_{N+1})\ge\sqrt N/12\) from the deck's theorem, and \(R(\mathrm{ED}_{N+1})\ge N/3\) classically.
  3. For a million entries compare: the cited quantum count \(M^{2/3}\), Grover's \(\sqrt M\), the classical \(M\), and part 2's bound.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

A reduction: an algorithm for element distinctness becomes one for OR, at a stated cost per query. Then two lower bounds by substitution, and four numbers.

Rung 2

The only possible repeated value is \(0\), and \(y_N = 0\) always. To answer a query to \(y_i\) reversibly, fetch \(x_i\) into a fresh qubit, use it to write \(y_i\), and then clear the qubit — unit 10's garbage must not be left behind.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: the entries \(y_i = i + 1\) for \(x_i = 0\) are distinct and nonzero, so a repeat can only be two zeros, and \(y_N = 0\) is one of them; so \(y\) has a repeat iff some \(x_i = 1\).

Worked solution

Idea. Search hides inside element distinctness as one planted collision, so element distinctness is at least as hard as search — but the reduction cannot see the extra difficulty that makes the truth \(M^{2/3}\).

1. If \(x = 0^N\), \(y = (1, 2, \dots, N, 0)\): all distinct. If \(x_i = 1\), then \(y_i = 0 = y_N\): a repeat. Conversely the nonzero entries are distinct, so a repeat needs a second zero, i.e. some \(x_i = 1\). So \(\mathrm{ED}(y) = 1 - \mathrm{OR}_N(x)\). To apply \(\ket i\ket b\mapsto\ket i\ket{b\oplus y_i}\): for \(i\lt N\), query \(x_i\) into a fresh qubit \(a\) (one query), XOR \(i + 1\) into \(b\) controlled on \(a = 0\), then query again to return \(a\) to \(0\) (a second query). For \(i = N\), which is not a position of \(x\), pass the oracle any fixed position, say \(0\), and skip the write — controlling it on \(i\lt N\) as well, a classical test on the index that costs no query; the second query still clears \(a\). Leaving \(a\) holding \(x_i\) would entangle garbage with the index and spoil interference (unit 10), so the second query is needed. Two queries to \(x\) per query to \(y\).

2. A \(T\)-query bounded-error algorithm for \(\mathrm{ED}_{N+1}\), with its answer negated, computes \(\mathrm{OR}_N\) with \(2T\) queries and the same error; it is even correct under the promise "at most one \(1\)". The deck's theorem gives \(2T\ge\sqrt N/6\), so \(T\ge\sqrt N/12\). Classically no uncomputation is needed — one query to \(x_i\) gives \(y_i\) — so \(R(\mathrm{ED}_{N+1})\ge R(\mathrm{OR}_N)\ge N/3\), the deck overview's classical bound.
