Lower bounds: what quantum computers provably cannot do

Every speed-up so far fed on structure. Take the structure away and almost nothing is left: search needs order $\sqrt N$ queries, so Grover is optimal; parity needs exactly $N/2$; and for total functions the quantum count is never below a constant times the fourth root of the classical one.

needs 17, 12~3 h slides + 5½ h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 18

Units 13–17 built algorithms. Each one beat its classical rivals by exploiting something: Deutsch–Jozsa a promise about the function's shape, Grover nothing at all but the ability to interfere. This unit asks the opposite question — what can a quantum algorithm not do — and answers it in the one model where such statements can be proved, the query model of unit 13.

Two methods are proved in full. The hybrid argument (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani, SIAM J. Comput. 26, 1510, 1997) watches where an algorithm looks and flips a bit it hardly looks at. The polynomial method (Beals, Buhrman, Cleve, Mosca and de Wolf, J. ACM 48, 778, 2001) observes that after $T$ queries an acceptance probability is a polynomial of degree at most $2T$ in the input bits, and turns lower bounds into questions about degree. A third, the adversary method, is named and not developed.

the running example: OR and parity on $N$ bits

An input is a string $x = x_0x_1\cdots x_{N-1}$ of bits, reachable only by queries. Two questions about it:

$$\mathrm{OR}_N(x) = 1\iff\text{some }x_i = 1,$$ $$\mathrm{PARITY}_N(x) = x_0\oplus x_1\oplus\cdots\oplus x_{N-1}.$$

Classically each needs order $N$ queries. Quantumly, Grover answers OR with order $\sqrt N$ (unit 17), and Deutsch's algorithm on pairs answers parity with $N/2$ (unit 13).

Can anything quantum do better? No: OR needs order $\sqrt N$ — proved twice — and parity needs exactly $N/2$.

Why these two. They are the two extremes of the polynomial method. OR has full degree $N$ but approximate degree only order $\sqrt N$, which is exactly the room Grover uses. Parity has full degree and full approximate degree, so the quantum saving collapses to the factor of two Deutsch already had on two bits. Between them they show what each method can and cannot see.

The classical counts, proved. Deterministically both need $N$: an adversary answers $0$ to every query, and until the last bit is read, setting it to $0$ or $1$ changes both OR (the rest being $0$) and parity — unit 13's fooling pair. With coins, parity still needs $N$: a tree of depth below $N$ leaves some bit unread on every path, and under the uniform distribution that bit makes its answer a fair coin, so by unit 13's averaging some input is answered correctly with probability at most $\tfrac12$. OR needs at least $N/3$: take $x = 0^N$ with probability $\tfrac12$ and a single uniformly placed $1$ otherwise. A depth-$k$ tree run on $0^N$ follows one path, reading $k$ positions, and outputs some $a$. If $a = 1$ it is wrong on half the mass. If $a = 0$ it is right on a single-$1$ input only if the $1$ sits on that path, which has probability $k/N$. So its success is at most $\tfrac12 + \tfrac{k}{2N}$, and success $\tfrac23$ needs $k\ge N/3$.

The figure's numbers. $174$ is the hybrid argument's lower bound with its sharp constant (section 1); $804$ is unit 17's iteration count for near-certainty, and $490$ queries already reach success $\tfrac23$ when at most one bit is $1$; $N/2 = 524\,288$ for parity. The axis is logarithmic, so the gap between $174$ and $804$ looks small: it is a factor $4.6$.

18 · overview

What a lower bound has to beat

the input, through an oracle

The input is $x\in\{0, 1\}^N$, reached only through unit 13's oracle with $f(i) = x_i$:

$$O_x\ket i\ket b = \ket i\ket{b\oplus x_i},$$

the bit oracle, and by kickback the phase oracle $O_x^\pm\ket i = (-1)^{x_i}\ket i$. Each is one query. Both are the identity when $x = 0^N$.

a $T$-query algorithm (unit 13)

Fixed unitaries $U_0, \dots, U_T$ on index, target and work registers; on input $x$ it prepares

$$\ket{\psi_T^x} = U_T\,O_x\,U_{T-1}\cdots U_1\,O_x\,U_0\ket{0\cdots0}$$

and accepts if an output qubit reads $1$, with probability $p(x)$.

$\ket{\psi_t}$ is the state just before query $t + 1$, and $\ket{\psi_T}$ the final one. $O_x$ acts on index and target; the work register passes by.

bounded error, and the two counts

It computes $f$ with bounded error if $|p(x) - f(x)|\le\tfrac13$ for every $x$. $\ Q(f)$: the least such $T$; $\ Q_E(f)$: the least for $p = f$ exactly.

every algorithm at once

A lower bound must hold for every choice of $U_0, \dots, U_T$ — any size, any work register, measurements deferred to the end (unit 10). Only the number of oracle calls counts.

Matching unit 13. Unit 13's input was a function $f:\{0, 1\}^n\to\{0, 1\}$, equivalently its truth table of $N = 2^n$ bits. Here the truth table is the object: $x_i = f(i)$, and $O_x$ is unit 13's $U_f$. When $N$ is not a power of two, pad the index register and set the extra $x_i = 0$; nothing below changes. On the full space the oracle is $O_x\otimes I_{\text{work}}$, and the notation drops the $\otimes I$.

Bit or phase. Unit 13 built $O_x^\pm$ from one call to $O_x$ with the target in $\ket-$, and its problem sheet built $O_x$ back from a controlled $O_x^\pm$. Every bound below is proved for an oracle that is the identity on index values $i$ with $x_i = 0$ — true of both forms — so either may be used.

Why measurements can wait. Unit 10's deferred-measurement principle replaces a mid-circuit measurement and classically controlled gates by controlled gates and one measurement at the end, without changing any outcome probability. So every query algorithm, adaptive or not, has the form on the slide, and $p(x) = \lVert P_{\rm acc} \ket{\psi_T^x}\rVert^2$ for the projection $P_{\rm acc}$ onto "output qubit reads $1$" (unit 01's Born rule).

Notation. $Q(f)$ is unit 13's $Q_{1/3}(f)$. Unit 13 also proved $Q(f)\le R(f)\le D(f)$: a classical algorithm is a quantum one that happens to query basis states.

The hybrid argument

Run the algorithm on the empty input, find the position it hardly looks at, and mark that one: the final state barely moves.

18 · the hybrid argument

Where an algorithm looks

Run the algorithm on $x = 0^N$ — the empty oracle, which is $I$ — and call its states $\ket{\psi_0}, \dots, \ket{\psi_T}$. Let $P_w = \ket w\!\bra w$ on the index register (times $I$ elsewhere).

the query magnitude of position $w$ $$q_w = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert^2 :$$

how often the index would be found at $w$ just before a query, summed over the $T$ queries.

lemma

$\sum_w q_w = T$, so some $w$ has $q_w\le T/N$.

Two algorithms on the empty oracle, $N = 8$. A scan never looks at $3, \dots, 7$; Grover looks everywhere equally.

For OR. Grover's empty-oracle states are all $\ket s$, since $O = I$ and $D\ket s = \ket s$; so $\lVert P_w\ket s\rVert^2 = 1/N$ and $q_w = T/N$ for every $w$ exactly.

proof

The $P_w$ are orthogonal projections summing to $I$, so $\sum_w\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert^2 = 1$ for each $t$; add over the $T$ queries, and take the least term. $\blacksquare$

Reading $q_w$. Measuring the index register of $\ket{\psi_t}$ would give $w$ with probability $\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert^2$ (unit 01). The algorithm does not measure; $q_w$ is bookkeeping about the empty run, a number attached to each position saying how much the algorithm leans on it.

The two bar charts. A classical scan of positions $0, 1, 2$ is a quantum algorithm whose states are basis states: before query $t + 1$ the index is $\ket t$, and on the empty oracle every answer is $0$, so the scan proceeds to the next position. Hence $q_0 = q_1 = q_2 = 1$ and the rest are $0$, summing to $T = 3$. Grover with two iterations on $N = 8$ has $q_w = 2\cdot\tfrac18 = \tfrac14$ for each of the eight, summing to $T = 2$.

The quantum version of an unread bit. Unit 13's fooling pairs used a position a deterministic tree never reads: flipping it changes the answer and not the run. A quantum algorithm may read every position a little. The lemma says it cannot read every position much: there is a total budget of $T$, so some position gets at most $T/N$ of it. The next slides show that flipping a position with small $q_w$ changes the final state by little.

Why the empty input. For OR the two inputs to be told apart are $0^N$ and a single $1$. The empty run is the one the algorithm cannot avoid making, and it decides which positions are lightly watched — before anyone chooses where to put the $1$. That order of quantifiers is the whole argument.

18 · the hybrid argument

Marking one position moves the final state by little

Let $O_w$ be the oracle of the input $e_w$, whose only $1$ is at $w$, and $\ket{\psi_t^w}$ the algorithm's states on it.

the hybrid argument (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, Vazirani 1997) $$\big\lVert\ket{\psi_T^w} - \ket{\psi_T}\big\rVert\ \le\ 2\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert .$$

The final states differ by a sum of $T$ perturbations, one per query: the $t$-th is the error made by answering query $t + 1$ with $O_w$ instead of $I$, carried to the end by unitaries that keep its length.

The intermediate hybrids answer the first $k$ queries with $I$ and the rest with $O_w$. Consecutive hybrids differ in one query.

Grover on $N = 64$ for $T = 6$ iterations, in unit 17's plane. The hybrids are the Grover states $G^j\ket s$; the six perturbations are chords of length $2/\sqrt{64} = \tfrac14$. Polygon $1.5$, straight chord $1.366$.

The hybrids, precisely. Let $\ket{h_k}$ be the final state when queries $1, \dots, k$ are answered by $I$ and queries $k + 1, \dots, T$ by $O_w$. Then $\ket{h_0} = \ket{\psi_T^w}$ and $\ket{h_T} = \ket{\psi_T}$, and $\ket{h_t} - \ket{h_{t+1}}$ comes from a single query, number $t + 1$, answered two ways on the same incoming state $\ket{\psi_t}$: it equals $V(O_w - I)\ket{\psi_t}$ for the unitary $V$ that runs the rest of the $O_w$-algorithm. So $$\ket{\psi_T^w} - \ket{\psi_T} = \sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\big(\ket{h_t} - \ket{h_{t+1}}\big),\qquad \big\lVert\ket{h_t} - \ket{h_{t+1}}\big\rVert = \lVert(O_w - I)\ket{\psi_t}\rVert\le2\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert .$$ This is unit 11's Errors add along a circuit, replacing one gate at a time, applied to the oracle gates; the worked example that follows does the same telescoping by induction, every line.

The figure, computed. In Grover's case the incoming state is always $\ket s$, and $V$ is a power of $G = DO_w$, so $\ket{h_k} = G^{T-k}\ket s$: the hybrids are Grover's own iterates, at angles $(2j + 1)\theta$ from $\ket{w^\perp}$ with $\sin\theta = 1/\sqrt{64} = \tfrac18$, $\theta = 0.1253$. Consecutive iterates are $2\theta$ apart on the unit circle, a chord of $2\sin\theta = \tfrac14$, which is exactly $2\lVert P_w\ket s\rVert$: each perturbation meets its bound with equality. The six chords add to $1.5$; the straight chord from $\ket s$ to $G^6\ket s$ is $2\sin6\theta = 1.366$. The final success probability is $\sin^213\theta = 0.9966$, unit 17's peak at six iterations.

The name. A hybrid is a run that is part one algorithm, part the other. The technique is everywhere in cryptography under the same name: to show two experiments are indistinguishable, walk from one to the other one step at a time and bound each step.

18 · the hybrid argument · example

Worked: the key inequality, every line

$\big\lVert\ket{\psi_T^w} - \ket{\psi_T}\big\rVert\le2\sum_t\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert$, by induction on $t$

(a) The two runs start at the same $\ket{\psi_0} = \ket{\psi_0^w} = U_0\ket{0\cdots0}$, and then

$$\ket{\psi_{t+1}} = U_{t+1}\ket{\psi_t},\quad \ket{\psi_{t+1}^w} = U_{t+1}O_w\ket{\psi_t^w},$$

the empty oracle being $I$. Write $d_t = \lVert\ket{\psi_t^w} - \ket{\psi_t}\rVert$, so $d_0 = 0$.

(b) Subtract, and add and subtract $O_w\ket{\psi_t}$:

$$\begin{aligned}\ket{\psi_{t+1}^w} - \ket{\psi_{t+1}} = U_{t+1}\Big[&O_w\big(\ket{\psi_t^w} - \ket{\psi_t}\big)\\ &+ (O_w - I)\ket{\psi_t}\Big].\end{aligned}$$

(c) Take norms. $U_{t+1}$ and $O_w$ are unitary, so they keep lengths; by the triangle inequality

$$d_{t+1}\le d_t + \lVert(O_w - I)\ket{\psi_t}\rVert .$$

(d) The oracle only acts at $w$. $O_w$ is the identity on index values $i\ne w$, so $O_w(I - P_w) = I - P_w$, that is, $O_w - I = (O_w - I)P_w$. And $\lVert O_w - I\rVert\le\lVert O_w\rVert + \lVert I\rVert = 2$ in unit 11's operator norm. So

$$\lVert(O_w - I)\ket{\psi_t}\rVert\le2\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert .$$

(e) Unroll from $d_0 = 0$:

$$d_T\le2\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert .\qquad\blacksquare$$
check: Grover on four items, one iteration

$\ket{\psi_0} = \ket s$, $\lVert P_w\ket s\rVert = \tfrac12$: bound $1$. True: $\ket{\psi_1^w} = \ket w$ (unit 17), $\ket{\psi_1} = \ket s$, and $\lVert\ket w - \ket s\rVert^2 = 2 - 2\braket{w|s} = 1$. Equality ✓.

Step (d) for each oracle. For the phase oracle, $O_w^\pm = I - 2P_w$ and $O_w^\pm - I = -2P_w$ exactly, so the inequality is an equality. For the bit oracle, $O_w = P_w\otimes X + (I - P_w)\otimes I$ on index and target, so $O_w - I = P_w\otimes(X - I)$; the eigenvalues of $X - I$ are $0$ and $-2$, so its norm is $2$. In both cases the bound $2$ is attained, and the factor cannot be improved in general.

Step (b) in words. The difference after query $t + 1$ has two sources: the difference already present, which $O_w$ and $U_{t+1}$ carry forward without changing its length, and the new error from answering this query with $O_w$ rather than $I$ on the empty run's state. Nothing is lost in the first part and nothing gained; the second part is what the query magnitudes control.

The check in full. On $N = 4$ with $w$ marked, $\ket s = \tfrac12\sum_i\ket i$, and unit 17's one iteration $G = DO_w$ sends it to $\ket w$ exactly ($\theta = \pi/6$, $3\theta = \pi/2$). On the empty oracle $G = D$ fixes $\ket s$. So the distance is $\lVert\ket w - \ket s\rVert = \sqrt{2 - 2\cdot\tfrac12} = 1$, and the bound is $2\lVert P_w\ket s\rVert = 2\cdot\tfrac12 = 1$.

Cauchy–Schwarz turns T perturbations into 2T/√N

corollary

For every $w$, $\ \big\lVert\ket{\psi_T^w} - \ket{\psi_T}\big\rVert\le2\sqrt{T\,q_w}$. For a $w$ with $q_w\le T/N$:

$$\big\lVert\ket{\psi_T^w} - \ket{\psi_T}\big\rVert\le\frac{2T}{\sqrt N}.$$
proof

Cauchy–Schwarz in $\mathbb R^T$, on $(1, \dots, 1)$ and $a_t = \lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert$: $\ \sum_ta_t\le\sqrt T\sqrt{\sum_ta_t^2} = \sqrt{Tq_w}$. Then $q_w\le T/N$. $\blacksquare$

For Grover on $N = 2^{20}$. Every $a_t = 1/\sqrt N$, so Cauchy–Schwarz is an equality and the bound is $2T/1024$. The truth is the chord $2\sin T\theta$, $\ \theta = \arcsin2^{-10}$. At $T = 804$:

$$\text{bound } 1.570,\qquad\text{truth } 1.414 .$$

The bound meets the truth at $T = 0$ and $T = 1$, and is its tangent at $0$ to seven figures. Grover spreads its queries evenly, so only the bending of the arc is lost.

Where the square root comes from. The $T$ perturbations have lengths $2a_t$ with $\sum_ta_t^2 = q_w$. If the algorithm puts a total weight $q_w$ on position $w$, the best it can do to move the final state is to spread that weight evenly over the queries, and then the perturbations add up to $2\sqrt{Tq_w}$. With $q_w\le T/N$ this is $2T/\sqrt N$: the distance grows linearly in $T$ but with slope $1/\sqrt N$, so reaching a constant takes $T$ of order $\sqrt N$.

The numbers. $\theta = \arcsin2^{-10} = 9.7656\times10^{-4}$. The bound at $T = 804$ is $2\cdot804/1024 = 1.5703$. The truth: the empty run stays at $\ket s$ (angle $\theta$), the marked run reaches angle $(2T + 1)\theta$, and two unit vectors at angle $2T\theta$ are a chord $2\sin T\theta$ apart: $2\sin(0.78516) = 1.4139$. The ratio is $0.90$. The curve and the line agree to first order because $\sin T\theta\approx T\theta$ and $\theta\approx1/\sqrt N$; in fact $\sin T\theta\le T\sin\theta$ for all $T$, which is the bound, for Grover, proved by trigonometry alone.

An uneven algorithm. If the query weight on $w$ is concentrated in a few queries, Cauchy–Schwarz is lossy, and the true distance is smaller still. The corollary is a worst case over how the algorithm schedules its looks.

18 · the hybrid argument

Close states, close probabilities

lemma

Let $\ket\psi, \ket\varphi$ be unit vectors with $\lVert\ket\psi - \ket\varphi\rVert = \varepsilon$, and $P$ a projection, $p = \lVert P\ket\psi\rVert^2$, $p' = \lVert P\ket\varphi\rVert^2$. Then

$$|p - p'|\ \le\ \varepsilon\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2/4}\ \le\ \varepsilon .$$
proof

$R = 2P - I$ is Hermitian, $R^2 = I$, $\bra\psi R\ket\psi = 2p - 1$. The cross terms $\bra\psi R\ket\varphi - \bra\varphi R\ket\psi$ are imaginary, so

$$\begin{aligned}2(p - p') &= \mathrm{Re}\,\big\langle\psi - \varphi\big|R\big|\psi + \varphi\big\rangle\\ &\le\varepsilon\,\lVert\psi + \varphi\rVert = \varepsilon\sqrt{4 - \varepsilon^2},\end{aligned}$$

by Cauchy–Schwarz, $\lVert R\rVert = 1$, and the parallelogram law. Swap $\psi$ and $\varphi$. $\blacksquare$

Checks. $\varphi = -\psi$, a global phase: $\varepsilon = 2$, bound $0$ ✓. Orthogonal: $\varepsilon = \sqrt2$, bound $1$ ✓.

The extreme case, which attains the bound: accepting with probabilities $\tfrac23$ and $\tfrac13$ needs the states $\varepsilon\ge0.338$ apart.

Full proof. $R^2 = 4P^2 - 4P + I = I$ since $P^2 = P$, and $R^\dagger = R$, so $R$ is unitary with $\lVert R\rVert = 1$. $\bra\psi R\ket\psi = 2\bra\psi P\ket\psi - 1 = 2\lVert P\psi\rVert^2 - 1 = 2p - 1$, using $P = P^\dagger P$. Expand $$\big\langle\psi - \varphi\big|R\big|\psi + \varphi\big\rangle = \bra\psi R\ket\psi - \bra\varphi R\ket\varphi + \bra\psi R\ket\varphi - \bra\varphi R\ket\psi .$$ Since $R$ is Hermitian, $\bra\varphi R\ket\psi = \overline{\bra\psi R\ket\varphi}$, so the last two terms are $z - \bar z = 2i\,\mathrm{Im}\,z$, purely imaginary. The real part is therefore $(2p - 1) - (2p' - 1) = 2(p - p')$. Cauchy–Schwarz (unit 00) bounds it by $\lVert\psi - \varphi\rVert\,\lVert R(\psi + \varphi)\rVert = \varepsilon\lVert\psi + \varphi\rVert$, and for unit vectors $\lVert\psi + \varphi\rVert^2 + \lVert\psi - \varphi\rVert^2 = 4$. Exchanging the roles gives the bound on $p' - p$.

Attained. In the figure $\ket\psi$ and $\ket\varphi$ are real, at angles $\alpha$ and $\beta$ from the accept axis with $\alpha + \beta = \pi/2$ and $\sin(\beta - \alpha) = \tfrac13$. Then $p = \cos^2\alpha = \tfrac12(1 + \sin(\beta - \alpha)) = \tfrac23$, $p' = \tfrac13$, and $\varepsilon = 2\sin\tfrac{\beta - \alpha}2 = 0.3382$; and $0.3382\sqrt{1 - 0.3382^2/4} = 0.3333$.

Phases. $\lVert\psi - \varphi\rVert$ depends on the representatives chosen; the lemma holds for every choice, so one may take the phase that makes $\varepsilon$ smallest. Unit 11's distance up to phase bounded outcome probabilities by $2d$ for operators; this is the sharper statement for two states. Unit 21 will recognise $\varepsilon\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2/4}$, minimised over phases, as the trace distance of two pure states.

Search needs order √N queries

theorem (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, Vazirani 1997)

Every algorithm computing $\mathrm{OR}_N$ with error at most $\tfrac13$ makes $T\ge\sqrt N/6$ queries — even if promised that at most one bit is $1$.

proof

Take $w$ with $q_w\le T/N$. The algorithm accepts $0^N$ with probability $p(0^N)\le\tfrac13$ and $e_w$ with $p(e_w)\ge\tfrac23$. By the lemma and the corollary,

$$\begin{aligned}\tfrac13\le p(e_w) - p(0^N)&\le\big\lVert\ket{\psi_T^w} - \ket{\psi_T}\big\rVert\\ &\le2T/\sqrt N .\qquad\blacksquare\end{aligned}$$

Search is no easier: an algorithm that finds a marked $w$ gives OR with one more query, to check it.

the numbers for $N = 2^{20}$

Simple constant: $T\ge1024/6 = 170.7$, so $T\ge171$.

Sharp lemma: $\tfrac13\le\varepsilon\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2/4}$ forces $\varepsilon^2\ge2 - \tfrac{4\sqrt2}3$, $\ \varepsilon\ge0.3382$, so $T\ge0.1691\sqrt N = 173.2$: $\ T\ge174$.

Grover: $489$ iterations give $\sin^2(979\,\theta) = 0.6674\ge\tfrac23$; with the check, $490$ queries. Near-certainty takes $804$.

Grover is optimal

Up to a constant factor: between $174$ and $490$ queries at $N = 2^{20}$ for this promise problem. No quantum algorithm searches an unstructured space faster than order $\sqrt N$.

The order of choices. The algorithm is fixed first; its empty run fixes the $q_w$; then $w$ is chosen with $q_w\le T/N$. The algorithm must be right on every input, in particular on $0^N$ and on $e_w$, and those two are the only inputs the proof uses — which is why the bound holds under the promise "at most one $1$".

The sharp constant. $g(\varepsilon) = \varepsilon\sqrt{1 - \varepsilon^2/4}$ increases on $[0, \sqrt2]$, so $g(\varepsilon)\ge\tfrac13$ needs $\varepsilon\ge\varepsilon_*$ with $\varepsilon_*^2(1 - \varepsilon_*^2/4) = \tfrac19$: a quadratic in $\varepsilon_*^2$ whose smaller root is $2 - 2\sqrt{8/9} = 2 - 4\sqrt2/3 = 0.11438$, so $\varepsilon_* = 0.33820$ and $T\ge\tfrac12\varepsilon_*\sqrt N = 0.16910\sqrt N$. Problem P6 derives it in full.

Grover's side. $\theta = \arcsin2^{-10}$; success after $k$ iterations is $\sin^2((2k + 1)\theta)$ (unit 17). $k = 488$ gives $0.6655$, $k = 489$ gives $0.6674$. Measure, then query the result and accept iff it is marked: on $0^N$ this never accepts, on $e_w$ it accepts with probability $0.6674$. So $490$ queries suffice under the promise. The lower and upper bounds differ by a factor $2.8$.

Exact optimality (cited): Zalka (Phys. Rev. A 60, 2746, 1999) proved that Grover's iteration count is optimal to leading order, not merely up to a constant: no algorithm reaches success near $1$ with fewer than $(\pi/4)\sqrt N(1 - o(1))$ queries. The proof sharpens exactly the two steps above — the triangle inequality is replaced by tracking angles.

section 2

Polynomials

After $T$ queries, every amplitude is a polynomial of degree at most $T$ in the input bits. Lower bounds become questions about degree.

18 · polynomials

Every function on the cube is one polynomial

theorem: the multilinear polynomial of a function

Every $f:\{0, 1\}^N\to\mathbb R$ equals $\ p(x) = \sum_{S\subseteq\{0, \dots, N-1\}}c_S\,x_S,\ \ x_S = \prod_{i\in S}x_i$, for unique real $c_S$. Its degree $\deg f$ is the largest $|S|$ with $c_S\ne0$.

proof

Exists: $\ p(x) = \sum_af(a)\prod_{a_i = 1}x_i\prod_{a_i = 0}(1 - x_i)$; each product is $1$ at $x = a$ and $0$ at every other point of the cube. Unique: if a nonzero multilinear $r$ vanishes on the cube, take $S$ minimal with $c_S\ne0$: then $r(1_S) = c_S$, which is not $0$. $\blacksquare$

The running example. $\mathrm{OR}_N = 1 - \prod_i(1 - x_i)$, top coefficient $(-1)^{N+1}$; $\mathrm{PARITY}_N = \tfrac12\big(1 - \prod_i(1 - 2x_i)\big)$, as $\prod_i(1 - 2x_i) = (-1)^{|x|}$, top coefficient $(-1)^{N+1}2^{N-1}$. Both have degree $N$.

Check, $N = 2$: $\mathrm{OR}_2 = x_0 + x_1 - x_0x_1$ and $\mathrm{PARITY}_2 = x_0 + x_1 - 2x_0x_1$; at $11$ they give $1$ and $0$ ✓.

$\mathrm{OR}_3$ on the cube, $x_0$ running into the page. Its polynomial is $x_0 + x_1 + x_2 - x_0x_1 - x_0x_2 - x_1x_2 + x_0x_1x_2$.

Multilinear. On the cube $x_i^2 = x_i$, so any polynomial in $x_0, \dots, x_{N-1}$ can be reduced, without changing its values there, to one in which no variable appears squared: replace every $x_i^k$ by $x_i$. This multilinearisation never raises the degree. The theorem says the result is unique, so "the degree of $f$" is well defined. There are $2^N$ monomials $x_S$ and $2^N$ points, and the theorem says the monomials form a basis of the functions on the cube.

The top coefficient. Reading off the existence formula, the coefficient of $x_0x_1\cdots x_{N-1}$ is $c_{\rm top} = \sum_af(a)(-1)^{N - |a|}$: each factor $(1 - x_i)$ contributes $-x_i$. For OR this is $-(-1)^N$ (only $a = 0^N$ has $f = 0$, and $\sum_a(-1)^{N-|a|} = 0$). For parity it is $\sum_{|a|\ \rm odd}(-1)^{N-|a|} = (-1)^{N+1}2^{N-1}$. Both are nonzero, so both functions have degree exactly $N$.

The parity formula. $1 - 2x_i$ is $1$ when $x_i = 0$ and $-1$ when $x_i = 1$, so the product is $(-1)^{|x|}$, with $|x|$ the number of ones; and $\tfrac12(1 - (-1)^{|x|})$ is $0$ or $1$ as $|x|$ is even or odd. This is the phase oracle's sign, $(-1)^{x_i} = 1 - 2x_i$, multiplied over all positions.

A query raises the degree by at most one

lemma

After $t$ queries, every amplitude of $\ket{\psi_t^x}$ is a complex multilinear polynomial in $x_0, \dots, x_{N-1}$ of degree at most $t$.

proof, by induction on $t$

$t = 0$: $U_0\ket{0\cdots0}$ does not depend on $x$; degree $0$.

A query. $O_x$ sends $\ket{i, b, w}$ to $\ket{i, b\oplus x_i, w}$, so the new amplitude of $\ket{i, b, w}$ is the old one of $\ket{i, b\oplus x_i, w}$:

$$\alpha'_{i,b,w} = (1 - x_i)\,\alpha_{i,b,w} + x_i\,\alpha_{i,1-b,w},$$

degree up by at most one; multilinearise. With $O_x^\pm$: $\alpha'_{i,w} = (1 - 2x_i)\alpha_{i,w}$, the same.

A unitary $U_t$. Each new amplitude is a fixed linear combination of old ones: degree unchanged. $\blacksquare$

The bound on the degree, step by step, for $T = 2$. Only the oracle can raise it, and by one each time.

The identity used. $(1 - x_i)a + x_ib$ is $a$ when $x_i = 0$ and $b$ when $x_i = 1$: a case split written as a polynomial of degree one.

Complex coefficients. The $U_t$ have complex entries, so the amplitudes are polynomials with complex coefficients; their real and imaginary parts are real polynomials of the same or lower degree. That is all the next slide needs.

Why a unitary cannot raise the degree. $U_t$ does not depend on $x$. Its new amplitudes are $\alpha'_j = \sum_k(U_t)_{jk}\alpha_k$ with constant coefficients, and a constant combination of polynomials of degree at most $t$ has degree at most $t$.

Why the query raises it by one. Multiplying a degree-$t$ polynomial by $x_i$ or by $1 - x_i$ gives degree at most $t + 1$. Multilinearising afterwards — replacing $x_i^2$ by $x_i$ where $x_i$ already appeared — keeps the values on the cube and never raises the degree. Intermediate measurements have been deferred (unit 10), so there is nothing else in the circuit.

The point of view. A classical $T$-query decision tree computes a function of degree at most $T$: summing, over the accepting leaves, the product of $x_i$ or $1 - x_i$ along each path. The lemma says a quantum algorithm's amplitudes are no worse; squaring them for probabilities is where the factor $2$ will enter.

The polynomial method

theorem: the polynomial method (Beals, Buhrman, Cleve, Mosca, de Wolf 2001)

After $T$ queries the acceptance probability $p(x)$ is a real multilinear polynomial of degree at most $2T$.

proof

$p(x) = \sum_{\rm accepting\ j}|\alpha_j(x)|^2 = \sum_j(\mathrm{Re}\,\alpha_j)^2 + (\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha_j)^2$, and $\mathrm{Re}\,\alpha_j, \mathrm{Im}\,\alpha_j$ are real polynomials of degree $\le T$. Multilinearise. $\blacksquare$

approximate degree

$\widetilde{\deg}(f)$: the least degree of a real polynomial $p$ with $|p(x) - f(x)|\le\tfrac13$ at every $x\in\{0, 1\}^N$.

corollary $$Q_E(f)\ge\tfrac12\deg f,\qquad Q(f)\ge\tfrac12\widetilde{\deg}(f).$$

Exact: $p = f$ on the cube, so $p$ is $f$'s polynomial, by uniqueness. Bounded error: $p$ is within $\tfrac13$ of $f$.

The running example. OR and parity have degree $N$: an exact algorithm for either needs $N/2$ queries. With bounded error, what matters is $\widetilde{\deg}$ — section 3 for OR, section 4 for parity.

degree and approximate degree differ

OR has degree $N$ but approximate degree of order $\sqrt N$. Exact and bounded-error query complexity can be far apart, and so can the two degrees.

Full proof. Let $\alpha_j(x) = r_j(x) + i\,s_j(x)$ with $r_j, s_j$ real polynomials of degree at most $T$ — the real and imaginary parts of the coefficients of $\alpha_j$. Then $|\alpha_j|^2 = r_j^2 + s_j^2$, a real polynomial of degree at most $2T$; the sum over the accepting basis states $j$ is too; and reducing $x_i^2\to x_i$ gives a multilinear one with the same values on the cube. That polynomial is $p$.

The corollaries. If the algorithm is exact, $p(x) = f(x)$ for every $x$, so by the uniqueness of the multilinear representation the multilinearised $p$ is $f$'s polynomial, of degree $\deg f\le2T$. If it has bounded error, $p$ is a polynomial of degree at most $2T$ within $\tfrac13$ of $f$, so $\widetilde{\deg}(f)\le2T$.

Why $\tfrac13$ is not special. Any constant error below $\tfrac12$ gives the same approximate degree up to a constant factor: amplification by majority vote (unit 12) is a polynomial in $p$ of constant degree, so composing raises the degree by a constant factor. The definition fixes $\tfrac13$ to match $Q(f)$.

History. Minsky and Papert (Perceptrons, 1969) used polynomial degree to bound what perceptrons can compute; Nisan and Szegedy (1994) studied degree and approximate degree as complexity measures; Beals, Buhrman, Cleve, Mosca and de Wolf (FOCS 1998, J. ACM 48, 778, 2001) saw that quantum query algorithms produce exactly such polynomials.

18 · symmetrisation

Averaging over the order of the bits

For a permutation $\pi$ of the positions, $(\pi x)_i = x_{\pi(i)}$. Write $|x|$ for the number of ones.

symmetrisation (Minsky and Papert 1969)

Let $p$ be multilinear of degree $d$ and $p^{\rm sym}(x) = \frac1{N!}\sum_\pi p(\pi x)$. There is a one-variable polynomial $q$ of degree $\le d$ with

$$p^{\rm sym}(x) = q(|x|)\quad\text{on the cube,}$$

and $q(k)$ is the average of $p$ over the layer $\{x : |x| = k\}$.

proof

$p^{\rm sym}$ is symmetric, so the coefficient of $x_S$ depends only on $|S|$: $\ p^{\rm sym} = \sum_{j\le d}a_jV_j$, with $V_j = \sum_{|S| = j}x_S$. On the cube $V_j(x) = \binom{|x|}{j}$, a polynomial of degree $j$ in $|x|$. And $\pi x$ runs evenly over $x$'s layer. $\blacksquare$

The layers $|x| = 0, 1, 2, 3$ of the cube, by colour. A symmetric function is constant on each layer. The numbers are $p = \mathrm{OR}(x_0, x_1)$, the next example.

For OR. OR is constant on layers. So if $p$ is within $\tfrac13$ of OR everywhere, each layer average is too: $p^{\rm sym}$ approximates OR as well as $p$ does, with no larger degree.

Full proof. Write $p = \sum_{|S|\le d}c_Sx_S$. Since $x_S(\pi x) = \prod_{i\in S}x_{\pi(i)} = x_{\pi(S)}$, each $p\circ\pi = \sum_Sc_Sx_{\pi(S)}$ is multilinear of degree $d$, and so is their average. For a fixed $S$ with $|S| = j$, as $\pi$ runs over all $N!$ permutations, $\pi(S)$ hits each $j$-element set exactly $j!(N - j)!$ times. Hence $$p^{\rm sym} = \sum_Sc_S\frac{j!(N-j)!}{N!}V_{|S|} = \sum_{j=0}^da_jV_j,\qquad a_j = \binom Nj^{-1}\sum_{|S| = j}c_S .$$ On the cube, $V_j(x)$ counts the $j$-element subsets of the positions of the ones, so $V_j(x) = \binom{|x|}j = |x|(|x| - 1)\cdots(|x| - j + 1)/j!$, a polynomial of degree $j$ in $|x|$. Set $q(k) = \sum_{j\le d}a_j\binom kj$.

The layer average. For fixed $x$ with $|x| = k$, the map $\pi\mapsto\pi x$ hits each $y$ with $|y| = k$ exactly $k!(N - k)!$ times, so $\frac1{N!}\sum_\pi p(\pi x)$ is the plain average of $p$ over the layer.

As an algorithm. Symmetrising is what happens if the algorithm first relabels the positions by a uniformly random permutation (shared randomness, free in the query model) and then runs. Its acceptance probability becomes $p^{\rm sym}$. So for a function that does not care about the order of the bits, there is no loss in assuming the algorithm does not either.

History. Minsky and Papert proved this in Perceptrons (MIT Press, 1969) to show that a perceptron computing parity needs a unit looking at all $N$ inputs — the same fact as section 4's, in another model.

$N = 3$: two polynomials, symmetrised and checked

(a) $\mathrm{OR}_3$ itself. Its polynomial is already symmetric:

With $V_j = \binom kj$ on the layer $|x| = k$:

$$q(k) = k - \binom k2 + \binom k3 = \frac{k^3 - 6k^2 + 11k}6 .$$

Check: $q(0) = 0$; $\ q(1) = \tfrac{1 - 6 + 11}6 = 1$; $\ q(2) = \tfrac{8 - 24 + 22}6 = 1$; $\ q(3) = \tfrac{27 - 54 + 33}6 = 1$ ✓. Degree $3 = N$, as it must be.

(b) $p = \mathrm{OR}(x_0, x_1) = x_0 + x_1 - x_0x_1$, which ignores $x_2$. Not symmetric, and not an approximation of $\mathrm{OR}_3$: $p(001) = 0$.

Coefficients averaged by size: $a_1 = \tfrac{1 + 1 + 0}3 = \tfrac23$, $\ a_2 = \tfrac{-1 + 0 + 0}3 = -\tfrac13$. So

$$q(k) = \tfrac23k - \tfrac13\binom k2 = \frac{k(5 - k)}6 .$$

Check by layer averages: $|x| = 1$: $p(100), p(010), p(001) = 1, 1, 0$, average $\tfrac23 = q(1)$ ✓; $|x| = 2$: $p = 1, 1, 1$, average $1 = q(2)$ ✓; $q(0) = 0$, $q(3) = 1$ ✓.

Read it: $q(k)$ is the chance that two random positions contain a $1$. It is within $\tfrac13$ of OR at every layer — a degree-$2$ approximation, though $p$ was none.

(a) expanded. $1 - (1 - x_0)(1 - x_1)(1 - x_2) = x_0 + x_1 + x_2 - x_0x_1 - x_0x_2 - x_1x_2 + x_0x_1x_2$, which is $V_1 - V_2 + V_3$. On a layer of weight $k$, $V_1 = k$, $V_2 = \binom k2$, $V_3 = \binom k3$. Multiplying out: $k - \tfrac{k(k-1)}2 + \tfrac{k(k-1)(k-2)}6 = \tfrac{6k - 3k^2 + 3k + k^3 - 3k^2 + 2k}6 = \tfrac{k^3 - 6k^2 + 11k}6$.

(b) in full. The degree-one coefficients of $p$ are $c_{\{0\}} = c_{\{1\}} = 1$, $c_{\{2\}} = 0$, averaged over the three singletons: $\tfrac23$. The degree-two ones are $c_{\{0,1\}} = -1$ and $0$ for the other two pairs: average $-\tfrac13$. Then $q(k) = \tfrac23k - \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac{k(k-1)}2 = \tfrac{4k - k^2 + k}6 = \tfrac{k(5 - k)}6$.

The algorithm behind (b). $p$ is the acceptance probability of the classical two-query algorithm "read $x_0$ and $x_1$, accept if either is $1$". Symmetrising it gives "read two random positions": with one $1$ among three positions it is caught with probability $\tfrac23$, exactly at the edge of bounded error. OR, approximated by a curve

Let $p$ approximate $\mathrm{OR}_N$ within $\tfrac13$, and $q$ its symmetrisation. Then $\deg q\le\deg p$ and

$$q(0)\in\big[-\tfrac13, \tfrac13\big],\quad q(k)\in\big[\tfrac23, \tfrac43\big]\ (1\le k\le N).$$

Conversely any such $q$ gives $P(x) = q(x_0 + \cdots + x_{N-1})$, of degree $\le\deg q$, within $\tfrac13$ of OR. So

a question about one variable

$\widetilde{\deg}(\mathrm{OR}_N)$ is the least degree of a one-variable polynomial that stays below $\tfrac13$ at $0$ and in $[\tfrac23, \tfrac43]$ at $1, 2, \dots, N$.

$N = 64$, computed by linear programming: degree $5$ cannot (best error $0.345$); degree $6$ can (error $0.297$). So $\widetilde{\deg}(\mathrm{OR}_{64}) = 6$, and every bounded-error algorithm for $\mathrm{OR}_{64}$ makes at least $3$ queries.

The best degree-$6$ approximation of $\mathrm{OR}_{64}$, as $q(k)$. Only the integer points are constrained. It must climb from below $\tfrac13$ to above $\tfrac23$ in one step, then stay in a band for $63$ more.

Why both directions. Symmetrising does not raise the degree and keeps the approximation (Averaging over the order of the bits). Conversely, $x_0 + \cdots + x_{N-1}$ has degree $1$, so substituting it into $q$ gives a polynomial in $x$ of degree at most $\deg q$, whose value at $x$ is $q(|x|)$. So the least degree over $N$-variable approximations equals the least over one-variable ones.

The computation. For each $d$, minimise $\varepsilon$ subject to $|q(k) - \mathrm{OR}(k)|\le\varepsilon$ at $k = 0, \dots, 64$, over polynomials $q$ of degree $d$ — a linear programme in $q$'s coefficients (written in the Chebyshev basis on $[0, 64]$, for numerical stability). The optimum is $0.3449$ at $d = 5$ and $0.2965$ at $d = 6$. The figure's polynomial has $q(0) = 0.2965$ and $q(k)$ between $0.7035$ and $1.2965$ on $k = 1, \dots, 64$, touching both edges of its own band, as a best approximation does.

Against Grover, and against the hybrid argument. Unit 17's six-qubit check: $N = 64$, one marked item, peak at $6$ iterations — under the promise of exactly one $1$; an algorithm right on every input needs more. The lower bound from degree is $3$. The approximate degree of OR is order √N

theorem (Nisan and Szegedy 1994; Paturi 1992) — cited $$\widetilde{\deg}(\mathrm{OR}_N) = \Theta(\sqrt N).$$

Why √N. $q$ climbs by $\ge\tfrac13$ between $k = 0$ and $1$, so $q'\ge\tfrac13$ somewhere there; yet it stays in a band of height $\tfrac53$ along $[0, N]$. The Markov brothers' inequality (cited): a degree-$d$ polynomial with $|q|\le1$ on $[-1, 1]$ has $|q'|\le d^2$ there. Rescaled to $[0, N]$, $|q'|\le\tfrac53d^2/N$, so $d^2\ge N/5$.

The missing step — that a band at the integers forces one on the interval when $d\ll\sqrt N$ — is Ehlich–Zeller (1964) and Rivlin–Cheney (1966), cited.

Grover is optimal, a second time

$Q(\mathrm{OR}_N)\ge\tfrac12\widetilde{\deg}(\mathrm{OR}_N) = \Omega(\sqrt N)$. And conversely: unit 17's random iteration counts give a bounded-error algorithm with $O(\sqrt N)$ queries, so $\widetilde{\deg}(\mathrm{OR}_N)\le2Q = O(\sqrt N)$.

$\widetilde{\deg}(\mathrm{OR}_N)$ computed exactly, by the linear programme of OR, approximated by a curve, for $N = 4, \dots, 1024$: close to $0.7\sqrt N$.

The sketch in full. By the mean value theorem there is $\xi\in(0, 1)$ with $q'(\xi) = q(1) - q(0)\ge\tfrac23 - \tfrac13 = \tfrac13$. Suppose $-\tfrac13\le q\le\tfrac43$ on the whole interval $[0, N]$, so $|q - \tfrac12|\le\tfrac56$. Put $t = 2k/N - 1$, mapping $[0, N]$ to $[-1, 1]$, and $r(t) = \tfrac65(q(k) - \tfrac12)$, so $|r|\le1$. Markov gives $|r'(t)|\le d^2$, and $q'(k) = \tfrac56\cdot\tfrac2N\,r'(t)$, so $|q'|\le\tfrac53d^2/N$. Then $\tfrac13\le\tfrac53d^2/N$, i.e. $d\ge\sqrt{N/5} = 0.447\sqrt N$. The computed values, about $0.7\sqrt N$, respect it.

What is cited. The band is only promised at the integers $0, \dots, N$; between them $q$ could in principle swing far out. The lemma of Ehlich and Zeller (1964) and Rivlin and Cheney (1966) says a polynomial of degree $d\le c\sqrt N$ bounded at the integers is bounded on the interval by a constant multiple, which rescues the argument with a smaller constant. Nisan and Szegedy, "On the degree of Boolean functions as real polynomials", Computational Complexity 4, 301 (1994), give exactly this proof; Paturi (STOC 1992) determined the approximate degree of every symmetric function. The Markov inequality is A. A. Markov's (1889); his brother V. A. Markov extended it to higher derivatives (1892). It is cited, not proved here: a proof is in Borwein and Erdélyi, Polynomials and Polynomial Inequalities (Springer, 1995), chapter 5.

The upper bound from an algorithm. Unit 17 proved that choosing the iteration count at random from a growing range finds a marked item in expected $O(\sqrt{N/M})$ queries when $M\ge1$ items are marked. Stop after three times the bound for $M = 1$ and output "nothing marked" if no checked candidate was marked: on $0^N$ it never errs, and otherwise Markov's inequality on the running time bounds the error by $\tfrac13$. The polynomial method turns that algorithm into a polynomial of degree $O(\sqrt N)$ approximating OR — explicit constructions use Chebyshev polynomials. Here an algorithm proves a theorem about polynomials.

Parity: exactly N/2

Full degree even approximately, so the polynomial method gives $N/2$; Deutsch's algorithm on pairs meets it.

18 · parity

Parity has full degree, even approximately

theorem

$\deg(\mathrm{PARITY}_N) = \widetilde{\deg}(\mathrm{PARITY}_N) = N$.

proof

The degree is $N$ by the top coefficient. Let $p$ approximate parity within $\tfrac13$ and $q$ be its symmetrisation. Parity is $k\bmod2$ on layer $k$, so

$$q(k)\le\tfrac13\ (k\text{ even}),\qquad q(k)\ge\tfrac23\ (k\text{ odd}).$$

So $q - \tfrac12$ changes sign between $k$ and $k + 1$ for each $k = 0, \dots, N - 1$: $N$ roots by the intermediate value theorem. It is not zero, so $\deg p\ge\deg q\ge N$. $\blacksquare$

corollary

Even with error $\tfrac13$, every algorithm for parity makes $T\ge N/2$ queries.

$N = 4$: the polynomial through $0, 1, 0, 1, 0$, of degree $4$. Anything within $\tfrac13$ of it at the integers must also cross $\tfrac12$ four times.

Against OR: OR's $q$ must jump once and then sit still, which a degree near $\sqrt N$ manages; parity's must turn round at every integer, which needs them all.

The sign changes. At each integer $k$, $q(k) - \tfrac12$ is $\le-\tfrac16$ for even $k$ and $\ge\tfrac16$ for odd $k$. A continuous function with opposite signs at $k$ and $k + 1$ has a root strictly between them, so there are at least $N$ distinct roots in $(0, N)$. A nonzero polynomial of degree $d$ has at most $d$ roots, and $q - \tfrac12$ is nonzero because it is $\le-\tfrac16$ at $k = 0$. So $d\ge N$.

The figure, computed. The interpolant is $q(k) = \tfrac12\big(1 - \sum_{j=0}^4\binom kj(-2)^j\big)$, which is $\tfrac12(1 - (1 - 2)^k)$ at the integers. It crosses $\tfrac12$ at $k = 0.108, 1.353, 2.647, 3.892$, and peaks at about $1.333$ between $3$ and $4$. The crossings are where the proof's roots are.

Exact and approximate agree. For OR the two degrees are $N$ and order $\sqrt N$; for parity both are $N$. That is why bounded error buys nothing for parity: a quantum algorithm allowed to err a third of the time still needs $N/2$ queries.

Deutsch on pairs

The experiment. Unit 13's interferometer: one photon through two paths, a slab of phase $0$ or $\pi$ in each, tells with certainty whether the slabs are equal. For $N$ slabs, pair them up: $N/2$ photons. Parity has full degree says no interferometer, however clever, uses fewer.

theorem: parity in $\lceil N/2\rceil$ queries, exactly

For each pair $(x_{2p}, x_{2p+1})$ run Deutsch's algorithm with the index in $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{2p} + \ket{2p + 1})$; output the XOR of the $N/2$ answers. For odd $N$ read the last bit classically.

proof

With the target in $\ket-$, kickback gives $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big((-1)^{x_{2p}}\ket{2p} + (-1)^{x_{2p+1}}\ket{2p+1}\big)$: Deutsch's state on the last index qubit, with $p$ on the others. $H$ there leaves $\pm\ket p\ket{x_{2p}\oplus x_{2p+1}}$. XOR of the pairs is the XOR of all. $\blacksquare$

One pair, $N = 4$. The index is $i = 2p + b$ with qubit 0 holding $p$; the query sees only the two positions $2p$ and $2p + 1$.

Check: each run ends in a basis state up to sign, so its reading is certain; $N/2$ runs, one query each.

The interferometer, recalled. In unit 13, a half-silvered mirror splits a photon into two paths, each with a glass slab adding phase $0$ or $\pi$ (that is, $\pi x_i$), and a second half-silvered mirror recombines them; detector $D_0$ fires with probability $\sin^2\tfrac{\pi(x_1 - x_0)}2 = x_0\oplus x_1$. One photon, through both slabs, gives the parity of two bits, and no photon through one slab says anything about the parity. The quantum circuit is the same device: $H$ is the splitter, the oracle the slabs, the measurement the detectors.

The proof in full. Qubit 0 is set to $p$ by an $X$ when $p = 1$; qubit 1 gets $H$, giving index state $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{p0} + \ket{p1}) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{2p} + \ket{2p + 1})$ — qubit 0 is the more significant bit. The target is prepared in $\ket-$ by $X$ then $H$. The phase oracle multiplies $\ket{2p + b}$ by $(-1)^{x_{2p+b}}$. Factoring out $(-1)^{x_{2p}}$ leaves $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + (-1)^{x_{2p}\oplus x_{2p+1}}\ket1)$ on qubit 1, which is $\ket+$ or $\ket-$, and $H$ maps those to $\ket0$ and $\ket1$.

Counting. Each run uses one query and its index never leaves $\{2p, 2p + 1\}$. Runs may share a circuit — the answer of one pair is written into a fresh qubit and the next pair begins — or be separate; the count is $N/2$ either way, and the final XOR is classical post-processing, free in the query model.

Worked: parity of four bits in two queries

$N = 4$, $\ x = 1011$: two runs, and why one query cannot do it

Run 1, pair $(x_0, x_1) = (1, 0)$. Qubits 0, 1, 2 start in $\ket{000}$; then $X$ and $H$ on the target, $H$ on qubit 1:

$$\ket0\ket+\ket- = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{00} + \ket{01}\big)\ket- .$$

The query multiplies $\ket{00}$ by $(-1)^{x_0} = -1$ and $\ket{01}$ by $(-1)^{x_1} = +1$:

$$\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(-\ket{00} + \ket{01}\big)\ket- = -\ket0\ket-\ket- .$$

$H$ on qubit 1: $\ -\ket0\ket1\ket-$. It reads $1 = x_0\oplus x_1$ ✓.

Run 2, pair $(x_2, x_3) = (1, 1)$. Start from $\ket1\ket+\ket-$; both signs are $-1$:

$$-\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket{10} + \ket{11}\big)\ket- = -\ket1\ket+\ket- ,$$

and $H$ on qubit 1 gives $-\ket1\ket0\ket-$. It reads $0 = x_2\oplus x_3$ ✓.

Output $1\oplus0 = 1$, and $x = 1011$ has three ones ✓. Two queries; every state normalised, every reading certain.

One query is impossible. One query gives an acceptance probability of degree $\le2$. But

$$\mathrm{PARITY}_4 = \tfrac12\Big(1 - \prod_{i=0}^3(1 - 2x_i)\Big)$$

has top coefficient $-\tfrac12(-2)^4 = -8$: degree $4$. Even approximately: $\widetilde{\deg} = 4$ by Parity has full degree, so $T\ge2$.

the factor two, nothing more

Classically every bit must be read: $4$ queries. Quantumly $2$, and the polynomial method says $2$ is the floor.

Run 1 in components. $\ket0\ket+\ket- = \tfrac12(\ket{000} - \ket{001} + \ket{010} - \ket{011})$, four amplitudes $\pm\tfrac12$, norm $1$. Through the bit oracle: indices $0$ ($x_0 = 1$) swap the target, $\ket{000}\leftrightarrow\ket{001}$, turning $\tfrac12(\ket{000} - \ket{001})$ into $\tfrac12(\ket{001} - \ket{000}) = -\tfrac12(\ket{000} - \ket{001})$; index $1$ ($x_1 = 0$) is untouched. That is the kickback sign $-1$ on $\ket{00}$ only. Then $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-\ket0 + \ket1) = -\ket-$ on qubit 1, and $H\ket- = \ket1$.

Run 2. Both index values $2 = \ket{10}$ and $3 = \ket{11}$ carry $x = 1$, so both pick up $-1$: a global sign on qubit 1's $\ket+$, which $H$ sends to $\ket0$. Equal bits, reading $0$, exactly Deutsch's constant case.

The top coefficient. In $\prod_i(1 - 2x_i)$ the coefficient of $x_0x_1x_2x_3$ is $(-2)^4 = 16$; with the factor $-\tfrac12$ it is $-8$. The problem sheet's P3 does general $N$, and checks this run with the referee.

Exactly N/2

theorem

For $f = \mathrm{PARITY}_N$:

$$Q_E(f) = Q(f) = \lceil N/2\rceil,$$

while $D(\mathrm{PARITY}_N) = N$, and even a randomised algorithm with error $\tfrac13$ needs $N$.

proof

Lower: $Q(\mathrm{PARITY}_N)\ge\tfrac12\widetilde{\deg} = N/2$, and $T$ is an integer. Upper: Deutsch on pairs, exact, $\lceil N/2\rceil$ queries. And $Q\le Q_E$. Classical: an unread bit leaves the answer a fair coin (notes). $\blacksquare$

Check, $N = 2$: $Q_E = 1$ is unit 13's Deutsch, and $D = 2$ is unit 13's two classical queries ✓.

the extreme case of the method

Full approximate degree, so the quantum saving is the bare factor of two Deutsch already had on two bits — on every $N$, and provably no more.

Classical, with coins. Take $x$ uniform on $\{0, 1\}^N$. A deterministic tree of depth $k\lt N$ outputs at each leaf having read at most $k$ bits; given the answers on that path, some bit is unread and uniform, so parity is a fair coin and the leaf is right with probability exactly $\tfrac12$. So every depth-$k$ tree is right with probability $\tfrac12$ on uniform $x$, and by unit 13's averaging every randomised algorithm of depth $k\lt N$ is right with probability at most $\tfrac12$ on some $x$. Error $\tfrac13$ is impossible below $N$; and $N$ queries suffice, reading everything.

Odd $N$. The lower bound $T\ge N/2$ with $T$ an integer gives $T\ge(N + 1)/2$; the algorithm pairs $N - 1$ bits in $(N - 1)/2$ queries and reads the last one directly: $(N + 1)/2$.

The exact query complexity of total functions is a subject of its own; parity is one of the few functions where it is known exactly for every $N$, and it is known because the lower bound (degree) and the upper bound (Deutsch) meet.

18 · what this says

Total functions: at most a fourth power

A total function is defined on every $x\in\{0, 1\}^N$ — no promise. OR and parity are total.

theorem (Aaronson, Ben-David, Kothari, Rao, Tal 2021) — cited

For every total $f:\{0, 1\}^N\to\{0, 1\}$, $\ D(f) = O\big(Q(f)^4\big)$.

Its proof rests on Huang's sensitivity theorem (2019). Beals et al. had $D = O(Q^6)$ in 2001. The fourth power is tight up to logarithms: a total "pointer" function has $D$ near $N$ and $Q$ near $N^{1/4}$ (Ambainis et al. 2016, cited).

corollary: no exponential speed-up without a promise

$Q(f)\ge(D(f)/C)^{1/4}$. If $D(f) = N$, as for OR and parity, then $Q(f) = \Omega(N^{1/4})$ — never polylogarithmic in $N$.

Exponents of $D$ and $Q$, as powers of $N$. Total functions live on or above the line $Q = D^{1/4}$; OR at $\tfrac12$, parity at $1$. Deutsch–Jozsa, a promise problem, sits below.

The corollary in one line. If $D(f)\le C\,Q(f)^4$ then $Q(f)\ge(D(f)/C)^{1/4}$. An exponential speed-up would need a family with $Q(f_N)$ polylogarithmic in $D(f_N)$, and $(D/C)^{1/4}$ eventually exceeds every power of $\log D$. For OR, $D = N$ and $Q = \Theta(\sqrt N)$: $D = \Theta(Q^2)$. For parity, $D = N$ and $Q = N/2$. Both sit comfortably inside the theorem.

Sensitivity, named. The sensitivity $s(f)$ is the largest number of single-bit flips that change $f$ at one input. Huang ("Induced subgraphs of hypercubes and a proof of the Sensitivity Conjecture", Ann. Math. 190, 949, 2019) proved $s(f)\ge\sqrt{\deg f}$, settling a thirty-year-old question in two pages. Aaronson, Ben-David, Kothari, Rao and Tal (STOC 2021, "Degree vs. approximate degree and quantum implications of Huang's sensitivity theorem") combined it with the polynomial method to get the fourth power. Not proved here.

The tight example. Ambainis, Balodis, Belovs, Lee, Santha and Smotrovs (STOC 2016; J. ACM 64, 32, 2017) built a total function from "pointers" with $D(f) = \Omega(N/\log N)$ and $Q(f) = \tilde O(N^{1/4})$. Its point on the figure is drawn at $(1, \tfrac14)$, ignoring the logarithms.

The figure's axes are $\log D/\log N$ and $\log Q/\log N$, so a function with $D\approx N^a$ and $Q\approx N^b$ sits at $(a, b)$. The shaded triangle below $b = a/4$ is empty of total functions.

Promises: where exponential speed-ups live

Deutsch–Jozsa (unit 13): $N = 2^n$ bits, promised constant, $|x|\in\{0, N\}$, or balanced, $|x| = N/2$. The amplitude of $\ket{0^n}$ is $N^{-1}\sum_i(-1)^{x_i} = 1 - 2|x|/N$, so one query accepts with

$$p(x) = \Big(1 - \frac{2|x|}N\Big)^2,$$

a polynomial of degree $2$ (as $|x|$ is linear), equal to $1$ on constant and $0$ on balanced inputs.

Yet $D = N/2 + 1$ (unit 13): an exponential gap. No contradiction — the theorem is about total functions, and off the promise $p$ takes every value in between.

a promise is a gift of structure

Its natural total version, "is $x\in\{0^N, 1^N\}$?", has approximate degree $\Omega(\sqrt N)$ (problem P9), and every total version needs $\Omega(N^{1/4})$ queries, by the fourth-power theorem (notes). The low degree lives only on the promise.

Deutsch–Jozsa's acceptance probability against $|x|$. The promise keeps the input on the three marked layers, where a parabola is enough.

Search has no promise to give. OR must be right on every layer, so its curve is forced to jump and stay flat — and that costs order $\sqrt N$.

The polynomial method for promise problems. When $f$ is defined only on a set $P$ of inputs, the acceptance probability is still a polynomial of degree at most $2T$ on the whole cube and still lies in $[0, 1]$ everywhere — it must be within $\tfrac13$ of $f$ only on $P$. The fewer the inputs in $P$, the easier it is to find low-degree polynomials, and the weaker the lower bound. Deutsch–Jozsa's promise set is three layers out of $N + 1$, and degree $2$ suffices.

Simon and Shor, which the learner may have met in units 14–16, are promise problems of the same kind: the input is promised to be periodic, or constant on the cosets of a hidden subgroup. Their exponential speed-ups are consistent with the fourth-power theorem only because of those promises. Grover's problem promises nothing, and gets a square root.

Every total extension. Write $\mathrm{CONST}_N(x) = 1$ iff $x\in\{0^N, 1^N\}$; problem P9 proves its approximate degree is at least that of $\mathrm{OR}_{N-1}$. Not only $\mathrm{CONST}_N$ pays. If a total $f$ agrees with Deutsch–Jozsa on the promise, a deterministic algorithm for $f$ decides Deutsch–Jozsa, so $D(f)\ge N/2 + 1$ (unit 13), and the fourth-power theorem gives $Q(f)\ge\big((N/2 + 1)/C\big)^{1/4}$. Whether every such $f$ needs order $\sqrt N$, as $\mathrm{CONST}_N$ does, is not claimed here.

The check. At $|x| = 0$ and $|x| = N$, $p = 1$; at $|x| = N/2$, $p = 0$; at $|x| = N/4$, off the promise, $p = \tfrac14$. The amplitude formula is unit 13's: after $H^{\otimes n}$, the phase oracle and $H^{\otimes n}$, the amplitude of $\ket{0^n}$ is $2^{-n}\sum_i(-1)^{x_i}$, and $\sum_i(-1)^{x_i} = (N - |x|) - |x|$.

Relative to an oracle, NP is not in BQP

theorem (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, Vazirani 1997)

Let $A$ be a random oracle, each string $y$ in $A$ independently with probability $2^{-|y|}$. With probability $1$, $\ L_A = \{1^n : A\text{ has a string of length }n\}$ is in $\mathsf{NP}^A$, not in $\mathsf{BQP}^A$.

proof, sketched

In $\mathsf{NP}^A$: the witness is the string; one query checks it. Not in $\mathsf{BQP}^A$: $M$ makes $T\le\mathrm{poly}(n)$ queries on $1^n$. With probability $0.74$, $A$ has at most one string of length $n$. Average the hybrid bound over the $N = 2^n$ strings:

$$\frac1N\sum_w\big\lVert\ket{\psi_T^w} - \ket{\psi_T}\big\rVert\le\frac{2T}{\sqrt N}\to0,$$

so $M$, if right on "none", is wrong on "$\{w\}$" for most $w$, and errs at $1^n$ with probability $\ge0.3$, independently at well-separated $n$. $\blacksquare$

How many strings of length $20$ the random oracle holds, computed: $0$ and $1$ each have probability $0.368$, close to $1/e$.

The oracle unit 12 promised: black-box search over NP witnesses gains a square root and no more.

The sketch, with the missing steps. (1) The averaged bound. For each $t$, by Cauchy–Schwarz over $w$, $\sum_w\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert\le\sqrt N\big(\sum_w\lVert P_w\ket{\psi_t}\rVert^2\big)^{1/2} = \sqrt N$; summing the hybrid bound over $w$ and $t$ gives $\sum_w\lVert\ket{\psi_T^w} - \ket{\psi_T}\rVert\le2T\sqrt N$. Here the states are $M$'s on input $1^n$, with $A$ fixed off length $n$; "none" and "$\{w\}$" differ only at the string $w$. (2) Most $w$ fool $M$. If $M$ accepts "none" with probability $\le\tfrac13$, then to accept "$\{w\}$" with probability $\ge\tfrac23$ it needs distance $\ge\tfrac13$ there (the lemma); by Markov's inequality on the average, that happens for at most a fraction $6T/\sqrt N$ of the $w$. (3) Probabilities. Given everything off length $n$, the number of strings at length $n$ is $0$ with probability $(1 - 2^{-n})^{2^n}$ and $1$ with probability $(1 - 2^{-n})^{2^n - 1}$ — $0.368$ each at $n = 20$, tending to $1/e = 0.3679$ — and given one, it is uniform. So $M$ fails at $1^n$ with probability at least $\min\big(0.36, 0.36(1 - 6T/\sqrt N)\big)\ge0.3$ for large $n$, whatever happens elsewhere.

(4) Independence. Choose lengths $n_1\lt n_2\lt\cdots$ with $n_{j+1}$ beyond every length $M$ can query on $1^{n_j}$. The event "$M$ right at $1^{n_j}$" depends only on lengths below $n_{j+1}$, and given all lengths except $n_k$, "$M$ right at $1^{n_k}$" has probability at most $0.7$. So $M$ is right at all of $1^{n_1}, \dots, 1^{n_k}$ with probability at most $0.7^k\to0$. (5) There are countably many machines, and a countable union of probability-zero events has probability zero. Problem P11 asks for this argument.

What it shows. Unit 12 stated that relative to some oracle NP is not contained in BQP; here is that oracle — in fact almost every oracle. Relative to it, a quantum computer searching $2^n$ candidate witnesses needs order $2^{n/2}$ queries, Grover's count, and no more cleverness helps.

What it does not say

not a proof that NP ⊄ BQP

A SAT formula is not a black box: its witnesses are checked by a circuit an algorithm can read. And the proof relativises, so by unit 12 it cannot settle the unrelativised question. Grover-level speed-ups for NP are the most a black-box method can give; whether structure gives more is open.

what it does say

An algorithm that uses a problem only through a membership test — brute force, in any clever order — gains a square root and no more. Exponential speed-ups need structure: a promise like Deutsch–Jozsa's, or the periodicity Fourier sampling finds.

Relativisation, recalled. Unit 12: a proof that works relative to every oracle cannot prove a statement that fails relative to some oracle. Relative to a PSPACE-complete oracle $B$, NP is inside BQP, since $\mathsf{NP}^B\subseteq \mathsf{PSPACE}^B = \mathsf{PSPACE}\subseteq\mathsf{P}^B\subseteq\mathsf{BQP}^B$; relative to the random oracle here, it is not. So no relativising argument decides the question, and this deck's arguments relativise: they never look inside the oracle.

Why this matters for cryptography. A cipher's key search is unstructured: Grover's square root is the attack (unit 17), and this unit says it is the best black-box attack, which is why doubling key lengths is a complete answer to it. Structured problems — factoring, discrete logarithm — are another matter, as Shor showed.
