Hamiltonian simulation: Feynman's reason, made into an algorithm

A classical computer simulating $n$ spins tracks $2^n$ numbers; a quantum one splits the evolution into short steps of each local piece, pays an error it can bound by a commutator, and reads energies off with phase estimation.

needs 15, 11~2½ h slides + 4½ h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 19

This unit returns to the reason the field exists. Feynman's argument of 1981 — published as "Simulating physics with computers", Int. J. Theor. Phys. 21, 467 (1982) — was not about factoring or search. It was that nature is quantum, that writing down a quantum state of $n$ spins takes $2^n$ numbers, and that a computer built out of spins might therefore simulate spins without that cost. Lloyd made it an algorithm fourteen years later ("Universal quantum simulators", Science 273, 1073, 1996): for a Hamiltonian made of local pieces, evolve under each piece for a short time in turn, and pay an error that shrinks as the steps do. This unit proves that error bound, derives the better symmetric splitting, counts the gates, and reads energies off with unit 15's phase estimation.

How to read this deck

One system runs through it: a chain of spins in a transverse field — the transverse-field Ising chain — and one question about it: how many gates does it take to simulate it for time $t$ to accuracy $\varepsilon$? Section 1 says what the system is and why it is hard classically; section 2 proves the first-order bound; section 3 measures it and goes to second order; section 4 turns steps into gates; section 5 turns the simulation into an energy measurement. Proofs are on the slides when they are one line and in these notes under Full proof when they are not; anything imported is labelled cited, with where its proof is. Every number was computed.

What is used from earlier units. Unit 00: Hermitian and unitary operators, the spectral theorem, the functional calculus, the matrix exponential and the fact that $e^{A}e^{B} = e^{A+B}$ when $A$ and $B$ commute, and the commutator. Unit 01: the Hamiltonian as the generator of $e^{-iHt}$, energies as its eigenvalues, the entrywise calculus of matrix-valued functions, and $\tfrac{d}{dt}e^{-iHt} = -iHe^{-iHt}$. Unit 02: the Pauli matrices and their products, rotations $R_P(\theta) = e^{-i\theta P/2}$, a spin in a magnetic field as $H\propto-\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. Unit 03: tensor products of operators. Unit 10: circuits, CNOT, controlled gates. Unit 11: the operator norm and its properties, the hybrid argument (errors add), Clifford gates, and the Ross–Selinger cost of about $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ T gates per $z$-rotation. Unit 15: phase estimation, its $4/\pi^2$ bound, and what it does to a superposition of eigenvectors. From the background: Cauchy–Schwarz, the triangle inequality, and one-variable calculus.

What is not here. This is not a survey of simulation algorithms. The modern optimal methods — qubitisation and quantum signal processing — and the sharp commutator-scaling analysis of higher-order formulas are named and cited with their scaling, not derived. Quantum chemistry is named as the application; no molecular Hamiltonian is written down.

Why this chain. It is the simplest Hamiltonian with two non-commuting parts, each of which is easy on its own: the $ZZ$ part is diagonal in the computational basis, the $X$ part is diagonal in the $\ket\pm$ basis, and neither basis diagonalises both. That is exactly the situation a product formula is built for. It is also a real model of real magnets, and the standard benchmark for quantum simulators, so its numbers mean something beyond this deck.

Notation. $n$ is always the number of spins, $t$ the simulated time, $r$ the number of Trotter steps and $\delta = t/r$ the length of one. Many texts write $n$ for the steps; this deck does not, because the chain needs $n$.

What the chain is, from nothing; why a classical computer chokes on it; and what a quantum simulator would have to deliver.

19 · Feynman's reason

Spins in a row

The physics, from nothing. Unit 02's proton is a spin: a qubit whose $Z$ is its magnetic moment along $z$, $\pm1$ for "up" $\ket0$ and "down" $\ket1$. A crystal can hold such spins on a line of atoms. Neighbours interact — in a magnet they prefer to point the same way — and an applied field along $x$ acts on each, as unit 02's field did on the proton.

the transverse-field Ising chain $$H = -J\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}Z_iZ_{i+1}\;-\;h\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}X_i ,$$

with $J, h \gt 0$. "Ising" for the $ZZ$ coupling (Ising 1925); "transverse" because the field is perpendicular to the $z$ axis the coupling uses.

Hermitian, checked: each term is a tensor product of Hermitian matrices, and $H$ is a real combination of them ✓.

One basis state of eight spins. The coupling counts agreeing neighbours; the field does not see $z$ at all — it acts along $x$.

Where the terms come from. Unit 02 wrote the Hamiltonian of a spin in a field $\mathbf B$ as $-\tfrac{\gamma\hbar}{2}\mathbf B\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. A field of strength $B$ along $x$ is therefore $-\tfrac{\gamma\hbar B}{2}X$; with $\hbar = 1$ and the constant collected into $h$, that is the term $-hX_i$ on spin $i$. The coupling $-JZ_iZ_{i+1}$ is the simplest interaction between two neighbouring moments that depends only on whether they agree along $z$. Real magnetic interactions are more symmetric (the Heisenberg coupling $\mathbf\sigma_i\cdot\mathbf\sigma_{i+1}$), but in many crystals the atoms' surroundings pick out one axis strongly, and the $ZZ$ part dominates. Then $H$ is a good model, not just a toy.

A real material. Cobalt niobate, CoNb$_2$O$_6$, contains chains of cobalt ions that behave as this model; neutron scattering in a transverse field traced its spectrum through the critical point (Coldea et al., Science 327, 177, 2010, cited).

Qubit order. As everywhere in the course, qubit 0 is the leftmost symbol of a ket: the state in the figure, spins 0–7 reading up, up, up, down, up, up, down, down, is $\ket{00010011}$.

History. Lenz proposed the classical model ($h = 0$) to his student Ising, whose thesis (1924, published 1925) solved it on a line and found no ordering at any positive temperature. The transverse field makes it quantum: de Gennes (1963) used it for hydrogen-bonded ferroelectrics, and Pfeuty solved the chain exactly (Ann. Phys. 57, 79, 1970).

19 · Feynman's reason

What the two terms want

The coupling alone ($h = 0$) is diagonal: on $\ket x$, $Z_iZ_{i+1} = (-1)^{x_i\oplus x_{i+1}}$, so

$$-J\sum_iZ_iZ_{i+1}\ket x = -J\big[(n - 1) - 2w(x)\big]\ket x ,$$

$w(x)$ the number of disagreeing neighbours. Lowest at $w = 0$: $\ket{0\cdots0}$ and $\ket{1\cdots1}$, energy $-J(n - 1)$.

The field alone ($J = 0$) wants each spin in $\ket+$, the top eigenvector of $X$: lowest state $\ket{+}^{\otimes n}$, energy $-hn$ — and $\ket+$ is an equal superposition of up and down.

Together they compete. No basis diagonalises both parts, because they do not commute (section 2 computes the commutator). For $h\ll J$ the lowest state is close to aligned; for $h\gg J$ close to $\ket{+}^{\otimes n}$. At $J = h$, in the infinite chain, the character of the lowest state changes abruptly: a quantum phase transition (Pfeuty 1970, cited).

Left: one bond prefers agreement. Right: one spin in the field prefers $\ket+$, which has no definite value along $z$.

check, at $n = 8$, $J = h = 1$

Aligned: $\braket{0^8|H|0^8} = -7$. All $\ket+$: $\braket{+^8|H|+^8} = -8$, since $\braket{+|Z|+} = 0$. The true lowest energy, computed, is $-9.838$: below both, as the variational principle of section 5 requires.

The diagonal formula. $Z\ket b = (-1)^b\ket b$, so $Z_iZ_{i+1}\ket x = (-1)^{x_i}(-1)^{x_{i+1}}\ket x = (-1)^{x_i\oplus x_{i+1}}\ket x$. Of the $n - 1$ bonds, $n - 1 - w$ contribute $+1$ and $w$ contribute $-1$, total $n - 1 - 2w$. With the minus sign and $J \gt 0$ the energy is lowest when $w = 0$. Two states achieve it: the coupling cannot tell "all up" from "all down".

The expectations in the check. $\braket{0^8|X_i|0^8} = 0$ and each $Z_iZ_{i+1}$ gives $+1$, so $-7$. For $\ket{+}^{\otimes8}$: $\braket{+|Z|+} = 0$, so $\braket{+^8|Z_iZ_{i+1}|+^8} = \braket{+|Z|+}^2 = 0$ (unit 03's rule for product states), and each $X_i$ gives $+1$: total $-8$.

The phase transition, stated. In the limit $n\to\infty$ the model is exactly solvable (by mapping spins to free fermions — not done here), and the ground state is "ordered" for $h \lt J$ — it has long-range correlations $\braket{Z_0Z_j}\not\to0$ — and "disordered" for $h \gt J$; at $h = J$ the gap above the ground state closes. The ground energy per spin at $h = J$ is $-4J/\pi = -1.273J$ (cited, Pfeuty 1970). The open chains computed for this deck give $-1.230$ per spin at $n = 8$, $-1.238$ at $n = 10$ and $-1.244$ at $n = 12$, creeping towards it as the ends matter less. Nothing in this unit uses the transition; it is why physicists care about $J = h$.

Feynman's count

A state of $n$ spins lives in $(\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes n}\cong\mathbb C^{2^n}$ (unit 03): $2^n$ complex amplitudes, and for an entangled state no shorter description is known in general.

the count, at $n = 50$

$2^{50} = 1.13\times10^{15}$ amplitudes. A complex number in double precision is two 8-byte reals, 16 bytes:

$$16\times2^{50} = 2^{54} = 1.80\times10^{16}\ \text{bytes},$$

18 petabytes.

One step of evolution multiplies that vector by a $2^{50}\times2^{50}$ matrix.

Feynman's proposal (1981): simulate spins with spins. A quantum computer holds the 50-spin state in 50 qubits, and one more spin costs one more qubit, not twice the memory.

Memory for a full state vector. Each spin doubles it: a laptop holds 30 spins; the record simulation of 2017 held 45.

The numbers. $2^{50} = 1{,}125{,}899{,}906{,}842{,}624$. Times 16 bytes: $18{,}014{,}398{,}509{,}481{,}984 = 1.80\times10^{16}$ bytes, 18.0 petabytes in decimal units (16 PiB in binary ones). At $n = 30$: $16\times2^{30} = 2^{34}$ bytes, exactly 16 GiB. At $n = 45$: $2^{49}$ bytes $= 0.5$ PiB $= 5.6\times10^{14}$ bytes — the size of the record simulation of Häner and Steiger ("0.5 petabyte simulation of a 45-qubit quantum circuit", SC17, 2017, cited).

"No shorter description is known in general." This is a claim about the general case, and it is honest only as that. Many states of physical interest have short classical descriptions — product states, states of low entanglement written as tensor networks, stabiliser states (unit 23). For one-dimensional chains with a gap, such methods are spectacularly good at ground states. What none of them does in general is follow the dynamics $e^{-iHt}\ket\psi$ for long times, where entanglement grows. That is where Feynman's argument bites.

Feynman, in his words. The 1982 paper ends: "Nature isn't classical, dammit, and if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical, and by golly it's a wonderful problem, because it doesn't look so easy." He did not give an algorithm, and he worried (rightly) about whether a universal quantum simulator could be built from local interactions. Lloyd's answer, fourteen years later, is section 2.

19 · Feynman's reason

What a simulator must deliver

Hamiltonian simulation

Given $H$ on $n$ qubits, a time $t$ and an accuracy $\varepsilon$: a circuit $W$ with

$$\lVert W - e^{-iHt}\rVert\le\varepsilon .$$

Why this is enough. By unit 11, every outcome probability of every measurement after $W$ is within $2\varepsilon$ of the true one, whatever the input.

What one then measures. The magnetisation $\braket{M} = \tfrac1n\sum_i\braket{Z_i}$; correlations $\braket{Z_0Z_j}$; or energies (section 5). Each expectation is an average of $\pm1$ outcomes, so $O(1/\eta^2)$ repetitions give it to $\pm\eta$ (Chernoff–Hoeffding).

Prepare, evolve, measure, repeat. The middle box is this unit.

It has been done. Lanyon et al. (2011) ran stepped spin-model evolutions on a handful of trapped ions; Kim et al. (2023) ran stepped dynamics of this model, on a 127-qubit lattice of superconducting qubits (both cited).

The 2ε, recalled. Unit 11 proved that if $\lVert U - V\rVert\le\varepsilon$ then for any state and any projective measurement the outcome probabilities differ by at most $2\varepsilon$: $|\lVert PU\psi\rVert^2 - \lVert PV\psi\rVert^2|\le2\lVert(U - V)\psi\rVert$. So operator-norm accuracy is the right currency — it is input-independent and composes by the hybrid argument.

The repetitions. A single run measuring $Z_i$ gives $\pm1$ with mean $\braket{Z_i}$; $N$ independent runs averaged are within $\eta$ of the mean with probability at least $1 - 2e^{-N\eta^2/2}$ (Hoeffding, for variables in $[-1, 1]$). Errors $\varepsilon$ in the simulation shift each mean by at most $2\varepsilon$, by the previous paragraph.

The experiments. Lanyon et al., "Universal digital quantum simulation with trapped ions", Science 334, 57 (2011): the first experiment to simulate spin Hamiltonians by exactly the stepped method of section 2, on a few ions. Kim et al., "Evidence for the utility of quantum computing before fault tolerance", Nature 618, 500 (2023): stepped evolution of the transverse-field Ising model on the 127 qubits of a processor whose couplings form a two-dimensional lattice rather than a chain, with error mitigation. Within weeks, classical tensor-network methods reproduced its numbers (Tindall et al., PRX Quantum 5, 010308, 2024, cited) — a reminder that Feynman's count is a statement about the general case, and that a particular instance may have classical structure to exploit.

The experiment is the motivation; the definition is the mathematics. What a laboratory asks of a simulator is "give me the measurement statistics of the evolved state". The definition above asks for less and more: an operator-norm guarantee, which delivers every statistic of every input at once.

19 · Feynman's reason

Local Hamiltonians

a k-local Hamiltonian $$H = \sum_{j=1}^{L}H_j ,$$

each $H_j$ Hermitian and acting on at most $k$ qubits ($I$ on the rest), with $k = O(1)$ and $L$ polynomial in $n$.

Why this class. Physical interactions are between few particles at a time, and usually nearby ones: two spins, two electrons, an electron and a nucleus. So the Hamiltonians nature hands us are local.

The chain is 2-local with $L = (n - 1) + n = 2n - 1$ terms: $H_j = -JZ_iZ_{i+1}$ or $-hX_i$.

A norm bound for free: $\lVert H\rVert\le\sum_j\lVert H_j\rVert$ (triangle inequality). For the chain, $\lVert H\rVert\le J(n - 1) + hn$: at $n = 8$, $J = h = 1$, $15$. The exact value is $9.838$.

The chain's terms: one per bond, one per site. Each touches at most two qubits.

what is easy, and what is not

Writing $H$ down takes $O(L)$ numbers. Writing $e^{-iHt}$ down takes $4^n$. The question is whether a circuit for it can be as short as the first.

Each term's norm. $\lVert Z_iZ_{i+1}\rVert = 1$: it is a tensor product of unitaries, hence unitary (unit 03), and Hermitian, with eigenvalues $\pm1$; by unit 11's property (c) its norm is 1. Likewise $\lVert X_i\rVert = 1$. So $\lVert H\rVert\le7 + 8 = 15$ at $n = 8$.

The exact norm. $H$ is Hermitian, so $\lVert H\rVert$ is its largest $|$eigenvalue$|$ (unit 11). Computed by diagonalising the $256\times256$ matrix: the spectrum runs from $-9.838$ to $+9.838$ — symmetric, because $\prod_i Z_i$ anticommutes with every $X_i$ and commutes with every $Z_iZ_{i+1}$, so conjugating by it sends $H$ to $-J\sum ZZ + h\sum X$; conjugating that by $\prod_{i\ \mathrm{odd}}X_i$ flips the sign of every $Z_iZ_{i+1}$ (exactly one of $i, i + 1$ is odd) and leaves the $X$'s alone. So $V = \prod_{i\ \mathrm{odd}}X_i\prod_iZ_i$ satisfies $VHV^\dagger = -H$, and $-H$ has the same spectrum as $H$.

Locality in space, not just in number. The definition asks only that each term touch few qubits. Physical Hamiltonians usually also respect geometry — each qubit is in $O(1)$ terms — and section 4 uses that to bound the commutators. A $k$-local $H$ on all $\binom nk$ subsets is still polynomial for fixed $k$, and everything in section 2 still holds; only the counting gets worse.

Quantum chemistry is the application most often named: the Hamiltonian of the electrons in a molecule, written in a basis of orbitals and mapped to qubits, becomes a sum of $O(N^4)$ Pauli-product terms on $N$ qubits — each acting on many qubits after the mapping, but still a polynomial sum of Pauli products, which is all section 4 needs. No molecular Hamiltonian is written down in this course.

19 · Lie–Trotter

If the terms commuted, there would be nothing to do

unit 00

If $[A, B] = 0$ then $e^{-i(A + B)t} = e^{-iAt}e^{-iBt}$, exactly.

The chain splits into two commuting families. Write $H = A + B$ with

$$A = -J\sum_iZ_iZ_{i+1},\qquad B = -h\sum_iX_i .$$

Inside $A$ every term is diagonal, and diagonal matrices commute. Inside $B$ the terms act on different qubits: $X_iX_j = X_jX_i$ by $(P\otimes I)(I\otimes Q) = P\otimes Q = (I\otimes Q)(P\otimes I)$. So, exactly,

$$e^{-iA\delta} = \prod_ie^{iJ\delta Z_iZ_{i+1}},$$ $$e^{-iB\delta} = \prod_iR_x(-2h\delta)_i .$$

The chain's 15 terms at $n = 8$. A line joins two that do not commute: bond $i$ and the sites $i$, $i + 1$ it touches.

But $[A, B]\ne0$. $Z_0Z_1$ and $X_0$ anticommute: $ZX = -XZ$ on qubit 0, while $Z_1$ and $I$ commute. So $e^{-iHt}\ne e^{-iAt}e^{-iBt}$, and the gap is the subject of this section.

The rotation. $e^{ih\delta X} = \cos(h\delta)I + i\sin(h\delta)X$ (unit 00's worked example, $\theta = h\delta$). The course's rotation is $R_x(\theta) = e^{-i\theta X/2} = \cos\tfrac\theta2I - i\sin\tfrac\theta2X$, so $e^{ih\delta X} = R_x(-2h\delta)$ ✓. Likewise $e^{iJ\delta Z_iZ_{i+1}} = e^{-i\theta Z\otimes Z}$ with $\theta = -J\delta$, which section 4 builds from two CNOTs.

The anticommutation. $Z_0Z_1X_0 = (ZX)\otimes Z\otimes I\cdots$ and $X_0Z_0Z_1 = (XZ)\otimes Z\otimes I\cdots$, and $ZX = -XZ$ (unit 02: $ZX = iY$, $XZ = -iY$). So $Z_0Z_1X_0 = -X_0Z_0Z_1$. A bond and a site that do not share a qubit act on different tensor factors and commute.

Why the split matters. Each family is a sum of commuting terms, so its exponential is a product of exponentials of single terms — each a gate on one or two qubits. The whole difficulty of the chain is concentrated in the fourteen lines of the figure.

The error, to leading order

A Trotter step replaces $U(\delta) = e^{-i(A + B)\delta}$ by $V(\delta) = e^{-iA\delta}e^{-iB\delta}$. Expand both with unit 00's series, writing $a = -iA$, $b = -iB$:

$$V = I + (a + b)\delta + \tfrac12(a^2 + 2ab + b^2)\delta^2 + \cdots$$ $$U = I + (a + b)\delta + \tfrac12(a + b)^2\delta^2 + \cdots$$

They agree to first order, and differ at second by $\tfrac12(ab - ba)\delta^2 = -\tfrac12[A, B]\delta^2$:

$$\lVert V(\delta) - U(\delta)\rVert = \tfrac12\lVert[A, B]\rVert\,\delta^2 + O(\delta^3).$$

The guess. $r$ steps of length $\delta = t/r$, errors adding: $r\cdot\tfrac12\lVert[A, B]\rVert(t/r)^2 = \lVert[A, B]\rVert t^2/2r$. The next slides prove exactly this, with no $O(\delta^3)$ left over.

$H = X + Z$ on one qubit, $t = 1$, from $\ket0$. Exact: one turn about $(x + z)/\sqrt2$. Three Trotter steps: turns about $z$, then $x$, alternately.

The expansion. $e^{a\delta}e^{b\delta} = (I + a\delta + \tfrac12a^2\delta^2 + \cdots)(I + b\delta + \tfrac12b^2\delta^2 + \cdots)$; the $\delta^2$ terms are $\tfrac12a^2 + ab + \tfrac12b^2$. For $e^{(a+b)\delta}$ they are $\tfrac12(a + b)^2 = \tfrac12(a^2 + ab + ba + b^2)$, and $(a + b)^2\ne a^2 + 2ab + b^2$ unless $a$ and $b$ commute (unit 00's Exponentials do not add). The difference is $\tfrac12(ab - ba) = \tfrac12(-i)^2[A, B] = -\tfrac12[A, B]$.

Why this is not yet a proof. The $O(\delta^3)$ hides a constant that depends on $\lVert A\rVert$ and $\lVert B\rVert$ — for the chain, on $n$ — and summing $r$ of them gives a term $O(t^3/r^2)$ whose size one would have to bound separately. The theorem One step costs at most half a commutator has no such remainder: the bound $\tfrac12\lVert[A, B]\rVert\delta^2$ holds for every $\delta \gt 0$. The leading-order computation shows its constant $\tfrac12$ cannot be improved in general.

The figure. Computed from unit 02's dictionary: $e^{-iX\delta} = R_x(2\delta)$ turns the Bloch sphere by $2\delta$ about $x$, and $e^{-i(X+Z)\delta} = R_{\mathbf n}(2\sqrt2\,\delta)$ with $\mathbf n = (1, 0, 1)/\sqrt2$, since $X + Z = \sqrt2\,\mathbf n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma$. In each Trotter step the $z$-turn acts first, because $V = e^{-iX\delta}e^{-iZ\delta}$ applies its right factor first. Three steps already land near the exact endpoint; the zigzag is the commutator, made visible.

19 · Lie–Trotter

Two tools for the proof

lemma 1: the norm of an integral

For a continuous $F:[a, b]\to\mathrm{End}(V)$, $\ \Big\lVert\int_a^bF(s)\,ds\Big\rVert\le\int_a^b\lVert F(s)\rVert\,ds$.

proof

Fix a unit $v$, let $w = \big(\int F\big)v = \int F(s)v\,ds$, and if $w\ne0$ let $u = w/\lVert w\rVert$. Then

$$\lVert w\rVert = \braket{u|w} = \int\braket{u|F(s)v}\,ds ,$$

which is at most $\int\lVert F(s)\rVert\,ds$ by Cauchy–Schwarz. Maximise over $v$. ∎

lemma 2: how fast conjugation moves $B$

For Hermitian $A$, any $B$, and $G(s) = e^{iAs}Be^{-iAs}$:

$$G'(s) = e^{iAs}\,i[A, B]\,e^{-iAs},$$ $$\lVert G(s) - B\rVert\le|s|\,\lVert[A, B]\rVert .$$
proof

Product rule (unit 01), with $\tfrac{d}{ds}e^{\pm iAs} = \pm iAe^{\pm iAs}$ and $A$ commuting with $e^{\pm iAs}$: $G' = e^{iAs}(iAB - BiA)e^{-iAs}$. Then $G(s) - B = \int_0^sG'$; lemma 1, and unit 11's $\lVert UMV\rVert = \lVert M\rVert$ for unitary $U, V$. ∎

Check: $[A, B] = 0$ gives $G\equiv B$ — $B$ commutes with $e^{\pm iAs}$ ✓.

Full proof of lemma 1

Integrals of matrix-valued functions are entrywise (unit 01), and so is the action on a fixed vector: $\big(\int F\big)v = \int F(s)v\,ds$, because each entry of $Fv$ is a fixed linear combination of entries of $F$. Likewise $\braket{u|\int F(s)v\,ds} = \int\braket{u|F(s)v}\,ds$, the inner product with a fixed $u$ being linear in its second slot. Since $\lVert w\rVert$ is real, $\lVert w\rVert = \mathrm{Re}\int\braket{u|F(s)v}\,ds = \int\mathrm{Re}\braket{u|F(s)v}\,ds\le\int|\braket{u|F(s)v}|\,ds$, and $|\braket{u|F(s)v}|\le\lVert u\rVert\lVert F(s)v\rVert\le\lVert F(s)\rVert$ by Cauchy–Schwarz and the definition of the operator norm. The function $s\mapsto\lVert F(s)\rVert$ is continuous (the norm is continuous, $|\lVert M\rVert - \lVert N\rVert|\le\lVert M - N\rVert$), so the right side exists. If $w = 0$ there is nothing to prove. The bound holds for every unit $v$, so for the maximum, which is $\lVert\int F\rVert$.

Full proof of lemma 2

By unit 01, $\tfrac{d}{ds}e^{-iAs} = -iAe^{-iAs} = e^{-iAs}(-iA)$, both $A$ and $e^{-iAs}$ being functions of $A$; replacing $A$ by $-A$ gives the other sign. The product rule for the three factors $e^{iAs}$, $B$ (constant), $e^{-iAs}$:

$$G'(s) = e^{iAs}(iA)Be^{-iAs} + e^{iAs}B(-iA)e^{-iAs} = e^{iAs}\,i(AB - BA)\,e^{-iAs}.$$

The fundamental theorem (unit 01) gives $G(s) - G(0) = \int_0^sG'(u)\,du$, and $G(0) = B$. For $s \gt 0$, lemma 1 and unitary invariance give $\lVert G(s) - B\rVert\le\int_0^s\lVert[A, B]\rVert\,du = s\lVert[A, B]\rVert$; for $s \lt 0$ swap the limits. The same argument shows something stronger and familiar: if $[A, B] = 0$ then $G' = 0$, so $G$ is constant (unit 01).

Where this comes from. $G(s)$ is $B$ seen from a frame that moves with $A$'s evolution — unit 02's rotating frame is the one-qubit case. Lemma 2 says the frame moves $B$ no faster than the commutator allows.

One step costs at most half a commutator

theorem: the one-step bound

For Hermitian $A$, $B$ and $\delta \gt 0$,

$$\big\lVert e^{-i(A + B)\delta} - e^{-iA\delta}e^{-iB\delta}\big\rVert\le\tfrac12\lVert[A, B]\rVert\,\delta^2 .$$

The idea. Look at the Trotter step from the exact evolution's point of view: $F(s) = U(s)^\dagger V(s)$ starts at $I$, and its velocity is a conjugated copy of $G(s) - B$ — which lemma 2 says grows at most linearly. Integrate a linear bound once: $\delta^2/2$.

Checks. $[A, B] = 0$: zero, as unit 00 says. $B = 0$: zero, trivially. And the constant $\tfrac12$ matches the leading-order expansion, so it is the best possible.

a known case: $A = X$, $B = Z$, $\delta = 1$

$[X, Z] = XZ - ZX = -2iY$, so $\lVert[X, Z]\rVert = 2$ and the bound is $\tfrac12\cdot2\cdot1^2 = 1$.

The actual one-step error, computed: $0.799$ ✓. The bound is not empty even at $\delta = 1$: any two unitaries are within distance 2.

Dimensions. With $\hbar = 1$, $A$ and $B$ are angular frequencies, in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$; $[A, B]$ is in $\mathrm{s^{-2}}$, and $\lVert[A, B]\rVert\delta^2$ is a pure number, as a distance between unitaries must be ✓.

The proof is the next slide, every line written out as a worked example, because it is the checkpoint's proof.

The known case, computed. $e^{-i(X+Z)} = \cos\sqrt2\,I - i\sin\sqrt2\,(X + Z)/\sqrt2$, because $((X + Z)/\sqrt2)^2 = I$; and $e^{-iX}e^{-iZ} = (\cos1 - i\sin1\,X)(\cos1 - i\sin1\,Z)$. Worked: one qubit, H = X + Z shows how to compute the distance between two such products by hand; at $\delta = 1$ it is $0.7992$.

Other proofs. The same bound follows from the variation-of-parameters (Duhamel) formula $e^{-i(A+B)\delta} - e^{-iA\delta}e^{-iB\delta} = \int_0^\delta\frac{d}{ds}\big[e^{-i(A+B)(\delta - s)}e^{-iAs}e^{-iBs}\big]ds$, which is the same computation organised from the other end. Worked: the one-step bound, every line

worked example 1 · the one-step bound

1. Compare from the exact frame. $U(s) = e^{-i(A+B)s}$, $V(s) = e^{-iAs}e^{-iBs}$, and $F(s) = U(s)^\dagger V(s)$:

$$F(s) = e^{i(A+B)s}e^{-iAs}e^{-iBs}.$$

$F(0) = I$, and $\lVert V(\delta) - U(\delta)\rVert = \lVert U(\delta)(F(\delta) - I)\rVert = \lVert F(\delta) - I\rVert$ by unitary invariance.

2. Differentiate the three factors (product rule), with $P = e^{i(A+B)s}$, $Q = e^{-iBs}$:

$$F' = iP\big[(A{+}B)e^{-iAs} - Ae^{-iAs} - e^{-iAs}B\big]Q$$

— the middle term because $A$ commutes with $e^{-iAs}$. The $A$ terms cancel:

$$F' = iP\big[Be^{-iAs} - e^{-iAs}B\big]Q .$$

3. Recognise lemma 2. Pull $e^{-iAs}$ out to the left of the bracket:

$$Be^{-iAs} - e^{-iAs}B = e^{-iAs}\big(G(s) - B\big),$$

$G(s) = e^{iAs}Be^{-iAs}$. So $F'(s) = i\,U(s)^\dagger e^{-iAs}\big(G(s) - B\big)e^{-iBs}$, a unitary sandwich, and

$$\lVert F'(s)\rVert = \lVert G(s) - B\rVert\le s\,\lVert[A, B]\rVert .$$

4. Integrate. $F(\delta) - I = \int_0^\delta F'(s)\,ds$ (fundamental theorem), and lemma 1:

$$\lVert F(\delta) - I\rVert\le\int_0^\delta s\,\lVert[A, B]\rVert\,ds ,$$

which is $\tfrac12\lVert[A, B]\rVert\,\delta^2$. ∎

5. Check: $\delta\to0$ gives $0$; $[A, B] = 0$ gives $F' = 0$, $F\equiv I$ ✓.

Step 2 in full. Unit 01 gives $\tfrac{d}{ds}e^{i(A+B)s} = e^{i(A+B)s}\,i(A + B)$, $\tfrac{d}{ds}e^{-iAs} = -iAe^{-iAs}$ and $\tfrac{d}{ds}e^{-iBs} = e^{-iBs}(-iB) = -iBe^{-iBs}$. The product rule for three factors $PQR$ is $P'QR + PQ'R + PQR'$:

$$F' = e^{i(A+B)s}i(A + B)e^{-iAs}e^{-iBs} + e^{i(A+B)s}(-iA)e^{-iAs}e^{-iBs} + e^{i(A+B)s}e^{-iAs}(-iB)e^{-iBs}.$$

Factor $e^{i(A+B)s}$ on the left and $e^{-iBs}$ on the right; inside: $i(A + B)e^{-iAs} - iAe^{-iAs} - ie^{-iAs}B = iBe^{-iAs} - ie^{-iAs}B$.

Step 3 in full. $e^{-iAs}(G(s) - B) = e^{-iAs}e^{iAs}Be^{-iAs} - e^{-iAs}B = Be^{-iAs} - e^{-iAs}B$ ✓. The operator norm of $i\cdot U^\dagger\cdot e^{-iAs}\cdot(G - B)\cdot e^{-iBs}$ is $\lVert G - B\rVert$, by unit 11's property (b), the factor $i$ having modulus 1.

Where Hermiticity was used. Only to make $e^{\pm iAs}$, $e^{-iBs}$ and $U(s)$ unitary, so that they drop out of norms. For general matrices the same computation gives a bound with factors like $e^{2s\lVert A\rVert}$ in it.

Why $s \gt 0$. Lemma 2 gives $|s|$; on $[0, \delta]$ that is $s$, and $\int_0^\delta s\,ds = \delta^2/2$.

r steps: the errors add

theorem: the first-order Trotter bound

For Hermitian $A$, $B$, $t \gt 0$ and $r$ steps,

$$\big\lVert e^{-i(A + B)t} - (e^{-iAt/r}e^{-iBt/r})^r\big\rVert$$ $$\le\frac{\lVert[A, B]\rVert\,t^2}{2r}.$$
proof

With $\delta = t/r$: $e^{-i(A+B)t} = U(\delta)^r$ (unit 00's group law). Unit 11's hybrid argument: $\lVert U^r - V^r\rVert\le r\lVert U - V\rVert$. The one-step bound: $\le r\cdot\tfrac12\lVert[A, B]\rVert(t/r)^2$. ∎

Checks. $r\to\infty$: the product converges to $e^{-i(A + B)t}$ — the Lie product formula. $[A, B] = 0$: exact for every $r$ ✓.

To reach accuracy $\varepsilon$, take $\ r\ge\lVert[A, B]\rVert t^2/2\varepsilon$: steps grow like $t^2$ and $1/\varepsilon$.

The hybrid argument, $r = 3$: replace one step at a time. Neighbouring rows differ in one step, so by at most the one-step error; three of them add.

Full proof of the telescoping, recalled

Unit 11: for unitaries, $\lVert U_r\cdots U_1 - V_r\cdots V_1\rVert\le\sum_k\lVert U_k - V_k\rVert$. With $W_k = U^{r-k}V^k$ (the first $k$ steps replaced), $U^r - V^r = \sum_{k=1}^r(W_{k-1} - W_k)$ and $W_{k-1} - W_k = U^{r-k}(U - V)V^{k-1}$, each of norm exactly $\lVert U - V\rVert$. So $\lVert U^r - V^r\rVert\le r\lVert U - V\rVert$.

The Lie product formula. For matrices, $\lim_{r\to\infty}(e^{X/r}e^{Y/r})^r = e^{X+Y}$ for any $X, Y$ is due to Lie (for matrix groups, 1870s); Trotter extended it to operators on infinite-dimensional spaces ("On the product of semi-groups of operators", Proc. AMS 10, 545, 1959), which is why physicists say "Trotterise". The theorem here is the finite-dimensional unitary case with an explicit rate.

Scaling, read off. Doubling $t$ at fixed $r$ quadruples the bound; doubling $t$ at fixed accuracy quadruples $r$. Halving $\varepsilon$ doubles $r$. For a simulation whose cost is proportional to $r$, the cost of first-order Trotter is $O(\lVert[A, B]\rVert t^2/\varepsilon)$ steps. Section 3 does better in $t$ and $\varepsilon$; section 4 counts what $\lVert[A, B]\rVert$ costs in $n$.

The order of the factors does not matter for the bound. $e^{-iBt/r}e^{-iAt/r}$ has the same guarantee, swapping the roles of $A$ and $B$, since $\lVert[B, A]\rVert = \lVert[A, B]\rVert$.

Many terms: count the pairs that clash

theorem: $L$ terms

For Hermitian $H_1, \dots, H_L$ with sum $H$, and $\delta \gt 0$,

$$\big\lVert e^{-iH\delta} - e^{-iH_1\delta}\cdots e^{-iH_L\delta}\big\rVert$$ $$\le\frac{\delta^2}2\sum_{j \lt k}\lVert[H_j, H_k]\rVert ,$$

so $r$ steps cost at most $\dfrac{t^2}{2r}\displaystyle\sum_{j \lt k}\lVert[H_j, H_k]\rVert$.

proof, by induction on $L$

Split $H = H_1 + R$, $R = H_2 + \cdots + H_L$. The one-step bound with $[H_1, R] = \sum_{k \gt 1}[H_1, H_k]$ gives $\tfrac{\delta^2}2\sum_{k \gt 1}\lVert[H_1, H_k]\rVert$; the induction hypothesis, inside $\lVert e^{-iH_1\delta}(\cdots)\rVert$, gives the rest. Triangle inequality. ∎

What the sum counts. Commuting pairs contribute nothing. For a geometrically local $H$ each term clashes with $O(1)$ others, so the sum is $O(L)$ — not $O(L^2)$.

Check, $L = 2$: the sum is the single term $\lVert[H_1, H_2]\rVert$: the one-step bound ✓.

Check, the chain: taking all $2n - 1$ terms separately, the sum counts exactly the 14 lines of the clash figure at $n = 8$, since within each group everything commutes. Grouping into $A$ and $B$ gives $\lVert[A, B]\rVert$, which that same count bounds.

the order of the terms

Any fixed order works with the same bound. Different orders give different errors; the bound does not see it.

Full proof

For $L = 1$ there is nothing to prove; for $L = 2$ it is the one-step bound. Suppose it holds for $L - 1$ terms. Let $R = H_2 + \cdots + H_L$ and $W = e^{-iH_2\delta}\cdots e^{-iH_L\delta}$. Then

$$\lVert e^{-iH\delta} - e^{-iH_1\delta}W\rVert\le\lVert e^{-iH\delta} - e^{-iH_1\delta}e^{-iR\delta}\rVert + \lVert e^{-iH_1\delta}(e^{-iR\delta} - W)\rVert .$$

The first term is at most $\tfrac{\delta^2}2\lVert[H_1, R]\rVert\le\tfrac{\delta^2}2\sum_{k\ge2}\lVert[H_1, H_k]\rVert$ by the one-step bound, $[H_1, \cdot\,]$ being linear, and the triangle inequality. The second equals $\lVert e^{-iR\delta} - W\rVert$ by unitary invariance, which by the hypothesis is at most $\tfrac{\delta^2}2\sum_{2\le j \lt k}\lVert[H_j, H_k]\rVert$. Adding gives every pair $j \lt k$ once. The $r$-step version follows from the hybrid argument as before.

The chain, in both groupings. As two terms $A, B$ the sum is $\lVert[A, B]\rVert$, bounded by counting on the next slide by $\sum_{\text{clashing pairs}}\lVert[H_j, H_k]\rVert$. As $2n - 1$ terms in the order "all bonds, then all sites" the sum over pairs is exactly that same count, since pairs inside a group commute. So the separate-terms bound equals the counted bound on the grouped one; the grouped bound with the exact $\lVert[A, B]\rVert$ is sharper ($19.0$ against $28$ at $n = 8$).

1. Expand. $[A, B] = Jh\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}[Z_iZ_{i+1}, X_j]$ — bilinearity, the two minus signs cancelling.

2. Most terms vanish. If $j\notin\{i, i + 1\}$ the two act on different qubits and commute.

$$\lVert[A, B]\rVert\le4(n - 1)\,Jh .$$

$$r\ge\frac{4(n - 1)t^2}{2\varepsilon} = \frac{2(n - 1)t^2}{\varepsilon}.$$

At $n = 8$, $t = 5$, $\varepsilon = 0.01$: $r\ge2\cdot7\cdot25/0.01 = 35{,}000$. With the exact $19.0$: $23{,}794$.

Step 1. $[-JP, -hQ] = Jh[P, Q]$, and the commutator is linear in each slot, so $[\sum_iP_i, \sum_jQ_j] = \sum_{i,j}[P_i, Q_j]$.

Step 3 in full. $Z_iZ_{i+1}X_i - X_iZ_iZ_{i+1} = (Z_iX_i - X_iZ_i)Z_{i+1}$, since $Z_{i+1}$ commutes with both. On qubit $i$, $ZX - XZ = iY - (-iY) = 2iY$. So $[Z_iZ_{i+1}, X_i] = 2iY_iZ_{i+1}$. The same with the roles of $i$ and $i + 1$ exchanged gives $2iZ_iY_{i+1}$. Hence, for $J = h = 1$, $[A, B] = 2i\sum_{i=0}^{n-2}(Y_iZ_{i+1} + Z_iY_{i+1})$.

This is the bound that ignores locality; it is what an analysis in terms of norms rather than commutators gives, and it costs a factor $n/2$ here.

Strang's symmetric splitting

the Strang splitting (second-order product formula) $$S(\delta) = e^{-iA\delta/2}\,e^{-iB\delta}\,e^{-iA\delta/2}.$$
theorem: second order

$\lVert S(\delta) - e^{-i(A+B)\delta}\rVert = O(\delta^3)$, so $r$ steps cost $O(t^3/r^2)$.

Why: the $\delta^2$ terms now agree. With $a = -iA$, $b = -iB$, the $\delta^2$ coefficient of $S$ is

$$\tfrac{a^2}8 + \tfrac{b^2}2 + \tfrac{a^2}8 + \tfrac{ab}2 + \tfrac{ba}2 + \tfrac{a^2}4 = \tfrac{(a + b)^2}2 ,$$

exactly that of $e^{(a+b)\delta}$. The remainder is bounded in the notes: per step at most $\tfrac13(\lVert A\rVert + \lVert B\rVert)^3\delta^3e^{(\lVert A\rVert + \lVert B\rVert)\delta}$.

Three steps of each. Adjacent half-steps of $A$ merge, so $r$ Strang steps cost one $A$-layer more than $r$ first-order steps.

the constant (cited)

$\lVert S(\delta) - e^{-i(A+B)\delta}\rVert\le\dfrac{\delta^3}{12}\lVert[B, [B, A]]\rVert + \dfrac{\delta^3}{24}\lVert[A, [A, B]]\rVert$.

Full proof of second order

The coefficients. $S(\delta) = (I + \tfrac a2\delta + \tfrac{a^2}8\delta^2 + \cdots)(I + b\delta + \tfrac{b^2}2\delta^2 + \cdots)(I + \tfrac a2\delta + \tfrac{a^2}8\delta^2 + \cdots)$. Order $\delta$: $\tfrac a2 + b + \tfrac a2 = a + b$. Order $\delta^2$, taking one $\delta^2$ factor or two $\delta$ factors in order: $\tfrac{a^2}8$ (left), $\tfrac{b^2}2$ (middle), $\tfrac{a^2}8$ (right), $\tfrac a2b$ (left, middle), $\tfrac a2\cdot\tfrac a2$ (left, right), $b\tfrac a2$ (middle, right). Total $\tfrac{a^2}2 + \tfrac{b^2}2 + \tfrac12(ab + ba) = \tfrac12(a + b)^2$ ✓.

The remainder. Let $\alpha = \lVert A\rVert + \lVert B\rVert$. Multiplying the three series out, $S(\delta) = \sum_{p,q,s\ge0}\frac{(a\delta/2)^p(b\delta)^q(a\delta/2)^s}{p!\,q!\,s!}$, and the terms with $p + q + s\ge3$ have total norm at most $\sum_{p+q+s\ge3}\frac{(\lVert A\rVert\delta/2)^p(\lVert B\rVert\delta)^q(\lVert A\rVert\delta/2)^s}{p!\,q!\,s!} = \sum_{m\ge3}\frac{(\alpha\delta)^m}{m!}$ by the multinomial theorem (the three numbers add to $\alpha\delta$). The same bound holds for the terms of degree $\ge3$ in $e^{(a+b)\delta}$, since $\lVert A + B\rVert\le\alpha$. The terms of degree $\le2$ agree. So

$$\lVert S(\delta) - e^{-i(A+B)\delta}\rVert\le2\sum_{m\ge3}\frac{(\alpha\delta)^m}{m!}\le2\cdot\frac{(\alpha\delta)^3}{6}\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(\alpha\delta)^k}{k!} = \frac{(\alpha\delta)^3e^{\alpha\delta}}3 ,$$

using $(k + 3)!\ge6\,k!$. With the hybrid argument, $r$ steps cost at most $\frac{\alpha^3t^3}{3r^2}e^{\alpha t/r}$: order $t^3/r^2$ ✓. All rearrangements are of absolutely convergent series. The same argument applied to first order gives $O(t^2/r)$ again — but with $\alpha^2$ where the theorem has the commutator.

Why the constant is cited, and what it says. The remainder bound above depends on $\lVert A\rVert + \lVert B\rVert$, which for the chain is $15$ at $n = 8$ and grows with $n$: true, and useless numerically. The nested-commutator bound is proved by the same method as the one-step bound — differentiate, integrate twice — with more bookkeeping (Childs, Su, Tran, Wiebe and Zhu, PRX 11, 011020, 2021, cited). For the chain the nested commutators are again sums of few local terms: counting as in worked example 3 gives $\lVert[B, [B, A]]\rVert$ and $\lVert[A, [A, B]]\rVert\le16(n - 1)$ at $J = h = 1$, so the per-step error is at most $2(n - 1)\delta^3$.

History. Strang, "On the construction and comparison of difference schemes", SIAM J. Numer. Anal. 5, 506 (1968), for splitting partial differential equations. The same symmetric splitting is the leapfrog (Störmer–Verlet) integrator of classical mechanics — see the close.

Higher orders, and sharper bounds

Suzuki formulas. From Strang's $S_2 = S$, a recursion builds $S_{2k}$ with error $O(\delta^{2k+1})$ per step (Suzuki 1991, cited), using about $2\cdot5^{k-1}$ exponentials of $A$ and $B$ per step. Then $r = O(t^{1 + 1/2k}/\varepsilon^{1/2k})$.

Commutator scaling. For a $p$-th order formula the error is at most a constant times $\tilde\alpha\,t^{p+1}/r^p$, where $\tilde\alpha$ sums the norms of the nested commutators of $p + 1$ terms (Childs, Su, Tran, Wiebe, Zhu 2021, cited). For $n$ spins with nearest-neighbour terms, $\tilde\alpha = O(n)$: total gates $O\big(n^{1+1/p}t^{1+1/p}/\varepsilon^{1/p}\big)$.

Beyond products. Quantum signal processing (Low and Chuang 2017) and qubitisation (Low and Chuang 2019) reach $O\big(\lambda t + \log(1/\varepsilon)\big)$ uses of a circuit encoding $H$, $\lambda$ the sum of $|$coefficients$|$ in its Pauli expansion: optimal in both $t$ and $\varepsilon$ (cited).

"Norm scaling" bounds $\lVert[A, B]\rVert$ by $2\lVert A\rVert\lVert B\rVert$: $112$ for the chain, growing like $n^2$. Counting commutators: $28$, like $n$. The truth: $19.0$ — and the actual error is still $16.5$ times below the bound.

Suzuki's recursion (J. Math. Phys. 32, 400, 1991): $S_{2k}(\delta) = S_{2k-2}(u_k\delta)^2\,S_{2k-2}((1 - 4u_k)\delta)\,S_{2k-2}(u_k\delta)^2$ with $u_k = 1/(4 - 4^{1/(2k-1)})$. The middle step runs backwards in time ($1 - 4u_k \lt 0$), which is why the order can rise. Each level multiplies the number of stages by 5; the constants grow quickly, so in practice orders 2 and 4 are used most. Not proved here.

Where the step count comes from. An error $C t^{p+1}/r^p\le\varepsilon$ needs $r\ge(Ct^{p+1}/\varepsilon)^{1/p} = C^{1/p}t^{1+1/p}/\varepsilon^{1/p}$. As $p$ grows the time dependence approaches linear — which is optimal: one cannot simulate time $t$ with $o(t)$ effort in general (the "no fast-forwarding" theorem of Berry, Ahokas, Cleve and Sanders, Comm. Math. Phys. 270, 359, 2007, cited).

Commutator scaling, precisely. Childs et al. prove $\lVert S_p(t/r)^r - e^{-iHt}\rVert = O(\tilde\alpha_{\mathrm{comm}}t^{p+1}/r^p)$ with $\tilde\alpha_{\mathrm{comm}} = \sum_{j_1, \dots, j_{p+1}}\lVert[H_{j_{p+1}}, \cdots[H_{j_2}, H_{j_1}]\cdots]\rVert$. For a lattice Hamiltonian each nested commutator is nonzero only when the terms form a connected cluster, so the sum is $O(n)$, and the gate count $L\cdot r$ becomes $O(n\cdot n^{1/p}t^{1+1/p}/\varepsilon^{1/p})$. With norms instead of commutators the same analysis gives $(nt)^{1+1/p}$ steps — $n$ times worse in the steps.

Qubitisation and QSP work not with $e^{-iH_j\delta}$ but with a unitary that has $H/\lambda$ as a block (a "block encoding", built from controlled Paulis when $H$ is a sum of Pauli products), and use a sequence of single-qubit rotations to transform its eigenvalues by a polynomial approximating $e^{-i\lambda t x}$. Low and Chuang, PRL 118, 010501 (2017) and Quantum 3, 163 (2019). The precise complexity is $O(\lambda t + \log(1/\varepsilon)/\log\log(1/\varepsilon))$. None of this is used later in the course.

Better bounds for product formulas still. The error measured against a particular input state, or averaged over steps that rotate each other's errors, can be far smaller than the operator-norm worst case; analyses that exploit this are an active subject. For the chain at $n = 8$ they would have to account for most of the factor $16.5$.

worked example 5 · $e^{-i\theta Z\otimes Z} = \mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\cdot(I\otimes R_z(2\theta))\cdot\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}$

1. The left side on $\ket{ab}$. $Z\otimes Z\ket{ab} = (-1)^{a\oplus b}\ket{ab}$, so by the functional calculus

$$e^{-i\theta Z\otimes Z}\ket{ab} = e^{-i\theta(-1)^{a\oplus b}}\ket{ab}.$$

2. The circuit on $\ket{ab}$, gate by gate: CNOT gives $\ket{a, a\oplus b}$; $R_z(2\theta) = \mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\theta}, e^{i\theta}) = e^{-i\theta Z}$ on qubit 1 gives the phase $e^{-i\theta(-1)^{a\oplus b}}$; CNOT restores $\ket{ab}$.

3. As matrices, in the basis $00, 01, 10, 11$: $I\otimes R_z(2\theta) = \mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\theta}, e^{i\theta}, e^{-i\theta}, e^{i\theta})$, and conjugating by CNOT swaps the last two entries:

$$\mathrm{diag}(e^{-i\theta}, e^{i\theta}, e^{i\theta}, e^{-i\theta}) = e^{-i\theta Z\otimes Z}\ \checkmark$$

Exactly — not up to global phase. Check, $\theta = \tfrac\pi2$: both sides are $-i\,Z\otimes Z$. For the chain: $e^{iJ\delta Z_iZ_{i+1}}$ is $\theta = -J\delta$, so $R_z(-2J\delta)$.

Step 2, the CNOT. $\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\ket{ab} = \ket{a, a\oplus b}$ (unit 10), so as a matrix it fixes $\ket{00}, \ket{01}$ and swaps $\ket{10}\leftrightarrow\ket{11}$. A diagonal matrix conjugated by a permutation matrix has its entries permuted the same way: $\mathrm{CNOT}\,D\,\mathrm{CNOT}$ is $D$ with entries 3 and 4 exchanged. Unitarity is automatic: a product of unitaries.

The check at $\theta = \pi/2$. $e^{-i\frac\pi2Z\otimes Z} = \cos\tfrac\pi2I - i\sin\tfrac\pi2Z\otimes Z = -iZ\otimes Z$ (unit 00's formula, $(Z\otimes Z)^2 = I$). Any Pauli product: change basis, compute the parity

$e^{-i\theta X\otimes Y\otimes Z}$: Cliffords turn each factor into $Z$, two CNOTs collect the parity onto one wire, one $z$-rotation, then everything undone.

a Pauli-product rotation

For $P = P_1\otimes\cdots\otimes P_k$, each $P_j\in\{X, Y, Z\}$: $e^{-i\theta P}$ costs one $R_z(2\theta)$, $2(k - 1)$ CNOTs, and at most $4k$ single-qubit Cliffords.

Proof. The CNOT ladder maps $\ket x$ to $\ket{x_1, \dots, x_{k-1}, x_1\oplus\cdots\oplus x_k}$, so the circuit for $Z^{\otimes k}$ applies $e^{-i\theta(-1)^{\oplus x}}$, which is $e^{-i\theta Z^{\otimes k}}$. With $C$ the basis change ($H$ for $X$, $HS^\dagger$ for $Y$), $CPC^\dagger = Z^{\otimes k}$, and $e^{-i\theta C^\dagger MC} = C^\dagger e^{-i\theta M}C$. ∎

The basis changes. $HXH = Z$ (unit 02). For $Y$: $SXS^\dagger = Y$ (multiply out: $\mathrm{diag}(1, i)\,X\,\mathrm{diag}(1, -i)$ has entries $-i$ above and $i$ below the diagonal), so $S^\dagger YS = X$ and $(HS^\dagger)Y(HS^\dagger)^\dagger = HS^\dagger YSH = HXH = Z$. In the circuit, time runs left to right, so $C = HS^\dagger$ appears as $S^\dagger$ then $H$, and $C^\dagger = SH$ as $H$ then $S$.

The functional calculus under conjugation. If $M = \sum\lambda P_\lambda$ then $C^\dagger MC = \sum\lambda\,C^\dagger P_\lambda C$, a spectral decomposition with the same eigenvalues, so $f(C^\dagger MC) = C^\dagger f(M)C$. Here $P = C^\dagger Z^{\otimes k}C$, so $e^{-i\theta P} = C^\dagger e^{-i\theta Z^{\otimes k}}C$, and $C^\dagger$, applied last, is the right-hand block of the figure.

The count. $k - 1$ CNOTs compute the parity and $k - 1$ uncompute it. The basis changes cost up to two Cliffords per qubit on each side, $4k$ in all. All of these are Clifford gates (unit 11); the single $R_z(2\theta)$ is the only gate that needs approximating, and the only one that costs T gates.

Checked numerically for $\theta = 0.37$: the circuit's matrix equals $e^{-i\theta X\otimes Y\otimes Z}$ to machine precision. Problem P9 asks for the proof on paper.

Controlled versions are almost free. To control $e^{-i\theta P}$ on another qubit — as section 5 must — control only the middle rotation: if the control is off, the outer blocks are $C^\dagger\cdot\text{ladder}^\dagger\cdot\text{ladder}\cdot C = I$.

One Trotter step of the chain, as a circuit

$n = 4$. The field layer, then the bonds in two layers: even bonds together, odd bonds together. Depth does not grow with $n$.

Per step, for $n$ spins: $n - 1$ bond blocks (2 CNOTs, one $R_z$ each) and $n$ field rotations $R_x(-2h\delta) = HR_z(-2h\delta)H$:

$$L = 2n - 1\ \text{rotations},\qquad 2(n - 1)\ \text{CNOTs}.$$

For the chain at $n = 8$: 15 rotations and 14 CNOTs per step. Check, computed: the product of these 13 gates' matrices at $n = 4$ equals $e^{-iA\delta}e^{-iB\delta}$ to machine precision.

Which product the circuit is. Time runs left to right, so the first layer applied is the field: the circuit is $e^{-iA\delta}e^{-iB\delta}$, with $e^{-iB\delta}$ acting first — exactly the Trotter step of section 2. The bond blocks commute with one another, so their order inside the $A$-layer is free; grouping even and odd bonds lets non-overlapping blocks run at the same time.

Depth. Three layers per step whatever $n$ is: field rotations, even bonds, odd bonds. Counting each bond block as depth 3 (CNOT, rotation, CNOT), one step has depth $1 + 3 + 3 = 7$ — independent of $n$. A simulation of $r$ steps has depth $7r$.

Locality pays twice. Once in the commutator, which grows like $n$ rather than $n^2$; once in parallelism, since terms on disjoint qubits run simultaneously.

Gate counts for a k-local Hamiltonian

The recipe. Write each term as a sum of Pauli products (always possible: they are a basis, unit 02 and 03) — say $L$ products in all, each on at most $k$ qubits. Per first-order step: $L$ rotations and at most $2(k - 1)L$ CNOTs.

The steps. $r = \lceil\Lambda t^2/2\varepsilon\rceil$ with $\Lambda = \sum_{j \lt l}\lVert[H_j, H_l]\rVert$. If each term clashes with at most $c$ others and $\lVert H_j\rVert\le1$, then $\Lambda\le cL$.

Lloyd (1996)

A $k$-local Hamiltonian on $n$ qubits, with $L$ terms of norm $\le1$ each clashing with at most $c$ others, is simulated to accuracy $\varepsilon$ with

$$O\!\Big(\frac{cL^2t^2}{\varepsilon}\Big)\ \text{rotations,}$$

and $O(k)$ times as many CNOTs — polynomial in $n$, $t$ and $1/\varepsilon$, since $c \lt L$. Feynman's simulator exists.

For the chain, $L = 2n - 1$ and $\Lambda\le4(n - 1)$: $O(n^2t^2/\varepsilon)$ rotations at first order.

a 50-spin chain, $t = 10$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$

First order: $r\ge\dfrac{4\cdot49\cdot10^2}{2\cdot10^{-3}} = 9.8\times10^6$ steps; $99$ rotations each: $9.7\times10^8$ rotations.

Strang, with the counted constant $2(n - 1)\delta^3$ per step: $\ 2\cdot49\cdot10^3/r^2\le10^{-3}$ gives $r\ge9{,}900$ — a thousand times fewer.

Classically: 18 PB, and no way to check either number.

Every operator is a sum of Pauli products. The $4^k$ Pauli products on $k$ qubits are orthogonal in the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product (unit 02 for $k = 1$; tensor products for more), so they are a basis of the operators on $k$ qubits, with real coefficients for a Hermitian operator. A $k$-local term therefore becomes at most $4^k - 1$ Pauli products (dropping the identity, which only adds a global phase), a constant for $k = O(1)$.

The bound $\Lambda\le cL$. Each pair $j \lt l$ that clashes contributes $\lVert[H_j, H_l]\rVert\le2\lVert H_j\rVert\lVert H_l\rVert\le2$, and there are at most $cL/2$ such pairs. Then $r\cdot L = O(cL^2t^2/\varepsilon)$: $O(L^2t^2/\varepsilon)$ for geometrically local terms, where $c = O(1)$, and never worse than $O(L^3t^2/\varepsilon)$, since $c \lt L$. The CNOT count is at most $2(k - 1)$ per rotation.

The 50-spin numbers. $4\cdot49\cdot100 = 19{,}600$; divided by $2\times10^{-3}$: $9.8\times10^6$. Rotations per step: $2\cdot50 - 1 = 99$; total $9.70\times10^8$. Strang: per-step bound $\delta^3\big(\tfrac{16(n-1)}{12} + \tfrac{16(n-1)}{24}\big) = 2(n - 1)\delta^3$ from the cited constant and the counted nested commutators; over $r$ steps $2(n - 1)t^3/r^2$; solve $98\times1000/r^2\le10^{-3}$: $r\ge\sqrt{9.8\times10^7} = 9{,}899.5$, so $9{,}900$.

History. Lloyd's 1996 paper gave the construction and the polynomial count, settling Feynman's question of whether local quantum systems can be efficiently simulated by a universal quantum computer. The counts here are the textbook form of his argument with commutators in place of norms (Nielsen and Chuang, §4.7).

Energies are eigenphases of the evolution

From unit 01: the eigenvalues of $H$ are its energies. If $H\ket E = E\ket E$, the functional calculus gives

$$e^{-iH\tau}\ket E = e^{-iE\tau}\ket E = e^{2\pi i\varphi}\ket E ,$$ $$\varphi = -\frac{E\tau}{2\pi}\bmod1 .$$
ground state, ground energy

An eigenvector of $H$ for its least eigenvalue $E_0$. Variational principle: $\braket{\psi|H|\psi} = \sum_s E_s|c_s|^2\ge E_0$ for every unit $\psi$.

Choosing $\tau$. If every energy lies in $[-\lambda, \lambda]$ and $\tau \lt \pi/\lambda$, then $\varphi$ lies in $(-\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$, the map is one-to-one, and $E = -2\pi\varphi/\tau$.

The chain: $\lambda = \sum_j\lVert H_j\rVert = 15$, so take $\tau = \pi/16$: $\ \varphi = -E/32$, and $E_0 = -9.838$ sits at $\varphi_0 = 0.3074$.

Energies wrapped onto the circle of eigenphases. With $\tau = \pi/16$ the whole range $[-15, 15]$ fits, leaving a gap around $\tfrac12$.

The variational principle, proved. Expand $\psi = \sum_sc_s\ket{E_s}$ in an orthonormal eigenbasis (spectral theorem, unit 00), $\sum|c_s|^2 = 1$. Then $\braket{\psi|H|\psi} = \sum_sE_s|c_s|^2$, an average of the energies with weights summing to 1, hence at least the smallest. Equality iff $\psi$ lies in the $E_0$-eigenspace. This is what the check on What the two terms want used: $-9.838\le-8$ and $\le-7$.

Why $\tau \lt \pi/\lambda$. Then $|E\tau|\le\lambda\tau \lt \pi$, so $-E\tau/2\pi\in(-\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$ and distinct energies give distinct phases. At $\tau = \pi/\lambda$ exactly, $E = \lambda$ and $E = -\lambda$ would both land on $\tfrac12$. The bound $\lambda = \sum\lVert H_j\rVert$ is free — for a sum of Pauli products it is the same $\lambda$ as qubitisation's, the sum of the coefficients' moduli — and a tighter one buys a larger $\tau$ and so more resolution per counting qubit.

Sign. With $\hbar = 1$ and evolution $e^{-iHt}$, negative energies have positive phase. Unit 15's table already listed this case, with eigenphase $-E\tau/2\pi$.

The ground energy at $n = 8$ was computed by diagonalising the $256\times256$ matrix: $E_0 = -9.83795$, next $E_1 = -9.46888$, a gap of $0.369$. At $n = 50$ nobody can do that, which is the point of this section.

The circuit, and what it costs

Unit 15's circuit with $U = e^{-iH\tau}$: counting qubit $l$ controls $U^{2^{m-1-l}} = e^{-iH\,2^{m-1-l}\tau}$, each built from Trotter steps with controlled rotations.

a clash of letters

Unit 15 wrote $t$ for the number of counting qubits. Here $t$ is time, so the counting register has $m$ qubits.

Precision. The nearest $m$-bit fraction, with probability $\ge4/\pi^2$, is within $2^{-m-1}$ of $\varphi$: $|\tilde E - E|\le\dfrac{2\pi}\tau2^{-m-1}$. For the chain: $16/2^m$.

Cost. The controlled powers simulate total time $(2^m - 1)\tau$; at $m = 11$, $2047\pi/16 = 402$ — eighty times the $t = 5$ of section 3.

Errors. If the simulated powers are within $\eta$ in all, every outcome probability moves by at most $2\eta$ (unit 11).

Phases matter here: a global phase on $U$ shifts every eigenphase — every energy — by the same amount.

The precision, from unit 15. The $4/\pi^2$ theorem gives the integer $y^*$ nearest $2^m\varphi$ with probability at least $0.405$, and then $|y^*/2^m - \varphi|\le2^{-m-1}$. Multiply by $2\pi/\tau$ to convert phase to energy. With $\tau = \pi/16$: $2\pi/\tau = 32$ and the energy error is at most $32\cdot2^{-m-1} = 16/2^m$. Unit 15's second theorem gives confidence $1 - \varepsilon$ with $\lceil\log(2 + 1/2\varepsilon)\rceil$ extra counting qubits.

Controlling the Trotter circuit. $e^{-iH2^j\tau}$ is approximated by Trotter steps; making it controlled needs each rotation controlled, and nothing else, since the Clifford ladders cancel when the control is off (Any Pauli product, notes). A controlled rotation costs two CNOTs and two rotations of half the angle in the standard construction (unit 10), so the controlled simulation costs a small constant factor more.

Why a global phase is not harmless here. $e^{-i(H + c)\tau} = e^{-ic\tau}e^{-iH\tau}$. Uncontrolled, the factor $e^{-ic\tau}$ is invisible; controlled, it is a phase on the $\ket1$ branch of the counting qubit, i.e. a relative phase, which phase estimation reads as a shift of every $\varphi$ by $-c\tau/2\pi$ — every energy by $c$. That is consistent: $H + c$ has energies $E + c$. Dropping an identity term in $H$, or a synthesis phase, therefore shifts the reported energies, and the shift must be tracked.

The cost, honestly. Phase estimation's precision $2^{-m}$ costs time $\propto2^m$ — unit 15's $O(1/\varepsilon)$ uses — and here each use is a simulation whose cost grows with its time. Energies to many digits are expensive, and resource estimates for molecules of practical interest run to very large T counts (cited, e.g. Reiher et al., PNAS 114, 7555, 2017).

The catch: the input must overlap the ground state

Unit 15: on $\ket\psi = \sum_sc_s\ket{E_s}$, phase estimation behaves as if $E_s$ were drawn with probability $|c_s|^2$ and then estimated. So each run sees the ground energy with probability the ground-state overlap $|\braket{E_0|\psi}|^2$.

In general it can be hopeless. If the true ground state were a product and a trial state matched each spin with overlap $0.9$, the overlap would be $0.9^n$: $5\times10^{-3}$ at $n = 50$.

What phase estimation would sample from each input, by energy. From $\ket0^{\otimes8}$ the most likely reading is not even the ground energy.

Unit 20 shows this is not bad luck: deciding a local Hamiltonian's ground energy is as hard as anything a quantum computer can verify.

How to read a run. From $\ket{+}^{\otimes8}$, a run returns an estimate near $E_0$ with probability at least $0.565\times0.405 = 0.229$, and in practice $0.565\times0.62\approx0.35$ for the single nearest outcome. Since $E_0$ is the least energy, the smallest estimate seen over a few runs is the one to keep — every eigenvalue is at least $E_0$, so no run can report something meaningfully below $E_0$, beyond the phase-estimation tail.

Why $\ket0^{\otimes8}$ does badly. The chain commutes with $\prod_iX_i$, which flips every spin; its eigenvectors split into flip-symmetric and flip-antisymmetric ones. The ground state is symmetric. $\ket{0^8} = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{\mathrm{sym}} + \ket{\mathrm{anti}})$ with $\ket{\mathrm{sym}}, \ket{\mathrm{anti}}\propto\ket{0^8}\pm\ket{1^8}$, so at most half its weight can be on symmetric eigenvectors; the first excited state is the antisymmetric partner of the ground state and takes $0.211$. The cat state $(\ket{0^8} + \ket{1^8})/\sqrt2$ puts $0.232$ on $E_0$ — twice $0.116$, as this argument predicts.

The $0.9^n$ example. The overlap of two product states is the product of the single-spin overlaps (unit 03), so per-spin agreement $0.9$ gives $0.9^n$; $0.9^{50} = 0.0052$. For many-body ground states the decay with $n$ is typically exponential for any fixed product trial state — the "orthogonality catastrophe" — which is why state preparation, by adiabatic evolution or variational methods, is a subject of its own (named, not developed).

Forward pointer. Branch unit 20 proves Kitaev's theorem: estimating the ground energy of a 5-local Hamiltonian to $1/\mathrm{poly}$ accuracy is complete for the quantum analogue of NP. So no efficient method, quantum or classical, is expected to prepare good ground-state approximations for every local Hamiltonian. Dynamics is easy for a quantum computer; ground energies, in the worst case, are not.

the running example, answered

$n = 8$, $t = 5$, $J = h = 1$. $\lVert[A, B]\rVert\le28$ by counting ($19.0$ exactly). First order, 1000 steps: error $0.0144$ against the bound $0.238$. 15,000 rotations, 14,000 CNOTs, about $9.0\times10^5$ T gates, total error $\le0.029$. Strang reaches the same accuracy in 133 steps and about 2,000 rotations.

$n = 50$, $t = 10$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-3}$: $9.8\times10^6$ first-order steps by the bound, about $10^4$ for Strang — against 18 PB of classical memory.

Energies: $E_0 = -9.838$ read as $-9.844$ with 11 counting qubits — if the input overlaps the ground state.

The bound is pessimistic by $16.5\times$ here. At $n = 8$ that costs nothing; at $n = 50$ it is a factor in the circuit, which is why commutator-scaling analyses matter.

Dynamics is easy; ground energies are not. The simulation is polynomial; the input state for phase estimation is the hard part. Unit 20 makes that a theorem.

The classical picture. Mechanics to Relativity's unit 14 treats a Hamiltonian as generating a flow on phase space; splitting that flow into kinetic and potential steps, symmetrically, is the leapfrog integrator — Strang's splitting, one level down.

Read next. Nielsen and Chuang §4.7; Childs et al. (2021), sections I–III, for commutator scaling.

What this unit proved. The one-step bound and the $r$-step first-order bound, for two terms and for $L$; that Strang's splitting is second order, with an explicit if crude constant; the CNOT–rotation–CNOT identity and its $k$-qubit generalisation; the gate count for a local Hamiltonian; and the energy readout with its precision. What it cited: the nested-commutator constant for Strang, Suzuki's higher orders, commutator scaling, qubitisation and QSP, Ross–Selinger's T count, and the phase transition.

The classical cross-reference, with its convention. In Mechanics to Relativity unit 14, Hamilton's equations generate a flow; for $H = T(p) + V(q)$ the flows of $T$ and $V$ alone are exactly solvable (drift and kick), and alternating them — with half kicks at each end — is the Störmer–Verlet integrator, symplectic and second order for the same reason as here: the symmetric product agrees with the true flow through second order.
