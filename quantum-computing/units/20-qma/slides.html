QMA and the local Hamiltonian problem: Cook–Levin, quantum

A computation written into the lowest energy of a Hamiltonian, with a clock: Kitaev's theorem that 5-local Hamiltonian is QMA-complete, proved — so finding a ground energy, what a chemist most wants, is hard in the worst case even for a quantum computer.

needs 12 · 19~3 h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 20

You have proved Cook–Levin: any polynomial-time verifier can be written as a formula whose satisfiability is the verifier's acceptance, because a computation's tableau can be checked by local windows. This unit proves the quantum analogue. The verifier is a quantum circuit checking a quantum proof — the class QMA — and the formula is replaced by a Hamiltonian, a sum of terms each acting on five qubits, whose lowest eigenvalue is small if and only if some proof is accepted. The construction is Kitaev's (1999), building on Feynman's 1985 idea of a computer whose clock is part of its quantum state.

Unit 19 showed that a quantum computer simulates the dynamics $e^{-iHt}$ of a local Hamiltonian efficiently. This unit is the other side of that coin: deciding whether the lowest energy of a local Hamiltonian is below a threshold is QMA-complete, so unless QMA = BQP no quantum computer does it efficiently on every instance. The difference between the two problems, and why the second one is hard, is the content of the theorem.

20 · QMA

From certificates to quantum proofs

NP as a game (assumed background). An all-powerful but untrusted prover, Merlin, sends a string $y$; a polynomial-time verifier, Arthur, checks $R(x, y)$. If $x\in L$ some $y$ convinces him; if $x\notin L$ none does.

Two upgrades, one at a time. Let Arthur toss coins and err with probability $\le\tfrac13$: that is MA (Babai 1985). Let him run a quantum circuit (BQP, unit 12), and let Merlin send a quantum state: that is QMA.

why a state could help

A state on $p$ qubits takes $2^p$ complex amplitudes to write down. Whether a quantum proof can ever be replaced by a classical one of polynomial length is open; relative to a quantum oracle it cannot (Aaronson and Kuperberg 2007, cited).

The prover and the verifier. Only the bottom row is new.

Names. Babai introduced Arthur–Merlin games ("Trading group theory for randomness", STOC 1985): Merlin the wizard has unbounded power, Arthur the king is a polynomial-time machine with coins. MA is the one-message version. The quantum class was defined by Kitaev in lectures in 1999, under the name BQNP, and published with the proof of this unit's main theorem in Kitaev, Shen and Vyalyi, Classical and Quantum Computation (AMS 2002), chapter 14; the name QMA is Watrous's ("Succinct quantum proofs for properties of finite groups", FOCS 2000). Knill had considered quantum nondeterminism earlier (1996).

The open question. QCMA is QMA with a classical proof and a quantum verifier. Whether QMA = QCMA is open; Aaronson and Kuperberg ("Quantum versus classical proofs and advice", Theory of Computing 3, 129, 2007) give a quantum oracle relative to which they differ. Nothing in this unit depends on it; it is here so that "a quantum proof" is not mistaken for a mere notational change.

Why bother with quantum proofs. Because of the main theorem: the local Hamiltonian problem is to QMA what 3-SAT is to NP. A ground state is the natural proof that a ground energy is low, and a ground state is a quantum state that, in general, has no short classical description.

20 · QMA

QMA

QMA, quantum Merlin–Arthur

$L\in\mathsf{QMA}$ iff there are polynomials $p, a$ and a polynomial-time machine that, given $x$, prints a circuit $V_x$ over a fixed universal gate set, acting on $a(|x|)$ ancillas starting in $\ket0$ and a witness register of $p(|x|)$ qubits, such that, writing

$$\mathrm{acc}_x(\xi) = \big\lVert(\ket1\!\bra1_0\otimes I)\,V_x\ket{0^a}\ket\xi\big\rVert^2$$

for the probability that $V_x$ accepts $\xi$,

completeness: $x\in L \Rightarrow \mathrm{acc}_x(\xi)\ge\tfrac23$ for some unit $\ket\xi$;
soundness: $x\notin L \Rightarrow \mathrm{acc}_x(\xi)\le\tfrac13$ for every unit $\ket\xi$.

Qubit 0, an ancilla, is the output, as in unit 12. Soundness quantifies over all states — superpositions and entangled ones included.

A QMA verifier: the BQP circuit of unit 12 with a second input that nobody trusts.

check: probabilities

$\Pr[\text{accept}] + \Pr[\text{reject}] = \lVert\Pi_1 v\rVert^2 + \lVert\Pi_0 v\rVert^2 = \lVert v\rVert^2 = 1$ for $v = V_x\ket{0^a}\ket\xi$, a unit vector since $V_x$ is unitary ✓.

The pieces, as in unit 12. "Uniform": a deterministic machine prints $V_x$ from $x$ in polynomial time, so $V_x$ has polynomially many gates. The gate set is fixed and universal, for instance unit 12's set of $\mathrm{Had}$, CNOT and the $\pi/8$ gate $\mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\pi/4})$; by unit 12's argument (The gate set does not matter) the choice does not change the class. The input $x$ can be written into the ancillas by $X$ gates, which is why the definition can let $V_x$ depend on $x$ rather than take $\ket x$ as an input.

Pure witnesses suffice. One might let Merlin send a random mixture of states. By linearity the acceptance probability of a mixture is the average of the acceptance probabilities of its components, so some component does at least as well (completeness) and none does better than the worst bound (soundness). Unit 04's density operators say the same thing in one line; here we never need them.

The constants. $\tfrac23$ and $\tfrac13$ are arbitrary, as for BQP — but proving that is harder here, and is the business of the next four slides. A polynomial gap $c - s \ge 1/\mathrm{poly}$ is enough: Any gap will do, and Marriott–Watrous.

20 · QMA · the running example

The running example as a verifier

Gates 2–7 are $C(\mathrm{Had}) = \ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes\mathrm{Had}$ (unit 10). Push $\ket0\ket\xi$ through:

$$\begin{aligned} \xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{Had}_0\ }&\ \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket0\ket\xi + \ket1\ket\xi\big)\\ \xrightarrow{C(\mathrm{Had})}&\ \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket0\ket\xi + \ket1\,\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi\big)\\ \xrightarrow{\ \mathrm{Had}_0\ }&\ \tfrac12\big(\ket0(\xi + \mathrm{Had}\,\xi) + \ket1(\xi - \mathrm{Had}\,\xi)\big)\\ \xrightarrow{\ \ X_0\ \ }&\ \tfrac12\big(\ket0(\xi - \mathrm{Had}\,\xi) + \ket1(\xi + \mathrm{Had}\,\xi)\big) \end{aligned}$$

Accept $=$ qubit 0 reads 1: $\ \tfrac14\lVert\xi + \mathrm{Had}\,\xi\rVert^2 = \tfrac12\big(1 + \bra\xi\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi\big)$, since $\mathrm{Had}^\dagger\mathrm{Had} = I$ and $\bra\xi\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi$ is real.

For a real witness $\ket\xi = \cos\theta\ket0 + \sin\theta\ket1$: $\bra\xi\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi = \cos(2\theta - \tfrac\pi4)$, so $\Pr[\text{accept}] = \cos^2(\theta - \tfrac\pi8)$.

Acceptance is $\cos^2$ of the angle to $\ket{h_+} = \cos\tfrac\pi8\ket0 + \sin\tfrac\pi8\ket1$, the $+1$ eigenvector of $\mathrm{Had}$.

check

$\ket0$: $\tfrac12(1 + \tfrac1{\sqrt2}) = \cos^2\tfrac\pi8 = 0.854$ ✓. $\ \ket1$: $\tfrac12(1 - \tfrac1{\sqrt2}) = 0.146$; the two sum to 1, as orthogonal witnesses must here ✓. $\ \ket{h_+}$: 1, completeness with no error.

Full proof

The amplitude algebra. $\mathrm{Had}\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)$ and $\mathrm{Had}\ket1 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - \ket1)$ on qubit 0 give the third line; $X_0$ swaps $\ket0$ and $\ket1$ on qubit 0, giving the fourth. The probability that qubit 0 reads 1 is the squared norm of its $\ket1$ branch: $\tfrac14\lVert\xi + \mathrm{Had}\,\xi\rVert^2 = \tfrac14(\braket{\xi|\xi} + \bra\xi\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi + \bra\xi\mathrm{Had}^\dagger\ket\xi + \bra\xi\mathrm{Had}^\dagger\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi) = \tfrac14(2 + 2\bra\xi\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi)$, using $\mathrm{Had} = \mathrm{Had}^\dagger$ (so the expectation is real) and $\mathrm{Had}^2 = I$.

The angle formula. $\bra\xi\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\cos^2\theta - \sin^2\theta + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\cos2\theta + \sin2\theta) = \cos(2\theta - \tfrac\pi4)$. Then $\tfrac12(1 + \cos(2\theta - \tfrac\pi4)) = \cos^2(\theta - \tfrac\pi8)$ by the half-angle formula.

The eigenvector. $\mathrm{Had}(\cos\tfrac\pi8, \sin\tfrac\pi8) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\cos\tfrac\pi8 + \sin\tfrac\pi8,\ \cos\tfrac\pi8 - \sin\tfrac\pi8) = (\cos\tfrac\pi8, \sin\tfrac\pi8)$, using $\cos a + \sin a = \sqrt2\cos(a - \tfrac\pi4)$ and $\cos a - \sin a = \sqrt2\sin(\tfrac\pi4 - a)$ with $a = \tfrac\pi8$. So $\ket{h_+}$ is accepted with certainty. Complex witnesses do no better: $\bra\xi\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi \le 1$ for every unit $\ket\xi$ because $\mathrm{Had}$ has eigenvalues $\pm1$ (next slide).

What the verifier decides. Read as a family, this is the Hadamard test (unit 15) asking Merlin to exhibit a state on which a given gate $U$ looks like the identity: $\Pr[\text{accept}] = \tfrac12(1 + \mathrm{Re}\bra\xi U\ket\xi)$. For $U = \mathrm{Had}$ the answer is yes, with witness $\ket{h_+}$. Section 5 uses a sister in which the answer is no.

Positive operators, and the best witness

positive semidefinite

A Hermitian $A$ is positive semidefinite, $A\ge0$, if $\bra vA\ket v\ge0$ for every $v$ — equivalently, every eigenvalue is $\ge0$. Write $A\le B$ when $B - A\ge0$.

the variational principle

For Hermitian $A$ with least and greatest eigenvalues $\lambda_{\min}, \lambda_{\max}$, and $v$ ranging over unit vectors: $\ \lambda_{\min} = \min_v\bra vA\ket v\ $ and $\ \lambda_{\max} = \max_v\bra vA\ket v$.

proof

Expand $v = \sum_ic_ie_i$ in an eigenbasis (unit 00): $\bra vA\ket v = \sum_i\lambda_i|c_i|^2$, a weighted average with weights summing to 1 — between the extremes, and equal to each at its eigenvector. $\blacksquare$

the acceptance operator

With $J\ket\xi = \ket{0^a}\ket\xi$: $\ Q_x = J^\dagger V_x^\dagger\,(\ket1\!\bra1_0\otimes I)\,V_xJ$. Then $\Pr[\text{accept }\xi] = \bra\xi Q_x\ket\xi$, and $0\le Q_x\le I$.

So the best witness is accepted with probability $\lambda_{\max}(Q_x)$, and soundness says exactly $\lambda_{\max}(Q_x)\le\tfrac13$.

$\bra\xi Q\ket\xi$ is an average of $Q$'s eigenvalues.

Running example: $Q = \tfrac12(I + \mathrm{Had})$, a projection ($Q^2 = \tfrac14(I + 2\mathrm{Had} + I) = Q$ ✓), eigenvalues 1 at $\ket{h_+}$ and 0 at $\ket{h_-}$.

Full proof

The two forms of positivity agree. If every eigenvalue is $\ge0$ then $\bra vA\ket v = \sum\lambda_i|c_i|^2\ge0$. Conversely, taking $v$ an eigenvector gives $\lambda\lVert v\rVert^2\ge0$. A projection $P$ is positive: $\bra vP\ket v = \bra vP^\dagger P\ket v = \lVert Pv\rVert^2$. So is $C^\dagger C$ for any $C$, by the same line.

$Q_x$ is what it is claimed to be. $J$ is an isometry, $J^\dagger J = I$. Write $\Pi = \ket1\!\bra1_0\otimes I$. Then $\bra\xi Q_x\ket\xi = \bra\xi J^\dagger V^\dagger\Pi^\dagger\Pi VJ\ket\xi = \lVert\Pi VJ\xi\rVert^2$, the acceptance probability. It is Hermitian as a product $C^\dagger C$ with $C = \Pi VJ$, hence positive; and $\lVert\Pi VJ\xi\rVert\le\lVert\xi\rVert$ gives $\bra\xi(I - Q_x)\ket\xi\ge0$, so $Q_x\le I$.

"Soundness iff $\lambda_{\max}\le\tfrac13$" is the variational principle applied to $Q_x$; completeness is "$\lambda_{\max}\ge\tfrac23$", with the eigenvector as the best witness. The verifier's whole behaviour is one positive operator between 0 and $I$.

The running example's $Q$. From the previous slide, $\bra\xi Q\ket\xi = \tfrac12(1 + \bra\xi\mathrm{Had}\ket\xi)$ for every $\xi$; two Hermitian operators with the same expectation in every state are equal (polarisation, unit 00), so $Q = \tfrac12(I + \mathrm{Had})$. Its eigenvalues are $\tfrac12(1\pm1)$.

NP ⊆ QMA and BQP ⊆ QMA

theorem

$\mathsf{NP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}$ and $\mathsf{BQP}\subseteq\mathsf{QMA}$.

proof

NP. Unit 10 turns the predicate $R(x, \cdot)$ into a reversible circuit $\ket{0^a}\ket y\mapsto\ket{R(x,y)}\ket{0^{a-1}}\ket y$, garbage uncomputed. On $\ket\xi = \sum_y\alpha_y\ket y$ the outputs for different $y$ are orthogonal, so

$$\Pr[\text{accept}] = \textstyle\sum_y|\alpha_y|^2R(x, y).$$

$x\in L$: send $\ket y$ with $R = 1$, accepted with certainty. $x\notin L$: every $R(x, y) = 0$, so every $\xi$ is accepted with probability 0.

BQP. Let $V_x$ be the BQP circuit, ignoring the witness register: $\Pr[\text{accept}]$ does not depend on $\xi$. $\blacksquare$

Where QMA sits. The two coloured edges are this slide; MA ⊆ QMA is the same argument with coins from Hadamards (unit 12).

Upper bound, cited: $\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{PP}$ (Kitaev and Watrous 2000; a short proof in Marriott and Watrous 2005), so QMA is no bigger than unit 12's ceiling for BQP.

Full proof

NP ⊆ QMA, the orthogonality step. The reversible circuit $C$ maps each basis input $\ket{0^a}\ket y$ to $\ket{R(x,y)}\ket{0^{a-1}}\ket y$, which keeps $\ket y$; so $C\ket{0^a}\ket\xi = \sum_y\alpha_y\ket{R(x,y)}\ket{0^{a-1}}\ket y$, and projecting qubit 0 on $\ket1$ leaves $\sum_{y:\,R = 1}\alpha_y\ket1\ket{0^{a-1}}\ket y$, with squared norm $\sum_{y:R=1}|\alpha_y|^2$. In words, Arthur might as well measure the witness first: a superposition of certificates is accepted with the total weight of its valid certificates, and in a no-instance there are none.

The register sizes. $p(|x|)$ is the certificate length, $a$ the work space of the reversible circuit, both polynomial (unit 10: a polynomial-time computation becomes a polynomial-size reversible circuit with its garbage uncomputed).

MA ⊆ QMA is the same proof with Arthur's coins supplied by Hadamards on fresh ancillas, as in unit 12's BPP ⊆ BQP; and QMA's completeness and soundness constants are then inherited from MA's.

The upper bound. Kitaev and Watrous, "Parallelization, amplification, and exponential time simulation of quantum interactive proof systems", STOC 2000, prove QMA ⊆ PP; Marriott and Watrous (Computational Complexity 14, 122, 2005) give a short proof using their in-place amplification. The proof is a GapP argument of the kind unit 12 used for BQP ⊆ PP, with the witness summed over a basis. It is cited here, not proved.

20 · QMA · amplification

Amplification is not free: the witness cannot be copied

For BQP (unit 12) Arthur reruns the circuit $k$ times on fresh $\ket{x, 0}$ and takes the majority. For QMA each run measures — and so changes — the witness. A second run needs a second copy.

no-cloning, the two lines needed here

No unitary $U$ satisfies $U\ket\psi\ket0 = \ket\psi\ket\psi$ for two states $\ket\psi, \ket\phi$ with $0\lt|\braket{\phi|\psi}|\lt1$.

proof

Unitaries preserve inner products (unit 00):

$$\braket{\phi|\psi} = \bra\phi\bra0\,U^\dagger U\,\ket\psi\ket0 = \braket{\phi|\psi}^2,$$

so $\braket{\phi|\psi}\in\{0, 1\}$. $\blacksquare$ Unit 05 proves the full theorem, with an environment and with approximate copies.

Arthur cannot copy Merlin's state, and Merlin might not send copies honestly.

the real difficulty

So Arthur asks for $k$ copies. Merlin may instead send one state of $k$ registers, entangled across them, designed so the runs fail together in a correlated way. The next slide shows this buys him nothing.

Full proof

If $U\ket\psi\ket0 = \ket\psi\ket\psi$ and $U\ket\phi\ket0 = \ket\phi\ket\phi$, take the inner product of the two equations: the left sides give $\bra\phi\bra0U^\dagger U\ket\psi\ket0 = \braket{\phi|\psi}\braket{0|0} = \braket{\phi|\psi}$; the right sides give $\braket{\phi|\psi}\braket{\phi|\psi}$. So $z = z^2$ with $z = \braket{\phi|\psi}$, whence $z = 0$ or $z = 1$; and $z = 1$ for unit vectors means $\ket\phi = \ket\psi$. With an extra environment register in which $U$ may leave anything, $U\ket\psi\ket0\ket e = \ket\psi\ket\psi\ket{e_\psi}$, the same computation gives $|z| = |z|^2|\braket{e_\phi|e_\psi}|\le|z|^2$, so again $|z|\in\{0, 1\}$. Unit 05 (No-cloning, proved twice) has this and the approximate version.

Why "measuring changes it" matters. After one run of $V_x$ the witness register is entangled with the ancillas and the output has been read; running $V_x$ again on what is left does not reproduce the first run's statistics. For BQP that never arises because the input $\ket{x, 0}$ is classical and can be rewritten for free.

Why entanglement is the worry. For a product $\ket{\xi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\xi_k}$ the runs are independent (unit 03), and Hoeffding applies at once. An entangled state need not give independent outcomes: measuring one register changes what the others hold. The question is whether those correlations can push the majority up in a no-instance. They cannot.

20 · QMA · amplification

Parallel repetition, and why entanglement does not help

parallel repetition

Arthur runs $V_x$ on each of $k$ witness registers, fresh ancillas each time, and accepts on a majority. Then a no-instance is accepted with probability $\le e^{-k/18}$ for every state $\ket\Xi$ of the $k$ registers, entangled or not; a yes-instance with $\ket\xi^{\otimes k}$ is rejected with probability $\le e^{-k/18}$.

proof

With $Q_1 = Q_x$, $Q_0 = I - Q_x$, the runs act on disjoint registers, so outcome $b\in\{0,1\}^k$ has

$$\Pr[b] = \bra\Xi\,Q_{b_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes Q_{b_k}\,\ket\Xi .$$

Diagonalise $Q_x = \sum_iq_i\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}$, all $q_i\le\tfrac13$. In the product basis $\ket{e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_k}}$ every $Q_{b_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes Q_{b_k}$ is diagonal, so $\Pr[b]$ is a mixture, with weights $|\braket{e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_k}|\Xi}|^2$, of independent coins of biases $q_{i_j}\le\tfrac13$. Hoeffding bounds each by $e^{-k/18}$; so does the mixture. $\blacksquare$

$k$ runs, one witness register each; Merlin chooses the joint state.

Why it works: each copy's measurement is the same operator $Q_x$; its eigenbasis splits any $\ket\Xi$ into product pieces, and on a product piece the coins are independent.

Full proof

The product formula. Run $j$ applies $V_x$ to (ancilla block $j$, witness register $j$). Its outcome projector is $\Pi^{(j)}_{b_j} = \ket{b_j}\!\bra{b_j}$ on its output qubit. The joint circuit is $V_x^{\otimes k}$ (with registers reordered), the ancillas start in $\ket{0^a}^{\otimes k}$, so the input is $J^{\otimes k}\ket\Xi$. Then $\Pr[b] = \lVert(\otimes_j\Pi_{b_j})V^{\otimes k}J^{\otimes k}\Xi\rVert^2 = \bra\Xi\otimes_j(J^\dagger V^\dagger\Pi_{b_j}VJ)\ket\Xi$, using $(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D) = AC\otimes BD$ (unit 03) and $\Pi^\dagger\Pi = \Pi$. And $J^\dagger V^\dagger\Pi_1VJ = Q_x$, $J^\dagger V^\dagger\Pi_0VJ = J^\dagger V^\dagger(I - \Pi_1)VJ = I - Q_x$. So the probability is exactly the formula on the slide; the outcomes are measured all at the end, which by deferred measurement (unit 10) loses nothing.

The diagonal form. $Q_x$ and $I - Q_x$ share the eigenvectors $e_i$, with eigenvalues $q_i$ and $1 - q_i$. Hence $Q_{b_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes Q_{b_k}\ket{e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_k}} = \prod_jr_j\,\ket{e_{i_1}\cdots e_{i_k}}$ with $r_j = q_{i_j}$ if $b_j = 1$ and $1 - q_{i_j}$ if $b_j = 0$. Write $\ket\Xi = \sum_{\vec\imath}c_{\vec\imath}\ket{e_{\vec\imath}}$; then $\Pr[b] = \sum_{\vec\imath}|c_{\vec\imath}|^2\prod_jr_j$. For fixed $\vec\imath$, $\prod_jr_j$ is the probability of $b$ when the $k$ bits are independent with $\Pr[b_j = 1] = q_{i_j}$. Summing over $b$ with a majority of ones: $\Pr[\text{majority accepts}] = \sum_{\vec\imath}|c_{\vec\imath}|^2\,P_{\vec\imath}$, with $P_{\vec\imath}$ that probability for independent coins and $\sum|c_{\vec\imath}|^2 = 1$.

Hoeffding for unequal coins. For independent $X_j\in[0,1]$ with $\mathbb E\bar X = \mu$, $\Pr[\bar X\ge\mu + t]\le e^{-2kt^2}$ (assumed background). Here $\mu\le\tfrac13$, and a majority means $\bar X\ge\tfrac12\ge\mu + \tfrac16$, so $P_{\vec\imath}\le e^{-k/18}$; averaging keeps the bound.

Check, $k = 3$. Three registers each in an eigenvector with $q = \tfrac13$: the majority accepts with $3\cdot\tfrac19\cdot\tfrac23 + \tfrac1{27} = \tfrac7{27} = 0.259$, within the bound $e^{-3/18} = 0.846$ ✓ — Hoeffding is weak for small $k$ and strong for large: at $k = 50$ it gives $e^{-50/18} = 0.062$.

Completeness. With $\ket\Xi = \ket\xi^{\otimes k}$ the state is a product, the bits are independent with bias $\ge\tfrac23$, and unit 12's computation gives error $\le e^{-k/18}$. The cost: the witness grows from $p$ to $kp$ qubits. The majority is computed reversibly, exactly as in unit 12.

Any gap will do, and Marriott–Watrous

amplification from a small gap

If a verifier has completeness $c$ and soundness $s$ with $c - s\ge1/q(n)$ for a polynomial $q$, then $k$ registers and the rule "accept iff more than $\tfrac{c+s}2$ of the runs accept" give errors $\le e^{-k(c-s)^2/2}$ both ways.

proof

The previous proof with the threshold moved: Hoeffding with $t = \tfrac{c - s}2$. So $k = O(q^2\cdot r)$ runs reach error $e^{-r}$. $\blacksquare$

Ground states: what matter does when it is cold

The experiment. A system with Hamiltonian $H$ (unit 01: the generator of its evolution, whose eigenvalues are its possible energies) is put in contact with a large cold body. Energy flows out of the system until both share a temperature $\tau$. Measured then, the system is found in the eigenstate of energy $E_i$ with probability proportional to $e^{-E_i/k_B\tau}$ (Boltzmann's law, cited). As $\tau\to0$ only the lowest level survives.

ground energy and ground state

$E_0(H) = \lambda_{\min}(H) = \min_{\lVert v\rVert = 1}\bra vH\ket v$ (variational principle). An eigenvector for $E_0$ is a ground state.

Why a chemist wants it. The ground energy of a molecule's electrons, as the nuclei are moved, fixes the molecule's shape, and differences of ground energies are the heat a reaction gives off.

Boltzmann weights $e^{-E_i/k_B\tau}/Z$ for four levels; each column sums to 1. Cold, the system sits in its ground state.

nature does not compute it for free

Cooling can take astronomically long when the energy landscape is rugged — glasses never finish. "Nature finds ground states" is false in general; this unit proves a version of why.

Boltzmann's law, stated. In thermal equilibrium at absolute temperature $\tau$, a system whose Hamiltonian has eigenvalues $E_i$ is found in eigenstate $i$ with probability $e^{-E_i/k_B\tau}/Z$, $Z = \sum_ie^{-E_i/k_B\tau}$, where $k_B = 1.380649\times10^{-23}$ J/K is Boltzmann's constant (exact in the SI). This is statistical mechanics, not derived in this course (any text on it, e.g. Kittel and Kroemer, Thermal Physics, ch. 3, cited). Nothing in the unit's proofs uses it; it is the reason the problem matters. The figure's numbers: at $k_B\tau = 2$ the weights $1, e^{-1/2}, e^{-1}, e^{-3/2}$ normalise to $0.455, 0.276, 0.167, 0.102$ (sum 1.000); at $k_B\tau = \tfrac14$ the weights $1, e^{-4}, e^{-8}, e^{-12}$ normalise to $0.982, 0.018, 0.0003, 6\times10^{-6}$. Energies are in arbitrary units, so only $E/k_B\tau$ matters.

Chemistry, carefully. In the Born–Oppenheimer picture the nuclei are treated as fixed points and the electrons' ground energy $E_0(R)$ is computed as a function of the nuclear positions $R$; its minimum is the molecule's equilibrium shape, and differences of minima between reactants and products are reaction energies. These numbers are what quantum chemistry spends most of its computer time on. Unit 19 named the quantum route: prepare a state close to the ground state and read the energy by phase estimation.

Glasses. Ordinary window glass is what a liquid becomes when it is cooled faster than it can find its crystalline ground state: an amorphous solid, stuck in a low but not lowest state. Spin glasses are the magnetic version, and finding their ground states is NP-hard (Barahona 1982, next slides). The physics of slow cooling and the complexity of ground energies are two faces of one fact.

The k-local Hamiltonian problem

the k-local Hamiltonian problem

Input: $n$; terms $H_1, \dots, H_m$, $m\le\mathrm{poly}(n)$, each $H_j = h_j\otimes I$ with $h_j$ a Hermitian matrix on a named set of at most $k$ qubits, entries given to $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ bits, and $0\le H_j\le I$; thresholds $a\lt b$ with $b - a\ge1/\mathrm{poly}(n)$.

Promise: for $H = \sum_jH_j$, either $E_0(H)\le a$ or $E_0(H)\ge b$. Decide which.

$b - a$ is the promise gap. Instances in neither case are not asked about: this is a promise problem, and QMA's definition applies to it with "$x\in L$" read as "a yes-instance".

Unit 19's chain $H = -J\sum Z_iZ_{i+1} - h\sum X_i$ is 2-local. Each term is brought into $[0, I]$ by $H_j\mapsto(H_j + \lVert H_j\rVert I)/2\lVert H_j\rVert$, which moves $E_0$ by a known affine map.

A local Hamiltonian: few-qubit terms, polynomially many. The promise leaves a gap $b - a$.

Running example: its Feynman–Kitaev Hamiltonian, built in section 3, is an instance with $n = 11$ qubits, $m = 19$ terms, $k = 5$.

Why the gap. Without a promise gap, deciding $E_0\le a$ versus $E_0\gt a$ asks for $E_0$ to unbounded precision, which even a perfect phase estimation cannot supply in polynomial time. A gap of $1/\mathrm{poly}$ is what a polynomial-time estimate can resolve, and it is the natural precision: energies of $m$ terms each of norm at most 1 lie in $[0, m]$, and a gap of $1/\mathrm{poly}$ is relative precision $1/\mathrm{poly}$.

The normalisation. If $h$ is Hermitian with $\lVert h\rVert\le\Lambda$, its eigenvalues lie in $[-\Lambda, \Lambda]$, so those of $(h + \Lambda I)/2\Lambda$ lie in $[0, 1]$: it is between 0 and $I$. Doing this to every term replaces $H$ by $H' = \sum_j(H_j + \Lambda_jI)/2\Lambda_j$; if all $\Lambda_j$ are equal to $\Lambda$, then $E_0(H') = (E_0(H) + m\Lambda)/2\Lambda$, an increasing affine map, so thresholds translate. For unequal norms, rescale all terms by the largest.

Locality is about qubits, not geometry. "$k$-local" means each term acts on at most $k$ qubits, anywhere; the qubits need not be neighbours on a line. Geometric locality (nearest neighbours on a line or a lattice) is a stronger restriction, and the hardness theorems for it are cited on Further: two-local, one dimension, quantum PCP.

Promise problems and QMA. A promise problem is a pair of disjoint sets $(L_{\mathrm{yes}}, L_{\mathrm{no}})$; the verifier must accept yes-instances with probability $\ge\tfrac23$ for some witness and reject no-instances for all witnesses, and may do anything elsewhere. BQP is also, strictly, a class of promise problems when stated this way; nothing in unit 12 or here depends on the distinction.

Worked: 3-SAT as a diagonal Hamiltonian

$\varphi = (x_1\lor x_2\lor x_3)\land(x_1\lor\lnot x_2\lor x_3)\land(\lnot x_1\lor x_2\lor\lnot x_3)$

1. One clause, one projector. A clause is false on exactly one assignment of its three variables — every literal false. For $C_3 = (\lnot x_1\lor x_2\lor\lnot x_3)$ that is $x_1x_2x_3 = 101$, so

$$\Pi_{C_3} = \ket1\!\bra1\otimes\ket0\!\bra0\otimes\ket1\!\bra1 = \ket{101}\!\bra{101},$$

a projection ($\Pi^2 = \Pi = \Pi^\dagger$ ✓), on 3 qubits, so $0\le\Pi_{C_3}\le I$.

2. The others. $C_1$ is false on $000$, $C_2$ on $010$: $\ \Pi_{C_1} = \ket{000}\!\bra{000}$, $\ \Pi_{C_2} = \ket{010}\!\bra{010}$.

3. The Hamiltonian. $H_\varphi = \Pi_{C_1} + \Pi_{C_2} + \Pi_{C_3}$ is diagonal, and $\bra zH_\varphi\ket z$ counts the clauses $z$ violates.

The diagonal of $H_\varphi$, assignment $x_1x_2x_3$ (qubit 0 is $x_1$).

4. Read off. The eigenvalues of a diagonal matrix are its entries: $E_0 = 0$, with ground space spanned by the five satisfying assignments $\ket{001}, \ket{011}, \ket{100}, \ket{110}, \ket{111}$.

Check. $\mathrm{tr}\,H_\varphi = 3$, one per rank-one projector, and the bars sum to $1 + 1 + 1 = 3$ ✓.

Step 1 in general. A clause $\ell_1\lor\ell_2\lor\ell_3$ on variables $x_{i_1}, x_{i_2}, x_{i_3}$ is false iff each literal is false: $x_{i_r} = 0$ for a positive literal and $x_{i_r} = 1$ for a negated one. That fixes one bit string $u\in\{0,1\}^3$, and $\Pi_C = \ket{u_1}\!\bra{u_1}_{i_1}\otimes\ket{u_2}\!\bra{u_2}_{i_2}\otimes\ket{u_3}\!\bra{u_3}_{i_3}$, tensored with $I$ on the other qubits. On a basis state $\ket z$ of all $n$ qubits, $\Pi_C\ket z = \ket z$ if $z$ violates $C$ and 0 otherwise.

Step 3. Summing, $H_\varphi\ket z = (\text{number of clauses violated by }z)\ket z$: every computational basis state is an eigenvector, and the eigenvalues are the violation counts. Since the $2^n$ basis states are an eigenbasis, there are no other eigenvalues.

The table. $000$ violates $C_1$ only ($C_2$ needs $x_2 = 1$, $C_3$ needs $x_1 = 1$); $010$ violates $C_2$ only; $101$ violates $C_3$ only; the other five violate nothing. On three variables each clause excludes one of the 8 assignments, so a three-clause formula on the same three variables is always satisfiable; unsatisfiability needs all 8 clauses, or more variables (problem P3 has four).

NP-hard before anything quantum happens

theorem

3-local Hamiltonian is NP-hard, already for diagonal terms with $a = 0$, $b = 1$.

proof

Map a 3-CNF $\varphi$ with $m$ clauses to $H_\varphi = \sum_C\Pi_C$ (previous slide), in polynomial time. $H_\varphi$ is diagonal with $\bra zH_\varphi\ket z$ the number of clauses $z$ violates, so $E_0 = \min_z(\text{violations})$: 0 if $\varphi$ is satisfiable, at least 1 if not. 3-SAT is NP-complete (Cook–Levin). $\blacksquare$

An unsatisfiable shadow. $(x)\land(\lnot x)$: $H = \ket0\!\bra0 + \ket1\!\bra1 = I$, so $E_0 = 1$ (a 1-local term is in particular 3-local). Every state pays for one clause.

Consequence. A polynomial-time quantum algorithm for local Hamiltonian would put NP inside BQP. Kitaev's theorem will say more: it would put all of QMA there.

Three spins, each bond penalised by $\ket{00}\!\bra{00} + \ket{11}\!\bra{11}$ when aligned: $E_0 = 1$. An odd cycle cannot be 2-coloured.

Physics knew. Such frustration is what makes magnetic glasses glassy, and finding the ground energy of an Ising spin glass is NP-hard (Barahona 1982, cited).

Full proof

The reduction runs in time linear in the formula: one $8\times8$ diagonal matrix per clause, with a single 1. Each term is a projection, so $0\le\Pi_C\le I$; there are $m$ terms. If $\varphi$ is satisfied by $z$ then $\bra zH_\varphi\ket z = 0$ and, $H_\varphi$ being positive (a sum of projections), $E_0 = 0\le a$. If $\varphi$ is unsatisfiable then every basis state violates at least one clause, so every diagonal entry — every eigenvalue — is at least 1, and $E_0\ge1 = b$. The promise gap is 1, a constant, much better than $1/\mathrm{poly}$.

The triangle. Spins are qubits (unit 02), and "aligned" means both $\ket0$ or both $\ket1$. The bond term $\ket{00}\!\bra{00} + \ket{11}\!\bra{11} = \tfrac12(I + Z\otimes Z)$ is a projection. On three spins the sum of the three bond terms is diagonal, and any assignment of three bits has two equal bits, so at least one bond pays: $E_0 = 1$, with six ground states (every assignment except $000$ and $111$, which pay 3). That is the 2-colouring problem on a triangle, unsatisfiable because the cycle is odd.

Barahona. F. Barahona, "On the computational complexity of Ising spin glass models", J. Phys. A 15, 3241 (1982): the ground energy of an Ising model with couplings $\pm1$ on a three-dimensional grid, or on a planar grid in a field, is NP-hard to find. Cited, not proved.

What this does and does not say. It does not use quantum mechanics at all: a diagonal Hamiltonian is just a weighted constraint satisfaction problem. What it says about quantum computers is the consequence on the slide. What it does not give is membership in NP for non-diagonal Hamiltonians — a ground state is not a classical certificate — and that is where QMA comes in.

claim

Exactly $T + 1 = 10$ of the $2^9 = 512$ strings have zero energy: $1^t0^{9-t}$, $t = 0, \dots, 9$.

Proof. Zero energy means no "01". Once a 0 appears, every later bit is 0, so the string is $1^t0^{9-t}$. $\blacksquare$

The ten legal clock strings, a staircase; and one illegal string, which pays for each "01".

The clause. $(c_i\lor\lnot c_{i+1})$ is false iff $c_i = 0$ and $c_{i+1} = 1$, so its projector is $\ket0\!\bra0_{c_i}\otimes\ket1\!\bra1_{c_{i+1}} = \ket{01}\!\bra{01}_{c_ic_{i+1}}$: 2-local. $H_{\mathrm{clock}}$ is diagonal with entry "number of 01 patterns" on each string.

The illegal string in the figure. $010010000$ contains "01" at positions $(c_1, c_2)$ and $(c_4, c_5)$, so it has energy 2. A string can have energy up to 4 here (at most $\lfloor9/2\rfloor$ disjoint "01"s): $010101010$.

Measuring one term with a projective measurement

Arthur holds $\ket\xi$ on $n$ qubits and one term $H_j = h_j\otimes I$, $h_j$ on a set $S_j$ of $k$ qubits. Diagonalise (unit 00):

$$h_j = \sum_{i=1}^{2^k}\lambda_i\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i},\qquad 0\le\lambda_i\le1 .$$
  1. Apply the $k$-qubit unitary $V_j$ with $V_j\ket{e_i} = \ket i$, built exactly from CNOTs and one-qubit gates (unit 10).
  2. Measure the $k$ qubits: outcome $i$ with probability $p_i = \bra\xi(\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}\otimes I)\ket\xi$ (Born rule, unit 01).
  3. Toss a coin: reject with probability $\lambda_i$, accept with $1 - \lambda_i$.

$\sum_ip_i = \bra\xi(I\otimes I)\ket\xi = 1$ ✓, because the $\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}$ sum to $I$.

One term, measured in its own eigenbasis; the coin turns outcome $i$ into a rejection with probability $\lambda_i$.

why not "measure $H_j$"?

Unit 01's measurement of an observable returns an eigenvalue, not a yes/no. Arthur needs a yes/no whose probability is linear in $\bra\xi H_j\ket\xi$ — the coin supplies it, with projective measurements only.

The pieces exist. $V_j$ sends one orthonormal basis of $(\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes k}$ to another, so it is unitary; its matrix is computed classically from $h_j$ (a $2^k\times2^k$ eigenproblem, constant size for constant $k$). Unit 10's exact universality builds it from $O(k^24^k)$ CNOTs and one-qubit gates. Over a finite gate set those one-qubit gates are approximated (unit 11) to accuracy $\delta$ with $\mathrm{polylog}(1/\delta)$ gates, which moves every outcome probability by at most $2\delta$ per gate; choosing $\delta$ a small inverse polynomial keeps the total error far below the gap $(b - a)/m$ found on the next slides.

The coin. With classical randomness: a BPP coin of bias $\lambda_i$, approximated to $\mathrm{poly}(n)$ bits, implemented by Hadamards on fresh qubits as in unit 12's BPP ⊆ BQP. Or quantumly: controlled on the outcome $i$, rotate a fresh qubit by $R_y(2\arcsin\sqrt{\lambda_i})$, which sends $\ket0$ to $\sqrt{1 - \lambda_i}\ket0 + \sqrt{\lambda_i}\ket1$, and read it. Either way the rejection probability given $i$ is $\lambda_i$, up to an error exponentially small in the number of bits used.

The measurement's probabilities. After $V_j$, measuring the $k$ qubits in the computational basis has outcome projectors $\ket i\!\bra i\otimes I$, so outcome $i$ has probability $\bra\xi(V_j^\dagger\otimes I)(\ket i\!\bra i\otimes I)(V_j\otimes I)\ket\xi = \bra\xi(\ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}\otimes I)\ket\xi$, because $V_j^\dagger\ket i = \ket{e_i}$. This is unit 01's measurement in the basis $\{e_i\}$, made concrete.

Worked: the random-term test accepts with 1 − ⟨ξ|H|ξ⟩/m

Arthur picks $j\in\{1, \dots, m\}$ uniformly, then runs the one-term test

1. Given $j$. Sum over outcomes, writing $P_i = \ket{e_i}\!\bra{e_i}\otimes I$:

$$\begin{aligned}\Pr[\text{acc}\mid j] &= \textstyle\sum_i(1 - \lambda_i)\bra\xi P_i\ket\xi\\ &= \textstyle\bra\xi\big(\sum_iP_i - \sum_i\lambda_iP_i\big)\ket\xi .\end{aligned}$$

2. Resolve. $\sum_iP_i = I$ and $\sum_i\lambda_iP_i = h_j\otimes I = H_j$, so

$$\Pr[\text{acc}\mid j] = 1 - \bra\xi H_j\ket\xi .$$

3. Average over $j$.

$$\begin{aligned}\Pr[\text{acc}] &= \frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m\big(1 - \bra\xi H_j\ket\xi\big)\\ &= 1 - \frac{\bra\xi H\ket\xi}m .\end{aligned}$$

4. Checks. $H_j = 0$: never rejects ✓. $H_j = I$: always rejects ✓. Since $0\le\bra\xi H\ket\xi\le m$, the answer lies in $[0, 1]$ ✓.

5. On the 3-SAT shadow with $\ket\xi = \ket z$ a basis state: $\Pr[\text{acc}] = 1 - (\text{violated clauses})/m$ — the classical test "pick a random clause and check it".

The local Hamiltonian problem is in QMA

theorem (Kitaev)

For every constant $k$, $k$-local Hamiltonian is in QMA.

proof

Arthur runs the random-term test. Yes-instance: Merlin sends a ground state $\ket g$; $\bra gH\ket g = E_0\le a$, so it is accepted with probability $\ge1 - a/m =: c$. No-instance: for every unit $\ket\xi$, $\bra\xi H\ket\xi\ge E_0\ge b$ by the variational principle, so acceptance is $\le1 - b/m =: s$. The gap $c - s = (b - a)/m\ge1/\mathrm{poly}$, and amplification from a small gap finishes. $\blacksquare$

Merlin's proof is a ground state: the physicist's natural certificate that an energy is low.

The energy gap becomes an acceptance gap, divided by the number of terms.

check on the shadow

For $H_\varphi$ with $a = 0$, $b = 1$: $c = 1$ and $s = 1 - 1/m$. A satisfying assignment passes every clause; an unsatisfiable formula fails a random clause with probability at least $1/m$ ✓.

Full proof

Completeness. A ground state exists: $H$ is Hermitian, so it has an orthonormal eigenbasis (unit 00) and the eigenvector of the least eigenvalue is a unit vector with $\bra gH\ket g = E_0$. If Merlin sends $k$ copies, $\ket g^{\otimes k}$, the runs are independent and each accepts with probability $1 - E_0/m\ge c$.

Soundness. The variational principle gives $\bra\xi H\ket\xi\ge\lambda_{\min}(H) = E_0\ge b$ for every unit $\xi$, so every single run accepts with probability $\le s$. For $k$ registers in any joint state, Parallel repetition, and why entanglement does not help applies verbatim with $Q = I - H/m$, which is the acceptance operator of one run (step 3 of the worked example says $\bra\xi Q\ket\xi$ is the acceptance probability for every $\xi$, and $0\le Q\le I$).

The gap. $b - a\ge1/p_1(n)$ and $m\le p_2(n)$ give $c - s\ge1/(p_1p_2)$, an inverse polynomial. The amplified verifier uses $O((p_1p_2)^2)$ copies of the ground state for error $\tfrac13$. All of Arthur's work is polynomial: choose $j$, apply a constant-size circuit, toss a coin, repeat.

Locality larger than constant. The same proof works for $k = O(\log n)$, since $V_j$ then has $O(k^24^k) = \mathrm{poly}(n)$ gates. For larger $k$ the diagonalisation of $h_j$ is itself exponential and the argument breaks.

Cook–Levin writes every row of a computation at once. A quantum computation cannot be written that way, so Feynman and Kitaev superpose the rows instead, with a clock to say which is which — and penalise everything that is not such a superposition.

20 · history · Cook–Levin

Cook–Levin, recalled: a tableau checked by windows

The assumed proof, in the form used here. A verifier running for $T$ steps has a tableau: row $t$ is its configuration after step $t$. A formula in the tableau's bits says

  • start: row 0 holds $x$, any certificate $y$, and blank work space;
  • step: every $2\times3$ window of adjacent rows is a legal move;
  • accept: row $T$ is accepting.

Satisfiable iff some $y$ is accepted. Each clause reads $O(1)$ cells.

the property Kitaev must copy

A tableau that is wrong anywhere violates at least one clause, which costs a full unit — however long the computation.

Cook–Levin's tableau: rows are time, cells are tape. Locality comes from looking at two adjacent rows at a time.

What is assumed. The learner has proved Cook–Levin (assumed background); this slide only names the three groups of clauses in the form the comparison needs. For a nondeterministic machine running in time $T$ on space $T$, the tableau is $(T + 1)\times O(T)$ cells, each cell a constant number of Boolean variables, and the window clauses are a constant-size CNF per window, so the formula has $O(T^2)$ clauses of constant width.

The gap of a tableau. If no certificate is accepted, every assignment to the tableau violates at least one clause: either some window is illegal, or row 0 is malformed, or row $T$ is not accepting. So the number of violated clauses jumps from 0 to at least 1 — the constant gap of NP-hard before anything quantum happens. (The PCP theorem says much more: the gap can be made a constant fraction of the clauses. Its quantum version is open: Further: two-local, one dimension, quantum PCP.)

The three groups, and their quantum counterparts. start $\leftrightarrow$ $H_{\mathrm{in}}$; step $\leftrightarrow$ $H_{\mathrm{prop}}$; accept $\leftrightarrow$ $H_{\mathrm{out}}$; plus a fourth with no classical need, $H_{\mathrm{clock}}$, because the quantum rows will be told apart by a register that could itself be corrupted.

Why a quantum tableau cannot be written down

Attempt. Rows $\ket{\psi_t} = U_t\cdots U_1\ket{0^a, \xi}$, written side by side: $\ket{\psi_0}\otimes\ket{\psi_1}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\psi_T}$. But the map $\xi\mapsto\ket{\psi_0}\otimes\ket{\psi_1}$ is quadratic in $\xi$, so no circuit, and no ground space of a fixed Hamiltonian, produces it for every $\xi$ — the no-cloning obstruction again.

Feynman's way (1985): add a clock register and add the rows, which is linear in $\xi$.

the history state

For a circuit $U_T\cdots U_1$ on $N$ qubits and an input $\ket\phi$,

$$\ket{\eta_\phi} = \frac1{\sqrt{T + 1}}\sum_{t=0}^{T}U_t\cdots U_1\ket\phi\otimes\ket t .$$

Normalised: the $\ket t$ are orthonormal and each row is a unit vector, so $\lVert\eta_\phi\rVert^2 = \tfrac1{T+1}\sum_t1 = 1$ ✓.

Tensoring the rows needs copies; superposing them, labelled by a clock, does not.

what Arthur cannot do with it

Measure the clock and only one row survives, chosen uniformly. The history state is a certificate for a Hamiltonian, checked by local energy terms — not one Arthur reads row by row.

Why "quadratic" is fatal. Any state produced from $\ket\xi$ by a unitary (or as a linear function of $\ket\xi$, as the ground space of a Hamiltonian parametrised by $\xi$ would have to be) depends linearly on $\xi$: the image of $\xi + \xi'$ is the sum of the images. The tableau state of $\xi + \xi'$ contains cross terms $\ket{\psi_0}\otimes\ket{\psi'_1}$ that the sum lacks. With $T = 1$ and $U_1 = I$ it would be the map $\xi\mapsto\xi\otimes\xi$ — cloning, impossible (Amplification is not free: the witness cannot be copied).

Feynman's computer. R. P. Feynman, "Quantum mechanical computers", Optics News 11, 11 (1985), reprinted in Found. Phys. 16, 507 (1986). He wanted a Hamiltonian whose dynamics carried out a computation, and wrote $H_F = \sum_t(U_t\otimes\ket t\!\bra{t - 1} + U_t^\dagger\otimes\ket{t - 1}\!\bra t)$: under $e^{-iH_Ft}$ the clock wanders along the line $0, 1, \dots, T$ like a particle hopping between sites, applying a gate at each step forward and undoing it at each step back. Kitaev's idea (1999) was to use the same operator statically: the propagation term is $\sum_t\tfrac12(\text{diagonal}) - \tfrac12H_F$, and its ground states are the histories.

Measuring the clock. If Arthur measured the clock register of $\ket{\eta_\phi}$ he would get each $t$ with probability $\tfrac1{T+1}$ and be left with row $t$. That is how the output term will be evaluated: the probability of finding $t = T$ and a rejecting output is $\tfrac1{T+1}(1 - \Pr[\text{accept}])$.

A clock that can be read locally: unary

the unary clock

$T$ clock qubits $c_1, \dots, c_T$; time $t$ is $\ket t = \ket{1^t0^{T-t}}$. The legal subspace is spanned by these $T + 1$ strings (with any state of the computation register).

On legal strings, with the conventions $c_0 = 1$, $c_{T+1} = 0$:

  • "the time is $t$" iff $c_tc_{t+1} = 10$: 2 qubits;
  • "step from $t - 1$ to $t$" flips $c_t$ when $c_{t-1}c_tc_{t+1} = 100$:
$$\ket t\!\bra{t - 1}\ \leftrightarrow\ \ket{110}\!\bra{100}_{c_{t-1}c_tc_{t+1}}\quad(\textbf{3 qubits}).$$

$\ket{t - 1}\!\bra{t - 1}$ and $\ket t\!\bra t$ are realised on the same three qubits as $\ket{100}\!\bra{100}$ and $\ket{110}\!\bra{110}$.

Time 3 to time 4 on the running example's nine-qubit clock: only $c_4$ changes, and the window $c_3c_4c_5$ is enough to check it.

a binary clock would not do

$\lceil\log(T + 1)\rceil$ bits are fewer, but going from $0111$ to $1000$ changes all of them, and "the time is $t$" reads all of them: terms of locality $\Theta(\log T)$ (problem P6).

Full proof

Reading the time. A legal string $1^s0^{T-s}$ has $c_tc_{t+1} = 10$ iff $c_t = 1$ (so $s\ge t$) and $c_{t+1} = 0$ (so $s\le t$), iff $s = t$. With $c_0 = 1$ and $c_{T+1} = 0$ by convention, $t = 0$ is read from $c_1 = 0$ alone and $t = T$ from $c_T = 1$ alone: one qubit at each end.

The step operator. $\ket{110}\!\bra{100}$ on $c_{t-1}c_tc_{t+1}$ annihilates a legal string unless $c_{t-1}c_tc_{t+1} = 100$, which (as above) happens exactly for $s = t - 1$; it then sets $c_t = 1$, giving $1^t0^{T-t} = \ket t$. So on the legal subspace it equals $\ket t\!\bra{t - 1}$. At the ends: for $t = 1$ there is no $c_0$, and the operator is $\ket{10}\!\bra{00}$ on $c_1c_2$; for $t = T$ there is no $c_{T+1}$, and it is $\ket{11}\!\bra{10}$ on $c_{T-1}c_T$.

It never makes an illegal string legal, or the reverse. Applied to any string with $c_{t-1}c_tc_{t+1} = 100$, flipping $c_t$ changes the adjacent pairs $(c_{t-1}c_t)$ from $10$ to $11$ and $(c_tc_{t+1})$ from $00$ to $10$ — neither is a "01" before or after — and no other pair changes. So the number of "01" patterns, the energy under $H_{\mathrm{clock}}$, is unchanged. This is what lets the analysis split into a legal and an illegal part (Each propagation term is a projector).

The cost. $T$ clock qubits instead of $\lceil\log(T + 1)\rceil$: a polynomial price, paid for locality.

Each propagation term is a projector

lemma

(a) Each $H_t$ is a projection; so all four parts of $H$ are positive. (b) $H$ maps the legal subspace into itself and its complement into itself. (c) On the complement, $H\ge I$.

proof of (a)

Write $P = \ket t\!\bra t + \ket{t{-}1}\!\bra{t{-}1}$ and $K = U_t\otimes\ket t\!\bra{t{-}1} + U_t^\dagger\otimes\ket{t{-}1}\!\bra t$, so $H_t = \tfrac12(I\otimes P - K)$. Then $K^2 = U_tU_t^\dagger\otimes\ket t\!\bra t + U_t^\dagger U_t\otimes\ket{t{-}1}\!\bra{t{-}1} = I\otimes P$ and $(I\otimes P)K = K$, so

$$H_t^2 = \tfrac14\big(I\otimes P - 2K + I\otimes P\big) = H_t,$$

and $H_t^\dagger = H_t$. $\blacksquare$

(b) and (c): the step operators never change the number of "01"s (previous slide's notes), the other terms are diagonal in the clock, and on an illegal string $H_{\mathrm{clock}}\ge1$.

For each $\psi$, $H_t$ acts on the plane of "$\psi$ before step $t$" and "$U_t\psi$ after": it charges 1 for the antisymmetric combination and nothing for the symmetric one.

Full proof

(a), the two identities. $K^2$: the cross terms contain $\ket t\!\bra{t - 1}\ket t\!\bra{t - 1} = 0$ and its adjoint, and the remaining products are $(U_t\otimes\ket t\!\bra{t-1})(U_t^\dagger\otimes\ket{t-1}\!\bra t) = U_tU_t^\dagger\otimes\ket t\!\bra t$ and the mirror image; $U_t$ is unitary. $(I\otimes P)K = K$ because $P$ is the identity on the span of $\ket{t - 1}, \ket t$, which contains the range of every clock operator in $K$; likewise $K(I\otimes P) = K$. Expanding, $H_t^2 = \tfrac14((I\otimes P)^2 - (I\otimes P)K - K(I\otimes P) + K^2) = \tfrac14(2I\otimes P - 2K)$. With Kitaev's 3-qubit realisation of the clock operators the same algebra holds on the whole space, because $\ket{100}\!\bra{100}, \ket{110}\!\bra{110}, \ket{110}\!\bra{100}, \ket{100}\!\bra{110}$ multiply exactly like the $2\times2$ matrix units. Numerically, all nine $H_t$ of the running example square to themselves on the full $2^{11}$-dimensional space.

The figure. For a unit $\psi$, the vectors $u = \psi\otimes\ket{t - 1}$ and $v = U_t\psi\otimes\ket t$ are orthonormal, and $H_tu = \tfrac12(u - v)$, $H_tv = \tfrac12(v - u)$. So $H_t(u + v) = 0$ and $H_t(u - v) = u - v$: on that plane $H_t$ is the projection onto $(u - v)/\sqrt2$. Every vector in the range of $I\otimes P$ lies in such planes, and $H_t$ kills the rest.

(b). $H_{\mathrm{in}}, H_{\mathrm{out}}, H_{\mathrm{clock}}$ and the diagonal parts of $H_t$ are diagonal in the clock basis, so they preserve any set of clock strings. The step operator $\ket{110}\!\bra{100}$ and its adjoint preserve the number of "01"s, hence legality and illegality.

(c). An illegal string contains at least one "01", so $H_{\mathrm{clock}}\ge I$ on the illegal subspace, and the other three parts are $\ge0$ there. So every state with an illegal clock costs energy at least 1, more than anything the legal subspace will need: from here on, the analysis happens on the legal subspace, where $\ket t$ really means time $t$.

Completeness: an accepting history costs (1 − p)/(T + 1)

theorem (completeness)

If the witness $\xi$ is accepted with probability $p$, the history state $\eta$ of $\ket\phi = \ket{0^a}\ket\xi$ has zero energy under $H_{\mathrm{prop}}$, $H_{\mathrm{clock}}$ and $H_{\mathrm{in}}$, and

$$\bra\eta H_{\mathrm{out}}\ket\eta = \frac{1 - p}{T + 1}.$$

So $E_0(H)\le(1 - p)/(T + 1)$.

proof

With $\psi_t = U_t\cdots U_1\phi$, $\ \sqrt{T{+}1}\,H_t\eta = \tfrac12\big(\psi_t\ket t + \psi_{t-1}\ket{t{-}1} - U_t\psi_{t-1}\ket t - U_t^\dagger\psi_t\ket{t{-}1}\big) = 0$, since $\psi_t = U_t\psi_{t-1}$. The clock is legal. At $t = 0$ the ancillas are 0. Only the row $t = T$ meets $H_{\mathrm{out}}$, and it rejects with probability $1 - p$, weighted $\tfrac1{T+1}$. $\blacksquare$

The running example's histories: $(1 - p)/10$ with $p = 1, 0.854, 0.146$.

check: the whole Hamiltonian

Diagonalising the running example's $2048\times2048$ matrix: $E_0 = 0$, with a single ground state, the history of $\ket{h_+}$; the next eigenvalue is $0.0123$. The bound is attained.

Full proof

Propagation. $H_t$ only sees the components at times $t - 1$ and $t$. Applying the four pieces of $H_t$ to $\sqrt{T + 1}\,\eta$: $I\otimes\ket t\!\bra t$ gives $\psi_t\ket t$; $I\otimes\ket{t - 1}\!\bra{t - 1}$ gives $\psi_{t-1}\ket{t - 1}$; $U_t\otimes\ket t\!\bra{t - 1}$ gives $U_t\psi_{t-1}\ket t = \psi_t\ket t$; and $U_t^\dagger\otimes\ket{t - 1}\!\bra t$ gives $U_t^\dagger\psi_t\ket{t - 1} = \psi_{t-1}\ket{t - 1}$. With the signs, everything cancels.

Input. $H_{\mathrm{in}}\eta = \tfrac1{\sqrt{T+1}}\sum_i(\ket1\!\bra1_i\psi_0)\otimes\ket0$, and $\psi_0 = \ket{0^a}\ket\xi$ has every ancilla in $\ket0$, so each term vanishes.

Output. $\bra\eta H_{\mathrm{out}}\ket\eta = \tfrac1{T+1}\bra{\psi_T}(\ket0\!\bra0_0\otimes I)\ket{\psi_T}$, cross terms between different times vanishing because the clock projector $\ket T\!\bra T$ is diagonal. That is $\tfrac1{T+1}\Pr[\text{qubit 0 reads 0}] = \tfrac{1-p}{T+1}$. The bound on $E_0$ is the variational principle.

The numbers. $p = \cos^2\tfrac\pi8 = 0.8536$ for $\ket0$ gives $0.1464/10 = 0.01464$; $p = 0.1464$ for $\ket1$ gives $0.08536$. These were also computed directly as $\bra\eta H\ket\eta$ on the 11-qubit space, term by term: propagation, clock and input energies $0$ to machine precision, output energy as stated. The full spectrum's lowest values are $0, 0.0123, 0.0123, 0.0489, 0.0489, \dots$; the ground state's overlap with the history of $\ket{h_+}$ is 1.

After amplification. A yes-instance amplified to completeness $1 - \varepsilon$ gives $E_0\le\varepsilon/(T + 1)$. That is the threshold $a$ of the reduction.

The propagation term is a path

One change of basis removes every gate from $H_{\mathrm{prop}}$ and leaves the Laplacian of a path of $T + 1$ vertices. Its spectrum is cosines — and its gap, $1 - \cos(\pi/(T+1))$, is where the price of the construction is set.

Untwisting the gates: the propagation term is a path

lemma

On the legal subspace $\mathbb C^{2^N}\otimes\mathrm{span}\{\ket0, \dots, \ket T\}$ let $W = \sum_{t=0}^{T}U_t\cdots U_1\otimes\ket t\!\bra t$. Then $W$ is unitary and

$$W^\dagger H_{\mathrm{prop}}W = I\otimes\tfrac12L,$$

where $L$ is the path Laplacian: $L = \sum_{t=1}^{T}(\ket t - \ket{t{-}1})(\bra t - \bra{t{-}1})$.

proof

$W^\dagger W = \sum_t(U_t\cdots U_1)^\dagger(U_t\cdots U_1)\otimes\ket t\!\bra t = I$. And

$$W^\dagger(U_t\otimes\ket t\!\bra{t{-}1})W = I\otimes\ket t\!\bra{t{-}1},$$

because $(U_t\cdots U_1)^\dagger\,U_t\,(U_{t-1}\cdots U_1) = I$;

the diagonal parts commute with $W$. So $W^\dagger H_tW = \tfrac12I\otimes(\ket t - \ket{t{-}1})(\bra t - \bra{t{-}1})$; sum over $t$. $\blacksquare$

Top: $H_{\mathrm{prop}}$ as a path whose edges carry the gates. Bottom: after $W$, a plain path, the same for every circuit with $T$ gates.

$L = D - A$, degrees minus adjacency: $\mathrm{diag}(1, 2, \dots, 2, 1)$ minus 1s beside the diagonal. Its quadratic form is $v^\top Lv = \sum_t(v_t - v_{t-1})^2\ge0$.

Full proof

Unitarity. $W$ is block diagonal in the clock, with unitary blocks $U_t\cdots U_1$ (the empty product, for $t = 0$, is $I$). A block-diagonal matrix with unitary blocks is unitary. So $W^\dagger H_{\mathrm{prop}}W$ has the same spectrum as $H_{\mathrm{prop}}$ (unit 00).

The off-diagonal piece. $W^\dagger(A\otimes\ket t\!\bra s)W = \sum_{r, r'}(U_r\cdots U_1)^\dagger A(U_{r'}\cdots U_1)\otimes\ket r\!\braket{r|t}\!\braket{s|r'}\!\bra{r'}$, and only $r = t$, $r' = s$ survives. With $A = U_t$ and $s = t - 1$: $(U_t\cdots U_1)^\dagger U_t(U_{t-1}\cdots U_1) = (U_{t-1}\cdots U_1)^\dagger U_t^\dagger U_t(U_{t-1}\cdots U_1) = I$. The adjoint term goes the same way. The diagonal pieces $I\otimes\ket t\!\bra t$ are unchanged since $(U_t\cdots U_1)^\dagger(U_t\cdots U_1) = I$.

Why "Laplacian". For any graph, $L = D - A$ with $D$ the diagonal of degrees and $A$ the adjacency matrix; equivalently $L = \sum_{\text{edges }\{s,t\}}(e_t - e_s)(e_t - e_s)^\top$, which is how the lemma produces it, one edge per gate. It is the discrete second derivative with a minus sign: $(Lv)_t = 2v_t - v_{t-1} - v_{t+1}$ in the interior. Its quadratic form $\sum_{\text{edges}}(v_t - v_s)^2$ shows $L\ge0$ and that its null space, for a connected graph, is the constant vectors: zero means every edge has equal ends.

What disappeared. Every gate. The spectrum of $H_{\mathrm{prop}}$ depends only on $T$ and the register size $N$, not on what the circuit computes. The circuit comes back only through $H_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $H_{\mathrm{out}}$, which $W$ twists: section 5.

Worked: two gates, three clock states

$T = 2$, one qubit: $U_1 = \mathrm{Had}$, $U_2 = S$

1. Before. In $2\times2$ blocks indexed by clock time $0, 1, 2$:

$$2H_{\mathrm{prop}} = \begin{pmatrix} I & -\mathrm{Had} & 0\\ -\mathrm{Had} & 2I & -S^\dagger\\ 0 & -S & I\end{pmatrix}.$$

Time 1 is touched by both $H_1$ and $H_2$, hence the $2I$.

2. Untwist with $W = \mathrm{diag}(I, \mathrm{Had}, S\,\mathrm{Had})$. Block $(1, 0)$: $\mathrm{Had}^\dagger(-\mathrm{Had})I = -I$. Block $(2, 1)$: $(S\mathrm{Had})^\dagger(-S)\mathrm{Had} = -I$. So

$$2W^\dagger H_{\mathrm{prop}}W = \begin{pmatrix}1 & -1 & 0\\ -1 & 2 & -1\\ 0 & -1 & 1\end{pmatrix}\otimes I = L\otimes I .$$

3. Eigenvectors of $\tfrac12L$, by multiplying out:

$$\begin{aligned}\tfrac12L\,(1, 1, 1)^\top &= 0,\\ \tfrac12L\,(1, 0, -1)^\top &= \tfrac12\,(1, 0, -1)^\top,\\ \tfrac12L\,(1, -2, 1)^\top &= \tfrac32\,(1, -2, 1)^\top.\end{aligned}$$

Eigenvalues $0, \tfrac12, \tfrac32$, each twice ($\otimes I$ on one qubit).

4. Checks. Trace: $2(0 + \tfrac12 + \tfrac32) = 4 = \tfrac12(2 + 4 + 2)$ ✓. The formula to come, $1 - \cos(\pi k/3)$, $k = 0, 1, 2$: $0, \tfrac12, \tfrac32$ ✓. The eigenvectors are orthogonal ✓.

5. Null space: $W$ applied to $\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1, 1, 1)\otimes\ket\phi$ is $\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(\ket\phi\ket0 + \mathrm{Had}\ket\phi\ket1 + S\mathrm{Had}\ket\phi\ket2)$, the history of $\ket\phi$.

Step 1, entry by entry. $H_1 = \tfrac12(I\otimes\ket1\!\bra1 + I\otimes\ket0\!\bra0 - \mathrm{Had}\otimes\ket1\!\bra0 - \mathrm{Had}^\dagger\otimes\ket0\!\bra1)$ fills blocks $(0,0) = \tfrac12I$, $(1,1) = \tfrac12I$, $(1,0) = -\tfrac12\mathrm{Had}$, $(0,1) = -\tfrac12\mathrm{Had}^\dagger$. $H_2$ fills $(1,1)$ with another $\tfrac12I$, $(2,2) = \tfrac12I$, $(2,1) = -\tfrac12S$, $(1,2) = -\tfrac12S^\dagger$. (The block $(0, 1)$ is $-\tfrac12\mathrm{Had}^\dagger$, which is $-\tfrac12\mathrm{Had}$ because $\mathrm{Had}$ is Hermitian; in general it is $-\tfrac12U_1^\dagger$.) The block $(r, s)$ multiplies the clock-$s$ component and lands in clock $r$.

Step 2. Conjugating a block matrix by $W = \mathrm{diag}(W_0, W_1, W_2)$ sends block $(r, s)$ to $W_r^\dagger(\cdot)W_s$. Diagonal blocks are multiples of $I$ and are unchanged. $W_1^\dagger(-\tfrac12\mathrm{Had})W_0 = -\tfrac12\mathrm{Had}^\dagger\mathrm{Had} = -\tfrac12I$; $W_2^\dagger(-\tfrac12S)W_1 = -\tfrac12\mathrm{Had}^\dagger S^\dagger S\,\mathrm{Had} = -\tfrac12I$. The result is written $L\otimes I$ rather than the lemma's $I\otimes\tfrac12L$ only because this block layout puts the clock index outside: the same operator, with the two factors listed in the other order.

Step 3, one product in full. $\tfrac12L(1, -2, 1)^\top = \tfrac12(1\cdot1 + (-1)(-2) + 0,\ (-1)(1) + 2(-2) + (-1)(1),\ 0 + (-1)(-2) + 1) = \tfrac12(3, -6, 3) = \tfrac32(1, -2, 1)$. Normalised: $\tfrac1{\sqrt3}(1,1,1)$, $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,0,-1)$, $\tfrac1{\sqrt6}(1,-2,1)$.

The gap here is $\tfrac12$: a two-gate history that is "half right" — say, correct amplitudes with a sign flipped between the ends, the vector $(1, 0, -1)$ — costs $\tfrac12$. With nine gates the same kind of slow twist will cost only $0.0489$.

The path's spectrum: cosines

theorem

For the path on $0, \dots, T$, the vectors $v_k(t) = \cos\frac{\pi k(t + \frac12)}{T + 1}$, $k = 0, \dots, T$, are an orthogonal eigenbasis, with

$$\tfrac12Lv_k = \Big(1 - \cos\frac{\pi k}{T + 1}\Big)v_k .$$

So $H_{\mathrm{prop}}$ has eigenvalues $1 - \cos\frac{\pi k}{T+1}$, each with multiplicity $2^N$, and spectral gap $1 - \cos\frac\pi{T+1}$.

proof

Put $\alpha = \pi k/(T + 1)$. Interior: $v(t - 1) + v(t + 1) = 2\cos\alpha\,v(t)$, by $\cos(x - \alpha) + \cos(x + \alpha) = 2\cos x\cos\alpha$; so $(Lv)(t) = 2v(t) - v(t{-}1) - v(t{+}1) = 2(1 - \cos\alpha)v(t)$. Ends: the formula gives $v(-1) = v(0)$ and $v(T + 1) = v(T)$ — mirrors half a step out — so the end rows $v(0) - v(1)$ and $v(T) - v(T{-}1)$ are the interior formula. $\blacksquare$

$v_1$ and $v_2$ on the running example's path, $T = 9$: a half-wave and a full wave, symmetric about mirrors half a step beyond the ends.

What the null space is, and why the gap is small

corollary

The null space of $H_{\mathrm{prop}} + H_{\mathrm{clock}}$ is exactly the set of history states $\ket{\eta_\phi}$, one for every input $\phi\in\mathbb C^{2^N}$ — dimension $2^N$. Every state orthogonal to it has energy $\ge1 - \cos\frac\pi{T+1}$.

Proof. Both terms are positive, so the null space of the sum is the intersection: legal clock, and $W(\phi\otimes v_0)$ with $v_0$ constant. The smallest non-zero eigenvalue is the gap on the legal part and $\ge1$ on the illegal part. $\blacksquare$

Running example: dimension 4, the histories of the four inputs $\ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}, \ket{11}$ and their combinations — including histories that start with a bad ancilla or end by rejecting. $H_{\mathrm{prop}}$ cannot tell; that is $H_{\mathrm{in}}$'s and $H_{\mathrm{out}}$'s job.

The gap against path length: slope $-2$ on log axes. A long path has slow waves, and slow waves are cheap.

why small

A history wrong by a slow twist — the right row at each time, with a phase drifting by $\pi$ from one end to the other — has neighbouring rows only $\pi/T$ apart. Each step pays the square of that times the row weight $\tfrac1{T+1}$, so $T$ steps cost $\approx\pi^2/2T^2$ in all.

Full proof

Intersection of null spaces. For positive $A, B$: $(A + B)v = 0$ implies $\bra vA\ket v + \bra vB\ket v = 0$, both terms $\ge0$, so both vanish, and $\bra vA\ket v = \lVert A^{1/2}v\rVert^2 = 0$ gives $Av = 0$ ($A^{1/2}$ by the functional calculus of unit 00). Conversely $Av = Bv = 0$ gives $(A + B)v = 0$.

The spectrum of the sum. $H_{\mathrm{prop}} + H_{\mathrm{clock}}$ preserves the legal and illegal subspaces (Each propagation term is a projector), so its spectrum is the union of the two parts'. On the legal part $H_{\mathrm{clock}} = 0$ and the spectrum is that of $I\otimes\tfrac12L$; on the illegal part it is $\ge1$. So the smallest non-zero eigenvalue is $\min(1 - \cos\frac\pi{T+1}, \ge1) = 1 - \cos\frac\pi{T+1}$. This is the $\lambda_A$ of section 5.

The slow twist, quantified. In the untwisted picture a state is a function $v(t)$ on the path (times a fixed register state), with energy $\tfrac12\sum_t|v(t) - v(t - 1)|^2$. A correct history is $v$ constant. Take $v(t) = e^{i\pi t/T}/\sqrt{T + 1}$, a phase that turns by $\pi$ along the path: each difference has size $\approx(\pi/T)/\sqrt{T + 1}$, and the energy is $\approx\tfrac12T(\pi/T)^2/(T + 1)\approx\pi^2/2T^2$ — matching the gap. Physically, Feynman's computer is a particle on a line of $T + 1$ sites, and the gap is the energy of its slowest standing wave, the lowest note of a long string.

The figure's points. $T = 1$: $1 - \cos\tfrac\pi2 = 1$. $T = 9$: $0.0489$. $T = 100$: $4.84\times10^{-4}$. $T = 1000$: $4.92\times10^{-6}$ (problem P9).

A cheating Merlin need not send a history. Kitaev's geometric lemma bounds the energy of every state by the angle between two null spaces, and the angle by the verifier's soundness. The price of spreading the check over $T + 1$ times is a promise gap of $1/\mathrm{poly}$.

The obstacle: a cheat need not be a history

Split $H = A + B$ with

$$A = H_{\mathrm{prop}} + H_{\mathrm{clock}},\qquad B = H_{\mathrm{in}} + H_{\mathrm{out}} .$$
  • $A$'s null space $N_A$: all history states — of any input, good or bad.
  • $B$'s null space $N_B$: states correct at $t = 0$ and accepting at $t = T$, with no relation between the two.

A no-instance has no accepting history, so $N_A\cap N_B = \{0\}$. But Merlin can choose a state between them, paying a little of $A$ and a little of $B$. How little?

positive is not enough

$A, B\ge0$ and $N_A\cap N_B = \{0\}$ give $E_0(A + B)\gt0$, but no bound: if the null spaces are nearly parallel, $A + B$ can be nearly singular. The bound must involve the angle.

Two null spaces at angle $\theta$. A vector close to both pays little to each — but not nothing, unless $\theta = 0$.

The sister circuit rejects every witness with probability at least $0.854$. Its $N_A$ and $N_B$ meet only in 0; we will find the angle between them, $14.4°$.

The null spaces, precisely. $N_A$ was found on What the null space is, and why the gap is small: $\{\ket{\eta_\phi} : \phi\in\mathbb C^{2^N}\}$. $B = H_{\mathrm{in}} + H_{\mathrm{out}}$ is diagonal in the computational basis of all qubits, with non-negative integer entries, so its null space is spanned by the basis strings on which no term fires; on legal clock states, that is $\mathrm{range}(P_{\mathrm{in}})\otimes\ket0\ \oplus\ \bigoplus_{0\lt t\lt T}\mathbb C^{2^N}\otimes\ket t\ \oplus\ \mathrm{range}(P_{\mathrm{acc}})\otimes\ket T$, where $P_{\mathrm{in}}$ projects onto "all ancillas 0" and $P_{\mathrm{acc}}$ onto "qubit 0 is 1". In the middle times $B$ asks nothing at all.

An intersection would be an accepting history. A history $\eta_\phi$ lies in $N_B$ iff $P_{\mathrm{in}}\phi = \phi$ (the input has ancillas 0, so $\phi = \ket{0^a}\ket\xi$) and $P_{\mathrm{acc}}U\phi = U\phi$ (the output is accepted with certainty). In a no-instance no witness is accepted with probability above $\varepsilon\lt1$, so there is none.

Why an angle. In the plane, take $A$ the projection onto the line perpendicular to a unit vector $x$ and $B$ the projection perpendicular to $y$, with $\braket{x|y} = \cos\theta$. Their null spaces are the lines through $x$ and $y$, and the smallest eigenvalue of $A + B$ is $1 - \cos\theta$ (next slide), which tends to 0 as $\theta\to0$. So no bound in terms of $A$'s and $B$'s spectra alone can exist.

On $\mathrm{span}\{x, y\}$ the eigenvalues are $1 + \cos\theta$ and $1 - \cos\theta$; on its orthogonal complement, 0.

Two lines: the sum of their projections is largest along the bisector and smallest across it.

4. Checks. Kitaev's geometric lemma

the angle between subspaces

For subspaces $N_A, N_B$ meeting only in 0: $\ \cos\theta = \max\{|\braket{x|y}| : x\in N_A, y\in N_B, \lVert x\rVert = \lVert y\rVert = 1\}$.

the geometric lemma (Kitaev)

Let $A, B\ge0$ have null spaces at angle $\theta$, and suppose every non-zero eigenvalue of $A$ is $\ge\lambda_A$ and of $B$ is $\ge\lambda_B$; then

$$A + B\ \ge\ \min(\lambda_A, \lambda_B)\cdot2\sin^2\tfrac\theta2 .$$

Check: the worked example is the case $A = I - \Pi_x$, $B = I - \Pi_y$, $\lambda_A = \lambda_B = 1$, and there the bound is an equality ✓.

proof

1. $A\ge\lambda_A(I - \Pi_A)$, with $\Pi_A$ the projection onto $N_A$: true on each eigenvector. Likewise $B$. So with $\lambda = \min(\lambda_A, \lambda_B)$,

$$A + B\ge\lambda\big(2I - \Pi_A - \Pi_B\big).$$

2. For a unit $v$, let $x = \Pi_Av/\lVert\Pi_Av\rVert$ and $y = \Pi_Bv/\lVert\Pi_Bv\rVert$. Then $\bra v\Pi_A\ket v = \lVert\Pi_Av\rVert^2 = |\braket{x|v}|^2$, and likewise for $B$; the sum is $\bra v(\ket x\!\bra x + \ket y\!\bra y)\ket v$, so

$$\begin{aligned}\bra v(\Pi_A + \Pi_B)\ket v &= |\braket{x|v}|^2 + |\braket{y|v}|^2\\ &\le1 + |\braket{x|y}|\\ &\le1 + \cos\theta,\end{aligned}$$

by the worked example and the variational principle.

3. So $A + B\ge\lambda(2 - 1 - \cos\theta) = \lambda\cdot2\sin^2\tfrac\theta2$. $\blacksquare$

Full proof

Step 1. Write $A = \sum_i\mu_iP_i$ (spectral theorem, unit 00). On $N_A$ both sides vanish. On an eigenvector with $\mu_i\gt0$, $\mu_i\ge\lambda_A$ and $(I - \Pi_A)$ acts as 1. Both sides being diagonal in the same basis, $A - \lambda_A(I - \Pi_A) = \sum_{\mu_i\gt0}(\mu_i - \lambda_A)P_i\ge0$.

Step 2, the identity. $\braket{x|v} = \bra v\Pi_A^\dagger\ket v/\lVert\Pi_Av\rVert = \lVert\Pi_Av\rVert^2/\lVert\Pi_Av\rVert = \lVert\Pi_Av\rVert$, using $\Pi_A^\dagger\Pi_A = \Pi_A$. If $\Pi_Av = 0$, drop that term (and the bound only improves). Then $|\braket{x|v}|^2 + |\braket{y|v}|^2 = \bra v(\ket x\!\bra x + \ket y\!\bra y)\ket v\le\lambda_{\max}(\ket x\!\bra x + \ket y\!\bra y) = 1 + |\braket{x|y}|$ (worked example, with its phase remark), and $|\braket{x|y}|\le\cos\theta$ because $x\in N_A$, $y\in N_B$ are unit vectors. Note that $x$ and $y$ depend on $v$; the bound holds for each $v$, which is all an operator inequality needs.

Step 3. Step 2 says $\Pi_A + \Pi_B\le(1 + \cos\theta)I$, hence $2I - \Pi_A - \Pi_B\ge(1 - \cos\theta)I$; multiply by $\lambda\gt0$ and combine with step 1.

Source. Kitaev, Shen and Vyalyi, Classical and Quantum Computation (AMS 2002), chapter 14; the form here, with $2\sin^2(\theta/2)$, is the one stated in Kempe, Kitaev and Regev, SIAM J. Comput. 35, 1070 (2006). The maximum defining $\cos\theta$ exists because unit spheres in finite dimension are compact; it equals $\lVert\Pi_A\Pi_B\rVert$.

The angle for a rejecting verifier

lemma

If every witness is accepted with probability $\le\varepsilon$, the null spaces of $A = H_{\mathrm{prop}} + H_{\mathrm{clock}}$ and $B = H_{\mathrm{in}} + H_{\mathrm{out}}$ satisfy

$$\sin^2\theta\ \ge\ \frac{1 - \sqrt\varepsilon}{T + 1}.$$
proof

For a unit history $\eta_\phi$, $\ \cos^2\theta = \max_\phi\lVert\Pi_B\eta_\phi\rVert^2$, and only the ends of the clock are constrained:

$$1 - \lVert\Pi_B\eta_\phi\rVert^2 = \tfrac{2 - \bra\phi(P_{\mathrm{in}} + U^\dagger P_{\mathrm{acc}}U)\ket\phi}{T + 1},$$

$U = U_T\cdots U_1$. The ranges of $P_{\mathrm{in}}$ ("ancillas 0") and $U^\dagger P_{\mathrm{acc}}U$ ("will be accepted") are at an angle with cosine $\le\sqrt\varepsilon$ — that is soundness. Step 2 of the lemma's proof, once more: the bracket is $\le1 + \sqrt\varepsilon$. $\blacksquare$

In a no-instance, no correctly started input is close to one that will be accepted.

the sister circuit

$\varepsilon = \sin^2\tfrac\pi8$, so $\sqrt\varepsilon = \sin\tfrac\pi8 = 0.383$ and $\sin^2\theta\ge(1 - 0.383)/10 = 0.0617$. Computed directly: $\sin^2\theta = 0.0617$, $\theta = 14.4°$. The bound is attained.

Full proof

$\cos^2\theta$ via histories. $N_A$ is the set of unit histories $\eta_\phi$; for fixed $\eta$, $\max_{y\in N_B}|\braket{\eta|y}| = \lVert\Pi_B\eta\rVert$, attained at $y = \Pi_B\eta/\lVert\Pi_B\eta\rVert$ (Cauchy–Schwarz). So $\cos^2\theta = \max_\phi\lVert\Pi_B\eta_\phi\rVert^2 = \max_\phi\bra{\eta_\phi}\Pi_B\ket{\eta_\phi}$.

The formula. On legal clock states $\Pi_B = P_{\mathrm{in}}\otimes\ket0\!\bra0 + \sum_{0\lt t\lt T}I\otimes\ket t\!\bra t + P_{\mathrm{acc}}\otimes\ket T\!\bra T$ (notes of The obstacle), diagonal in time. So $\bra\eta\Pi_B\ket\eta = \tfrac1{T+1}\big(\bra\phi P_{\mathrm{in}}\ket\phi + (T - 1) + \bra{U\phi}P_{\mathrm{acc}}\ket{U\phi}\big)$, and subtracting from $1 = \tfrac{T+1}{T+1}$ gives the slide's expression.

The inner angle. $P' = U^\dagger P_{\mathrm{acc}}U$ is a projection. For a unit $\psi$ in $\mathrm{range}(P_{\mathrm{in}})$, $\psi = \ket{0^a}\ket\xi$ for a unit witness $\xi$, and $\lVert P'\psi\rVert^2 = \lVert P_{\mathrm{acc}}U\psi\rVert^2 = \Pr[\text{accept }\xi]\le\varepsilon$. For unit $y\in\mathrm{range}(P')$, $|\braket{\psi|y}| = |\braket{P'\psi|y}|\le\lVert P'\psi\rVert\le\sqrt\varepsilon$. So the angle between the two ranges has cosine at most $\sqrt\varepsilon$, and step 2 of the geometric lemma gives $\bra\phi(P_{\mathrm{in}} + P')\ket\phi\le1 + \sqrt\varepsilon$ for unit $\phi$. Hence $1 - \cos^2\theta\ge(2 - 1 - \sqrt\varepsilon)/(T + 1)$.

Soundness, assembled: Kitaev's theorem

theorem (soundness)

If every witness is accepted with probability $\le\varepsilon$, then

$$\begin{aligned}E_0(H) &\ge \Big(1 - \cos\frac\pi{T + 1}\Big)\frac{1 - \sqrt\varepsilon}{2(T + 1)}\\ &\ge \frac{1 - \sqrt\varepsilon}{(T + 1)^3}.\end{aligned}$$
proof

Geometric lemma with $\lambda_A = 1 - \cos\frac\pi{T+1}$ (section 4) and $\lambda_B\ge1$ ($B$ is diagonal with integer entries), so $\min = \lambda_A$. Then $2\sin^2\tfrac\theta2 = 1 - \cos\theta = 1 - \sqrt{1 - \sin^2\theta}\ge\tfrac12\sin^2\theta$. Insert the angle lemma, then $\lambda_A\ge2/(T + 1)^2$. $\blacksquare$

theorem (Kitaev 1999)

5-local Hamiltonian is QMA-complete.

proof

In QMA: The local Hamiltonian problem is in QMA. Hard: given $L\in\mathsf{QMA}$ and $x$, amplify $V_x$ until both errors are $\le\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon\le\frac1{4(T+1)^2}$, $T$ its gate count; output the Feynman–Kitaev $H$, $a = \frac\varepsilon{T+1}$, $b = \frac{1 - \sqrt\varepsilon}{(T+1)^3}$. Completeness: $E_0\le a$. Soundness: $E_0\ge b$. And $b - a\ge\frac1{4(T + 1)^3}$. $\blacksquare$

The reduction is computed in polynomial time: one term per gate, per clock pair, per ancilla, and one for the output.

Full proof

Soundness. $\lambda_A$: the smallest non-zero eigenvalue of $A$ is the propagation gap (What the null space is). $\lambda_B$: $B$ is diagonal in the computational basis of all $N + T$ qubits, its entries are counts of firing terms, hence integers, and its smallest non-zero eigenvalue is $\ge1$. Since $1 - \cos\frac\pi{T+1}\le1$, the minimum is $\lambda_A$. The lemma gives $H = A + B\ge\lambda_A(1 - \cos\theta)$. With $s = \sin^2\theta\in[0, 1]$, $\sqrt{1 - s}\le1 - s/2$ (square both sides: $1 - s\le1 - s + s^2/4$), so $1 - \cos\theta\ge s/2\ge(1 - \sqrt\varepsilon)/2(T + 1)$. Multiply. For the last form, $\lambda_A\ge2/(T + 1)^2$ (Worked: nine gates, gap 0.0489, step 4).

Choosing $\varepsilon$. Amplifying with $k$ copies multiplies the gate count by about $k$ (plus the majority circuit, compiled into two-qubit gates), so $T(k)$ grows polynomially in $k$ while $\varepsilon = e^{-k/18}$ falls exponentially; $k = O(\log T_0)$ makes $\varepsilon\le1/4(T(k) + 1)^2$. Then $\sqrt\varepsilon\le\frac1{2(T+1)}\le\tfrac12$, so $b\ge\frac1{2(T+1)^3}$, while $a\le\frac1{4(T+1)^3}$: the gap is at least $\frac1{4(T+1)^3}$, an inverse polynomial in $|x|$.

Locality and normalisation. Every term is a projection on at most 5 qubits (Four penalties), so $0\le H_j\le I$; the entries are those of the gates, from a fixed finite set, given exactly or to polynomially many bits. That makes $(H, a, b)$ a valid instance, and the map $x\mapsto(H, a, b)$ is a polynomial-time reduction from $L$. Together with membership, 5-local Hamiltonian is QMA-complete.

Why the bound is loose. The lemma charges as if the worst vector were simultaneously at the edge of $A$'s spectrum and at the worst angle, and those two extremes need not coincide. For the asymptotics this does not matter: the true ground energy of a rejecting circuit's Hamiltonian really does fall like $1/T^2$ or faster in general (the low-lying propagation modes are real), and the promise gap of the Feynman–Kitaev construction is inverse polynomial, not constant.

The quantum check is local but its evidence is spread: a history state puts weight $\tfrac1{T+1}$ on each row, so an error at one step is seen with weight $\tfrac1{T+1}$, and the slow twists of section 4 cost only $\Theta(1/T^2)$.

What a wrong step costs, nine gates: a whole clause, or the propagation gap.

Where the $T^3$ comes from. One factor $T^2$ from the propagation gap; one factor $T$ from the $\tfrac1{T+1}$ weight that the history state puts on its first and last rows, which is all that $H_{\mathrm{in}}$ and $H_{\mathrm{out}}$ can see. Both are intrinsic to spreading a computation over a superposition of times. Sharper analyses and modified clocks improve the exponent, but every known circuit-to-Hamiltonian construction for general QMA verifiers has a promise gap that shrinks polynomially in $T$; none gives a constant.

The PCP theorem (Arora, Lund, Motwani, Sudan and Szegedy; Arora and Safra; 1998, cited) says that for some constant $c\gt0$ it is NP-hard to tell a satisfiable 3-CNF from one in which every assignment violates a fraction $c$ of the clauses: a promise gap of $cm$ in Hamiltonian language. 20 · further

Further: two-local, one dimension, quantum PCP

Fewer qubits per term (cited). 2-local Hamiltonian is QMA-complete (Kempe, Kitaev and Regev, SIAM J. Comput. 35, 1070, 2006), by perturbation theory: a strong 2-local term whose low-energy subspace reproduces a 3-local one.

A line (cited). Nearest-neighbour terms on a chain of 12-level particles are already QMA-complete (Aharonov, Gottesman, Irani and Kempe, Commun. Math. Phys. 287, 41, 2009) — though classical 1D problems of this kind are easy.

open: the quantum PCP conjecture

Is it QMA-hard to approximate $E_0$ to within a constant fraction $cm$ of the number of terms? Classically, the PCP theorem says yes for constraint satisfaction.

Evidence: the NLTS theorem (Anshu, Breuckmann and Nirkhe, STOC 2023): local Hamiltonians exist all of whose states of energy $\le cm$ need circuits of depth growing with $n$ — a consequence quantum PCP requires, now proved.

QMA-hard even on a line with nearest-neighbour terms.

None of this is proved here. It is named so that the theorem of this unit is seen as the start of a field, Hamiltonian complexity, not its end.

2-local. J. Kempe, A. Kitaev and O. Regev, "The complexity of the local Hamiltonian problem", SIAM J. Comput. 35, 1070–1097 (2006). The method: add a large 2-local term $\Delta H_0$ whose ground space is a "code", so that at second or third order in perturbation theory the effective Hamiltonian on that ground space reproduces a given 3-local term. The promise gap stays inverse polynomial but $\Delta$ is polynomially large. Oliveira and Terhal (2008) extended it to 2-local terms on a 2D square lattice of qubits.

1D. D. Aharonov, D. Gottesman, S. Irani and J. Kempe, "The power of quantum systems on a line", Commun. Math. Phys. 287, 41–65 (2009): nearest-neighbour Hamiltonians on a line of 12-state particles are QMA-complete (with an inverse-polynomial gap). The contrast: for a classical 1D constraint problem, dynamic programming along the line solves it in polynomial time; and for gapped 1D quantum Hamiltonians, ground states can be found efficiently (Landau, Vazirani and Vidick 2015, cited). The hard instances have gaps that close polynomially.

Quantum PCP. D. Aharonov, I. Arad and T. Vidick, "The quantum PCP conjecture", SIGACT News 44 (2013), is the survey. NLTS ("no low-energy trivial states"): A. Anshu, N. P. Breuckmann and C. Nirkhe, "NLTS Hamiltonians from good quantum codes", STOC 2023. Quantum PCP would imply NLTS, because a low-energy state preparable in constant depth would be a classical certificate (an NP witness) for the energy, and quantum PCP plus NP ≠ QMA forbids that. NLTS is proved; quantum PCP is open.

Dynamics is easy, ground energies are not

the running example, closed

Nine gates, a one-qubit witness. Its Feynman–Kitaev Hamiltonian: 11 qubits, 19 terms, each on at most 5 qubits. Ground energy 0, attained by the history of $\ket{h_+}$ — the circuit accepts. Its sister, which accepts nothing above $0.146$: ground energy $0.00693$, guaranteed $\ge0.00153$ by Kitaev's lemma, through a propagation gap of $0.0489$ and an angle of $14.4°$.

What this unit bought

QMA, with amplification that survives entangled cheats. The local Hamiltonian problem in QMA. The history state, the unary clock, the four penalties; the propagation term as a path Laplacian with gap $1 - \cos\frac\pi{T+1}$; the geometric lemma; Kitaev's theorem.

What it means, stated carefully

Unit 19: simulating $e^{-iHt}$ for a local $H$ is in BQP. This unit: deciding $E_0(H)\le a$ versus $\ge b$ is QMA-complete. So a quantum computer that found ground energies of every local Hamiltonian would show QMA = BQP — and, by section 2 alone, NP ⊆ BQP — neither believed.

worst case, not typical

The hard instances are built from circuits. Molecules are a special family; whether they are hard is not settled by this theorem. In practice the quantum route works when a state with non-negligible ground-state overlap can be prepared (units 15, 19) — exactly what QMA-hardness says cannot always be done.

The two problems side by side. Simulation: given $H$, $t$, a state, and an observable, estimate the observable after time $t$. Unit 19 does this with $\mathrm{poly}(n, t, 1/\epsilon)$ gates by Trotterisation. Ground energy: given $H$, decide whether the minimum of $\bra vH\ket v$ over all $2^n$-dimensional unit vectors is below $a$ or above $b$. The first is a question about one trajectory from a given start; the second is an optimisation over all states, and optimisation is where NP-hardness lives, classically and quantumly.

Chemistry, carefully. Electronic-structure Hamiltonians in a fixed basis are local after a fermion-to-qubit encoding, and some families of interacting-electron problems are known to be QMA-hard (Schuch and Verstraete, "Computational complexity of interacting electrons and fundamental limitations of density functional theory", Nature Physics 5, 732, 2009, cited). That does not make every molecule hard; it says no algorithm can be expected to succeed on every instance. Whether quantum computers give an exponential advantage for the molecules chemists care about is debated: the obstacle is preparing a state with good overlap with the ground state, which classical heuristics often also manage (Lee et al., "Evaluating the evidence for exponential quantum advantage in ground-state quantum chemistry", Nature Communications 14, 1952, 2023, cited).

checkpoint

On paper and without notes, you can:

Failing it is unambiguous

A membership proof that "measures $H_j$" without saying how the outcome becomes accept or reject. A Hamiltonian with a binary clock, or one that forgets the witness is unconstrained at $t = 0$. A gap proof that checks the interior of the path and not its ends, or that gives $\pi^2/T^2$ as the exact answer.
