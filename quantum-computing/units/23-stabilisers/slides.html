Stabilisers: codes as abelian groups, and the circuits a classical computer can run

Describe a code by the Paulis that fix it instead of the vectors in it, and every question about it becomes linear algebra over $\mathbb F_2$ — which also proves that Clifford circuits, however entangling, are easy to simulate.

needs 22~3 h slides + 5½ h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 23

Unit 22 analysed codes as subspaces, by hand: write down $\ket{0_L}$ and $\ket{1_L}$, apply errors, compute the Knill–Laflamme matrix. That works for three qubits and nine; it does not work for seven hundred. This unit replaces the vectors by the operators that fix them. A code becomes the joint $+1$ eigenspace of an abelian group of Pauli operators, and the group is described by $r$ generators, each a string of $n$ letters. Its dimension, its syndromes, its logical operators and its distance all become questions about a binary matrix with $2n$ columns.

Three things come out of it. The CSS construction turns a pair of nested classical codes into a quantum one, and Steane's seven-qubit code falls out of unit 22's Hamming code. The quantum Hamming bound says how many qubits a code needs, and the five-qubit code meets it exactly. And the same bookkeeping proves the Gottesman–Knill theorem: a circuit of $H$, $S$ and CNOT gates on $\ket{0^n}$ with Pauli measurements is simulated by a classical computer in polynomial time. Entanglement alone, then, does not make a computation hard; the $T$ gate is where the power is, and unit 24 will pay for it.

23 · stabiliser groups

The experiment: measuring a product of Paulis

What hardware does. Trapped ions, superconducting circuits: each measures one qubit at a time, in the computational basis. To read the parity of four qubits without reading the qubits, it couples them to a fresh ancilla and measures that (unit 22 did this for $Z_0Z_1$).

the circuit measures $g$

For a Pauli product $g$ with $g = g^\dagger$, $g^2 = I$: the outcome is $\pm1$ with probability $\bra\psi\tfrac{I\pm g}2\ket\psi$, and the data are left in $\tfrac{I\pm g}{2}\ket\psi$, normalised.

proof

$\ket0\ket\psi\xrightarrow{H}\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket\psi + \ket1\ket\psi)\xrightarrow{C(g)}\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket\psi + \ket1 g\ket\psi)\xrightarrow{H}\ket0\tfrac{I + g}{2}\ket\psi + \ket1\tfrac{I - g}2\ket\psi.$ And $\big(\tfrac{I\pm g}2\big)^2 = \tfrac{I \pm 2g + g^2}4 = \tfrac{I\pm g}{2}$: projectors, summing to $I$.

Measuring $g_1 = X_4X_5X_6X_7$ of the running example: $C(X_4X_5X_6X_7)$ is four CNOTs from the ancilla. The data qubits are never measured.

The experiment, from nothing. A quantum computer's qubits are physical two-level systems: in a trapped-ion machine, two energy levels of one ion held in vacuum by electric fields; in a superconducting machine, the two lowest levels of a small electrical circuit cooled to a hundredth of a kelvin (unit 25). The one measurement each machine does well is the computational-basis measurement of a single qubit: shine a laser on the ion and see whether it fluoresces, or send a microwave pulse past the circuit and see which way its phase shifts. Anything else — a measurement in another basis, of a product, of a parity — is built from gates followed by that one measurement.

The circuit on this slide is the general recipe. For $g = Z_0Z_1$ it is unit 22's syndrome circuit with the roles of control and target exchanged — a controlled-$Z$ is symmetric in its two qubits, and the two $H$ gates on the ancilla, one before and one after, turn each controlled-$Z$ into a CNOT from that data qubit onto the ancilla, since $HZH = X$ — but written this way it works for any product of Paulis: $C(g)$ for $g = P_1\otimes\cdots\otimes P_m$ on the data qubits is the product of the single-qubit controlled gates $C(P_j)$, all with the ancilla as control, because $\ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes AB = (\ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes A)(\ket0\!\bra0\otimes I + \ket1\!\bra1\otimes B)$ for $A$, $B$ on different qubits. For $X$ that is a CNOT; for $Z$ a controlled-$Z$.

Full proof. The state after the last $H$ is computed on the slide: the last step uses $H\ket0 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + \ket1)$ and $H\ket1 = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 - \ket1)$ and collects terms. By the Born rule (unit 01) the probability of reading the ancilla as $0$ is $\big\lVert\tfrac{I + g}2\ket\psi\big\rVert^2 = \bra\psi\tfrac{I + g}{2}\ket\psi$, using that $\tfrac{I+g}2$ is a Hermitian idempotent; similarly for $1$, which is reported as the outcome $-1$. The two probabilities sum to $\bra\psi I\ket\psi = 1$. The post-measurement data state is the normalised $\tfrac{I\pm g}2\ket\psi$ — exactly the Lüders rule for the observable $g$, whose spectral projections are $\tfrac{I\pm g}2$ because $g$ is Hermitian with $g^2 = I$, so its eigenvalues are $\pm1$. The circuit therefore is a projective measurement of $g$, and nothing else about $\ket\psi$ is learnt.

History. This is how every error-correcting experiment reads its syndromes. Seven trapped calcium ions were used to encode one qubit in the running example's code and operate on it (Nigg et al., Science 345, 302, 2014, cited); the superconducting experiments of unit 24 repeat a circuit of this shape on every generator of a surface code, every microsecond.

The Pauli group, and when two strings commute

the Pauli group $\mathcal P_n$

All $i^c P_0\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{n-1}$ with $c\in\{0,1,2,3\}$ and each $P_j\in\{I, X, Y, Z\}$: $\ 4\cdot4^n$ elements. Written as strings, $XZYIZ$, qubit 0 leftmost. The weight of a string is its number of non-$I$ letters.

commute or anticommute

Any two $P, Q\in\mathcal P_n$ satisfy $PQ = \pm QP$, with $-$ exactly when the number of positions where both letters are non-$I$ and different is odd.

proof

Per qubit, two distinct non-$I$ Paulis anticommute and any other pair commutes. So $PQ = \bigotimes_j P_jQ_j = \bigotimes_j(\pm Q_jP_j) = (-1)^{\#}QP$.

Count the highlighted columns, where the letters differ and neither is $I$. Even: commute. Odd: anticommute.

A group: single-qubit Paulis multiply to $\pm1, \pm i$ times Paulis ($XY = iZ$, unit 02), and $(A\otimes B)(C\otimes D) = AC\otimes BD$ (unit 03). Every element is unitary, squares to $\pm I$, and is Hermitian exactly when $c$ is even.

Check, $n = 1$: 16 elements, $\{\pm1, \pm i\}\times\{I, X, Y, Z\}$; $X$ and $Z$ differ in one position, so $XZ = -ZX$ ✓.

Why these operators. Unit 22's discretisation theorem says a code that corrects $X$, $Y$ and $Z$ on a qubit corrects every error on that qubit, because $I, X, Y, Z$ span the $2\times2$ matrices. Tensor products of them span all operators on $n$ qubits, so Pauli strings are the natural error basis — and, as the previous slide showed, they are also what hardware measures. The phases $\pm1, \pm i$ are carried along so that the set is closed under multiplication; physically, an overall phase on an error is invisible.

Full proof of the group properties. Closure: by unit 02, $XY = iZ$, $YZ = iX$, $ZX = iY$, the reverse products carry $-i$, and each Pauli squares to $I$; so the product of two single-qubit Paulis is a phase in $\{\pm1, \pm i\}$ times a Pauli, and a tensor product of such things is again of the form $i^cP_0\otimes\cdots\otimes P_{n-1}$. Inverses: $P_j^2 = I$, so $(i^cP)^{-1} = i^{-c}P$. Each string $P$ is a tensor product of unitary Hermitian matrices, hence unitary and Hermitian with $P^2 = I$; then $(i^cP)^2 = i^{2c}I = \pm I$ and $(i^cP)^\dagger = i^{-c}P$, which equals $i^cP$ exactly when $c$ is even. Count: four phases times $4^n$ strings, all distinct because distinct strings are linearly independent matrices (they are orthogonal in the Hilbert–Schmidt inner product, unit 02).

The figure. $P = XZYIZ$, $Q = ZZXXI$. Position 0: $X, Z$, anticommute. Position 1: $Z, Z$, commute. Position 2: $Y, X$, anticommute. Positions 3 and 4 have an $I$. Two anticommuting positions, so $PQ = (-1)^2QP = QP$.

A stabiliser group, and the code it fixes

stabiliser group and stabiliser code

An abelian subgroup $S\subseteq\mathcal P_n$ with $-I\notin S$. Its code is $$V_S = \{\psi : g\psi = \psi\ \text{ for all } g\in S\},$$ the joint $+1$ eigenspace. If $S = \langle g_1, \dots, g_r\rangle$ it is enough that $g_j\psi = \psi$ for each generator. The generators are independent if none is a product of the others.

Example (unit 22). $S = \langle Z_0Z_1, Z_1Z_2\rangle$: the parities that the bit-flip code checks. $V_S = \mathrm{span}\{\ket{000}, \ket{111}\}$.

why the two conditions

If $V_S\ne\{0\}$, then $-I\notin S$, $S$ is abelian, and every element of $S$ is $\pm$ a string, so Hermitian.

proof

Take $\psi\ne0$ in $V_S$. If $-I\in S$: $\psi = -\psi$, so $\psi = 0$. If $g, h\in S$ anticommute: $\psi = gh\psi = -hg\psi = -\psi$. If $g = \pm iP$: $g^2 = -I\in S$.

two ways to get nothing

$\langle Z_0, -Z_0\rangle\ni -I$ fixes only $0$; so does $\langle X_0, Z_0\rangle$, whose generators anticommute ($XZXZ = -I$). The definition rules both out in advance.

Why "stabiliser". In group theory the stabiliser of a point is the set of group elements fixing it. Here the roles are turned round: $S$ is the group, and $V_S$ is the set of vectors every element of $S$ fixes. The name and the formalism are Gottesman's (PhD thesis, Caltech, 1997); Calderbank, Rains, Shor and Sloane found the same structure independently, phrased over the field with four elements (1997–98, cited).

Generators suffice. If $g_j\psi = \psi$ for each $j$, then any product $g_{j_1}\cdots g_{j_m}$ also fixes $\psi$, applying the factors right to left; and since every $g_j^2 = I$ (being Hermitian Paulis), inverses are not needed. So $V_S = \bigcap_j\ker(g_j - I)$.

The example. $Z_0Z_1\ket{b_0b_1b_2} = (-1)^{b_0 + b_1}\ket{b_0b_1b_2}$, so its $+1$ eigenspace is spanned by the basis states with $b_0 = b_1$; similarly $Z_1Z_2$ asks $b_1 = b_2$. Both: $b_0 = b_1 = b_2$, which is $\ket{000}$ and $\ket{111}$ — unit 22's code space, now described by two operators instead of two vectors. The operators are also exactly what unit 22's syndrome circuit measured.

The converse — an abelian group without $-I$ fixes a nonzero space — is the dimension theorem two slides on.

The overlaps. Rows of $M$ as sets: $h_1 = \{4,5,6,7\}$, $h_2 = \{2,3,6,7\}$, $h_3 = \{1,3,5,7\}$. Then $h_1\cap h_2 = \{6,7\}$, $h_1\cap h_3 = \{5,7\}$, $h_2\cap h_3 = \{3,7\}$, and each $h_i\cap h_i = h_i$ has 4 elements. All even. Over $\mathbb F_2$ the integer overlap mod 2 is the dot product $h\cdot h'$, so the table says $MM^{\mathsf T} = 0$: every row of $M$ is orthogonal to every row, including itself. Unit 22 proved the same fact as "the simplex code lies inside the Hamming code", the Hamming code being the simplex code's dual.

Why $-I\notin S$. Every element of $S$ is a product of generators, and since all generators commute it can be written with the $X$-type ones first: $X^aZ^b$, where $a$ is a sum of rows of $M$ and so is $b$. Multiplying two such elements, $X^aZ^bX^{a'}Z^{b'} = (-1)^{b\cdot a'}X^{a + a'}Z^{b + b'}$ (move $Z^b$ past $X^{a'}$: one sign per position where both have a 1), and $b\cdot a' = 0$ because rows of $M$ are mutually orthogonal and the dot product is bilinear. So the 64 operators $X^aZ^b$ with $a, b$ in the row space of $M$ form a group with every sign $+$; the only one equal to $\pm I$ is $a = b = 0$, which is $+I$. So $-I\notin S$.

Why these, historically. Andrew Steane found the code in 1996 (Phys. Rev. Lett. 77, 793) by asking what Hamming's 1950 code does when it is used once in the computational basis and once in the Hadamard basis — exactly the two readings of $M$ drawn here. Section 4 makes that idea general.

Each independent generator halves the dimension

dimension of a stabiliser code

If $S\subseteq\mathcal P_n$ is a stabiliser group with $r$ independent generators, then $$\dim V_S = 2^{n-r}.$$ So $V_S$ encodes $k = n - r$ qubits: an $[[n, k]]$ code.

proof

The projector onto $V_S$ is $P_S = \prod_{j=1}^r\tfrac{I + g_j}2 = 2^{-r}\sum_{g\in S}g$ (expand; independence makes each $g\in S$ appear once). $\operatorname{tr}I = 2^n$, and every $g\ne I$ in $S$ is $\pm$ a string other than $I^{\otimes n}$, whose trace is a product containing some $\operatorname{tr}X$, $\operatorname{tr}Y$ or $\operatorname{tr}Z = 0$. So $\dim V_S = \operatorname{tr}P_S = 2^{n-r}$.

Imposing Steane's generators one at a time: $2^7 = 128$ halves six times, to $2$ — one logical qubit.

One at a time: $P_{j+1} = P_j\tfrac{I + g_{j+1}}2$ has $\operatorname{tr}P_{j+1} = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}P_j + \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}(P_jg_{j+1})$, and the last trace is $0$ by the same argument.

Full proof

$P_S$ is the projector onto $V_S$. Each $Q_j = \tfrac{I + g_j}2$ is the orthogonal projector onto the $+1$ eigenspace of $g_j$ (shown on The experiment: measuring a product of Paulis). The $Q_j$ commute because the $g_j$ do. A product of commuting orthogonal projectors is Hermitian ($(Q_1Q_2)^\dagger = Q_2Q_1 = Q_1Q_2$) and idempotent ($Q_1Q_2Q_1Q_2 = Q_1^2Q_2^2 = Q_1Q_2$), so it is an orthogonal projector. Its range is the intersection of the ranges: if $Q_1Q_2\psi = \psi$ then $Q_1\psi = Q_1Q_1Q_2\psi = \psi$, and likewise $Q_2\psi = \psi$; conversely a vector in both ranges is fixed by the product. So $P_S$ projects onto $\bigcap_j\ker(g_j - I) = V_S$.

The expansion. $\prod_j\tfrac{I + g_j}2 = 2^{-r}\sum_{T\subseteq\{1..r\}}\prod_{j\in T}g_j$. Distinct subsets $T\ne T'$ give distinct products: if $\prod_Tg_j = \prod_{T'}g_j$, multiply by $\prod_{T'}g_j$ and use commutativity and $g_j^2 = I$ to get $\prod_{T\triangle T'}g_j = I$, so some $g_j$ with $j\in T\triangle T'$ is a product of the others — contradicting independence. Every element of $S$ is some such product, so the $2^r$ products are exactly the elements of $S$, each once; in particular $|S| = 2^r$.

The traces. Every $g\in S$ is $\pm P$ for a string $P$ (previous slide). If $P = I^{\otimes n}$ then $g = \pm I$, and $-I\notin S$, so $g = I$. Otherwise $\operatorname{tr}P = \prod_j\operatorname{tr}P_j$ (unit 03) contains a factor $\operatorname{tr}X$, $\operatorname{tr}Y$ or $\operatorname{tr}Z$, all $0$. Hence $\operatorname{tr}P_S = 2^{-r}\operatorname{tr}I = 2^{n-r}$, and the trace of an orthogonal projector is its rank, since its eigenvalues are 0 and 1 (unit 00).

The halving view. $P_j g_{j+1} = 2^{-j}\sum_{g\in S_j}g\,g_{j+1}$, with $S_j = \langle g_1..g_j\rangle$; no $g\,g_{j+1}$ is $\pm I$, by independence and $-I\notin S$, so each has trace 0. Thus each new independent generator cuts the dimension exactly in half — never by less, never by more. A dependent generator would change nothing.

Check. $r = 0$: $S = \{I\}$, $V_S$ is everything, $2^n$ ✓. $r = n$: a single state up to phase — the stabiliser states of section 5.

Paulis as bit strings

Forget the phase and a Pauli string is $2n$ bits. Multiplication becomes addition, commuting becomes a bilinear form, and a stabiliser group becomes a subspace.

23 · symplectic algebra

Linear algebra over the field with two elements

$\mathbb F_2$

$\{0, 1\}$ with addition XOR and multiplication AND: a field. Vectors are bit strings in $\mathbb F_2^m$; $u\cdot v = \sum_ju_jv_j$ mod 2.

What carries over. Every theorem whose proof uses only the field axioms: span, independence, basis, dimension, Gaussian elimination, rank–nullity. A subspace of dimension $d$ has exactly $2^d$ elements.

what does not

Positivity. $v\cdot v$ is the weight of $v$ mod 2, so $1100\cdot1100 = 0$: a nonzero vector can be orthogonal to itself, and $W\cap W^\perp$ need not be $\{0\}$. The simplex code lies inside its own dual (unit 22). No Gram–Schmidt.

what survives: the complement's dimension

Let $\beta$ be a symmetric non-degenerate bilinear form on $\mathbb F_2^m$ ($\beta(u, \cdot) = 0 \Rightarrow u = 0$) and $W$ a subspace. Then $$\dim W^\beta = m - \dim W,\qquad (W^\beta)^\beta = W,$$ where $W^\beta = \{v : \beta(w, v) = 0\ \forall w\in W\}$.

proof

With $w_1, \dots, w_d$ a basis of $W$, $W^\beta$ is the kernel of $v\mapsto(\beta(w_i, v))_i$, a linear map onto $\mathbb F_2^d$; rank–nullity gives $m - d$. And $W\subseteq(W^\beta)^\beta$, both of dimension $d$.

For the learner who took unit 14. This is that unit's The field with two elements, restated so that this unit stands on its own path; skip to the theorem.

Why the field axioms suffice. The proofs of the exchange lemma, of rank–nullity and of the correctness of Gaussian elimination divide only by nonzero scalars and never compare sizes. Over $\mathbb F_2$ the only nonzero scalar is 1, so elimination is just adding rows (XOR). The count $2^d$: a basis gives a bijection $\mathbb F_2^d\to W$, $(c_1..c_d)\mapsto\sum c_iw_i$.

Full proof of the theorem. Write $\beta(u, v) = u^{\mathsf T}Av$ with $A$ the Gram matrix of $\beta$ in the standard basis. Non-degeneracy says $u^{\mathsf T}A = 0\Rightarrow u = 0$, i.e. $A$ is invertible. The map $v\mapsto(\beta(w_1, v), \dots, \beta(w_d, v))$ is $v\mapsto BAv$, where $B$ is the $d\times m$ matrix with rows $w_i^{\mathsf T}$. $B$ has rank $d$ (its rows are a basis) and $A$ is invertible, so $BA$ has rank $d$: the map is onto $\mathbb F_2^d$. Rank–nullity: its kernel $W^\beta$ has dimension $m - d$. For the second claim, every $w\in W$ satisfies $\beta(v, w) = \beta(w, v) = 0$ for all $v\in W^\beta$, using symmetry, so $W\subseteq(W^\beta)^\beta$; and the first claim applied twice gives $\dim(W^\beta)^\beta = m - (m - d) = d$.

Check. The dot product on $\mathbb F_2^7$ is symmetric and non-degenerate (its Gram matrix is $I$). Hamming has dimension 4, so its dual has dimension $7 - 4 = 3$: the simplex code ✓.

The binary symplectic representation

the map $v$, and the symplectic form

Code each letter by two bits, $I = (0|0)$, $X = (1|0)$, $Z = (0|1)$, $Y = (1|1)$, and a string by $v(P) = (x\,|\,z)\in\mathbb F_2^{2n}$, ignoring the phase. On $\mathbb F_2^{2n}$, $$\omega\big((x|z), (x'|z')\big) = x\cdot z' + z\cdot x'.$$

products add, commutators pair

For $P, Q\in\mathcal P_n$: $\ v(PQ) = v(P) + v(Q)$, and $\ PQ = (-1)^{\omega(v(P), v(Q))}QP$.

proof

Both are per qubit, then summed. Products: $XZ\propto Y$ is $10 + 01 = 11$; $XY\propto Z$ is $10 + 11 = 01$; and so on. Commutators: $x_jz_j' + z_jx_j' = 1$ exactly when the two letters are different and neither is $I$ — the count of the earlier slide.

One qubit: $\mathbb F_2^2$, and $\omega = 1$ between any two of $X, Y, Z$.

$\omega$ is bilinear, symmetric, has $\omega(u, u) = 0$, and is non-degenerate: its Gram matrix $J = \begin{pmatrix}0 & I\\ I & 0\end{pmatrix}$ is invertible.

Full proof. Every single-qubit Pauli is, up to phase, $X^xZ^z$ with $(x, z)$ its code: $I = X^0Z^0$, $X$, $Z$, and $Y = iXZ$ (unit 02: $XZ = -iY$). So every string is $i^c X^xZ^z$, meaning $\bigotimes_jX^{x_j}Z^{z_j}$. Then $X^xZ^zX^{x'}Z^{z'} = (-1)^{z\cdot x'}X^{x+x'}Z^{z+z'}$: move each $Z^{z_j}$ past $X^{x'_j}$ on the same qubit, which costs a sign exactly when $z_j = x'_j = 1$ (since $ZX = -XZ$), and use $X^2 = Z^2 = I$, so exponents add mod 2. That is the first claim, with the phase tracked. For the second, compare with the product in the other order: $X^{x'}Z^{z'}X^xZ^z = (-1)^{z'\cdot x}X^{x+x'}Z^{z+z'}$. The ratio is $(-1)^{z\cdot x' + z'\cdot x} = (-1)^{\omega}$.

The sixteen one-qubit cases. $\omega((x|z), (x'|z')) = xz' + zx'$. If either letter is $I$, both terms vanish. If the letters are equal, $xz' + zx' = 2xz = 0$. If they are two different non-identity letters, a check of the three pairs $X, Z$ ($1\cdot1 + 0\cdot0$), $X, Y$ ($1\cdot1 + 0\cdot1$), $Z, Y$ ($0\cdot1 + 1\cdot1$) gives 1 each time.

What is lost. $v$ is a homomorphism from $(\mathcal P_n, \cdot)$ onto $(\mathbb F_2^{2n}, +)$ whose kernel is the four phases $\{\pm I, \pm iI\}$. So $v$ forgets exactly the phase, and the $4^n$ strings correspond to the $2^{2n}$ vectors. Signs are tracked separately when they matter — in section 5 they will.

Why "symplectic". A non-degenerate bilinear form with $\omega(u, u) = 0$ for all $u$ is called symplectic, after the form of classical Hamiltonian mechanics, where $x$ would be position and $z$ momentum. Over $\mathbb F_2$, $-1 = +1$, so the form is also symmetric, and the theorem of the previous slide applies to it.

proof of the first row

The kernel of $v$ is $\{\pm I, \pm iI\}$, and $S$ meets it only in $I$: $-I\notin S$, and $(\pm iI)^2 = -I$.

the check matrix

The $r\times2n$ binary matrix $G$ whose rows are $v(g_1), \dots, v(g_r)$. Then: $$\text{commuting}\iff GJG^{\mathsf T} = 0,$$ $$\text{independent}\iff\operatorname{rank}G = r.$$

A bound for free. Isotropic means $v(S)\subseteq v(S)^\omega$, so $r\le2n - r$: at most $n$ independent commuting generators, and $k = n - r\ge0$ ✓.

Cost. Deciding independence is elimination on an $r\times2n$ matrix: $O(r^2n)$ bit operations, against a $2^n\times2^n$ projector.

Full proof

One-to-one. A homomorphism is injective iff its kernel is trivial. $v|_S$ has kernel $S\cap\{\pm I, \pm iI\}$. $-I\notin S$ by definition; if $\pm iI\in S$ then its square $-I\in S$. So the kernel is $\{I\}$, and $S\cong v(S)$ as groups. A subgroup of $(\mathbb F_2^{2n}, +)$ is automatically a subspace (the only scalars are 0 and 1).

Abelian iff isotropic. By the previous slide, $g, h$ commute iff $\omega(v(g), v(h)) = 0$. Since $\omega$ is bilinear, it vanishes on all of $v(S)$ iff it vanishes on the generators, which is the matrix condition: the $(i, j)$ entry of $GJG^{\mathsf T}$ is $\omega(v(g_i), v(g_j))$.

Independence. $g_j$ is a product of the others iff $v(g_j)$ is a sum of the others' vectors — the forward direction by the homomorphism property, the backward one because $v$ is one-to-one on $S$. Over $\mathbb F_2$ "a sum of some of the others" is "a linear combination of the others".

The bound. By Linear algebra over the field with two elements, $\dim v(S)^\omega = 2n - r$, and isotropy puts $v(S)$ inside it. When $r = n$ the two are equal: $v(S)$ is then called Lagrangian, and that equality is what makes measurements on stabiliser states deterministic or fair coins, in section 5.

Check, the bit-flip code. $v(ZZI) = (000|110)$, $v(IZZ) = (000|011)$: independent, rank 2; $\omega = 0$ since both $x$ parts vanish ✓.

23 · syndromes and logicals

The syndrome is a matrix–vector product

the syndrome

For a Pauli error $E$ and generators $g_1, \dots, g_r$: $$s(E) = \big(\omega(v(g_j), v(E))\big)_{j=1}^r = GJ\,v(E)^{\mathsf T}\in\mathbb F_2^r.$$

what measuring the generators returns

If $\psi\in V_S$, then $E\psi$ is a $(-1)^{s_j}$ eigenvector of every $g_j$. Measuring $g_j$ returns $(-1)^{s_j(E)}$ with certainty and leaves $E\psi$ unchanged — whatever $\psi$ was.

proof

$g_jE\psi = (-1)^{s_j}Eg_j\psi = (-1)^{s_j}E\psi$.

Unit 22's third objection, answered in one line. The outcome depends on $E$ and not on $\psi$: the measurement learns nothing about the encoded state, and does not disturb it.

Linear. $s(EF) = s(E) + s(F)$, since $v$ is additive and $\omega$ bilinear. A decoder is any rule choosing, for each syndrome, a Pauli $R$ with that syndrome; it applies $R$.

when correction succeeds

After $E$ and the correction $R$, the state is $RE\psi$ with $s(RE) = 0$, so $RE\in N(S)$ (next slides). Success means $RE$ acts on $V_S$ as a phase: $RE\in\langle S, iI\rangle$. Not $RE = I$: an error and its correction may differ by a stabiliser.

The proof, slowly. $g_j$ and $E$ are Paulis, so $g_jE = \pm Eg_j$, with the sign $(-1)^{\omega(v(g_j), v(E))}$ by The binary symplectic representation. And $g_j\psi = \psi$ because $\psi\in V_S$. So $E\psi$ lies in an eigenspace of $g_j$, and by The experiment: measuring a product of Paulis the measurement of $g_j$ has outcome probability $\bra{E\psi}\tfrac{I\pm g_j}2\ket{E\psi}$, which is 1 for the sign $(-1)^{s_j}$; the Lüders state is $E\psi$ itself. The $r$ measurements commute, so their order does not matter.

Connection to unit 22. The three objections were: no-cloning (answered by encoding into an entangled subspace rather than copying), continuous errors (answered by discretisation), and measurement destroying the state (answered here: the syndrome measurement acts as the identity on every corrupted code state $E\psi$). For a general error, a sum $\sum_a c_aE_a$ of Paulis with different syndromes, the syndrome measurement collapses the sum onto one term — the discretisation theorem seen from the measurement side.

The bit-flip code, as a check. Generators $ZZI$, $IZZ$. An $X_0$ error has $v = (100|000)$, syndrome $(\omega(000|110, 100|000), \omega(000|011, 100|000)) = (1, 0)$; $X_1$ gives $(1, 1)$; $X_2$ gives $(0, 1)$; no error $(0, 0)$. That is unit 22's syndrome table, read off a $2\times6$ matrix.

Why not $RE = I$. If $RE = g\in S$ then $RE\psi = g\psi = \psi$ for every code state: perfect recovery although $R\ne E^{-1}$. This freedom is degeneracy, and the Knill–Laflamme slide shows it is harmless.

2. The $X$-type checks $g_1, g_2, g_3$ have $v = (h|0)$, so $\omega = h\cdot z = h_5$: the fifth column of $M$, $\mathbf{101}$.

3. The $Z$-type checks $g_4, g_5, g_6$ have $v = (0|h)$, so $\omega = h\cdot x = h_5$: again $\mathbf{101}$.

5. Correct. Apply $R = Y_5$: $RE = Y_5^2 = I$, and the encoded state is restored exactly ✓.

Qubit $j$ sits inside the circles of the rows where $M$ has a 1 in column $j$. Qubit 5: top and right circles, so outcomes $(-1, +1, -1)$ from $g_1, g_2, g_3$ and again from $g_4, g_5, g_6$.

The outcomes, letter by letter. $g_1 = IIIXXXX$ has $X$ on qubit 5; $Y$ and $X$ anticommute, so $g_1$ reads $-1$. $g_2 = IXXIIXX$ has $I$ on qubit 5, reads $+1$. $g_3 = XIXIXIX$ has $X$ there, $-1$. Why it is a decoder, not a guess. Every single-qubit Pauli on qubit $j$ gives one of three syndromes: $X_j\mapsto(000\,|\,\mathrm{bin}\,j)$, $Z_j\mapsto(\mathrm{bin}\,j\,|\,000)$, $Y_j\mapsto(\mathrm{bin}\,j\,|\,\mathrm{bin}\,j)$. Since the columns of $M$ are the seven distinct nonzero 3-bit strings, the 21 single-qubit errors have 21 distinct nonzero syndromes, and no error has syndrome $0$. So the table "syndrome $\mapsto$ the unique weight-one error with it" corrects every single-qubit Pauli error — and by unit 22's discretisation, every single-qubit channel. The normaliser, and $2k$ logical operators

the normaliser $N(S)$

The Paulis commuting with every element of $S$. They map $V_S$ to itself: if $\psi\in V_S$ and $P\in N(S)$, then $gP\psi = Pg\psi = P\psi$. Their syndrome is $0$: the code cannot see them.

counting logical operators

$v(N(S)) = v(S)^\omega$, of dimension $2n - r$, containing $v(S)$. The quotient $v(N(S))/v(S)$ has dimension $2(n - r) = 2k$, and $\omega$ is non-degenerate on it. So there are logical operators $\bar X_1, \bar Z_1, \dots, \bar X_k, \bar Z_k$ that anticommute in pairs $\bar X_i, \bar Z_i$ and commute otherwise.

Everything up to phase: detected errors outside $N(S)$; harmless ones in $S$; logical operators in between.

Check, bit-flip code ($n = 3$, $r = 2$): $2k = 2$ — and $\bar X = XXX$, $\bar Z = ZII$ commute with $ZZI$, $IZZ$ and anticommute with each other ✓.

Full proof

$v(N(S)) = v(S)^\omega$. $P\in N(S)$ iff $P$ commutes with each element of $S$ iff $\omega(v(g), v(P)) = 0$ for all $g\in S$ iff $v(P)\in v(S)^\omega$. By Linear algebra over the field with two elements, $\dim v(S)^\omega = 2n - r$. Isotropy gives $v(S)\subseteq v(S)^\omega$. The quotient has dimension $(2n - r) - r = 2k$.

Non-degenerate on the quotient. $\omega$ is well defined on cosets because $\omega(u + s, w) = \omega(u, w)$ for $s\in v(S)$, $w\in v(S)^\omega$. If $u\in v(S)^\omega$ has $\omega(u, w) = 0$ for every $w\in v(S)^\omega$, then $u\in(v(S)^\omega)^\omega = v(S)$, so its coset is zero.

Pairs. A non-degenerate alternating form on a space of dimension $2k$ has a basis $a_1, b_1, \dots, a_k, b_k$ with $\omega(a_i, b_i) = 1$ and all other pairs 0. Induction: pick any $a_1\ne0$; non-degeneracy gives $b_1$ with $\omega(a_1, b_1) = 1$; the subspace $W = \{u : \omega(u, a_1) = \omega(u, b_1) = 0\}$ has dimension $2k - 2$ (two independent conditions), meets $\mathrm{span}(a_1, b_1)$ only in 0 (if $u = \alpha a_1 + \beta b_1\in W$ then $\omega(u, b_1) = \alpha$ and $\omega(u, a_1) = \beta$ both vanish), so the whole space is $\mathrm{span}(a_1, b_1)\oplus W$, and the form is non-degenerate on $W$; repeat. Choosing Paulis $\bar X_i, \bar Z_i$ with $v$ in the cosets $a_i, b_i$ gives the logical operators. They obey the commutation relations of $k$ separate qubits' $X$ and $Z$.

Normaliser, the word. In group theory the normaliser of $S$ is $\{P : PSP^{-1} = S\}$. For Paulis, $PgP^{-1} = \pm g$, and $-g\in S$ would put $-I = (-g)g$ in $S$; so $PSP^{-1} = S$ forces $P$ to commute with every $g$. Inside the Pauli group, normaliser and centraliser coincide, and the literature says normaliser.

The check. $XXX$ against $ZZI$: two positions with $X$ against $Z$, even, commute. Against $IZZ$: two, commute. $ZII$ commutes with both (all $Z$'s). $XXX$ against $ZII$: one position, anticommute. On the code, $XXX$ swaps $\ket{000}$ and $\ket{111}$, and $ZII$ gives them signs $+, -$: they are $\bar X$ and $\bar Z$.

Logical means nontrivial, and distance means lightest

lemma: which normaliser elements act trivially

$P\in N(S)$ acts on $V_S$ as a scalar if and only if $P\in\langle S, iI\rangle$, that is $P = i^cg$ with $g\in S$.

proof

If $PP_S = cP_S$, then $c\ne0$ ($P$ is invertible) and $\operatorname{tr}(PP_S) = c\,2^k\ne0$. But $\operatorname{tr}(PP_S) = 2^{-r}\sum_{g\in S}\operatorname{tr}(Pg)$, and $\operatorname{tr}(Pg) = 0$ unless $Pg\propto I$, i.e. $P\propto g$. So some $g$ has $P\propto g$.

distance, and $[[n, k, d]]$

$d$ is the least weight of an element of $N(S)\setminus\langle S, iI\rangle$: the lightest Pauli that changes the encoded state without the syndrome noticing. An $[[n, k, d]]$ code has $n$ qubits, $k$ encoded, distance $d$.

Detection. A Pauli of weight $\lt d$ either has nonzero syndrome or lies in $\langle S, iI\rangle$, where it does nothing. Unit 22 defined distance as the least weight of an error the code cannot detect; this is the same number, now computable.

Check: bit-flip code, $\bar Z = ZII$ has weight 1: $d = 1$, as unit 22 found for phase flips.

Full proof of the lemma

If $P = i^cg$ with $g\in S$, then $P\psi = i^c\psi$ on $V_S$. Conversely, $P\in N(S)$ maps $V_S$ into itself, so "acts as a scalar $c$" means $PP_S = cP_S$. $P$ is unitary and $P_S\ne0$ (dimension $2^k\ge1$), so $c\ne0$ and $\operatorname{tr}(PP_S) = c\operatorname{tr}P_S = c\,2^k\ne0$. Expand $P_S = 2^{-r}\sum_{g\in S}g$. Each $Pg$ is in $\mathcal P_n$; its trace is 0 unless it is a phase times $I^{\otimes n}$. If none is, the sum vanishes — a contradiction. So $Pg = i^cI$ for some $g$, and $P = i^cg^{-1} = i^cg$ ($g^2 = I$).

Consequence. Every element of $N(S)$ outside $\langle S, iI\rangle$ acts on the code as a non-scalar unitary: it changes some encoded state. In the language of the previous slide, a normaliser element whose coset in $v(N(S))/v(S)$ is nonzero acts nontrivially — so the $2k$ logical operators are all genuinely different operations on the encoded qubits.

Detection, in full. Let $E$ have weight $\lt d$. If $s(E)\ne0$ some generator flags it. If $s(E) = 0$ then $E\in N(S)$, and since its weight is below $d$ it is not in $N(S)\setminus\langle S, iI\rangle$; so it is in $\langle S, iI\rangle$ and multiplies every code state by the same phase. Either way the code state is safe: flagged, or untouched.

The other convention. Some texts define $d$ with $N(S)\setminus S$, ignoring phases. Since phases do not change weight, the two definitions give the same number.

Knill–Laflamme for stabiliser codes

unit 22's conditions, decided by $\omega$

For Pauli errors $\{E_a\}$, the Knill–Laflamme conditions $P_SE_a^\dagger E_bP_S = \alpha_{ab}P_S$ hold for every $a, b$ if and only if no $E_a^\dagger E_b$ lies in $N(S)\setminus\langle S, iI\rangle$.

proof: three cases for $F = E_a^\dagger E_b$

(i) $F$ anticommutes with some $g\in S$. Then $P_S = P_Sg = gP_S$, so $P_SFP_S = P_SFgP_S = -P_SgFP_S = -P_SFP_S = 0$: $\alpha_{ab} = 0$.
(ii) $F = i^cg$, $g\in S$: $P_SFP_S = i^cP_S$.
(iii) $F\in N(S)\setminus\langle S, iI\rangle$: $P_SFP_S = FP_S$, not a multiple of $P_S$ by the lemma. Fails.

corollary

A stabiliser code of distance $d$ corrects every Pauli error of weight $\le t$ when $d\ge2t + 1$ — and so, by unit 22's discretisation, every error acting on at most $t$ qubits.

Proof. $E_a^\dagger E_b$ has weight $\le2t\lt d$, so case (iii) is impossible.

Read the cases. (i) is detected: $E_a$ and $E_b$ have different syndromes. (ii) is degenerate: different errors, the same action on the code. (iii) is a logical error: undetectable, and harmful.

Full proof. $F$ is a Pauli, so exactly one of three things holds: it anticommutes with some element of $S$ (equivalently some generator), or it commutes with all of $S$ — in which case it lies in $\langle S, iI\rangle$ or it does not. Case (i): $g\in S$ fixes $V_S$ pointwise, so $gP_S = P_S$, and taking adjoints ($g$ and $P_S$ Hermitian) $P_Sg = P_S$. Then the chain on the slide uses $Fg = -gF$. Case (ii) is immediate from $gP_S = P_S$. Case (iii): $F$ commutes with each $g_j$, hence with $P_S$, so $P_SFP_S = FP_S^2 = FP_S$; if this were $\alpha P_S$, then $F$ would act on $V_S$ as the scalar $\alpha$, and the lemma would put $F$ in $\langle S, iI\rangle$. So the condition fails. The matrix $\alpha$ in cases (i)–(ii) has entries 0 or a phase, and it is Hermitian because $(E_a^\dagger E_b)^\dagger = E_b^\dagger E_a$.

Corollary. Weight is subadditive: $\operatorname{wt}(PQ)\le\operatorname{wt}P + \operatorname{wt}Q$, since $PQ$ is non-$I$ only where $P$ or $Q$ is. So $\operatorname{wt}(E_a^\dagger E_b)\le2t$. Every product of two errors from the set is in case (i) or (ii); Knill–Laflamme holds; the set of Pauli errors of weight $\le t$ is correctable. Any operator on $t$ given qubits is a linear combination of Pauli strings supported there, so unit 22's corollary (correcting a set corrects its span) extends it to every error on at most $t$ qubits.

Degeneracy, matched to unit 22. There, a code was degenerate for an error set when the matrix $\alpha$ is singular. For Pauli errors: if $E_a^\dagger E_b = i^cg\in\langle S, iI\rangle$ for some $a\ne b$, then $E_b = i^cE_ag$ acts on the code exactly as $i^cE_a$, so rows $a$ and $b$ of $\alpha$ are proportional and $\alpha$ is singular. If no such pair exists, every off-diagonal entry is case (i), zero, and $\alpha = I$. So for Pauli errors: degenerate iff two distinct errors differ by an element of $\langle S, iI\rangle$.

Steane's logical operators, and distance 3

The normaliser. $(a|b)$ commutes with the $X$-checks $(h|0)$ iff $h\cdot b = 0$ for every row $h$ of $M$: $b\in$ (row space of $M$)$^\perp$ = simplex$^\perp$ = Hamming. Likewise $a\in$ Hamming. So $$N(S) = \{\,i^cX^aZ^b : a, b\in\mathrm{Ham}\,\},\qquad S = \{X^aZ^b : a, b\in\mathrm{Sim}\}.$$ Dimensions $4 + 4 = 8 = 2\cdot7 - 6$ ✓ and $3 + 3 = 6$ ✓.

the logical pair

$\bar X = X^{\otimes7}$, $\bar Z = Z^{\otimes7}$: $1111111$ is in Hamming ($M$'s rows have even weight) but not in simplex (weights 0 and 4). They overlap in 7 places, odd: $\bar X\bar Z = -\bar Z\bar X$ ✓.

Distance. Outside $\langle S, iI\rangle$, $a$ or $b$ is a Hamming word not in simplex, so nonzero, so of weight $\ge3$. And $X_1X_2X_3 = \bar Xg_1$ has weight 3. So $d = 3$: $[[7, 1, 3]]$, and $2\cdot1 + 1 = 3$ — one error corrected.

The seven weight-3 Hamming words are the seven lines of the Fano plane; each is a weight-3 $\bar X$ (and $\bar Z$). Highlighted: $\{1, 2, 3\}$.

The normaliser, in full. $v(P) = (a|b)$. Against an $X$-type generator $(h|0)$: $\omega = h\cdot b + 0\cdot a = h\cdot b$. Against a $Z$-type one $(0|h)$: $\omega = a\cdot h$. So $P\in N(S)$ iff $a$ and $b$ are both orthogonal to all rows of $M$, i.e. both lie in $\ker M$ = the Hamming code (that is what the parity-check matrix means). $S$ itself is $\{X^aZ^b : a, b\in\text{row space of }M\}$, the simplex code, as shown in the notes to Steane's six generators.

Distance 3, in full. If $i^cX^aZ^b\notin\langle S, iI\rangle$ then not both $a, b$ are in simplex; say $a\notin$ simplex (the other case is the same). Then $a\ne0$ (0 is in simplex) and $a\in$ Hamming, whose least nonzero weight is 3 (unit 22). The weight of $X^aZ^b$ is $|\mathrm{supp}\,a\cup\mathrm{supp}\,b|\ge|a|\ge3$. And $X_1X_2X_3$: $1110000\in$ Hamming since columns 1, 2, 3 of $M$ sum to $001 + 010 + 011 = 000$; it is not in simplex (weight 3); so it is a logical operator of weight 3. Its product with $\bar X$ is $X^{0001111} = g_1$, so $X_1X_2X_3 = \bar Xg_1$ acts on the code exactly as $\bar X$.

Action on the code. Which of $\bar X$, $\bar Z$ is "logical $X$" is a choice, fixed by declaring $\ket{0_L}$ to be the $+1$ eigenvector of $\bar Z$ in $V_S$; section 4 writes $\ket{0_L}$ and $\ket{1_L}$ out and checks $\bar X$ swaps them.

The Fano plane. The weight-3 codewords $\{i, j, k\}$ are exactly the triples with $i\oplus j = k$ as 3-bit numbers (their columns of $M$ sum to zero): $\{1,2,3\}$, $\{1,4,5\}$, $\{1,6,7\}$, $\{2,4,6\}$, $\{2,5,7\}$, $\{3,4,7\}$, $\{3,5,6\}$. Any two points lie on exactly one such triple — the Fano plane, the projective plane over $\mathbb F_2$.

Shor's code in the formalism: degeneracy made visible

Unit 22's nine qubits, qubit 0 first: eight generators, $r = 8$, $k = 9 - 8 = 1$.

Generators. $Z_0Z_1, Z_1Z_2, Z_3Z_4, Z_4Z_5, Z_6Z_7, Z_7Z_8$ (bit-flip checks inside blocks) and $X_0\cdots X_5$, $X_3\cdots X_8$ (phase comparisons between blocks). Each $ZZ$ meets each $X^{\otimes6}$ in 0 or 2 places: all commute.

Logicals. $\bar X = Z_0Z_3Z_6$ (one $Z$ per block flips each block's sign) and $\bar Z = X_0X_1X_2$; each commutes with all eight generators, and they overlap once: anticommute ✓. Weight 3, and nothing lighter is logical: $d = 3$.

degenerate, and still fine

$Z_0$ and $Z_1$ have the same syndrome, and $Z_0^\dagger Z_1 = Z_0Z_1\in S$: case (ii). Correcting $Z_1$ by $Z_0$ leaves $Z_0Z_1$, a stabiliser, which does nothing. Of the 27 one-qubit Paulis, 21 distinct syndromes: three $Z$'s per block share one.

Unit 22 saw this as a singular $\alpha$; here it is one line: $Z_0Z_1\in S$.

The code space. Unit 22's Shor code has $\ket{0_L} = \tfrac1{2\sqrt2}(\ket{000} + \ket{111})^{\otimes3}$ and $\ket{1_L} = \tfrac1{2\sqrt2}(\ket{000} - \ket{111})^{\otimes3}$. Each $Z_iZ_{i+1}$ inside a block fixes both $\ket{000}$ and $\ket{111}$. The operator $X_0\cdots X_5$ swaps $\ket{000}\leftrightarrow\ket{111}$ in blocks 1 and 2, which multiplies each of those blocks by $+1$ (for $+$) or $-1$ (for $-$); two such signs cancel. So both basis states are fixed by all eight generators, and the dimension $2^{9-8} = 2$ says there is nothing else.

Logicals. $Z_0$ sends $\ket{111}$ to $-\ket{111}$ in block 1, turning $\ket{000} + \ket{111}$ into $\ket{000} - \ket{111}$; one per block turns $\ket{0_L}$ into $\ket{1_L}$ and back. So $Z_0Z_3Z_6$ is a logical $X$ — the letters swap roles, because Shor's outer code is a phase-flip code. $X_0X_1X_2$ maps block 1's $\ket{000}\pm\ket{111}$ to $\pm(\ket{000}\pm\ket{111})$: $+1$ on $\ket{0_L}$, $-1$ on $\ket{1_L}$, a logical $Z$. Commutation with the generators: $Z_0Z_3Z_6$ against $X_0\cdots X_5$ overlaps at 0 and 3, even ✓; against $X_3\cdots X_8$ at 3 and 6 ✓. $X_0X_1X_2$ against $Z_0Z_1$ and $Z_1Z_2$ overlaps twice each ✓.

Distance 3. Checked by listing every Pauli of weight 1 and 2 in the normaliser: all lie in $\langle S, iI\rangle$ (for instance $Z_0Z_1$). Problem P6 does it by hand, using the block structure.

Degeneracy is not a defect. A degenerate code can correct errors it cannot tell apart, because telling them apart was never needed. It also escapes the counting argument of the quantum Hamming bound in section 4, which needs every correctable error to move the code to its own orthogonal subspace.

CSS: quantum codes from classical ones

Two nested classical codes, one to catch bit flips and one to catch phase flips, make a quantum code. Steane's is the first; the surface code of unit 24 is another.

23 · CSS

Coset states

the CSS code of $C_2\subseteq C_1$

Classical linear codes $C_2\subseteq C_1\subseteq\mathbb F_2^n$ of dimensions $k_2\lt k_1$. For $x\in C_1$, the coset state $$\ket{x + C_2} = \frac1{\sqrt{|C_2|}}\sum_{y\in C_2}\ket{x + y}.$$ $\mathrm{CSS}(C_1, C_2)$ is the span of all of them.

parameters

$\mathrm{CSS}(C_1, C_2)$ has dimension $2^{k_1 - k_2}$: it is an $[[n, k_1 - k_2]]$ code, with the coset states as an orthonormal basis.

Steane's case: $C_1$ Hamming, $C_2$ simplex, $2^{4-3} = 2$ cosets, so one qubit.

Proof. Normalised: $|C_2| = 2^{k_2}$ distinct basis vectors, each amplitude $2^{-k_2/2}$ ✓. $\ket{x + C_2}$ depends only on the coset $x + C_2$. Distinct cosets are disjoint, so their states have disjoint supports: orthogonal. There are $|C_1|/|C_2| = 2^{k_1 - k_2}$ cosets.

Where the idea comes from. Calderbank and Shor (Phys. Rev. A 54, 1098, 1996) and Steane (Proc. R. Soc. A 452, 2551, 1996), independently. The intuition: superpose a whole classical codeword class, so that a bit flip moves the state off $C_1$ — detectable with $C_1$'s parity checks — while a phase flip, after a Hadamard on every qubit, becomes a bit flip relative to a different classical code, $C_2^\perp$. Two classical codes, one per kind of error.

Full proof of the parameters. If $x' = x + c$ with $c\in C_2$, then $\{x' + y : y\in C_2\} = \{x + y : y\in C_2\}$ because $y\mapsto y + c$ is a bijection of $C_2$; so the state depends only on the coset. Two cosets of a subgroup are equal or disjoint (assumed background), so distinct coset states are sums over disjoint sets of computational basis vectors: their inner product is 0. Each coset has $|C_2|$ elements and $C_1$ is their disjoint union, so there are $|C_1|/|C_2| = 2^{k_1}/2^{k_2}$ of them (Lagrange), each a unit vector. An orthonormal set spans a space of its own size.

Steane's two coset states. Hamming has 16 words, simplex 8, so two cosets: simplex itself, and $1111111 +$ simplex (since $1111111\in$ Hamming $\setminus$ simplex). Written out on Steane is CSS of Hamming and simplex.

CSS codes are stabiliser codes

the CSS construction (Calderbank–Shor, Steane, 1996)

Let $M_1$ be a parity-check matrix of $C_1$ ($C_1 = \ker M_1$, $n - k_1$ rows) and $G_2$ a generator matrix of $C_2$ ($k_2$ rows). Then $\mathrm{CSS}(C_1, C_2) = V_S$ for $$S = \langle\,Z^h\ (h \text{ a row of } M_1),\ \ X^g\ (g\text{ a row of }G_2)\,\rangle.$$

proof

Commute: $X^g$ and $Z^h$ differ where both are 1, so commute iff $g\cdot h = 0$; and $g\in C_2\subseteq C_1 = \ker M_1$ is orthogonal to every row $h$ of $M_1$. This is where $C_2\subseteq C_1$ is used.
Count: $r = (n - k_1) + k_2$ independent generators, so $\dim V_S = 2^{k_1 - k_2}$.
Fixed: $Z^h\ket{x + y} = (-1)^{h\cdot(x + y)}\ket{x + y} = \ket{x + y}$ as $x + y\in C_1$; $\ X^g\ket{x + C_2} = \ket{x + g + C_2} = \ket{x + C_2}$.

So the span of the coset states lies in $V_S$ and has the same dimension: they are equal. ∎

$G_2$ is also a parity-check matrix

$\ker G_2 = \{v : g\cdot v = 0\ \forall g\in C_2\} = C_2^\perp$. So the $X$-checks are the parity checks of the classical code $C_2^\perp$: that is the code phase flips will be decoded in.

Check on the bit-flip code: $C_1 = \{000, 111\} = \ker\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\0&1&1\end{pmatrix}$, $C_2 = \{000\}$: generators $ZZI$, $IZZ$, no $X$'s, $k = 1 - 0 = 1$, coset states $\ket{000}$, $\ket{111}$ ✓.

Full proof

Commutation. $X^g$ and $Z^h$ have $v = (g|0)$ and $(0|h)$, $\omega = g\cdot h$. Rows of $M_1$ are orthogonal to every element of $C_1$ (that is what $C_1 = \ker M_1$ says), and $g\in C_2\subseteq C_1$. Two $X$-type or two $Z$-type generators commute trivially.

Independence and $-I$. The check matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}0 & M_1\\G_2 & 0\end{pmatrix}$; $M_1$ has independent rows (a parity-check matrix has full row rank, $n - k_1$, by rank–nullity on $\ker M_1 = C_1$) and so does $G_2$; the blocks occupy different columns. So rank $= n - k_1 + k_2$. And $-I\notin S$ by the sign argument of Steane's six generators: every element is $X^aZ^b$ with $a\in C_2$, $b\in C_1^\perp$, and $b\cdot a' = 0$ for all such pairs, so products never pick up a sign.

Coset states are fixed. $Z^h\ket u = (-1)^{h\cdot u}\ket u$ for any basis state $u$; every $u = x + y$ in the sum lies in $C_1$, so $h\cdot u = 0$. $X^g\ket u = \ket{u + g}$, and adding $g\in C_2$ permutes the terms of the coset sum. Since generators fixing a vector means the whole group does, each coset state lies in $V_S$. They are $2^{k_1-k_2}$ orthonormal vectors in a space of dimension $2^{n-r} = 2^{n - (n - k_1) - k_2} = 2^{k_1 - k_2}$: a basis of it.

The check. $C_1 = $ repetition code $\{000, 111\}$, $k_1 = 1$, and $C_2 = \{0\}$, $k_2 = 0$; $C_2\subseteq C_1$ trivially. No $X$ generators because $G_2$ has no rows. This is unit 22's bit-flip code; the phase-flip code is the CSS code with the roles of $X$ and $Z$ exchanged.

What a CSS code corrects: two classical decoders

bit flips by $C_1$, phase flips by $C_2^\perp$

For an error $X^eZ^f$ the $Z$-checks read $M_1e$ and the $X$-checks read $G_2f$. So $\mathrm{CSS}(C_1, C_2)$ corrects any $t$ bit flips and any $t$ phase flips together if $C_1$ and $C_2^\perp$ both have distance $\ge2t + 1$.

Logicals. $X^u$ for $u\in C_1\setminus C_2$; $\ Z^w$ for $w\in C_2^\perp\setminus C_1^\perp$. Hence $d\ge\min(d(C_1), d(C_2^\perp))$.

A $Y$ on one qubit is $X$ and $Z$ there: each half goes to its own decoder, as in the running example.

Why phase flips meet $C_2^\perp$: $H^{\otimes n}$ turns $Z$ errors into $X$ errors and maps $\ket{x + C_2}$ to a superposition over $C_2^\perp$ (notes).

Full proof

Syndromes. $v(X^eZ^f) = (e|f)$. Against $(0|h)$: $\omega = e\cdot h$, so the $Z$-checks read $M_1e$. Against $(g|0)$: $\omega = g\cdot f$, so the $X$-checks read $G_2f$. The first is the classical syndrome of $e$ in $C_1$, the second the classical syndrome of $f$ in $C_2^\perp$ (whose parity-check matrix is $G_2$). Suppose each classical code has distance $\ge2t + 1$, and run each classical decoder: it returns the unique $\hat e$ of weight $\le t$ with $M_1\hat e = M_1e$, which is $e$ itself when $|e|\le t$; likewise $\hat f = f$. Applying $X^{\hat e}Z^{\hat f}$ undoes the error up to a phase. Any error on at most $t$ qubits has $|e|, |f|\le t$.

Logicals. $X^u$ commutes with the $Z$-checks iff $M_1u = 0$ iff $u\in C_1$, and is in $S$ iff $u\in C_2$. $Z^w$ commutes with the $X$-checks iff $G_2w = 0$ iff $w\in C_2^\perp$, and is in $S$ iff $w$ is in the row space of $M_1$, which is $C_1^\perp$. A mixed normaliser element $X^aZ^b$ outside $\langle S, iI\rangle$ has $a\in C_1\setminus C_2$ or $b\in C_2^\perp\setminus C_1^\perp$, of weight at least $d(C_1)$ or $d(C_2^\perp)$ respectively.

The Hadamard picture. $H\ket b = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\sum_c(-1)^{bc}\ket c$, so $H^{\otimes n}\ket u = 2^{-n/2}\sum_w(-1)^{u\cdot w}\ket w$ (unit 03). Then $H^{\otimes n}\ket{x + C_2} = \frac{2^{-n/2}}{\sqrt{|C_2|}}\sum_w(-1)^{x\cdot w}\Big(\sum_{y\in C_2}(-1)^{y\cdot w}\Big)\ket w$. The inner sum is $|C_2|$ if $w\in C_2^\perp$ and 0 otherwise: if $w\notin C_2^\perp$, then $y\mapsto y\cdot w$ is a nonzero linear map $C_2\to\mathbb F_2$, so exactly half the $y$ give $0$ and half $1$. So the state becomes a signed uniform superposition over $C_2^\perp$, and $HZH = X$ turns phase flips into bit flips on it: the $X$-checks become $C_2^\perp$'s $Z$-type parity checks.

The five-qubit code: not CSS, and smaller

the five-qubit code (1996)

$$g_1 = XZZXI,\quad g_2 = IXZZX,\quad g_3 = XIXZZ,\quad g_4 = ZXIXZ,$$ each the cyclic shift of the last; the fifth shift $ZZXIX$ is $g_1g_2g_3g_4$.

Commute: each pair meets in exactly two anticommuting positions — e.g. $g_1, g_2$ at qubits 1 ($Z$ vs $X$) and 3 ($X$ vs $Z$) ✓. Independent: rank 4, so $k = 5 - 4 = 1$.

Distance 3. The 15 single-qubit errors have 15 distinct nonzero syndromes — all of $\mathbb F_2^4\setminus\{0\}$. Logicals $\bar X = XXXXX$, $\bar Z = ZZZZZ$: each meets every generator in two anticommuting places, and they meet each other in five.

The generators are one pattern turned round the pentagon. Each mixes $X$ and $Z$ in one string: not CSS form, unlike Steane's.

History. Found by Laflamme, Miquel, Paz and Zurek (Phys. Rev. Lett. 77, 198, 1996) and by Bennett, DiVincenzo, Smolin and Wootters (Phys. Rev. A 54, 3824, 1996), within months of Shor's nine-qubit code and Steane's seven. The stabiliser description is Gottesman's.

The commutation check. Positions where two generators have different non-$I$ letters: $g_1 = XZZXI$, $g_2 = IXZZX$ — qubit 0 has $I$; qubit 1, $Z$ vs $X$; qubit 2, $Z$ vs $Z$; qubit 3, $X$ vs $Z$; qubit 4 has $I$. Two, even. By cyclic symmetry the same count holds for every pair one shift apart; for pairs two apart, e.g. $g_1, g_3 = XIXZZ$: qubit 0, $X$ vs $X$; qubit 2, $Z$ vs $X$; qubit 3, $X$ vs $Z$; qubit 4 has $I$ in $g_1$: two again. All six pairs commute.

The syndromes (bits from $g_1..g_4$): $X$ on qubits 0–4 gives $0001, 1000, 1100, 0110, 0011$; $Z$ gives $1010, 0101, 0010, 1001, 0100$; $Y$ gives $1011, 1101, 1110, 1111, 0111$. Fifteen different nonzero strings.

Distance 3, in full. For two Paulis $E_a\ne E_b$ of weight $\le1$, $s(E_a^\dagger E_b) = s(E_a) + s(E_b)\ne0$, because the sixteen syndromes (fifteen plus $0$ for $I$) are distinct. So every such product anticommutes with a generator — case (i) of Knill–Laflamme — and no Pauli of weight 1 or 2 (each is such a product) is in $N(S)$ except $I$. Hence $d\ge3$. And $\bar Xg_1 = XXXXX\cdot XZZXI = -IYYIX$ (using $XZ = -iY$ twice) is a logical operator of weight 3, so $d = 3$. The five-qubit code is non-degenerate: distinct correctable errors never act alike.

Quantum LDPC codes — families whose generators each touch a bounded number of qubits, with rate and distance both growing — are the modern descendants of these small codes; they are beyond this course.

The quantum Hamming bound

the quantum Hamming bound

A non-degenerate $[[n, k]]$ stabiliser code correcting all Pauli errors of weight $\le t$ satisfies $$2^k\sum_{j=0}^{t}\binom nj3^j\le2^n.$$

proof

There are $N = \sum_j\binom nj3^j$ such errors (choose $j$ qubits, then one of $X, Y, Z$ on each). Non-degenerate and correctable: for $a\ne b$, $E_a^\dagger E_b$ is in case (i), so $P_SE_a^\dagger E_bP_S = 0$ and the subspaces $E_aV_S$ are mutually orthogonal, each of dimension $2^k$. They fit: $N2^k\le2^n$.

worked example 5 · perfect, and not

Five-qubit, $t = 1$: $N = 1 + 3\cdot5 = 16$, and $2\cdot16 = 32 = 2^5$: equality, a perfect code ✓. Steane: $N = 1 + 3\cdot7 = 22$, $2\cdot22 = 44\le128$, about a third full. $n = 4$: $2\cdot13 = 26\gt16$, so no non-degenerate $[[4, 1, 3]]$.

Full proof

Let $\{E_a\}$ be the $N$ Pauli strings of weight $\le t$ (including $I$). "Non-degenerate" means, as on Knill–Laflamme for stabiliser codes, that no $E_a^\dagger E_b$ with $a\ne b$ lies in $\langle S, iI\rangle$; "correctable" means none lies in $N(S)\setminus\langle S, iI\rangle$. So every such product anticommutes with some element of $S$, which is case (i): $P_SE_a^\dagger E_bP_S = 0$. For $\psi, \phi\in V_S$: $\braket{E_a\psi|E_b\phi} = \bra\psi P_SE_a^\dagger E_bP_S\ket\phi = 0$. Each $E_aV_S$ has dimension $2^k$ since $E_a$ is unitary. Pairwise orthogonal subspaces of $\mathbb C^{2^n}$ have dimensions summing to at most $2^n$.

Perfect. Equality means the orthogonal subspaces fill the space: every syndrome is used exactly once. For the five-qubit code, the 16 syndromes in $\mathbb F_2^4$ are the 15 single-qubit errors' plus $0$ for no error — the previous slide's list.

The numbers. $n = 5$: $\binom51\cdot3 = 15$. $n = 7$: $\binom71\cdot3 = 21$; $44/128 = 0.344$. $n = 4$: $1 + 12 = 13$, $26\gt16$. For $t = 2$, $k = 1$ the smallest $n$ the bound allows is $n = 10$: $2(1 + 30 + 405) = 872\le1024$, while $n = 9$ gives $2(1 + 27 + 324) = 704\gt512$.

Degenerate codes. The proof needs the error subspaces to be orthogonal, which degeneracy breaks: Shor's code has $Z_0V_S = Z_1V_S$. A bound that holds for every code, degenerate or not, is the quantum Singleton bound $n - k\ge2(d - 1)$ (Knill and Laflamme 1997, cited); for $k = 1$, $d = 3$ it gives $n\ge5$. So five qubits is the fewest any code correcting one arbitrary error can have, degenerate or not.

Follow the stabiliser, not the state

stabiliser state

A stabiliser group with $n$ independent generators fixes a space of dimension $2^{n-n} = 1$: a single state up to phase. $\ket{0^n}$ is the stabiliser state of $\langle Z_0, \dots, Z_{n-1}\rangle$.

conjugate the generators

If $g\psi = \psi$ then $(UgU^\dagger)\,U\psi = U\psi$. So $U\psi$ is the stabiliser state of $\langle Ug_1U^\dagger, \dots, Ug_nU^\dagger\rangle$ — and for $U$ Clifford these are Pauli strings again.

Conjugation is a group isomorphism, so the new generators still commute, are independent, and exclude $-I$.

the tableau

The $n\times2n$ check matrix of the generators plus $n$ sign bits: $2n^2 + n$ bits, against $2^n$ complex amplitudes.

One gate, $O(n)$ work. A gate on qubits $a, b$ changes only columns $a, b$ of each row and its sign, by the rules (row read as $\pm$ letters, $Y = (1|1)$, $r$ the sign bit):

$H_a$: $r \mathrel{\oplus}= x_az_a$; swap $x_a, z_a$. $\quad S_a$: $r\mathrel{\oplus}= x_az_a$; $z_a\mathrel{\oplus}= x_a$.
$\mathrm{CNOT}_{ab}$: $r\mathrel{\oplus}= x_az_b(x_b\oplus z_a\oplus1)$; $x_b\mathrel{\oplus}= x_a$; $z_a\mathrel{\oplus}= z_b$.

Check: $H$ on $Y$ ($x = z = 1$) flips the sign and keeps $Y$: $HYH = -Y$ ✓.

This is the Heisenberg picture. Instead of evolving the state, evolve the observables that describe it. Gottesman's 1998 paper is titled for it. For a general circuit nothing is gained, since an operator is a bigger object than a vector; for Clifford circuits, the operators stay Pauli strings, which are tiny.

Full proof of the update rules

Write a letter string in the form $L = i^{\sum_jx_jz_j}X^xZ^z$, which is right because each $Y = iXZ$ contributes one $i$. $H$ on one qubit: $HX^xZ^zH = Z^xX^z = (-1)^{xz}X^zZ^x$, while the letter form with $x' = z$, $z' = x$ has the same prefactor $i^{xz}$; so the image is $(-1)^{xz}$ times the new letters: sign flips iff $x = z = 1$. $S$: $SXS^\dagger = Y = iXZ$, $SZS^\dagger = Z$, so $X^xZ^z\mapsto i^xX^xZ^{x+z}$; with $z' = x\oplus z$ the letter prefactor is $i^{x(x\oplus z)}$, and the ratio $i^{xz + x - x(x\oplus z)}$ is $1$ unless $x = z = 1$, when it is $i^2 = -1$. CNOT sends $X_a\mapsto X_aX_b$, $Z_b\mapsto Z_aZ_b$ and fixes $Z_a$, $X_b$, so $X_a^{x_a}Z_a^{z_a}X_b^{x_b}Z_b^{z_b}\mapsto X_a^{x_a}Z_a^{z_a\oplus z_b}X_b^{x_a\oplus x_b}Z_b^{z_b}$ (the factors moved past each other are on different qubits). The sign is $i^e$ with $e = x_az_a + x_bz_b - x_a(z_a\oplus z_b) - (x_a\oplus x_b)z_b$. If $x_a = 0$ or $z_b = 0$, $e = 0$. If $x_a = z_b = 1$, the four cases $(z_a, x_b) = (0,0), (0,1), (1,0), (1,1)$ give $e = -2, 0, 0, 2$: a sign flip exactly when $x_b\oplus z_a\oplus1 = 1$. That is the rule.

A two-qubit check. $X_aZ_b$ under CNOT: $X_aX_b\cdot Z_aZ_b = (X_aZ_a)(X_bZ_b) = (-iY_a)(-iY_b) = -Y_aY_b$. The rule: $x_a = z_b = 1$, $x_b = z_a = 0$, so $r$ flips; $x_b$ becomes 1 and $z_a$ becomes 1: letters $YY$, sign $-$ ✓.

The cost. Each row changes in two columns and one sign bit: $O(1)$ per row, $O(n)$ per gate.

Measuring a Pauli on a stabiliser state

two cases, nothing in between

$\psi$ the stabiliser state of $\langle g_1, \dots, g_n\rangle$; $P$ a Hermitian Pauli.
(a) If $P$ commutes with every $g_j$, then $\pm P\in S$: the outcome is certain.
(b) If $P$ anticommutes with $g_1$, say, then the outcomes $\pm1$ have probability $\tfrac12$ each. After outcome $\lambda$, the generators are $\lambda P$, and $g_1g_j$ in place of each other $g_j$ that anticommuted with $P$.

proof of the probabilities

(a) $v(P)\in v(S)^\omega$, of dimension $2n - n = n$, containing $v(S)$ of dimension $n$: equal. (b) $\bra\psi P\ket\psi = \bra\psi Pg_1\ket\psi = -\bra\psi g_1P\ket\psi = -\bra\psi P\ket\psi$, so $0$, and $\Pr(\lambda) = \bra\psi\tfrac{I + \lambda P}2\ket\psi = \tfrac12$.

A measurement on a stabiliser state is never a biased coin: certain, or fair.

Costs. (a): write $v(P)$ as a sum of rows by elimination, $O(n^3)$, and multiply out the signs. (b): $n$ row products, $O(n^2)$.

Full proof

(a) Since $v(S)$ is isotropic of dimension $n$ and $v(S)^\omega$ has dimension $2n - n = n$, they are equal. If $P$ commutes with all $g_j$, $v(P)\in v(S)^\omega = v(S)$, so $P = cg$ for some $g\in S$ and phase $c$. Both $P$ and $g$ are Hermitian, so $c$ is real: $c = \pm1$. Then $P\psi = cg\psi = c\psi$: the outcome is $c$ with probability 1, and the state is unchanged. To find $c$, solve $v(P) = \sum_jc_jv(g_j)$ by elimination, multiply those generators (tracking phases with the rule $X^xZ^zX^{x'}Z^{z'} = (-1)^{z\cdot x'}X^{x+x'}Z^{z+z'}$) and compare with $P$.

(b) The expectation computation is on the slide; it uses $g_1\psi = \psi$ and its adjoint $\bra\psi g_1 = \bra\psi$. The Born rule with spectral projections $\tfrac{I\pm P}2$ gives $\tfrac12$ each. The post-measurement state is $\psi_\lambda\propto(I + \lambda P)\psi$ (Lüders). It is fixed by $\lambda P$: $\lambda P(I + \lambda P) = \lambda P + I$. It is fixed by every $h\in S$ that commutes with $P$: $h(I + \lambda P)\psi = (I + \lambda P)h\psi = (I + \lambda P)\psi$. The new list — $\lambda P$; $g_j$ for $j\ge2$ commuting with $P$; $g_1g_j$ for $j\ge2$ anticommuting with $P$ — consists of elements of $S$ commuting with $P$ (a product of two anticommuting-with-$P$ elements commutes with $P$), plus $\lambda P$. They commute pairwise. They are independent: the last $n - 1$ commute with $g_1$ while $P$ does not, so $P$ is not in their span; and they are independent among themselves because $v(g_2), \dots, v(g_n)$ are, and adding $v(g_1)$ to some of them cannot create a dependence without $v(g_1)$ being in the span of the others. A common $+1$ eigenvector $\psi_\lambda\ne0$ exists, so $-I$ is not in the new group. So $\psi_\lambda$ is its stabiliser state.

The general fact behind (b) is that two Paulis that anticommute cannot both have definite values; it is Robertson's relation of unit 01 in its sharpest form.

The Gottesman–Knill theorem

the Gottesman–Knill theorem (Gottesman 1998, after Knill)

A circuit on $n$ qubits that starts in $\ket{0^n}$, applies $m$ gates from $\{H, S, \mathrm{CNOT}\}$, and makes $q$ measurements of Pauli operators (later gates may depend on earlier outcomes) can be simulated on a classical computer in time $O(nm + qn^3)$: it samples the outcomes with exactly the right joint distribution, and computes the probability of any given outcome sequence.

proof

Start the tableau at $Z_0, \dots, Z_{n-1}$: $O(n^2)$. Each gate: the rules of Follow the stabiliser, $O(n)$. Each measurement: case (a), $O(n^3)$, output the certain sign; case (b), $O(n^2)$, toss a fair coin and update. The tableau is exact at every step, so every outcome has its true conditional probability: 1, 0 or $\tfrac12$. A sequence's probability is $2^{-\#\text{coins}}$, or 0.

A single computational-basis measurement of qubit $j$ is measuring $Z_j$, so the theorem covers the usual readout.

Faster. Aaronson and Gottesman (2004, cited) keep $n$ extra "destabiliser" rows and do case (a) in $O(n^2)$; their simulator handles thousands of qubits.

what it does not say

Nothing about $T$, about non-stabiliser inputs, or about Clifford circuits interleaved with other gates. Each of those breaks the tableau — the last slide of this section says how.

Full proof, the details. Correctness: by induction on the steps, the tableau always describes the true state of the circuit (given the outcomes so far). Gates: Follow the stabiliser, not the state. Measurements: Measuring a Pauli on a stabiliser state, which gives the conditional distribution of the next outcome (a point mass or a fair coin) and the new tableau. Sampling each outcome from its conditional distribution samples the joint distribution exactly. Adaptive gates are no harder: the simulator knows the outcomes it has produced. Running time: $m$ gates at $O(n)$, $q$ measurements at $O(n^3)$ worst case, plus $O(n^2)$ to initialise.

History. Gottesman credited the observation to Emanuel Knill; it appears in Gottesman's "The Heisenberg representation of quantum computers" (1998). Aaronson and Gottesman, "Improved simulation of stabilizer circuits" (Phys. Rev. A 70, 052328, 2004), gave the tableau algorithm whose sign rules are on the previous slide; they also showed that simulating stabiliser circuits is complete for the class parity-L, which lies inside P and is believed to be strictly smaller — not needed here.

Why it is a theorem about computation, not about codes. Encoding, syndrome measurement and many decoders are Clifford circuits: so error correction itself can be simulated classically, which is how error-correction experiments and threshold estimates (unit 24) are designed at thousands of qubits. The flip side follows on Entanglement is not enough; T is where the power is.

Entanglement is not enough; $T$ is where the power is

Entangled and easy. Bell states (unit 03), GHZ, Steane's $\ket{0_L}$, the surface-code states of unit 24: all stabiliser states, all made by Clifford circuits, all classically simulable. A quantum speedup must use something else.

$T$ is not Clifford

$$TXT^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}0 & e^{-i\pi/4}\\ e^{i\pi/4} & 0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{X + Y}{\sqrt2}\notin\mathcal P_1.$$

Why the tableau breaks. $T\ket+$ is fixed by $(X + Y)/\sqrt2$, a sum of two Paulis: no row to write. After $t$ such gates the stabiliser is in general a sum of up to $2^t$ strings.

The six one-qubit stabiliser states sit on the axes. $T\ket+$, Bloch vector $(0.7071, 0.7071, 0)$, sits between them.

And $T$ suffices. Clifford gates with $T$ approximate every unitary (cited; unit 11). So the number of $T$ gates is the price of being quantum — known simulators run in time exponential only in that number (Bravyi–Gosset 2016, cited).

The computation. $T = \mathrm{diag}(1, \zeta)$ with $\zeta = e^{i\pi/4}$ (not the symplectic form). $TXT^\dagger$ has entries $(TXT^\dagger)_{01} = 1\cdot1\cdot\bar\zeta = e^{-i\pi/4}$, $(TXT^\dagger)_{10} = \zeta$, zeros on the diagonal. And $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(X + Y) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 - i\\1 + i & 0\end{pmatrix}$, with $\tfrac{1 - i}{\sqrt2} = e^{-i\pi/4}$ ✓. This is not $\pm1, \pm i$ times $I, X, Y$ or $Z$: two of its Pauli coefficients are nonzero. $TZT^\dagger = Z$, since both are diagonal; so $T$ fails only on $X$ (and $Y$). In Bloch-sphere terms, $T = e^{i\pi/8}R_z(\pi/4)$ is a rotation by $45^\circ$, while the one-qubit Clifford gates are, up to phase, exactly the 24 rotations that map the octahedron with vertices $\pm\hat x, \pm\hat y, \pm\hat z$ to itself: quarter-turns about the axes ($S$ is one), but also half-turns about edge midpoints ($H$ is the half-turn about $(\hat x + \hat z)/\sqrt2$) and third-turns about the diagonals ($SH$ is one, up to phase). A $45^\circ$ turn sends $\hat x$ to no vertex, so it is none of them.

One-qubit stabiliser states. A single generator $\pm X$, $\pm Y$ or $\pm Z$ fixes one state: $\ket\pm$, $\ket{\pm i}$, $\ket0$, $\ket1$ — six in all, the green points. $T\ket+ = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{i\pi/4}\ket1)$, normalised ($\tfrac12 + \tfrac12$), Bloch vector $(\cos45^\circ, \sin45^\circ, 0) = (0.7071, 0.7071, 0)$, length 1 ✓, on none of the six points: not a stabiliser state. Unit 24 will call it a magic state and use it to perform $T$ fault-tolerantly.

Cited results. Approximate universality of Clifford + $T$: Boykin, Mor, Pulver, Roychowdhury and Vatan (1999); with efficient approximation by Solovay–Kitaev, proved in unit 11 for those who took it. Simulation exponential only in the $T$ count: Bravyi and Gosset, Phys. Rev. Lett. 116, 250501 (2016).

The moral. Entanglement is necessary for a speedup on pure states (a circuit whose state stays a product at every step is simulated by tracking its $n$ one-qubit factors, $O(1)$ work per gate) but not sufficient: Clifford circuits make maximal entanglement and are still easy. What the quantum computer has that the tableau cannot follow is the continuum of phases between the octahedron's vertices.

transversal $H$ on Steane's code

$H^{\otimes7}$ maps $V_S$ to itself and acts on it as the logical Hadamard: $\ket{0_L}\mapsto\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{0_L} + \ket{1_L})$, $\ \ket{1_L}\mapsto\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{0_L} - \ket{1_L})$.

proof

$H^{\otimes7}X^hH^{\otimes7} = Z^h$ and back. The $X$- and $Z$-checks are the same rows of $M$, so conjugation swaps $g_1\leftrightarrow g_4$, $g_2\leftrightarrow g_5$, $g_3\leftrightarrow g_6$: $S\mapsto S$, hence $V_S\mapsto V_S$. And $\bar X = X^{\otimes7}\leftrightarrow Z^{\otimes7} = \bar Z$.

One gate per qubit: a single fault touches one qubit of the block, which the code corrects. Unit 24 builds on this.

The running example, closed. Three parity checks read twice gave six commuting generators, a qubit, its syndromes, its logicals, distance 3, an encoder a laptop can follow — and a logical gate for free.

Full proof. $H^{\otimes7}$ conjugates qubit by qubit, $HXH = Z$ and $HZH = X$, so it maps $X^h\mapsto Z^h$ and $Z^h\mapsto X^h$. The six generators are permuted, so the group $S$ is mapped onto itself, and $V_S$ (what $S$ fixes) onto itself: if $g\psi = \psi$ for all $g\in S$ then $g'(H^{\otimes7}\psi) = H^{\otimes7}(H^{\otimes7}g'H^{\otimes7})\psi = H^{\otimes7}\psi$ for all $g'\in S$. On the code, the restriction $U$ of $H^{\otimes7}$ satisfies $U\bar XU^\dagger = \bar Z$ and $U\bar ZU^\dagger = \bar X$. Writing operators on the code in the basis $\ket{0_L}, \ket{1_L}$, where $\bar X$ and $\bar Z$ are the matrices $X$ and $Z$, the unitary $\bar H^\dagger U$ commutes with both $X$ and $Z$, hence with every $2\times2$ matrix, so it is a scalar: $U$ is the logical Hadamard up to a phase, and the state computation below shows the phase is 1.

The state, in full. $H^{\otimes7}\ket{0_L} = \tfrac1{\sqrt8}\sum_{y\in C_2}2^{-7/2}\sum_w(-1)^{y\cdot w}\ket w = \tfrac{8}{\sqrt8\cdot\sqrt{128}}\sum_{w\in C_2^\perp}\ket w$, by the character sum in the notes to What a CSS code corrects: two classical decoders. $\tfrac{8}{\sqrt{1024}} = \tfrac14$, and $C_2^\perp$ is the Hamming code, whose 16 words are the 8 of $\ket{0_L}$ and the 8 of $\ket{1_L}$: so $\tfrac14(\sqrt8\ket{0_L} + \sqrt8\ket{1_L}) = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{0_L} + \ket{1_L})$ ✓. The same computation with $\ket{1_L} = \ket{1111111 + C_2}$ gives the signs $(-1)^{1111111\cdot w} = (-1)^{|w|}$: $+$ on the even-weight words ($\ket{0_L}$'s), $-$ on the odd ones: $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{0_L} - \ket{1_L})$ ✓. Both were also checked numerically.

Transversal, and why it matters. A gate is transversal on a code if it acts as a tensor product over the qubits of the block. An error before or during such a gate stays on one qubit: it cannot spread inside the block, as a CNOT within the block would spread it (The Clifford group, the propagation pictures). On any CSS code, CNOT between two blocks, qubit by qubit, is also transversal and implements logical CNOT; and $S^{\otimes7}$ on Steane's code implements logical $S^\dagger$, since $S^{\otimes7}X^{\otimes7}S^{\dagger\otimes7} = Y^{\otimes7} = i^7X^{\otimes7}Z^{\otimes7} = -(i\bar X\bar Z) = -\bar Y$. So the whole logical Clifford group is transversal on Steane's code — and by Gottesman–Knill, that is exactly not enough.
