Fault tolerance: the threshold theorem, magic states, and the surface code

Correction circuits are built from faulty gates too. Build them so one fault does limited damage, and below a constant physical error rate the logical error falls as fast as you like — at a price in qubits that this unit computes.

needs 23, 11~3 h slides + 5 h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 24

Unit 22 built codes and unit 23 made them computable, and both assumed something false: that the circuit which measures the syndrome and applies the correction works perfectly. It is made of the same gates, preparations and measurements as everything else, and fails as often. Worse, a CNOT moves errors from one qubit to another, so a careless correction circuit can turn one fault into two errors — and a distance-3 code cannot survive two.

This unit is about the discipline that fixes this, and its payoff. Fault-tolerant gadgets keep the damage of one fault to one error per block. Concatenation then turns "failure needs two faults" into a doubly exponential suppression of logical errors below a threshold — the threshold theorem. Two facts shape every real design: no code has a transversal universal gate set (Eastin–Knill), so the $T$ gate is supplied by distilled magic states; and the surface code, with a threshold near 1 per cent and only nearest-neighbour checks, is what experiments now run.

This is the last unit of the spine. The capstone combines it with unit 16's Shor to estimate what factoring a 2048-bit number would cost; this unit sizes one logical qubit, which is what the capstone multiplies.

24 · faults

What a fault is

The physics, from nothing. A physical qubit is a small physical system — a superconducting circuit on a chip, a single trapped ion. A gate is a pulse of microwaves or laser light, set by analogue knobs of finite precision; between pulses it couples to its surroundings and drifts (unit 21's channels). So every step can go wrong: preparation, gate, wait and readout.

fault locations and stochastic Pauli noise

A circuit's locations are its preparations, gates, idle waits and measurements. Each fails independently with probability at most $p$. A failed operation is followed by an arbitrary Pauli on its qubits; a failed measurement reports the wrong bit.

Why Paulis are enough. By unit 22's discretisation, correcting $X$, $Y$, $Z$ on a qubit corrects every error on it. The depolarising channel is this model, with $p$ the chance of some Pauli (unit 21's strength $\tfrac43p$): Kraus operators $\sqrt{1 - p}\,I$, $\sqrt{p/3}\,X, Y, Z$; $\sum K^\dagger K = I$ ✓.

A small circuit has ten locations. Logical error rates are quoted per code cycle: one round of syndrome measurement on every check, with its correction.

What the model leaves out, on purpose. Real noise is not exactly Pauli, not exactly independent, and not exactly the same at every location. The threshold theorem tolerates the first fully (discretisation: any error is a combination of Paulis, and correction projects onto one of them), the second partly (it needs correlations that decay fast enough — slide The threshold theorem, and what it assumes), and the third trivially ($p$ is an upper bound). Two kinds of noise it does not tolerate are named in section 5: rare correlated bursts that hit many qubits at once, and leakage out of the two-level subspace, which branch unit 25 derives for a transmon.

Idle locations matter. A qubit that waits while others are worked on still decoheres. That is why the full theorem needs operations in parallel: a scheme that applied one gate at a time would let idle errors grow with the size of the computer.

The check on the depolarising Kraus family. $I^\dagger I = X^\dagger X = Y^\dagger Y = Z^\dagger Z = I$, so $\sum_k K_k^\dagger K_k = (1 - p)I + 3\cdot\frac p3 I = I$: trace-preserving, as unit 21 requires of a channel.

Which $p$. Unit 21 writes the depolarising channel as $(1 - q)\rho + q\frac I2$, with Kraus operators $\sqrt{1 - \frac{3q}4}\,I$ and $\frac{\sqrt q}2X, Y, Z$. The fault model counts the probability that some Pauli acts, which is $p = \frac{3q}4$; so this slide's channel is unit 21's with $q = \frac43p$. The fault-tolerance literature uses this slide's $p$, and so does the rest of the deck.

An error before a gate is another error after it

pushing an error through a gate $$UE = (UEU^\dagger)\,U .$$

Proof. Insert $U^\dagger U = I$ to the right of $E$. ∎

So an error $E$ just before a gate $U$ acts exactly as the error $UEU^\dagger$ just after it. If $U$ is a Clifford gate — unit 11: it maps Paulis to Paulis under conjugation, up to phase — a Pauli error stays a Pauli error. It may be a different one, and it may now sit on more qubits.

Check on one qubit: $HXH = Z$ and $SXS^\dagger = Y$ (unit 11). A single-qubit gate never spreads an error to another qubit; only multi-qubit gates can.

The same operator, read two ways.

why the bookkeeping works

It works because Cliffords keep Paulis Pauli. Through $T$ it breaks: $TXT^\dagger = (X + Y)/\sqrt2$ (unit 11), not a Pauli. Section 4 is about exactly this gate.

Why it matters. The whole analysis of fault tolerance is bookkeeping of where Pauli errors are at the end of a circuit. Faults happen at many places in the middle; this identity moves each one to the end, where the next round of correction will meet it. For a circuit of Clifford gates the moved error is again a Pauli, possibly up to a sign or a factor $\pm i$, which is a global phase on the error and changes nothing.

The two checks. $HXH = \frac12(X + Z)X(X + Z) = \frac12(X + Z)(I + XZ) = \frac12(X + Z + Z + ZXZ)$, using $X^2 = I$; and $ZXZ = -X$, so $HXH = \frac12(2Z) = Z$. For $S$: $SXS^\dagger = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix} = Y$, since $SX = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\i&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $S^\dagger = \mathrm{diag}(1, -i)$.

Worked: a CNOT moves errors

worked example 1: error propagation through CNOT

CNOT$\ket{a, b} = \ket{a, a\oplus b}$ and CNOT is its own inverse (unit 10). For every basis state:

$X$ on the control. $\mathrm{CNOT}(X\otimes I)\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{a, b} = \mathrm{CNOT}\ket{a\oplus1, a\oplus b} = \ket{a\oplus1, b\oplus1}$, which is $(X\otimes X)\ket{a, b}$.

$Z$ on the target. $\mathrm{CNOT}(I\otimes Z)\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{a, b} = (-1)^{a\oplus b}\,\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{a, a\oplus b} = (-1)^a(-1)^b\ket{a, b}$, which is $(Z\otimes Z)\ket{a, b}$.

The other two. $X$ on the target and $Z$ on the control commute with CNOT: they stay put.

$Y = iXZ$ follows: $Y\otimes I \mapsto i(X\otimes X)(Z\otimes I) = Y\otimes X$, and $I\otimes Y\mapsto Z\otimes Y$.

$X$ flows with the arrow of the CNOT; $Z$ flows against it.

Check on $\ket{10}$ with $X$ on the control: the faulty circuit gives $\mathrm{CNOT}\,\ket{00} = \ket{00}$, the clean one $\ket{11}$; and $(X\otimes X)\ket{11} = \ket{00}$ ✓.

Full proof of the last two rules. $X$ on the target: $\mathrm{CNOT}(I\otimes X)\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{a, b} = \mathrm{CNOT}\ket{a, a\oplus b\oplus1} = \ket{a, b\oplus1} = (I\otimes X)\ket{a, b}$. $Z$ on the control: $\mathrm{CNOT}(Z\otimes I)\mathrm{CNOT}\ket{a, b} = (-1)^a\ket{a, b}$, since the control bit is unchanged.

The $Y$ rules in full. Conjugation is multiplicative: $U(AB)U^\dagger = (UAU^\dagger)(UBU^\dagger)$. With $Y = iXZ$ (check: $iXZ = i\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}$ ✓), $\mathrm{CNOT}(Y\otimes I)\mathrm{CNOT} = i(X\otimes X)(Z\otimes I) = iXZ\otimes X = Y\otimes X$, and $\mathrm{CNOT}(I\otimes Y)\mathrm{CNOT} = i(I\otimes X)(Z\otimes Z) = Z\otimes iXZ = Z\otimes Y$. Problem P1 asks for these from scratch.

Reading the rule. The CNOT copies the computational-basis value forward, so a bit flip on the control is copied forward too. In the Hadamard basis the roles of control and target swap — $(H\otimes H)\mathrm{CNOT}(H\otimes H)$ is a CNOT with control and target exchanged — and a phase flip is a bit flip in that basis, so it is copied the other way.

A naive syndrome circuit makes things worse

Measure Steane's check $Z_4Z_5Z_6Z_7$ (unit 23) as unit 22 measured $Z_0Z_1$: one ancilla, four CNOTs into it, one meter.

One fault. A $Z$ on the ancilla after the second CNOT is copied back through the last two: the data leave with $Z_6Z_7$. (On the ancilla it is harmless: it is measured in $Z$.)

The decoder. The $X$-type checks read columns 6 and 7 of Hamming's matrix added, $110\oplus111 = 001$: "position 1". It applies $Z_1$.

The result. $Z_1Z_6Z_7$: syndrome $000$, weight 3, a logical $\bar Z$ (unit 23). For the running example, one fault gives a logical error, so $p_L\sim p$: the code buys nothing.

One ancilla touching four data qubits: one fault on it becomes two errors.

The positions. Steane's code from unit 23: qubits at positions 1 to 7, Hamming's parity-check matrix with column $j$ the binary expansion of $j$ (rows $0001111$, $0110011$, $1010101$), and the same three rows used as $X$-type and as $Z$-type checks. A $Z$ error anticommutes with an $X$-type check on whose support it lies an odd number of times, so its syndrome is the sum of the columns at its positions — for a single $Z_j$, the binary expansion of $j$.

Why $Z_1Z_6Z_7$ is logical. It commutes with every check (syndrome $000$) and, as a $Z$-type operator, with every $Z$-type check trivially. Its support $\{1, 6, 7\}$ is a Hamming codeword of weight 3, not a word of the simplex code $C^\perp$; unit 23 identified $Z$-type operators supported on $C\setminus C^\perp$ as nontrivial logical $Z$s. So it acts as $\bar Z$ on the code (computed directly: it equals $\bar Z$ on $\ket{\bar0}, \ket{\bar1}$).

The general lesson. A single ancilla that touches $w$ data qubits can, by one fault on it, deposit up to $w - 1$ errors. For a weight-4 check that is up to three, and a distance-3 code corrects one. The cure is to give each data qubit its own ancilla qubit, in Extracting a syndrome without spreading errors.

24 · gadgets

Fault-tolerant gadgets, and transversal gates

a fault-tolerant gadget

An encoded gate, preparation, measurement or round of correction, built so that one fault anywhere inside it causes at most one error in each output block.

A distance-3 code corrects one error per block, so a computation of such gadgets fails only when two faults coincide: probability of order $p^2$, not $p$.

a transversal gate

A gate applied qubit by qubit: $U_1\otimes\cdots\otimes U_n$ on one block, or, between two blocks, a two-qubit gate on qubit $i$ of each, for every $i$.

transversal is fault-tolerant

Qubit $i$ of one block meets only qubit $i$ of the other, so one fault touches at most one qubit per block, and so does everything it propagates to. ∎

A transversal CNOT between two seven-qubit blocks: no two qubits of one block ever meet.

The precise requirement. The definition on the slide is the one this unit uses; the rigorous version (Aliferis, Gottesman and Preskill 2006) adds a condition for inputs that already carry errors: a gadget with $s$ faults, fed blocks with $r$ errors where $r + s \le 1$, must output blocks with at most $s$ errors beyond those propagated from the input, and an error-correction gadget with no faults must remove one input error. The slide's version is the heart of it, and it is what makes "failure needs two faults" true.

Full proof that transversal gates qualify. A fault at a location of a transversal gate on qubit $i$ is a Pauli on the qubits that location touches — qubit $i$ of each block. Pushed through the remaining transversal gates (previous slides) it stays on qubits labelled $i$, because no gate connects label $i$ to label $j\ne i$. So at the output each block has at most one erroneous qubit, the $i$-th.

Not every useful gate is transversal. The definition is about the circuit, not the logical operation: the same logical gate can have transversal and non-transversal implementations. Eastin–Knill (section 4) says that some logical gate in every universal set must be non-transversal.

$H$, $S$ and CNOT generate the Clifford group (unit 11; Gottesman 1998, cited), so every logical Clifford is transversal on Steane's code.

a sign to watch

$S^{\otimes7}$ is logical $S^\dagger$, not $S$: it sends $\bar X = X^{\otimes7}$ to $Y^{\otimes7} = i^7X^{\otimes7}Z^{\otimes7} = -i\bar X\bar Z = -\bar Y$, with $\bar Y = i\bar X\bar Z$.

$S^{\otimes7}$ keeps the code

On an $X$-type check $g = X^{\otimes4}$ (on its support), $S^{\otimes4}gS^{\dagger\otimes4} = Y^{\otimes4} = i^4X^{\otimes4}Z^{\otimes4}$: the product of the $X$-type and $Z$-type checks on the same four qubits, both in the stabiliser. $Z$-type checks commute with $S$. So the stabiliser group is mapped to itself, and with it the code space. ∎

$T$ fails. $TXT^\dagger = (X + Y)/\sqrt2$, so $T^{\otimes4}$ turns an $X$-type check into $\frac14$ times a sum of $2^4 = 16$ different Pauli strings — not a Pauli at all, so not a stabiliser — and $T^{\otimes7}$ maps $\ket{\bar0}$ out of the code space (computed).

Check. On $\ket{\bar0}, \ket{\bar1}$, $(S^\dagger)^{\otimes7}$ acts as $\mathrm{diag}(1, i) = S$, computed directly from the codewords.

Why preserving the stabiliser group preserves the code. If $UgU^\dagger\in\mathcal S$ for every $g\in\mathcal S$ and $\ket\psi$ is in the code, then $g(U\ket\psi) = U(U^\dagger gU)\ket\psi$; and $U^\dagger gU\in\mathcal S$ too, since conjugation by $U$ is a bijection of the finite group $\mathcal S$ onto itself. So $gU\ket\psi = U\ket\psi$, and $U\ket\psi$ is in the code. Which logical operation $U$ performs is then read off from where it sends $\bar X$ and $\bar Z$, up to a global phase.

The direct check. $\ket{\bar0}$ is the uniform superposition of the eight simplex codewords, of weights 0 and 4, and $\ket{\bar1} = \bar X\ket{\bar0}$ that of their complements, of weights 7 and 3. $(S^\dagger)^{\otimes7}$ multiplies $\ket x$ by $(-i)^{|x|}$: by $1$ on weights 0 and 4, and by $(-i)^7 = i$ and $(-i)^3 = i$ on weights 7 and 3. So it is $\mathrm{diag}(1, i) = S$ on the logical basis — and this check is also a proof.

Why $T$ cannot work the same way. $T^{\otimes7}$ multiplies $\ket x$ by $e^{i\pi|x|/4}$: by 1 and $e^{i\pi} = -1$ on weights 0 and 4. The phases inside $\ket{\bar0}$ no longer agree, so the image is not $\ket{\bar0}$ up to phase. Its overlap with $\ket{\bar0}$ is $\frac18(1 - 7) = -\frac34$ and with $\ket{\bar1}$ is $0$ (disjoint supports), so its projection onto the code has squared norm $\frac9{16}\lt1$: it does not lie in the code space (problem P12). Unit 23's Gottesman–Knill theorem says what is at stake: the transversal gates of Steane's code are exactly the Cliffords, and circuits of those alone are classically simulable. The gate that fails is the one that carries the power.

Extracting a syndrome without spreading errors

cat-state extraction (Shor 1996)

Give each data qubit of the check its own ancilla qubit. Prepare the four ancillas in $$\ket S = \tfrac1{\sqrt8}\sum_{e\ \mathrm{even}}\ket e,$$ the eight even-weight strings: norm $8\cdot\tfrac18 = 1$ ✓. CNOT data $i$ into ancilla $i$, then measure the ancillas.

What it reveals. $\ket x\ket S\mapsto\ket x\tfrac1{\sqrt8}\sum_{e}\ket{e\oplus x}$, a uniform superposition of the strings of parity $x_4\oplus x_5\oplus x_6\oplus x_7$. The outcome's parity is the syndrome bit; the rest is uniformly random and says nothing about the data.

Why it is safe. A fault on ancilla $i$ is copied back onto data $i$ only: one error.

Steane's method (1997) uses a whole encoded block as the ancilla, coupled by a transversal CNOT and read out qubit by qubit; one classical decoding gives every syndrome bit at once.

Four ancillas, each touching one data qubit. $\ket S$ is the cat state $\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(\ket{0000} + \ket{1111})$ seen through $H^{\otimes4}$.

Full proof of the parity claim. The map $e\mapsto e\oplus x'$, with $x' = (x_4, x_5, x_6, x_7)$, is a bijection of $\mathbb F_2^4$ that adds $|x'|$ to the parity. So it maps the eight even strings onto the eight strings of parity $|x'| \bmod 2$. The ancilla ends in their uniform superposition, and a measurement gives each with probability $\frac18$: the parity is determined, nothing else depends on $x$. For a code state, a superposition of many $x$, every component has the same parity (the check's eigenvalue), so the ancilla factors off and the data are not disturbed.

Why $\ket S$ is the cat state through $H^{\otimes4}$. $H^{\otimes4}\ket{0000} = \frac14\sum_e\ket e$ and $H^{\otimes4}\ket{1111} = \frac14\sum_e(-1)^{|e|}\ket e$; their sum over $\sqrt2$ keeps the even $e$ with amplitude $\frac{2}{4\sqrt2} = \frac1{\sqrt8}$.

The catch. Preparing $\ket S$ is itself a circuit, and a fault in it can leave correlated errors on several ancilla qubits, which would then be copied onto several data qubits. Shor's scheme therefore verifies the ancilla before use — measuring parities of its qubits and discarding it if they disagree — so that only ancillas with at most one error are coupled to the data. This is named, not developed. Shor's paper is "Fault-tolerant quantum computation", Proc. 37th FOCS, 56 (1996); Steane's is Phys. Rev. Lett. 78, 2252 (1997).

Measure it more than once

A fault mid-extraction gives a syndrome that is half before, half after. On Steane's code, measure the three $Z$-type checks in turn, and let $X_7$ strike after the first. The bits read $0, 1, 1$: "position 3".

The decoder applies $X_3$, leaving $X_3X_7$. The next round reads $011\oplus111 = 100$, applies $X_4$, and leaves $X_3X_4X_7$: syndrome $000$, weight 3, a logical $\bar X$. One fault, a logical error — again.

A faulty readout does the same with no data error at all: the decoder corrects an error that is not there.

the remedy

Repeat the extraction, and act only on a syndrome seen twice running. To fool it now takes two faults. In the surface code (section 3) every round is kept and the decoder reads them together.

One syndrome bit over six rounds. A real error persists; a misreading blinks once. Repetition tells them apart.

The example, bit by bit. Hamming's columns: $3 = 011$, $4 = 100$, $7 = 111$. The first check (row $0001111$) is measured before $X_7$ arrives and reads 0; the second and third rows both contain position 7 and read 1. So the syndrome is $011$. After $X_3$ is applied, the error is $X_3X_7$, whose syndrome is $011\oplus111 = 100$; the decoder applies $X_4$. Now $X_3X_4X_7$ has syndrome $011\oplus100\oplus111 = 000$, and $\{3, 4, 7\}$ is a weight-3 Hamming codeword: a logical $\bar X$, by the same argument as for $Z_1Z_6Z_7$.

With repetition. The first round reads $011$; the second, with $X_7$ now present throughout, reads $111$. They disagree, so nothing is done and a third round is taken: $111$ again, now seen twice, and $X_7$ is removed. For a fault to cause a wrong action now, it must be accompanied by a second fault that makes two consecutive rounds agree on a wrong answer — probability of order $p^2$.

How much repetition. For a distance-$d$ code, faults in the measurements can mimic data errors, and a decoder must see enough rounds that a pattern of fewer than about $d/2$ faults in space and time cannot fool it. The surface code measures $d$ rounds per logical operation for this reason; problem P2 does the arithmetic for a single bit and a majority of three.

Codes inside codes

concatenation

Encode a qubit in Steane's code; then encode each of its seven qubits in Steane's code again; and so on. A level-$k$ block holds one logical qubit in $7^k$ physical ones.

Unit 22 met the idea once: Shor's nine-qubit code is the phase-flip code with each qubit replaced by a bit-flip code.

Gadgets compose the same way. A level-$k$ gadget is the level-1 gadget — say a transversal CNOT followed by error correction — with every physical location replaced by the corresponding level-$(k-1)$ gadget. Its physical size is the level-1 size raised to the power $k$.

Check at $k = 1$: one Steane block, 7 qubits. At $k = 2$: $7\times7 = 49$, drawn.

Level 2: a Steane code of Steane codes. Each level multiplies the qubit count by 7.

Why the logical structure carries over. A level-$(k-1)$ block is, logically, one qubit, and a level-$(k-1)$ gadget is, logically, one gate on it. So the level-1 construction can use these as if they were physical qubits and gates. Transversal gates make this especially simple: a transversal CNOT at level $k$ is seven level-$(k-1)$ CNOTs, each of which is seven level-$(k-2)$ CNOTs, down to $7^k$ physical CNOTs.

The cost of distance in space. At level $k$ a logical CNOT between two blocks joins qubits that may sit far apart on a chip. In one line of qubits, the two partners of a level-$k$ transversal CNOT are of order $7^k$ sites apart. This is fine in a model with gates between any pair of qubits, and it is the first thing that breaks on real hardware — section 3's reason for a different code.

History. Concatenated codes were introduced for classical communication by Forney (1966). Their quantum use, and the threshold that follows, is due independently to Aharonov and Ben-Or, Kitaev, and Knill, Laflamme and Zurek, all in 1996–97.

The simplified model

assumptions of the model

1. A level-1 gadget has $A$ locations, each failing independently with probability $p$. 2. It is fault-tolerant, so it fails (leaves a logical error) only if at least two of its locations fail. 3. At level $k$, each level-$(k-1)$ gadget plays the part of one location, failing independently with probability $p_{k-1}$.

one level $$p_1\le\binom A2p^2 =: cp^2 .$$

Proof. Failure needs some pair of locations to both fail. Each of the $\binom A2$ pairs does so with probability $p^2$; the union bound adds them. ∎

Check: $A = 2$ gives $p_1\le p^2$, the probability both fail, exactly.

Fault tolerance makes a single fault harmless; failure needs a pair.

What is simplified. Assumption 3 is the real simplification. A level-$(k-1)$ gadget that fails does not fail like a single physical location: its failures can be correlated with those of the gadgets around it, and a gadget fed an erroneous input behaves differently from one fed a clean one. The rigorous proofs handle this with "extended rectangles" — a gadget together with the error correction before and after it — and show that the simplified recursion holds with a different constant (Aliferis, Gottesman and Preskill 2006). The structure of the argument is exactly the one on the next slide.

The union bound is pessimistic. Most pairs of faults are harmless: two faults in different blocks, or two that happen to cancel. Only malignant pairs — pairs that actually cause a logical error — count, and counting those instead of all $\binom A2$ pairs is how serious estimates get a larger threshold from the same circuit. The slide uses the crude count because it is what can be proved in one line.

The exact expression, for comparison. With exactly the rule "fails iff at least two locations fail", $p_1 = 1 - (1 - p)^A - Ap(1 - p)^{A-1}$, which is $\binom A2p^2 - O(p^3)$; the bound is its leading term and is never below it, since the union bound counts each outcome with two or more failures at least once.

Worked: k levels of concatenation

worked example 2a: the simplified threshold theorem

Claim. Under the model, $\displaystyle p_k\le\frac{(cp)^{2^k}}{c}$ for every $k\ge0$.

Proof, by induction. $k = 0$: $(cp)^1/c = p$ ✓. Step: by "one level" applied to level-$(k-1)$ gadgets, $p_k\le cp_{k-1}^2$, so $$cp_k\le(cp_{k-1})^2\le\big((cp)^{2^{k-1}}\big)^2 = (cp)^{2^k}. \;∎$$

Check at $k = 1$: $(cp)^2/c = cp^2$, the one-level bound ✓.

Reading it. Set $p_{\rm th} = 1/c$. Then $$p_k\le p_{\rm th}\Big(\frac p{p_{\rm th}}\Big)^{2^k}.$$ If $p\lt p_{\rm th}$ the ratio is below one, and its exponent doubles with every level: doubly exponential suppression. If $p\gt p_{\rm th}$, concatenating makes things worse.

the threshold

$p_{\rm th} = 1/c = 1/\binom A2$ in this model: a constant, independent of the size of the computation.

Why "doubly exponential" is the right phrase. With $r = p/p_{\rm th}\lt1$, $p_k\le p_{\rm th}r^{2^k}$: the exponent $2^k$ is itself exponential in $k$. One more level squares the ratio $r$. Compare a single code of distance $d$, where the logical error falls like $r^{(d+1)/2}$. Concatenation's distance is $3^k$, so an optimal decoder would give the exponent $(3^k + 1)/2$; the model's level-by-level decoder fails as soon as $2^k$ suitably placed faults occur, which is why its exponent is $2^k$. Either way the exponent is exponential in $k$.

At the threshold. $p = p_{\rm th}$ gives $p_k\le p_{\rm th}$ for every $k$: the recursion's fixed point. The bound is a statement about the model: below $p_{\rm th}$ the bound shrinks; above it, the bound grows past 1 and says nothing, and in the true recursion $p_k\mapsto cp_k^2$ the error then really does grow.

The overhead is polylogarithmic

polylogarithmic overhead

If each level multiplies the size of a gadget by $G$, logical error $\varepsilon$ costs a factor $O\big(\log^{\log_2G}(1/\varepsilon)\big)$ in size.

proof

Write $L = \log(1/c\varepsilon)/\log(1/cp)$. $p_k\le\varepsilon$ holds once $(cp)^{2^k}\le c\varepsilon$, i.e. $2^k\ge L$. For $\varepsilon\lt p$, $L\gt1$, so the least such $k$ is $\ge1$ and has $2^k\lt2L$. Then $$G^k = (2^k)^{\log_2G}\lt(2L)^{\log_2G},$$ and $L = O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ for fixed $p\lt p_{\rm th}$. ∎

Check on the running example: $c = 100$, $\varepsilon = 10^{-12}$, $p = 10^{-3}$ give $L = \log10^{10}/\log10 = 10$, so $2^k\ge10$ and $k = 4$, as found.

For a whole computation of $N$ gates with total failure probability $\delta$, give each gate $\varepsilon = \delta/N$ (union bound). The cost is $N\cdot O(\log^{\log_2G}(N/\delta))$: a polylogarithmic factor over the ideal circuit.

Steane's qubits: $G = 7$, exponent $\log_27 = 2.81$.

so

Below threshold, an arbitrarily reliable computation costs only polylogarithmically more than an ideal one. That is the threshold theorem's content.

Full proof, with the logs written out. We need $(cp)^{2^k}\le c\varepsilon$. Taking logarithms of numbers below one, $2^k\log(1/cp)\ge\log(1/c\varepsilon)$, i.e. $2^k\ge L$. If $\varepsilon\lt p$ then $c\varepsilon\lt cp$, so $L\gt1$; let $k = \lceil\log_2L\rceil\ge1$, so $2^{k-1}\lt L$ and $2^k\lt2L$. (For $\varepsilon\ge p$ no concatenation is needed at all.) The identity $G^k = 2^{k\log_2G} = (2^k)^{\log_2G}$ gives the bound. Since $cp\lt1$ is fixed, $\log(1/cp)$ is a positive constant, and $L\le\log(1/c\varepsilon)/\log(1/cp) = O(\log(1/\varepsilon))$.

What "size" counts. The factor $G$ can be read for qubits ($G = 7$ data qubits per level, more with ancillas) or for gates (the number of locations in the level-1 gadget, in the hundreds). Either way it is a constant, and the overhead is a polynomial in $\log(1/\varepsilon)$ whose degree is $\log_2G$. Time grows the same way, since each level-$k$ gadget is a constant number of level-$(k-1)$ steps deep.

Why it matters. Without fault tolerance, a computation of $N$ gates needs $p\lesssim1/N$ per gate, so larger computations need ever better hardware. With it, a fixed $p$ below threshold suffices for any $N$, and the price is polylogarithmic. This is what makes quantum computing a possibility in principle rather than a race against $N$.

The threshold theorem, and what it assumes

the threshold theorem (cited)

There is a constant $p_{\rm th}\gt0$ such that any circuit of $N$ gates can be simulated with failure probability at most $\delta$ by a circuit of $O\big(N\,\mathrm{polylog}(N/\delta)\big)$ gates whose every location fails with probability at most $p\lt p_{\rm th}$.

Aharonov and Ben-Or (STOC 1997; SIAM J. Comput. 38, 1207, 2008); Kitaev (Russ. Math. Surv. 52, 1191, 1997); Knill, Laflamme and Zurek (Science 279, 342, 1998). The modern proof: Aliferis, Gottesman and Preskill (QIC 6, 97, 2006).

The hypotheses, each needed:

  • Local, weakly correlated noise: faults on many locations at once must be correspondingly rare.
  • Fresh ancillas, supplied throughout: correction removes entropy, which must go somewhere.
  • Parallel operations: otherwise idle qubits accumulate errors while they wait.
  • Fast, reliable classical computation to decode syndromes.
what p_th really is

The union bound gives $1/\binom A2$, and real gadgets have hundreds of locations. Proved thresholds for concatenated Steane schemes are of order $10^{-5}$ (Aliferis–Gottesman–Preskill). The running example's $10^{-2}$ is the surface code's, lent to concatenation.

Why each hypothesis. Correlated noise: if a single event could flip $d$ qubits of a block at once with probability $p$, a distance-$d$ code would fail at rate $p$ and no level of concatenation would help; the theorem needs the probability of $m$ specified faults to fall like $p^m$. Fresh ancillas: noise injects entropy, and error correction moves it out of the data into ancillas, which must be reset or replaced; without a supply of fresh $\ket0$s the computer heats up. Parallelism: in a sequential machine a qubit waits a time proportional to the size of the computation between operations. Classical computation: decoding must keep pace with the cycle; the theorem treats it as free and perfect, and section 5 shows it is a real engineering constraint.

The numbers. $\binom{15}2 = 105$, so the toy model would need about 15 locations per gadget to reach $p_{\rm th} = 10^{-2}$, and a real Steane gadget with ancilla verification has well over a hundred. Counting malignant pairs instead of all pairs is what raises the proved bound to order $10^{-5}$; numerical simulations of concatenated schemes give larger values, and Knill's post-selected schemes (Nature 434, 39, 2005) reported thresholds of a few per cent under strong assumptions. None of these is used below.

Further reading. Gottesman's survey "An introduction to quantum error correction and fault-tolerant quantum computation" (arXiv:0904.2557), section 5, gives the threshold argument in more detail than this deck, at a level this course has prepared you for.

The chip is flat

The hardware, from nothing. Superconducting qubits are small electrical circuits patterned on a chip and cooled close to absolute zero. Each is wired to a few neighbours by couplers, and a two-qubit gate is possible only along a coupler. The layout is a planar grid.

The problem. A gate between distant qubits must be built from a chain of SWAPs through the qubits in between, and every SWAP is three CNOTs — three more places for faults, which propagate.

What is wanted: a code whose checks act only on neighbours in a grid, all of small weight, whose distance grows by adding more of the same grid.

the surface code

Kitaev (1997, published 2003); planar versions with boundaries, Bravyi and Kitaev (1998); as a fault-tolerant memory, Dennis, Kitaev, Landahl and Preskill (J. Math. Phys. 43, 4452, 2002).

Couplers only between neighbours. Concatenation's high levels would need the dashed one.

Why a grid. Couplers are physical wires or capacitors on the chip, and two crossing wires would couple to each other. A planar grid with nearest-neighbour couplers is the densest layout that avoids crossings and gives every qubit four partners. Trapped-ion and neutral-atom machines can move qubits and so have more flexible connectivity; unit 26 surveys them. The surface code is the natural code for the grid, which is why superconducting experiments run it.

The SWAP cost. $\mathrm{SWAP} = \mathrm{CNOT}_{01}\mathrm{CNOT}_{10}\mathrm{CNOT}_{01}$ (unit 10), and by section 1's rules a single fault inside one of these CNOTs can leave errors on both qubits. Moving a qubit $m$ sites therefore costs $3m$ CNOTs and $3m$ chances to spread an error.

Names. "Surface code" and "toric code" are used for Kitaev's construction on a torus and its planar versions; this deck means the planar one, in the "rotated" layout of the next slide, which uses the fewest qubits for a given distance (Bombín and Martin-Delgado, Phys. Rev. A 76, 012305, 2007; Horsman, Fowler, Devitt and Van Meter, New J. Phys. 14, 123011, 2012).

The rotated surface code, drawn at d = 3

the rotated layout, distance $d$ odd

Data qubits at the $d\times d$ points of a square grid. Checks on the faces, coloured like a chessboard: each square face is an $X$-type or $Z$-type check on its four corners. Along the boundary, every other edge carries a weight-2 half-face: $Z$-type on top and bottom, $X$-type on left and right.

A measurement qubit sits in each face, coupled to its corners.

Counting. $(d - 1)^2$ squares and $2(d - 1)$ half-faces: $d^2 - 1$ checks. With the $d^2$ data qubits, $$2d^2 - 1\ \text{qubits in all.}$$

Check at $d = 3$: $4 + 4 = 8$ checks, $9 + 8 = 17$ qubits, as drawn.

Distance 3: nine data qubits, eight checks. Every check acts on neighbours only.

Coordinates, for the proofs. Put data qubit $(r, c)$ at row $r$, column $c$, $0\le r, c\le d - 1$. The face with top-left corner $(r, c)$, for $-1\le r, c\le d - 1$, holds those of the four points $(r, c), (r, c + 1), (r + 1, c), (r + 1, c + 1)$ that lie in the grid. It is $X$-type if $r + c$ is even and $Z$-type if odd. Keep every face with four points; of the faces with two points, keep the $Z$-type ones on the top and bottom rows of faces and the $X$-type ones on the left and right columns; discard the rest, and the one-point corner faces.

The count, in coordinates. Faces with four points: $0\le r, c\le d - 2$, $(d - 1)^2$ of them. Two-point faces along one side: $d - 1$, of which alternate ones, $(d - 1)/2$, have the kept type. Four sides: $2(d - 1)$.

Why "rotated". The original planar code puts qubits on the edges of a square lattice and uses $d^2 + (d - 1)^2$ data qubits for distance $d$, with checks of weight 3 at the boundary. A stabiliser code with one logical qubit

the checks form a stabiliser group

They commute. Two checks of the same type commute trivially. An $X$ face and a $Z$ face are either far apart or neighbours across an edge — the chessboard puts same-type faces corner to corner — and neighbours share exactly the two qubits of that edge: an even overlap, so they commute (unit 23).

They are independent (notes), $d^2 - 1$ of them on $d^2$ qubits.

So the code space has dimension $2^{d^2 - (d^2 - 1)} = 2$ (unit 23): one logical qubit.

It is a CSS code: every check is all-$X$ or all-$Z$.

An $X$ face and the $Z$ face beside it share an edge: two qubits.

Full proof of independence. Take the $X$-type checks; the $Z$-type case is the same with rows and columns exchanged. A qubit $(r, c)$ lies in at most two $X$ faces: those with top-left corners $(r - 1, c - 1)$ and $(r, c)$ if $r + c$ is even, or $(r - 1, c)$ and $(r, c - 1)$ if odd — whichever of them exist. Suppose a nonempty set $F$ of $X$ faces has product $I$, so every qubit lies in an even number of faces of $F$: zero or two. Then if a face is in $F$, so is every $X$ face sharing a qubit with it. The $X$ faces, joined when they share a qubit, form a connected diagonal grid (visible in the figure on the previous slide), so $F$ is all of them. But the corner qubit $(0, 0)$ lies in one $X$ face only, the one at $(0, 0)$ — the face at $(-1, -1)$ does not exist — so it is covered once. Contradiction. Independence of $X$-type from $Z$-type checks is automatic: a product of $X$s cannot equal a product of $Z$s unless both are $I$.

The boundary half-faces. A top half-face is $Z$-type and lies in row 0; its neighbours across an edge are the square faces below it, which are $X$-type and share its two qubits. The left half-faces are $X$-type, and their square neighbours are $Z$-type, again sharing two qubits. Same-type faces only ever meet at a corner, one qubit, and commute anyway.

Checked by elimination for $d = 3, 5, 7$: the $(d^2 - 1)\times2d^2$ symplectic matrix has full rank $8$, $24$, $48$, and every $X$-row is orthogonal to every $Z$-row.

Logical operators are strings, and the distance is d

$\bar X$ = $X$ on any row, left to right. It meets every $Z$ check in 0 or 2 qubits, so commutes with all of them. $\bar Z$ = $Z$ on any column, top to bottom, likewise. A row and a column meet in one qubit, so $\bar X\bar Z = -\bar Z\bar X$: neither is in the stabiliser group, and they are logical operators.

distance $d$

Every nontrivial logical operator has weight at least $d$, and the rows and columns have weight $d$.

proof

The rows are all the same logical $\bar X$: rows $r$ and $r + 1$ differ by the product of the $X$ faces between them. A $Z$-type logical anticommutes with $\bar X$, so it has odd overlap with each of the $d$ rows: at least one qubit in each, weight $\ge d$. $X$-type: the same with columns. A general logical is a product of an $X$ part and a $Z$ part, one of which is itself a nontrivial logical (CSS). ∎

Distance 5: $\bar X$ along a row, $\bar Z$ down a column. To cause a logical error, noise must build a whole string across the lattice.

Full proof that rows are equivalent. Between rows $r$ and $r + 1$ lie $d - 1$ square faces, alternately $X$ and $Z$, and at one end of the strip an $X$-type half-face. The $X$ faces of the strip cover alternate pairs of columns, and the half-face covers the one column left over at the end; together they contain every qubit of rows $r$ and $r + 1$ exactly once. So their product is $X$ on both rows, which is $\bar X_r\bar X_{r+1}$, a stabiliser. Hence $\bar X_{r+1} = \bar X_r\cdot(\text{stabiliser})$: the same logical operator. (Checked for $d = 3, 5$.)

Full proof of the CSS step. A Pauli $P$ commutes with all checks iff its $X$ part commutes with all $Z$ checks and its $Z$ part with all $X$ checks, since an $X$ check sees only $P$'s $Z$ part and vice versa. So both parts are in the normaliser. If both were stabilisers, $P$ would be (up to phase) a stabiliser. So one part is a nontrivial logical, and $P$'s weight is at least that part's weight.

Why it anticommutes. A nontrivial $Z$-type logical is $\bar Z$ times a stabiliser (there is one logical qubit, and the $Z$-type logicals up to stabilisers are $I$ and $\bar Z$), so it anticommutes with $\bar X$, hence with every row. Anticommuting with an $X$-string means odd overlap.

Brute force. For $d = 3$ and $d = 5$ the least weight of an operator commuting with all checks but not a stabiliser was found by search: 3 and 5, attained by a row or a column.

Measuring a face with its neighbours

Each measurement qubit talks only to its four corners. All faces run at once: one cycle is a preparation, four CNOT layers and a measurement.

Why the $X$ check works. $H$ then CNOTs from the measurement qubit is the $Z$ check's circuit seen in the Hadamard basis, where $X$ and $Z$ swap roles (section 1).

hook errors

These are section 1's naive circuits: one measurement qubit, four data qubits. A fault on it after two CNOTs is copied onto the last two data qubits — two errors from one fault.

The surface code survives because the order of the four CNOTs is chosen so that the pair lies across the logical string it could shorten, not along it. Then the hook does not reduce the distance.

No cat states needed. The price of a bad order is only a smaller effective distance, and the right order costs nothing (Tomita and Svore, Phys. Rev. A 90, 062320, 2014, cited).

The $X$ check, in full. Starting the measurement qubit in $\ket0$ and applying $H$ gives $\ket+$. CNOTs with the measurement qubit as control apply $X_aX_bX_cX_d$ to the data in the branch where it is $\ket1$: $\frac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0\ket\psi + \ket1X^{\otimes4}\ket\psi)$. If $X^{\otimes4}\ket\psi = \pm\ket\psi$, this is $\ket\pm\ket\psi$, and $H$ then a $Z$ meter reads $0$ or $1$. Normalisation: $\frac12 + \frac12 = 1$ ✓.

Why a hook along a logical string is dangerous. Two errors on adjacent qubits of a row, if the row direction is the one a logical string runs, cover two steps of that string for the price of one fault. A distance-$d$ code would then fail after about $d/2$ faults instead of $(d + 1)/2$ — an effective distance near $d/2$. Choosing the CNOT order so that the two last-touched qubits of an $X$ face sit one above the other (across $\bar X$'s rows) and those of a $Z$ face side by side (across $\bar Z$'s columns) avoids this.

Parallelism. Every measurement qubit does its first CNOT at the same time, then its second, and so on, with the four directions scheduled so that no data qubit is asked to do two CNOTs at once. One full cycle is therefore a constant number of steps, whatever $d$ is.

24 · surface code

Decoding: pairing up the alarms

What is seen. A detection event is a check whose outcome differs from its previous round. A chain of $Z$ errors lights the two $X$ checks at its ends, and nothing in between: each interior check meets the chain twice.

What is guessed. The decoder must pair each lit check with another, or with the boundary, by a chain of errors, then undo the chains. The likeliest explanation uses the fewest errors: minimum-weight matching (Edmonds' algorithm, 1965; Dennis et al. 2002).

In space-time. With repeated rounds, a measurement error lights the same check in two consecutive rounds: a "vertical" chain of length one. Matching runs on the three-dimensional pattern.

When it fails. If the true chain plus the decoder's chain wraps from one boundary to the other, their product is a logical operator.

Three $Z$ errors at distance 5; four lit $X$ checks; matching pairs them by shortest chains.

Why the ends light up. An $X$ check detects a $Z$ error on an odd number of its qubits. Along a connected chain of $Z$ errors, an interior face contains two consecutive qubits of the chain, an even number, and stays dark; the faces at the ends contain one. In the figure, $Z$ errors on two adjacent qubits of row 1 light the $X$ faces at either end of the pair, and a single $Z$ on the right edge lights the bulk $X$ face beside it and the right half-face.

Why fewest errors is likeliest. Under independent noise with $p\lt\frac12$, a configuration of $w$ errors has probability proportional to $(p/(1 - p))^w$, which decreases with $w$; so among the error chains consistent with the lit checks, the shortest are the most probable. Matching finds them in polynomial time. It is not exactly optimal — it ignores that many different chains give the same logical outcome, and treats $X$ and $Z$ errors separately — and better decoders exist. They are named, not developed: union-find (Delfosse and Nickerson 2021), belief-matching, and neural-network decoders.

Decoding must keep up. A round of syndromes arrives every cycle, and the decoder must process them at least as fast; otherwise a backlog grows without bound. This is the threshold theorem's "fast classical computation" hypothesis made concrete.

The threshold, and a heuristic below it

the surface code's threshold (cited)

Under circuit-level depolarising noise — every location of the measurement circuit faulty — with matching, $p_{\rm th}\approx1$ per cent (Wang, Fowler and Hollenberg, Phys. Rev. A 83, 020302, 2011; Fowler et al., Phys. Rev. A 86, 032324, 2012).

the logical error heuristic (cited) $$p_L\approx A\Big(\frac p{p_{\rm th}}\Big)^{(d+1)/2}$$

per cycle, a fit to simulations, not a theorem. Its form is Fowler et al.'s; resource estimates use $A = 0.1$, $p_{\rm th} = 10^{-2}$, and so does this deck.

Why $(d+1)/2$. The decoder completes a chain the short way, so a chain of $(d + 1)/2$ faults across the width $d$ is completed into a logical. Fewer cannot fool it.

Below threshold, each step of 2 in $d$ divides $p_L$ by $\Lambda = p_{\rm th}/p$. At threshold, nothing changes; above, a bigger code is worse.

Where the heuristic comes from. A logical failure needs at least $t + 1 = (d + 1)/2$ faults, since a distance-$d$ code corrects $t = (d - 1)/2$ (unit 22). The probability of a particular set of $(d + 1)/2$ faults is $p^{(d+1)/2}$, and the number of such sets that cause failure grows with $d$ roughly like a constant to the power $d$; lumping that constant into $p_{\rm th}$ and the rest into $A$ gives the form on the slide. It is reliable for $p$ well below $p_{\rm th}$ and $d$ not too small, and it is what every published resource estimate uses.

The suppression factor. $p_L(d)/p_L(d + 2) = (p_{\rm th}/p)^{(d+3)/2 - (d+1)/2} = p_{\rm th}/p$. Call it $\Lambda$. Below threshold $\Lambda\gt1$; at $p = 10^{-3}$, $\Lambda = 10$. Problem P8 proves the other side: above threshold, $\Lambda\lt1$ and increasing $d$ makes $p_L$ worse.

Thresholds compared. The surface code's $\approx1\%$ is some hundreds of times the proved bounds for concatenated Steane codes. Part of the gap is honest — weight-4 checks with one measurement qubit each are very simple circuits — and part is the difference between a number found by simulation and one proved for every noise process in a class.

Eastin–Knill: no code has it all

the Eastin–Knill theorem (cited)

No quantum code that detects every single-qubit error has a universal set of transversal logical gates. (Eastin and Knill, Phys. Rev. Lett. 102, 110502, 2009.)

The idea of the proof, in four steps; the notes give each.

For Steane's code, the transversal logical gates are the Clifford group; $T$ is missing. For the 15-qubit Reed–Muller code $T$ is transversal but $H$ is not.

Ways round. Supply the missing gate as a state — magic states, next. Or code switching between two codes with complementary transversal gates (Anderson, Duclos-Cianci and Poulin, PRL 113, 080501, 2014).

The proof, step by step (cited; the steps are the paper's). Transversal unitaries $U_1\otimes\cdots\otimes U_n$ that preserve the code form a compact group $G$. Suppose a continuous family $U(t)$ in $G$ passes through $I$; its derivative at $t = 0$ is $i\sum_jK_j$ with each $K_j$ a Hermitian operator on qubit $j$ alone. Since the code detects every single-qubit error, unit 22's definition of detection, $PEP = c_EP$, holds for the Paulis on qubit $j$, and by linearity $PK_jP = c_jP$ for scalars $c_j$: each $K_j$, seen from inside the code, is a multiple of the identity. So the generator acts on the code as a scalar, and every logical gate in the connected component of $I$ is a global phase. A compact group has finitely many connected components, so up to phase the transversal logical gates form a finite group. A finite group of unitaries is not dense in $SU(2^k)$, so it is not universal. The paper makes "transversal" precise for blocks larger than one qubit per code subsystem.

Why it is the right obstacle. Unit 23's Gottesman–Knill theorem says Clifford circuits are classically simulable. On Steane's code the transversal gates are exactly the Cliffords, so a machine restricted to them would gain nothing over a classical computer: the gate it lacks is the one that carries the power. That is one code. Eastin–Knill says it is every code, by a different route: whatever the transversal gates are, they form a finite group, so something non-transversal is always needed.

Code switching works because Steane's code and the 15-qubit Reed–Muller code are related: a state can be moved from one to the other fault-tolerantly by measuring checks. Use Steane for $H$, Reed–Muller for $T$. The switch itself is not transversal, which is how Eastin–Knill is respected. It is named, not developed.

24 · T gates

The magic state

the magic state $$\ket A = T\ket+ = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\ket0 + e^{i\pi/4}\ket1\big).$$

Normalised: $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ ✓. Bloch vector $(\cos\frac\pi4, \sin\frac\pi4, 0) = (\frac1{\sqrt2}, \frac1{\sqrt2}, 0)$ (unit 02), of length 1 ✓.

Not a stabiliser state. The one-qubit stabiliser states are the six points $\pm x, \pm y, \pm z$ of the sphere; $\ket A$ sits halfway between $+x$ and $+y$.

The idea. A gate must be applied correctly to an unknown state, at the moment it is needed. A state is a fixed, known object: it can be made in advance, by an unprotected method, then checked and purified before it is used. Then a Clifford circuit consumes it to apply $T$.

$T$ turns the Bloch sphere by $\pi/4$ about $z$, carrying $\ket+$ to $\ket A$.

The Bloch vector. For $\frac1{\sqrt2}(\ket0 + e^{i\varphi}\ket1)$ unit 02 gives $\mathbf r = (\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi, 0)$: $\langle X\rangle = \mathrm{Re}(e^{i\varphi}) = \cos\varphi$, $\langle Y\rangle = \mathrm{Im}(e^{i\varphi}) = \sin\varphi$, $\langle Z\rangle = \frac12 - \frac12 = 0$. At $\varphi = \pi/4$: $(0.7071, 0.7071, 0)$.

Why "not a stabiliser state" matters. A one-qubit stabiliser state is the $+1$ eigenvector of one of $\pm X, \pm Y, \pm Z$, so its Bloch vector is one of the six axis points. $\ket A$ is not, so Gottesman–Knill does not apply to circuits fed with it — which is exactly why it can supply the missing power. The name and the idea of supplying non-Clifford power through states are from Bravyi and Kitaev (2005).

Making one. On an encoded qubit, a noisy $\ket A$ is made by "injection": prepare it on a single physical qubit and grow the code around it. The result is an encoded $\ket A$ whose error is of order the physical $p$, because the first steps were unprotected. That is good enough to start from, and section 4's distillation does the rest.

24 · T gates

Worked: teleporting a T gate

worked example 4: gate teleportation

$\ket\psi = \alpha\ket0 + \beta\ket1$, $|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2 = 1$; $\omega = e^{i\pi/4}$.

① $\ket\psi\ket A = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big(\alpha\ket{00} + \alpha\omega\ket{01} + \beta\ket{10} + \beta\omega\ket{11}\big)$.

② CNOT$_{01}$ swaps the last two terms' second bits: $$\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big[(\alpha\ket0 + \beta\omega\ket1)\ket0 + (\alpha\omega\ket0 + \beta\ket1)\ket1\big].$$

③ Measure qubit 1. Outcome $m = 0$ has probability $\tfrac12(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2) = \tfrac12$ and leaves $\alpha\ket0 + \omega\beta\ket1 = T\ket\psi$. Outcome $m = 1$, probability $\tfrac12$, leaves $\alpha\omega\ket0 + \beta\ket1 = \omega\,T^\dagger\ket\psi$.

④ If $m = 1$ apply $S$: $ST^\dagger = T^2T^\dagger = T$, giving $\omega T\ket\psi$, which is $T\ket\psi$ up to global phase. ∎

Data on top, magic state below. The output stays on the data wire.

Check. $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$ ✓, whatever $\alpha, \beta$: the outcome says nothing about $\ket\psi$. On $\ket\psi = \ket0$: both branches leave $\ket0 = T\ket0$ ✓. On $\ket+$: the output is $\ket A$ ✓.

Step ③ in detail. The branch with qubit 1 in $\ket1$ is $\frac1{\sqrt2}(\alpha\omega\ket0 + \beta\ket1)$. Factor out $\omega$: $\alpha\omega\ket0 + \beta\ket1 = \omega(\alpha\ket0 + \bar\omega\beta\ket1)$, and $\bar\omega = e^{-i\pi/4}$ is the phase of $T^\dagger = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{-i\pi/4})$. Its squared norm is $\frac12(|\alpha|^2 + |\beta|^2) = \frac12$, so after normalising, qubit 0 holds $\omega T^\dagger\ket\psi$.

Step ④. $S = \mathrm{diag}(1, i) = \mathrm{diag}(1, \omega^2) = T^2$, so $ST^\dagger = T$. The global phase $\omega$ is unobservable and is dropped, with those words. Both branches therefore leave $T\ket\psi$, with probability one in total.

Why the independence matters. Both outcomes have probability $\frac12$ for every $\psi$, so the measurement learns nothing about the data. That is the same feature as unit 22's syndrome measurements, and for the same reason: what is measured is a property of the magic state's half of the entanglement, not of $\psi$.

The other circuit. Nielsen and Chuang draw the reverse: CNOT from the magic qubit into the data, measure the data qubit, and correct the magic qubit, which carries the output, with $SX$. Problem P10 does it. History: gate teleportation is due to Gottesman and Chuang (Nature 402, 390, 1999), and this one-bit form to Zhou, Leung and Chuang (Phys. Rev. A 62, 052316, 2000).

24 · T gates

Why the correction is cheap: the Pauli frame

Encoded, every step is transversal on Steane's code: the CNOT, the $Z$ measurement (measure all seven qubits, decode the classical word; its weight's parity is the logical bit), and the correction $S$, a Clifford. Only the input $\ket{\bar A}$ is unprotected.

the Pauli frame

Pauli corrections need not be applied. Record them classically, and push them through later Clifford gates, which map Paulis to Paulis (unit 11); at the end, reinterpret the measurement outcomes. Correction costs no gates and adds no faults.

Through a $T$ a frame $X$ becomes $TXT^\dagger = (X + Y)/\sqrt2 = e^{-i\pi/4}SX$: the frame gains a Clifford $S$, applied after the $T$. Still cheap.

An $X$ waiting in the frame, pushed through $T$, leaves an $S$ behind.

Check of $e^{-i\pi/4}SX$: $SX = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\i&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $e^{-i\pi/4}SX = \begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-i\pi/4}\\e^{i\pi/4}&0\end{pmatrix}$, and $\frac1{\sqrt2}(X + Y) = \frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1 - i\\1 + i&0\end{pmatrix}$, the same ✓.

The logical $Z$ measurement on Steane's code. Measuring all seven qubits of $\ket{\bar0}$ in $Z$ gives a uniformly random simplex codeword, of weight 0 or 4; of $\ket{\bar1}$, a complement, of weight 7 or 3. So the parity of the weight is the logical bit. With up to one error, the measured word is within distance 1 of a Hamming codeword; the classical Hamming decoder corrects it first. One faulty measurement flips one bit, which the decoder corrects: the gadget is fault-tolerant.

The frame in practice. Surface-code machines never apply Pauli corrections physically. The decoder's output is a running record, the frame, and only measurement results are adjusted. This is what makes the threshold theorem's "correct every cycle" affordable.

Why $T$ cannot be "framed". A frame works because the set of Paulis is closed under Clifford conjugation. $T$ takes $X$ outside the Paulis, so the frame must then hold a Clifford, which has to be applied before the next $T$ (whose effect would not be Clifford otherwise). That is why a circuit's $T$ gates are applied in order, each waiting for the measurement outcome of the one before: the $T$-depth sets the time.

24 · T gates

Worked: distillation, once and twice

magic-state distillation: 15 to 1 (cited)

From 15 copies of $\ket A$, each wrong with probability $p$, Clifford operations and measurements make one copy wrong with probability about $35p^3$, accepted with probability about $1 - 15p$. (Bravyi and Kitaev, Phys. Rev. A 71, 022316, 2005; built on the 15-qubit Reed–Muller code.)

worked example 5

One round from $p = 10^{-3}$: $35\times10^{-9} = 3.5\times10^{-8}$, above $10^{-12}$ ✗.

Two rounds: $35\times(3.5\times10^{-8})^3 = 35\times4.29\times10^{-23} = 1.5\times10^{-21}\le10^{-12}$ ✓, overshooting by nine orders.

Cost: $15\times15 = 225$ raw states per output.

The running example's $T$ gate: two rounds, 225 raw states.

Check. The map $p\mapsto35p^3$ shrinks $p$ only if $35p^2\lt1$, $p\lt1/\sqrt{35} = 0.169$: distillation has a threshold of its own, far above $10^{-3}$.

Where the numbers come from (cited). The 15-qubit code has transversal $T$; Bravyi and Kitaev use it in reverse. Its checks are measured on 15 noisy copies; if any check fails the batch is discarded. A single faulty copy always trips a check, and so does any pair; an undetected error needs three faulty copies arranged as one of the code's 35 weight-3 logical operators — the 35 lines of the projective space over $\mathbb F_2$ with 15 points. So the output error is $35p^3$ to leading order. Acceptance needs no faulty copy among 15 to leading order: $(1 - p)^{15}\approx 1 - 15p$.

The arithmetic. $(3.5\times10^{-8})^3 = 42.875\times10^{-24} = 4.2875\times10^{-23}$; times 35 is $1.5006\times10^{-21}$.

The overshoot is wasteful. Two rounds of the same protocol give nine orders more than needed. Real designs mix protocols — a cheaper first round, a 15-to-1 second, or recent "magic state cultivation" (Gidney, Shutty and Jones 2024) — to land nearer the target; Litinski, "Magic state distillation: not as costly as you think" (Quantum 3, 205, 2019), is a readable account. Named, not developed. Problem P11 asks how good the input must be for one round to suffice.

24 · T gates

Factories, and what a rotation costs

Unit 11's price. A rotation to accuracy $\varepsilon$ costs about $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ $T$ gates; at $\varepsilon = 10^{-11}$, about $110$.

Each $T$ consumes one distilled $\ket A$, and each distilled $\ket A$, at two rounds, consumes 225 raw ones. One rotation: $$110\times225 = 24\,750\ \text{raw magic states.}$$

A distillation factory is a region of the machine — surface-code patches and their measurement qubits — that does nothing but distil, delivering $\ket A$s to the computation as fast as its $T$ gates consume them.

the shape of every design

Cliffords are cheap, $T$ gates are made from states, and the states are made in factories. So a circuit's T-count sets the factories' output, and its T-depth its running time. In published resource estimates the factories occupy a large share of the qubits (Fowler et al. 2012; Litinski 2019).

Check on the Toffoli of unit 10: seven $T$ gates, so $7\times225 = 1575$ raw states at two rounds.

Why T-count became the currency. Before fault tolerance, circuits were measured in CNOTs. In a fault-tolerant machine CNOTs are a few cycles of lattice surgery and $T$ gates are factory output, many times more expensive. Unit 11's synthesis results — exact Clifford + $T$ synthesis and the $3\log_2(1/\varepsilon)$ rotations — were developed precisely because of this.

Reading the 24 750. It counts raw injected states, each on a small patch for a few cycles; factories run many distillations in parallel and pipeline the two rounds. The number is a measure of work, not of qubits at one time: the capstone turns it into factory footprints and hours.

Unit 11's ε and this unit's. Unit 11's $10^{-11}$ is a synthesis accuracy — how close the Clifford + $T$ word is to the wanted rotation. This unit's $10^{-12}$ is a failure probability per cycle. They are different budgets, and both must be met: the rotation must be synthesised accurately, and each of its 110 $T$ gates must be executed reliably.

24 · experiment

Below threshold, measured

The experiment, from nothing. Google Quantum AI's Willow chip: 105 superconducting qubits on a grid. They ran surface-code memories of distance 3, 5 and 7: prepare a logical $\ket0$ or $\ket+$, run $n$ cycles of syndrome extraction, measure every data qubit, decode, and see whether the logical value survived. The logical error per cycle is read off from how fast success decays with $n$.

the suppression factor Λ $$\Lambda = \frac{\varepsilon_d}{\varepsilon_{d+2}}$$

with $\varepsilon_d$ the logical error per cycle at distance $d$. Below threshold, operationally: $\Lambda\gt1$ — a bigger code is better. In the heuristic, $\Lambda = p_{\rm th}/p$.

as reported (cited)

$\Lambda = 2.14\pm0.02$; at $d = 7$, $\varepsilon_7 = 0.143\%\pm0.003\%$ per cycle; and the distance-7 logical qubit outlived the chip's best physical qubit by a factor $2.4\pm0.3$ — break-even passed.

Google Quantum AI and collaborators, "Quantum error correction below the surface code threshold", Nature 638, 920 (2025); arXiv:2408.13687 (2024).

Check on the layout: $2\cdot7^2 - 1 = 97$ code qubits, within the chip's 105. (The paper's "101-qubit" count also includes the qubits that remove leakage.)

Reading Λ. $\Lambda = p_{\rm th}/p\approx2.14$ puts the chip at $p\approx0.47\,p_{\rm th}$: below threshold by a factor of two, not ten.

Why a memory. Storing a logical qubit is the simplest thing a code must do, and it already exercises every part: repeated syndrome extraction, measurement errors, decoding. It does not test logical gates. The two initial logical states, $\ket0$ and $\ket+$, test protection against $X$ and $Z$ errors respectively.

What "below threshold" had meant before. In 2023 the same group reported a distance-5 code only slightly better than distance 3 (Nature 614, 676, 2023): $\Lambda$ barely above 1. The 2024 result is the first in which each increase of distance clearly paid, by a factor of two, over three distances. It also decoded in real time at distance 5, keeping up with the cycle — the threshold theorem's fast-classical-computation hypothesis, met in practice.

Quoted, not derived. The numbers on the slide are the paper's, from its abstract. The deck does not compute $\varepsilon_3$ or $\varepsilon_5$ from them; the paper reports its own values, and fitting $\Lambda$ across three points is the paper's analysis, not this deck's.

Break-even. A logical qubit "beyond break-even" stores information longer than any physical qubit in the same device could on its own. It is a weaker milestone than a useful $p_L$, but it is the first one at which error correction is a net gain.

24 · experiment

What Λ = 2.14 would buy

Extrapolate — assuming $\Lambda$ stays $2.14$ as $d$ grows, itself an assumption: $$\varepsilon_d = \varepsilon_7\,\Lambda^{-(d-7)/2}.$$

At $d = 21$, seven steps: $1.43\times10^{-3}/2.14^7 = 1.43\times10^{-3}/205.5 = 7.0\times10^{-6}$ — six orders short of $10^{-12}$.

To reach $10^{-12}$: $2.14^n\ge1.43\times10^9$, so $n\ge\ln(1.43\times10^9)/\ln2.14 = 27.7$, $n = 28$ steps: $d = 7 + 56 = 63$, and $2\cdot63^2 - 1 = 7937$ qubits.

the lesson

At $\Lambda\approx2$, one logical qubit costs about nine times the running example's 881. Each factor of 10 costs $\log_\Lambda10$ steps of 2 in $d$ — three here, one at $\Lambda = 10$ — so a lower $p$ is what shrinks the machine.

From the measured point at $d = 7$, $\Lambda = 2.14$ reaches the target near $d = 63$. The running example's line starts lower, at its own $10^{-5}$, and falls by $\Lambda = 10$ to reach it at $d = 21$.

The arithmetic. $2.14^2 = 4.5796$, $2.14^4 = 20.97$, $2.14^7 = 2.14^4\cdot2.14^2\cdot2.14 = 205.5$. Then $1.43\times10^{-3}/205.5 = 6.96\times10^{-6}$. For the target: $\ln(1.43\times10^9) = 21.08$, $\ln2.14 = 0.7608$, ratio $27.71$, so 28 steps of 2 beyond 7. At $d = 63$, $\varepsilon = 1.43\times10^{-3}/2.14^{28} = 8.0\times10^{-13}$ ✓, and at $d = 61$ it is $1.7\times10^{-12}$, just too high. Problem P9 does the same with $\Lambda = 2$: $d = 69$, $9521$ qubits.

Why the extrapolation is fragile. Three points fix a slope, not a law. Rare correlated events — the paper reports that long runs of a simpler code, the repetition code, hit a floor set by occasional error bursts affecting many qubits at once — are exactly the noise the threshold theorem's hypotheses exclude, and they matter more at large $d$ and long times. Leakage out of the qubit subspace (unit 25) is another. So the honest reading is: the experiment shows the mechanism working; it does not yet show it continuing to $10^{-12}$.

Rate versus distance. Each factor of 10 in $\varepsilon$ costs $\log_{\Lambda}10$ steps of 2 in $d$: about 3 steps ($\log_{2.14}10 = 3.03$) at $\Lambda = 2.14$, one at $\Lambda = 10$. The qubit count grows with $d^2$, so with the square of the number of steps.

24 · experiment

What has not been shown

A memory is not a computer. The experiment stores one logical qubit. A fault-tolerant computation also needs, at the same distances and error rates:

  • logical two-qubit operations between patches (lattice surgery);
  • magic-state factories feeding $T$ gates;
  • real-time decoding for many patches at once;
  • thousands of logical qubits, each a few hundred to a few thousand physical ones.

Each has been demonstrated, if at all, in small instances; unit 26 surveys what exists.

the hypotheses, in hardware

Correlated noise. The paper reports rare bursts that hit many qubits at once — outside the theorem's hypotheses. Leakage: a superconducting qubit has a third level the code knows nothing about (unit 25). Fresh ancillas: measurement qubits must reset every cycle.

What has been shown is the one thing the threshold theorem promises first: at fixed $p$, a larger code is better, by a measured factor.

Why list what is missing. The threshold theorem is a statement about all sizes at once, and an experiment can only check a few. The unit's arithmetic — 881 qubits, two rounds of distillation — rests on the heuristic and on $\Lambda$ staying put as codes grow. The capstone will multiply those numbers by thousands of logical qubits and billions of operations; it is worth knowing which factors have been measured and which are extrapolated.

The scale gap. Section 3's 881 qubits per logical qubit at $\Lambda = 10$, or about 8000 at $\Lambda = 2.14$, times thousands of logical qubits plus factories, is millions of physical qubits. The largest chips have of order a hundred to a thousand. Unit 26 makes this comparison with the numbers.
