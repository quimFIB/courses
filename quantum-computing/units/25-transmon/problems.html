Part E · unit 25 · problems

The physical qubit: driving a transmon, and the third level that will not stay empty

the slides · the curriculum

Checkpoint. You derive the rotating-frame Hamiltonian of a driven three-level transmon under the rotating-wave approximation, \(\tilde H = \alpha\ket2\!\bra2 + \tfrac{\Omega_x}{2}(b + b^\dagger) + \tfrac{\Omega_y}{2}(ib^\dagger - ib)\); compute the \(3.0\times10^{-4}\) leakage of a 10 ns Gaussian \(\pi\)-pulse at \(\alpha/2\pi = -250\) MHz, and what the derivative correction does to it (\(3.9\times10^{-6}\), while the miss rises to \(2.8\%\)); and say which of leakage, Stark shift and \(T_1\) dominates the gate error — the Stark shift, then leakage, then \(T_1\) (\(t/3T_1 = 3.3\times10^{-5}\)).

Work each problem on paper first. Under it, Hints opens a ladder one rung at a time — each rung appears only once the one before it is open, and the last gives the first line of the working — and Worked solution has the whole thing: the idea, the working, then a check. Read the solution after your attempt, including when you got it right. F folds everything back; D switches dark and light.

Twelve problems, about five hours. Work them on paper first; the numerical ones need a short loop, which you may write in anything — the referee blocks show one.

Conventions, as in the deck: \(\braket{u|v}\) is conjugate-linear in the first slot; \(\ket0, \ket1, \ket2\) are the transmon's lowest levels; \(n = \mathrm{diag}(0, 1, 2)\); the truncated lowering operator has \(b\ket1 = \ket0\), \(b\ket2 = \sqrt2\ket1\), \(b\ket0 = 0\). \(R_x(\theta) = e^{-i\theta X/2}\). With \(\hbar = 1\), frequencies are in rad/ns and times in ns; \(\alpha = -2\pi\times0.25 = -1.5708\) rad/ns and \(\omega = 2\pi\times5\) rad/ns unless a problem says otherwise.

The drive is \(H_d = (\Omega_x\cos\omega t + \Omega_y\sin\omega t)(b + b^\dagger)\), and after the rotating-wave approximation the model is \(\tilde H(t) = \alpha\ket2\!\bra2 + \tfrac{\Omega_x}{2}(b + b^\dagger) + \tfrac{\Omega_y}{2}(ib^\dagger - ib)\). A Gaussian \(\pi\)-pulse of duration \(T\) has \(\sigma = T/4\), \(\Omega_x(t) = A[e^{-(t - T/2)^2/2\sigma^2} - e^{-2}]\) on \([0, T]\), and \(A\) chosen so that \(\int_0^T\Omega_x = \pi\); for \(T = 10\), \(A = 0.6788\) rad/ns. DRAG is \(\Omega_y = -\lambda\Omega_x'/\alpha\); the detuning knob adds \(c\,\Omega_x^2n/\alpha\) to \(\tilde H\). "Leakage" is \(P_2\) and "the miss" is \(1 - P_1\), both at the end of a pulse started in \(\ket0\).

Some solutions end with an optional check by the course's referee, qclib, run from the course directory with uv run python. Every solution is complete without it.

The model and the frame

P1 Derive the rotating-frame Hamiltonian medium

exercises: the rotating frame as a time-dependent change of basis, conjugating the ladder operator, the rotating-wave approximation; a proof

  1. Prove that if \(i\partial_t\ket\psi = H(t)\ket\psi\) and \(\ket{\tilde\psi} = e^{iGt}\ket\psi\) for a fixed Hermitian \(G\), then \(i\partial_t\ket{\tilde\psi} = \big(e^{iGt}He^{-iGt} - G\big)\ket{\tilde\psi}\).
  2. With \(G = \omega n\) and \(H = \omega n + \tfrac\alpha2 n(n - 1) + H_d\), compute \(\tilde H\) exactly, every term.
  3. Make the rotating-wave approximation: say which terms are dropped, at what frequency they turn, and the condition that justifies dropping them. Write the result as a \(3\times3\) matrix and check it is Hermitian.
  4. Restrict to \(\ket0, \ket1\) and identify the result with a rotation of the Bloch sphere.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

The answer to part 2 is a \(3\times3\) Hermitian matrix depending on \(t\) through \(\Omega_x\), \(\Omega_y\) and factors \(e^{\pm2i\omega t}\). Everything follows from one fact: how \(e^{i\omega nt}\) conjugates \(b\).

Rung 2

Test \(e^{i\omega nt}be^{-i\omega nt}\) on a basis vector \(\ket k\). For the products, write \(\cos\omega t\) and \(\sin\omega t\) as exponentials and multiply out.

Rung 3

First line: \(e^{i\omega nt}be^{-i\omega nt}\ket k = e^{i\omega nt}\,b\,e^{-i\omega kt}\ket k = e^{-i\omega kt}\sqrt k\,e^{i\omega(k - 1)t}\ket{k - 1} = e^{-i\omega t}b\ket k\).

Worked solution

Idea. Differentiate the change of basis (that gives the \(-G\)), then conjugate each operator in \(H\): the number operator is unchanged, \(b\) picks up \(e^{-i\omega t}\), and the drive's \(\cos\) and \(\sin\) split each product into a slow part and a part turning at \(2\omega\).

1. \(i\partial_t(e^{iGt}\psi) = i(iG)e^{iGt}\psi + e^{iGt}(i\partial_t\psi) = -Ge^{iGt}\psi + e^{iGt}He^{-iGt}\,e^{iGt}\psi\), using \(e^{-iGt}e^{iGt} = I\). That is the claim.

2. \(n\) commutes with \(e^{i\omega nt}\), so \(\omega n + \tfrac\alpha2n(n - 1)\) is unchanged and, minus \(G = \omega n\), leaves \(\tfrac\alpha2n(n - 1) = \mathrm{diag}(0, 0, \alpha) = \alpha\ket2\!\bra2\). By rung 3, \(b \mapsto e^{-i\omega t}b\), and taking adjoints \(b^\dagger \mapsto e^{i\omega t}b^\dagger\). So the drive becomes \((\Omega_x\cos\omega t + \Omega_y\sin\omega t)(e^{-i\omega t}b + e^{i\omega t}b^\dagger)\). With \(\cos\omega t\,e^{\mp i\omega t} = \tfrac12(1 + e^{\mp2i\omega t})\), \(\sin\omega t\,e^{-i\omega t} = \tfrac1{2i}(1 - e^{-2i\omega t})\) and \(\sin\omega t\,e^{i\omega t} = \tfrac1{2i}(e^{2i\omega t} - 1)\):

\[\begin{aligned}\tilde H &= \alpha\ket2\!\bra2 + \tfrac{\Omega_x}2(b + b^\dagger) + \tfrac{\Omega_y}2(ib^\dagger - ib)\\ &\quad + \tfrac{\Omega_x}2\big(e^{-2i\omega t}b + e^{2i\omega t}b^\dagger\big) + \tfrac{\Omega_y}2\big(ie^{-2i\omega t}b - ie^{2i\omega t}b^\dagger\big),\end{aligned}\]

using \(\tfrac1{2i}(b - b^\dagger) = \tfrac12(ib^\dagger - ib)\). This is exact.

3. The last two brackets turn at \(2\omega\) (period \(\pi/\omega = 0.1\) ns). The rotating-wave approximation drops them; it is justified when every rate in the kept part is small against \(\omega\): \(|\Omega_x|, |\Omega_y|, |\alpha| \ll \omega\), with envelopes varying slowly on the scale \(1/\omega\). Its first correction is unit 02's Bloch–Siegert shift \(\approx\Omega^2/4\omega\). The result, with \(\braket{1|b^\dagger|0} = 1\) and \(\braket{2|b^\dagger|1} = \sqrt2\):

\[\tilde H = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \frac{\Omega_x - i\Omega_y}2 & 0\\ \frac{\Omega_x + i\Omega_y}2 & 0 & \frac{\Omega_x - i\Omega_y}{\sqrt2}\\ 0 & \frac{\Omega_x + i\Omega_y}{\sqrt2} & \alpha\end{pmatrix}.\]

Each entry below the diagonal is the complex conjugate of its mirror, and the diagonal is real: Hermitian.

4. Deleting row and column 2 leaves \(\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}0 & \Omega_x - i\Omega_y\\ \Omega_x + i\Omega_y & 0\end{pmatrix} = \tfrac12(\Omega_xX + \Omega_yY)\): a rotation about the axis \((\Omega_x, \Omega_y, 0)\) at rate \(\sqrt{\Omega_x^2 + \Omega_y^2}\). With \(\Omega_y = 0\) it generates \(R_x\).

Check. With the drive off, \(\tilde H = \alpha\ket2\!\bra2\): in the frame turning at \(\omega\), \(\ket0\) and \(\ket1\) stand still and \(\ket2\) turns at \(\alpha\), because its lab frequency \(2\omega + \alpha\) exceeds \(2\omega\) by \(\alpha\) ✓. Dimensions: every term is a frequency ✓.

P2 The √2 and the leakage transition routine

exercises: matrix elements of the truncated ladder, the detuning of the leakage transition, unit 02's Rabi formula; ends in a number

  1. Find the eigenvalues of \(M = b + b^\dagger\) on three levels, and \(\lVert M\rVert\).
  2. In \(\tilde H\) with \(\Omega_y = 0\), what are the coupling and the detuning of the pair \(\ket0, \ket1\), and of the pair \(\ket1, \ket2\)?
  3. Suppose the drive were held constant at the peak of the 10 ns pulse, \(\Omega = 0.587\) rad/ns, and the qubit started in \(\ket1\). Using unit 02's Rabi formula on the pair \(\ket1, \ket2\) alone, what is the largest population \(\ket2\) could reach? Repeat for the 4 ns pulse's peak, \(\Omega = 1.467\) rad/ns.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a characteristic polynomial of a real symmetric \(3\times3\); part 3 is one number from unit 02, the peak of a detuned Rabi oscillation.

Rung 2

Unit 02: a two-level system with Rabi frequency \(\Omega_{\rm R}\) and detuning \(\Delta\) reaches at most \(\Omega_{\rm R}^2/(\Omega_{\rm R}^2 + \Delta^2)\). Identify \(\Omega_{\rm R}\) and \(\Delta\) from the \(\ket1\)–\(\ket2\) block of \(\tilde H\).

Rung 3

The block is \(\begin{pmatrix}0 & \Omega/\sqrt2\\ \Omega/\sqrt2 & \alpha\end{pmatrix}\); compare with \(\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}-\Delta & \Omega_{\rm R}\\ \Omega_{\rm R} & \Delta\end{pmatrix}\) plus a multiple of \(I\).

Worked solution

Idea. The leakage pair is a detuned Rabi problem with a stronger coupling: read its two parameters off the matrix and apply unit 02.

1. \(M = \begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & 0\\ 1 & 0 & \sqrt2\\ 0 & \sqrt2 & 0\end{pmatrix}\), \(\det(M - \lambda I) = -\lambda(\lambda^2 - 2) - (-\lambda) = -\lambda^3 + 3\lambda\). Eigenvalues \(0, \pm\sqrt3\); \(M\) is Hermitian, so \(\lVert M\rVert = \sqrt3 = 1.732\).

2. \(\ket0, \ket1\): diagonal entries \(0, 0\), so detuning \(0\) (resonant), coupling \(\braket{1|\tilde H|0} = \Omega/2\). \(\ket1, \ket2\): diagonal entries \(0, \alpha\), so detuning \(\alpha\), coupling \(\braket{2|\tilde H|1} = \Omega/\sqrt2\), larger by \(\sqrt2\).

3. The block equals \(\tfrac\alpha2I + \tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}-\alpha & \sqrt2\Omega\\ \sqrt2\Omega & \alpha\end{pmatrix}\): Rabi frequency \(\Omega_{\rm R} = \sqrt2\Omega\), detuning \(\Delta = \alpha\) (the constant is a global phase). The peak population is

\[\frac{2\Omega^2}{2\Omega^2 + \alpha^2} = \frac{2(0.587)^2}{2(0.587)^2 + 1.5708^2} = \frac{0.689}{3.156} = 0.218\quad(10\text{ ns peak}),\] \[\frac{2(1.467)^2}{2(1.467)^2 + 2.467} = \frac{4.306}{6.774} = 0.636\quad(4\text{ ns peak}).\]

So a drive held at the 4 ns pulse's peak could push nearly two thirds of \(\ket1\) up the ladder; the slide's quarter at 4 ns is that, softened by the pulse's rise and fall.

Check. \(\Omega \to 0\) gives \(0\); \(\alpha \to 0\) gives \(1\), full transfer on resonance, as unit 02 says ✓. In MHz: the leakage Rabi frequency \(\sqrt2\Omega/2\pi\) is \(132\) MHz at the 10 ns peak, against \(|\alpha|/2\pi = 250\) MHz — the same order, which is why the third level matters.

P3 Piecewise-constant error medium

exercises: comparing two evolutions, the telescoping bound, choosing a step; a proof that ends in a number

  1. Let \(iU' = H(t)U\) and \(iW' = K(t)W\) with \(U(0) = W(0) = I\) and \(H, K\) Hermitian. Prove \(\lVert U(t) - W(t)\rVert \le \int_0^t\lVert H - K\rVert\).
  2. Prove that for unitaries \(\lVert A_N\cdots A_1 - B_N\cdots B_1\rVert \le \sum_k\lVert A_k - B_k\rVert\).
  3. Deduce that midpoint steps of length \(\Delta t = T/N\) approximate \(U(T, 0)\) to within \(MT\Delta t/4\), \(M = \max\lVert H'\rVert\).
  4. For the 10 ns Gaussian \(\pi\)-pulse (\(\Omega_y = 0\)), how many steps does the bound require for accuracy \(10^{-6}\)? Use \(\max|\Omega_x'| = A/(\sigma\sqrt e)\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is about the single operator \(W^\dagger U\), which equals \(I\) when the two evolutions agree. Part 4 is a number of steps, millions or fewer.

Rung 2

Differentiate \(W^\dagger U\) and integrate. For part 2, write the difference as a sum of \(N\) terms, each with one factor \(A_k - B_k\). For part 3, apply part 1 on each step with \(K\) constant.

Rung 3

First line: \((W^\dagger U)' = (W')^\dagger U + W^\dagger U' = (iW^\dagger K)U + W^\dagger(-iHU) = -iW^\dagger(H - K)U\).

Worked solution

Idea. Two evolutions drift apart at most at the rate their generators differ; per step that is \(O(\Delta t^2)\), and \(N\) steps add.

1. From rung 3, integrating with \(W^\dagger U = I\) at \(0\): \(W^\dagger(t)U(t) - I = -i\int_0^tW^\dagger(H - K)U\). Unitaries have norm \(1\) and \(\lVert XY\rVert \le \lVert X\rVert\lVert Y\rVert\), so \(\lVert W^\dagger U - I\rVert \le \int_0^t\lVert H - K\rVert\). Finally \(\lVert U - W\rVert = \lVert W(W^\dagger U - I)\rVert = \lVert W^\dagger U - I\rVert\).

2. \(A_N\cdots A_1 - B_N\cdots B_1 = \sum_{k=1}^NA_N\cdots A_{k+1}(A_k - B_k)B_{k-1}\cdots B_1\) (expand: consecutive terms cancel in pairs). Each product of unitaries has norm \(1\).

3. Let \(U_k\) be the exact evolution over step \(k\) (it composes: \(U(T, 0) = U_N\cdots U_1\)). On that step, with \(K = H(t_k)\) constant, part 1 gives \(\lVert U_k - e^{-iH(t_k)\Delta t}\rVert \le \int\lVert H(t) - H(t_k)\rVert dt \le M\int|t - t_k|\,dt = M\Delta t^2/4\), since \(|t - t_k| \le \Delta t/2\) and \(\int_{-\Delta t/2}^{\Delta t/2}|u|\,du = \Delta t^2/4\). Part 2 sums \(N\) of these: \(NM\Delta t^2/4 = MT\Delta t/4\).

4. \(H' = \tfrac12\Omega_x'M\) with \(\lVert M\rVert = \sqrt3\) (P2), and \(\max|\Omega_x'| = 0.6788/(2.5\times1.6487) = 0.1647\) rad/ns², so \(M_{H} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}2\times0.1647 = 0.1426\) rad/ns². Accuracy \(10^{-6}\) needs \(\Delta t \le 4\times10^{-6}/(0.1426\times10) = 2.8\times10^{-6}\) ns, i.e. \(N \ge 10/2.8\times10^{-6} = 3.6\times10^6\) steps.

Check. The bound is linear in \(\Delta t\), as it should be for an error per step of \(\Delta t^2\) summed over \(1/\Delta t\) steps; with \(H\) constant, \(M = 0\) and the bound says the steps are exact, which they are ✓. In practice midpoints do much better: the error is \(O(\Delta t^2)\) (not proved here), falling fourfold per doubling of \(N\), and about \(1000\) steps already give \(10^{-6}\).

With the referee

import numpy as np
from qclib import linalg
alpha = -2 * np.pi * 0.25                              # rad/ns
b = np.diag([1, np.sqrt(2)], k=1)                      # truncated lowering operator
n = np.diag([0, 1, 2])

def pulse(T, lam=0.0, c=0.0, steps=4000):
    """Gaussian pi-pulse, sigma = T/4, offset to start at zero; returns the final 3x3 unitary."""
    s, m = T / 4, T / 2
    env = lambda t: np.exp(-(t - m)**2 / (2 * s * s)) - np.exp(-2)
    denv = lambda t: -(t - m) / (s * s) * np.exp(-(t - m)**2 / (2 * s * s))
    grid = np.linspace(0, T, 100001)
    A = np.pi / np.trapezoid(env(grid), grid)          # area pi
    dt, U = T / steps, np.eye(3, dtype=complex)
    for t in (np.arange(steps) + 0.5) * dt:            # midpoints
        ox, oy = A * env(t), -lam * A * denv(t) / alpha
        H = (alpha * n * (n - 1) / 2 + c * ox**2 / alpha * n
             + ox / 2 * (b + b.T) + oy / 2 * (1j * b.T - 1j * b))
        U = linalg.exp_hermitian(H, -1j * dt) @ U
    return U

exact = pulse(10, steps=64000)
for N in (250, 500, 1000, 2000):
    print(N, f"{linalg.op_norm(pulse(10, steps=N) - exact):.1e}")
# 250 1.4e-05   500 3.4e-06   1000 8.6e-07   2000 2.1e-07   (fourfold per doubling)

The function pulse is reused by the referee blocks of P5, P6, P8 and P10.

P4 A harmonic oscillator cannot be a qubit medium

exercises: commuting time-dependent Hamiltonians, Cayley–Hamilton, functional calculus; a proof

Set \(\alpha = 0\) and \(\Omega_y = 0\), with \(\Omega_x(t)\) any real envelope of area \(\theta\), on three levels.

  1. Prove that \(U(T, 0) = e^{-i\theta M/2}\) with \(M = b + b^\dagger\), whatever the envelope's shape.
  2. Find \(P_0, P_1, P_2\) from \(\ket0\) as functions of \(\theta\), and prove \(P_1 \le \tfrac13\) for every pulse.
  3. Evaluate them at \(\theta = \pi\), and find the area at which \(P_1\) first reaches \(\tfrac13\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is the area theorem with a \(3\times3\) matrix in place of \(X\). Part 2 needs \(e^{-ixM}\) in closed form; the tool is a polynomial identity for \(M\).

Rung 2

\(M\)'s eigenvalues are \(0, \pm\sqrt3\) (P2), so by Cayley–Hamilton \(M^3 = 3M\). Split the exponential series into odd and even powers.

Rung 3

First line: \(e^{-ixM} = I + \sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-ix)^{2k}3^{k-1}}{(2k)!}M^2 + \sum_{k\ge0}\frac{(-ix)^{2k+1}3^k}{(2k+1)!}M\).

Worked solution

Idea. With no anharmonicity every instant's Hamiltonian is a multiple of one matrix, so only the area matters, and the matrix's cubic identity gives the exponential exactly.

1. \(\tilde H(t) = \tfrac{\Omega_x(t)}2M\). Let \(\Theta(t) = \int_0^t\Omega_x\). Then \(\tfrac{d}{dt}e^{-i\Theta M/2} = -\tfrac i2\Omega_xMe^{-i\Theta M/2}\) (the series differentiates term by term, and \(M\) commutes with itself), and it is \(I\) at \(t = 0\). By uniqueness it is the propagator; at \(T\), \(\Theta = \theta\).

2. From rung 3, the even part is \(I + \tfrac{M^2}3\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-1)^k(\sqrt3x)^{2k}}{(2k)!} = I + \tfrac{\cos\sqrt3x - 1}3M^2\) and the odd part is \(-\tfrac{i}{\sqrt3}\sum_k\frac{(-1)^k(\sqrt3x)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}M = -i\tfrac{\sin\sqrt3x}{\sqrt3}M\). With \(M\ket0 = \ket1\), \(M^2\ket0 = \ket0 + \sqrt2\ket2\), \(x = \theta/2\) and \(s = \sqrt3\theta/2\):

\[e^{-i\theta M/2}\ket0 = \tfrac{2 + \cos s}3\ket0 - i\tfrac{\sin s}{\sqrt3}\ket1 + \tfrac{\sqrt2(\cos s - 1)}3\ket2,\] \[P_0 = \Big(\tfrac{2 + \cos s}3\Big)^2,\quad P_1 = \tfrac{\sin^2s}3 \le \tfrac13,\quad P_2 = \tfrac{2(1 - \cos s)^2}9 .\]

3. At \(\theta = \pi\): \(s = 2.7207\), \(\cos s = -0.9127\), \(\sin s = 0.4086\); \(P_0 = 0.1314\), \(P_1 = 0.0556\), \(P_2 = 0.8130\). \(P_1 = \tfrac13\) needs \(\sin^2s = 1\), first at \(s = \pi/2\), i.e. \(\theta = \pi/\sqrt3 = 1.814\) — and there \(P_0 = \tfrac49\), \(P_2 = \tfrac29\).

Check. Normalisation: \(\tfrac{(2 + c)^2 + 3(1 - c^2) + 2(1 - c)^2}{9} = \tfrac{9}{9} = 1\) for every \(c = \cos s\) ✓; at \(\theta = \pi\), \(0.1314 + 0.0556 + 0.8130 = 1.0000\) ✓. At \(s = \pi/2\): \(\tfrac49 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac29 = 1\) ✓. Small \(\theta\): \(P_1 \approx \tfrac{3\theta^2/4}{3} = \theta^2/4 = \sin^2(\theta/2)\) to leading order, the two-level answer — for a weak pulse the ladder is not yet climbed ✓.

P5 Two levels are not enough routine

exercises: the area theorem, reading a three-level computation against a two-level one; ends in a number

  1. On two levels, what does the 10 ns Gaussian \(\pi\)-pulse do to \(\ket0\)? Prove it, without computing anything about the envelope beyond its area.
  2. The three-level evolution of the same pulse gives \(P_0 = 0.02534\), \(P_1 = 0.97436\), \(P_2 = 3.0\times10^{-4}\). How large is the two-level model's error in \(P_1\), and where did the missing population go?
  3. Explain why a longer pulse of the same area would make the two models agree better.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is a rotation of the Bloch sphere; part 2 is a subtraction and a split of \(1 - P_1\) into two populations.

Rung 2

On two levels with \(\Omega_y = 0\), all the \(\tilde H(t)\) are multiples of \(X\). For part 3, ask how \(\Omega\) compares with \(|\alpha|\) as \(T\) grows.

Rung 3

First line: \(\tilde H_{\rm qubit}(t) = \tfrac{\Omega_x(t)}2X\), so \(U = R_x(\int\Omega_x) = R_x(\pi) = -iX\).

Worked solution

Idea. Two levels see only the area, so they promise perfection; every deviation in the three-level answer is the third level's doing.

1. By the area theorem (all instants commute), \(U = R_x(\pi) = -iX\), so \(\ket0 \mapsto -i\ket1\): \(P_1 = 1\) exactly.

2. The two-level model overstates \(P_1\) by \(1 - 0.97436 = 0.02564\), i.e. \(2.6\%\). Of that, \(P_0 = 0.02534\) never left \(\ket0\) and \(P_2 = 0.00030\) leaked: \(0.02534 + 0.00030 = 0.02564\) ✓. So the miss is \(99\%\) a rotation that fell short, \(1\%\) leakage — the third level matters mainly by moving the qubit's frequency (the Stark shift), not by stealing population.

3. Area \(\pi\) fixes \(\int\Omega_x\), so the drive strength scales as \(1/T\). The third level's effects are controlled by \(\Omega/|\alpha|\): the Stark miss is \(\approx 2.6/T^2\) (\(T\) in ns) and leakage falls faster still. As \(T\) grows, \(\ket2\) is ever further off-resonant compared with the drive, and the three-level answer tends to the two-level one.

Check. \(0.02534 + 0.97436 + 0.00030 = 1.00000\) ✓. At 20 ns the three-level miss is \(0.65\%\), a quarter of \(2.6\%\), as \(1/T^2\) predicts ✓.

P6 Leakage against duration medium

exercises: the piecewise-constant evolution, the leading-order leakage formula, spectral width against anharmonicity; ends in a number

  1. Compute the leakage \(P_2\) of Gaussian \(\pi\)-pulses of 4, 6, 8, 10 and 20 ns.
  2. Use \(P_2 \approx \Omega_x'(T)^2/2\alpha^4\) to predict the 20 ns value on paper, and compare.
  3. Explain the trend: why leakage falls like \(T^{-4}\) for long pulses, and why the formula fails at 4 ns.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is five numbers from a short loop: the deck's worked example, repeated. Part 2 needs only \(\Omega_x'\) at the end of the pulse.

Rung 2

\(\Omega_x'(T) = -2Ae^{-2}/\sigma\), and since every time scales with \(T\), \(AT = 6.788\) for all durations. For part 3 compare the pulse's spectral width, about \(1/\sigma\), with \(|\alpha|\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: at \(T = 20\), \(A = 0.3394\), \(\sigma = 5\), so \(|\Omega_x'(T)| = 2\times0.3394\times0.13534/5 = 0.01837\) rad/ns².

Worked solution

Idea. Leakage is the Fourier transform of a smooth function at the frequency \(\alpha\); for long pulses only the kink at the switch-off contributes, and its size scales as \(1/T^2\).

\(T\) (ns)4681020

P7 The leakage formula, and smoother pulses hard

exercises: variation of constants, integration by parts as an asymptotic expansion, the DRAG cancellation; a proof

  1. From the third row of \(\tilde H\), prove that exactly \(c_2(T) = -ie^{-i\alpha T}\int_0^Te^{i\alpha t}g(t)c_1(t)\,dt\), with \(g = (\Omega_x + i\Omega_y)/\sqrt2\) and \(c_2(0) = 0\).
  2. For \(\Omega_y = 0\) and an envelope vanishing at both ends, integrate by parts twice to show that, to leading order in \(1/\alpha\), \(|c_2(T)| \approx |\Omega_x'(T)|\,|c_1(T)|/\sqrt2\alpha^2\).
  3. Show that \(\Omega_y = -\Omega_x'/\alpha\) cancels this leading term.
  4. Suppose instead the envelope's slope also vanishes at both ends (a "smooth" pulse) and \(\Omega_y = 0\). Find the leading term.
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is the variation-of-constants formula for a first-order linear ODE. Parts 2–4 are about boundary terms: which survive at \(t = 0\) and at \(t = T\), and at which power of \(1/\alpha\).

Rung 2

Multiply \(i\dot c_2 = \alpha c_2 + gc_1\) by \(e^{i\alpha t}\). In the integrations by parts use \(c_1(0) = 0\) (the pulse starts in \(\ket0\)) and \(\Omega_x(0) = \Omega_x(T) = 0\). In part 3 the \(\Omega_y\) term is already \(O(1/\alpha)\), so one integration by parts suffices.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\int_0^Te^{i\alpha t}f\,dt = \big[\tfrac{e^{i\alpha t}}{i\alpha}f\big]_0^T - \tfrac1{i\alpha}\int_0^Te^{i\alpha t}f'\,dt\) with \(f = \Omega_xc_1/\sqrt2\), and the bracket vanishes.

Worked solution

Idea. The leakage amplitude is a Fourier coefficient at \(\alpha\). Each integration by parts trades a factor \(1/\alpha\) for a derivative, and leaves boundary values; the first non-vanishing boundary value is the answer, and a second quadrature can be chosen to cancel it.

1. The third row of \(i\dot{\mathbf c} = \tilde H\mathbf c\) is \(i\dot c_2 = gc_1 + \alpha c_2\), since \(\braket{2|\tilde H|0} = 0\) and \(\braket{2|\tilde H|1} = (\Omega_x + i\Omega_y)/\sqrt2\). Then \(\tfrac{d}{dt}(e^{i\alpha t}c_2) = e^{i\alpha t}(\dot c_2 + i\alpha c_2) = -ie^{i\alpha t}gc_1\); integrate from \(0\) with \(c_2(0) = 0\) and multiply by \(e^{-i\alpha T}\).

2. With rung 3 and a second integration by parts, using \((i\alpha)^2 = -\alpha^2\):

\[\int_0^Te^{i\alpha t}f = \frac{1}{\alpha^2}\Big[e^{i\alpha t}f'\Big]_0^T + \frac{i}{\alpha^3}\Big[e^{i\alpha t}f''\Big]_0^T + O(\alpha^{-4}).\]

Now \(f' = (\Omega_x'c_1 + \Omega_x\dot c_1)/\sqrt2\). At \(t = 0\) both terms vanish (\(c_1(0) = 0\), \(\Omega_x(0) = 0\)); at \(t = T\) only \(\Omega_x'(T)c_1(T)/\sqrt2\) survives. So \(|c_2(T)| = |\Omega_x'(T)||c_1(T)|/(\sqrt2\alpha^2) + O(\alpha^{-3})\), and \(P_2 \approx \Omega_x'(T)^2/2\alpha^4\) when \(|c_1(T)| \approx 1\).

3. With \(\Omega_y \ne 0\), add \(\int e^{i\alpha t}f_y\), \(f_y = i\Omega_yc_1/\sqrt2\). One integration by parts: \(\big[\tfrac{e^{i\alpha t}}{i\alpha}f_y\big]_0^T + O(\alpha^{-3}) = \tfrac1\alpha\big[e^{i\alpha t}\Omega_yc_1/\sqrt2\big]_0^T + O(\alpha^{-3})\), and at \(t = 0\) it vanishes. The total \(O(\alpha^{-2})\) term is \(\tfrac{e^{i\alpha T}c_1(T)}{\sqrt2\alpha^2}\big(\Omega_x'(T) + \alpha\Omega_y(T)\big)\), which is zero for \(\Omega_y = -\Omega_x'/\alpha\).

4. If also \(\Omega_x'(0) = \Omega_x'(T) = 0\), then \(f'(T) = 0\) and the first bracket vanishes. In the second, \(f'' = (\Omega_x''c_1 + 2\Omega_x'\dot c_1 + \Omega_x\ddot c_1)/\sqrt2\): at \(T\) only \(\Omega_x''(T)c_1(T)/\sqrt2\) survives, and at \(0\) everything vanishes (\(c_1(0) = 0\), \(\Omega_x'(0) = 0\), \(\Omega_x(0) = 0\)). So \(|c_2(T)| \approx |\Omega_x''(T)|/(\sqrt2|\alpha|^3)\) and \(P_2 \approx \Omega_x''(T)^2/2\alpha^6\): one more power of \(\Omega/\alpha\)-type smallness. Smoothness at the ends buys leakage suppression order by order — which is why real pulses are shaped to switch on and off gently.

Check. Part 2 against computation: at 20 ns, \(2.77\times10^{-5}\) predicted, \(2.70\times10^{-5}\) computed; with \(\alpha\) scaled up eightfold at 10 ns the ratio is \(0.998\) (the deck's table) ✓. Part 3 against computation: at 10 ns DRAG takes \(P_2\) from \(3.0\times10^{-4}\) to \(3.9\times10^{-6}\) ✓. Dimensions in part 4: \(\Omega''\) is ns\(^{-3}\), \(\alpha^3\) is ns\(^{-3}\) ✓.

  2. The deck's first-order theorem says \(1 - P_1 \approx \kappa^2\) with \(\kappa = (\lambda + c - \tfrac12)I_s/2\alpha\), \(I_s = \int_0^T\Omega_x^2\sin\Theta\,dt\). Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is two numbers from the same loop as P6, with the quadrature switched on. Part 2 is one number, the same at both values of \(\lambda\).

Rung 2

\(\lambda + c - \tfrac12\) is \(-\tfrac12\) at \(\lambda = 0\) and \(+\tfrac12\) at \(\lambda = 1\). The miss depends on \(\kappa^2\).

Rung 3

Worked solution

Idea. DRAG cancels the leakage amplitude, but the miss comes from a different mechanism — the drive Stark-shifting the qubit — whose first-order size depends on \(|\lambda + c - \tfrac12|\), and \(\lambda = 1\) overshoots exactly as far as \(\lambda = 0\) undershoots.

1. Computed: \(P_2 = 3.95\times10^{-6}\) (down 76-fold from \(3.0\times10^{-4}\)), \(1 - P_1 = 0.0281\) (up from \(0.0256\)). Sign: \(\Omega_y = -\Omega_x'/\alpha = \Omega_x'/|\alpha|\), positive while \(\Omega_x\) rises, peaking at \(0.1647/1.5708 = 0.105\) rad/ns \(= 2\pi\times16.7\) MHz.

DRAG was designed to cancel the leakage amplitude, which needs \(\lambda = 1\); the turn about \(Y\) needs \(\lambda = \tfrac12\). Check. Rung 1

Part 1 is a quadratic equation; part 2 is three frequencies in MHz; part 3 is unit 02's \(\Omega^2/(\Omega^2 + \Delta^2)\).

Rung 2

The block \(\begin{pmatrix}0 & \Omega/\sqrt2\\ \Omega/\sqrt2 & \alpha\end{pmatrix}\) has trace \(\alpha\) and determinant \(-\Omega^2/2\). For \(\alpha \lt 0\), write \(\sqrt{\alpha^2 + 2\Omega^2} = |\alpha|\sqrt{1 + 2\Omega^2/\alpha^2}\).

Idea. The drive mixes \(\ket1\) with \(\ket2\), and two coupled levels repel; \(\ket0\) is not coupled to \(\ket2\) and stays, so the qubit's frequency moves by however much \(\ket1\) does.

1. For \(\alpha \lt 0\) the root tending to \(0\) is the \(+\) one: \(E_1 = \tfrac12(\alpha + |\alpha|\sqrt{1 + 2\Omega^2/\alpha^2}) = \tfrac{|\alpha|}2(\sqrt{1 + 2\Omega^2/\alpha^2} - 1)\). With \(\sqrt{1 + u} = 1 + \tfrac u2 - \tfrac{u^2}8 + \dots\): \(E_1 = \tfrac{|\alpha|}2\big(\tfrac{\Omega^2}{\alpha^2} - \tfrac{\Omega^4}{2\alpha^4}\big) + \dots = \tfrac{\Omega^2}{2|\alpha|} - \tfrac{\Omega^4}{4|\alpha|^3} + \dots\). Positive: \(\ket1\) is pushed up, away from \(\ket2\) (which lies at \(\alpha \lt 0\) in this frame), so the qubit frequency rises and a drive fixed at \(\omega\) is left below resonance.

2. Exact: \(\tfrac12(-1.5708 + \sqrt{2.4674 + 0.6890}) = \tfrac12(-1.5708 + 1.7766) = 0.1029\) rad/ns \(= 2\pi\times16.4\) MHz. P10 The detuning medium

exercises: a one- and a two-parameter search, reading a first-order prediction against computation; ends in a number

  1. Show that adding \(c\,\Omega_x^2n/\alpha\) to \(\tilde H\) is the same as driving at the time-dependent frequency \(\omega - c\,\Omega_x^2/\alpha\), and find the \(c\) that would track the Stark shift exactly.
  2. At \(\lambda = \tfrac12\), search \(c\) in steps of \(0.01\) for the smallest score \(P_2 + (1 - P_1)\) for the 10 ns pulse. Report \(c\), \(P_2\), \(1 - P_1\).
  3. Search \((\lambda, c)\) together. Where is the best point, and how does it compare with the first-order line \(\lambda + c = \tfrac12\) and with the leakage-optimal \(\lambda \approx 1.1\)?
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 is the rotating-frame theorem with \(G\) replaced by the drive's own (changing) phase; parts 2 and 3 are loops over a grid, reusing the pulse code.

Rung 2

Driving at \(\omega + \Delta_d\) and computing in the frame of the drive replaces \(G = \omega n\) by \((\omega + \Delta_d)n\), which leaves \(-\Delta_dn\) in \(\tilde H\). For part 3 a grid of \(\lambda\) from \(0.5\) to \(0.95\) and \(c\) from \(-0.34\) to \(-0.10\) suffices.

Rung 3

First line of part 1: \(H_0 - (\omega + \Delta_d)n = \alpha\ket2\!\bra2 - \Delta_dn\), so \(c\,\Omega_x^2/\alpha = -\Delta_d\).

Worked solution

Idea. The Stark shift is a detuning that follows the pulse; a detuning of the drive that follows it too can cancel it — but the DRAG quadrature also acts on the qubit, and the two knobs must be tuned together.

1. Drive with phase \(\varphi(t) = \int_0^t(\omega + \Delta_d)\), i.e. \(H_d = (\Omega_x\cos\varphi + \Omega_y\sin\varphi)(b + b^\dagger)\), and change basis by \(e^{i\varphi(t)n}\). The rotating-frame proof goes through unchanged with \(\omega t\) replaced by \(\varphi(t)\): since \(n\) commutes with itself, \(\tfrac{d}{dt}e^{i\varphi n} = i\varphi'ne^{i\varphi n}\), so the frame term is \(-\varphi'n = -(\omega + \Delta_d)n\), and conjugation gives \(b \mapsto e^{-i\varphi}b\), so the drive terms come out as before with \(\omega t \to \varphi\). After the rotating-wave approximation the only change is the extra \(-\Delta_dn\) of rung 3. So the term \(c\,\Omega_x^2n/\alpha\) is a drive detuning \(\Delta_d = -c\,\Omega_x^2/\alpha\) (implemented as a phase ramp on the envelopes, since a frequency offset is a phase growing in time). The qubit moves up by \(\delta_S = -\Omega_x^2/2\alpha\) (P9), so tracking it needs \(\Delta_d = \delta_S\): \(c = \tfrac12\) — at \(\lambda = 0\). The first-order theorem says more generally that the miss vanishes on \(\lambda + c = \tfrac12\).

2. At \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) the best grid point is \(c = -0.03\): \(P_2 = 9.3\times10^{-5}\), \(1 - P_1 = 3.3\times10^{-4}\), score \(4.3\times10^{-4}\). (At \(c = 0\): score \(5.2\times10^{-4}\).) So half-strength DRAG alone removes almost all the miss — consistent with \(\lambda + c = \tfrac12\) — at the price of leakage about \(24\) times higher than at \(\lambda = 1\).

3. The coarse grid finds \((\lambda, c) = (0.70, -0.22)\), score \(3.7\times10^{-5}\); refining (a simplex search) gives \((0.706, -0.216)\) with \(P_2 = 1.39\times10^{-5}\), \(1 - P_1 = 1.40\times10^{-5}\), score \(2.8\times10^{-5}\). It lies on the first-order line (\(0.706 - 0.216 = 0.49\)), between \(\lambda = \tfrac12\) (best for the miss alone) and \(\lambda \approx 1.1\) (best for leakage alone). First order's own choice, \((1, -\tfrac12)\), scores \(1.3\times10^{-3}\): the detuning term also shifts \(\ket2\), a second-order effect that at \(\Omega/|\alpha| = 0.37\) is not small. The correction that minimises leakage is not the one that minimises the gate error (Gambetta, Motzoi, Merkel and Wilhelm, Phys. Rev. A 83, 012308, 2011).

Check. Every one-dimensional optimum (at \(\lambda = 0, \tfrac12, 1\): \(c = 0.45, -0.03, -0.47\)) has \(\lambda + c\) within \(0.05\) of \(\tfrac12\), the first-order prediction ✓. The best score is \(900\) times smaller than the plain Gaussian's \(0.026\).

P11 t/3T₁ medium

exercises: the average gate fidelity, the six-state lemma, amplitude damping on Bloch vectors; a proof that ends in a number

  1. Prove that the average of any polynomial of degree at most 2 in the Bloch vector over the sphere equals its average over \(\pm\hat x, \pm\hat y, \pm\hat z\).
  2. Prove that amplitude damping with \(\gamma = 1 - e^{-t/T_1}\) has average infidelity \(\tfrac{1 - \sqrt{1 - \gamma}}3 + \tfrac\gamma6\), and that this is \(t/3T_1\) to first order.
  3. Evaluate it for a 20 ns gate at \(T_1 = 100\ \mu\)s. Then check the result against the cited formula \(F_{\rm avg} = (2F_e + 1)/3\) with entanglement fidelity \(F_e = \sum_k|\operatorname{tr}K_k|^2/4\) for Kraus operators \(K_k\).
Hints · 3 rungs
Rung 1

Part 1 needs only symmetries of the sphere, checked on monomials. Part 2 is six fidelities and an average; part 3 is a number near \(7\times10^{-5}\).

Rung 2

Unit 21: amplitude damping sends \((x, y, z)\) to \((sx, sy, \gamma + (1 - \gamma)z)\), \(s = \sqrt{1 - \gamma}\). Unit 04: the fidelity of a pure state with Bloch vector \(\mathbf r\) and a state with Bloch vector \(\mathbf r'\) is \(\tfrac12(1 + \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r')\).

Rung 3

First line of part 2: at \(+\hat x\), \(\mathbf r' = (s, 0, \gamma)\), so the fidelity is \(\tfrac12(1 + s)\); the same at \(-\hat x, \pm\hat y\).

Worked solution

Idea. The fidelity of a pure input with its output is quadratic in the input's Bloch vector, and six well-placed points average quadratics exactly; amplitude damping then costs \(\gamma/4\) on each of the four equatorial inputs, \(\gamma\) on \(\ket1\), nothing on \(\ket0\).

1. Both averages are linear, so check the monomials \(1, r_i, r_ir_j\). \(1\): both \(1\). \(r_i\), and \(r_ir_j\) with \(i \ne j\): the reflection \(r_i \mapsto -r_i\) preserves the uniform measure and the six-point set, so both averages are \(0\). \(r_i^2\): on the sphere, permuting coordinates preserves the measure, so the three averages are equal and sum to \(\langle r_1^2 + r_2^2 + r_3^2\rangle = 1\): each \(\tfrac13\). On the six points \(r_1^2\) is \(1\) at two of them and \(0\) at four: \(\tfrac13\).

2. The integrand \(\operatorname{tr}\big(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\,\Phi(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi)\big)\) is bilinear in \(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)\), so of degree \(\le 2\) in \(\mathbf r\). The six fidelities: \(\pm\hat x, \pm\hat y\): \(\tfrac12(1 + s)\) each; \(+\hat z \mapsto (0, 0, 1)\): \(1\); \(-\hat z \mapsto (0, 0, 2\gamma - 1)\): \(\tfrac12(1 + 1 - 2\gamma) = 1 - \gamma\). Average \(\tfrac16[2(1 + s) + 2 - \gamma] = \tfrac{4 + 2s - \gamma}6\), so \(1 - F_{\rm avg} = \tfrac{2 - 2s + \gamma}{6} = \tfrac{1 - s}3 + \tfrac\gamma6\). With \(s = 1 - \tfrac\gamma2 + O(\gamma^2)\) and \(\gamma = t/T_1 + O(t^2)\): \(\tfrac\gamma6 + \tfrac\gamma6 = \tfrac{t}{3T_1}\) to first order.

3. \(t/T_1 = 20/100\,000 = 2\times10^{-4}\), \(\gamma = 1.9998\times10^{-4}\), \(s = e^{-t/2T_1} = 0.999900005\): \(1 - F_{\rm avg} = 6.6662\times10^{-5}\), against \(t/3T_1 = 6.6667\times10^{-5}\). Via \(F_e\): \(\operatorname{tr}K_0 = 1 + s\), \(\operatorname{tr}K_1 = 0\), so \(F_e = (1 + s)^2/4\) and \(1 - \tfrac{2F_e + 1}3 = \tfrac23\big(1 - \tfrac{(1 + s)^2}4\big) = \tfrac{3 - 2s - s^2}6 = \tfrac{2 - 2s + \gamma}{6}\), using \(s^2 = 1 - \gamma\): the same formula, hence the same \(6.6662\times10^{-5}\).

Check. \(\gamma = 0\): infidelity \(0\) ✓. \(\gamma = 1\): \(\tfrac13 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac12\), the infidelity of the channel that sends everything to \(\ket0\) ✓. The two routes agree exactly, not just to first order ✓.

Part 1 is a six-state average; part 2 solves a cubic \(T^3 = \dots\); part 3 is three numbers put in order, twice.

Rung 2

Average infidelity is unchanged by composing with a unitary (the measure is rotation-invariant), so compare \(e^{i\kappa Y}\) with \(I\). For part 2, \(\kappa = 10.13/(4|\alpha|T) = 1.612/T\) with \(T\) in ns.

Rung 3

First line of part 2: \(\tfrac23\big(\tfrac{1.612}{T}\big)^2 = \tfrac{T}{3T_1}\) gives \(T^3 = 2T_1(1.612)^2\).

Worked solution

Idea. The uncorrected control error falls as \(1/T^2\) and the \(T_1\) error rises as \(T\); they cross where the cube of the duration equals a constant set by \(\alpha\) and \(T_1\). Correcting the pulse moves the crossover far down.

1. \(F_{\rm avg}\) of \(R_x(\pi)e^{i\kappa Y}\) against \(R_x(\pi)\) equals that of \(e^{i\kappa Y}\) against \(I\), since the amplitude in the integrand is \(\braket{\psi|R_x(\pi)^\dagger R_x(\pi)e^{i\kappa Y}|\psi} = \braket{\psi|e^{i\kappa Y}|\psi}\). Six states: \(\pm\hat y\) are eigenvectors of \(Y\), fidelity \(1\); \(\pm\hat x, \pm\hat z\) are turned by \(2\kappa\) about \(\hat y\), fidelity \(\cos^2\kappa\). Average: \(\tfrac{2 + 4\cos^2\kappa}6\), so \(1 - F_{\rm avg} = \tfrac46\sin^2\kappa = \tfrac23\sin^2\kappa\).

2. With \(\sin\kappa \approx \kappa = 1.612/T\): control \(\approx 1.733/T^2\), and \(T_1\) error \(T/3T_1\). From rung 3, at \(T_1 = 10^5\) ns: \(T^3 = 2\times10^5\times2.599 = 5.20\times10^5\), \(T = 80.4\) ns. At \(T_1 = 2\times10^4\) ns: \(T^3 = 1.04\times10^5\), \(T = 47.0\) ns. Below the crossover the Stark miss dominates; above it \(T_1\) does. The crossover scales as \(T_1^{1/3}\): a five-times-worse qubit moves it only from 80 to 47 ns.

3. Uncorrected, 10 ns: Stark miss \(\tfrac23\times0.0258 = 1.7\times10^{-2}\) (average infidelity), leakage \(3.0\times10^{-4}\), \(T_1\) \(3.3\times10^{-5}\). Ranking: Stark shift \(\gg\) leakage \(\gg\) \(T_1\), by factors of about 60 and 9. Searched pulse: control \(1.4\times10^{-5}\) (nearly all of it leakage) against \(T_1\) \(3.3\times10^{-5}\) at 10 ns, and \(2.2\times10^{-6}\) against \(6.7\times10^{-5}\) at 20 ns: \(T_1\) now dominates. (At 10 ns this depends on the model: with a fourth level the searched control error is \(9.0\times10^{-5}\), above \(T_1\); at 20 ns three and four levels agree.)

Check. The formula against the computed average infidelities: \(1.733/T^2\) gives \(1.73\times10^{-2}\) at 10 ns and \(1.73\times10^{-4}\) at 100 ns; computed, \(1.72\times10^{-2}\) and \(1.73\times10^{-4}\) ✓. At the crossover, both errors are \(80.4/(3\times10^5) = 2.7\times10^{-4}\) — the computed curves cross between 80 and 90 ns at that height ✓. Dimensions: \(T_1(1.612)^2\) is ns\(\cdot\)ns\(^2\) ✓.
