the running example: a 10 ns Gaussian π-pulse

section 1

A ladder, not a qubit

A superconducting qubit is an electrical oscillator whose levels are almost, but not quite, evenly spaced. The qubit is the bottom two; nothing but the unevenness keeps the third away.

25 · the model

An oscillator on a chip

From nothing. A capacitor is two metal plates close together; charge $\pm Q$ on them stores energy $Q^2/2C$. A coil (inductor) resists changes of current $I$, storing $\tfrac12 LI^2$. Join them in a loop and the charge sloshes back and forth: the LC oscillator, angular frequency $\omega = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, exactly a mass on a spring.

Superconductivity. Some metals, aluminium among them, lose all electrical resistance below a critical temperature (1.2 K for aluminium). An oscillator made of them rings without losing energy to heat — the property a qubit needs.

The transmon replaces the coil by a Josephson junction: two superconductors separated by an insulating layer a few atoms thick. It behaves as a coil whose inductance depends on the current through it: a nonlinear oscillator. On a chip it is two metal pads about half a millimetre across, joined by the junction, cooled to about 10 mK (unit 26 says why).

Above, the LC oscillator. Below, a transmon: the pads are the capacitor, the junction (×) a nonlinear coil.

The analogy, exactly. In the LC loop, Kirchhoff's rule says the voltages round the loop sum to zero: $Q/C + L\,\dot I = 0$ with $I = \dot Q$, so $\ddot Q = -Q/LC$. That is a mass on a spring, $\ddot x = -(k/m)x$, with charge in place of position, $L$ in place of mass and $1/C$ in place of stiffness. The energy $Q^2/2C + \tfrac12L\dot Q^2$ is the spring's potential plus kinetic energy. All of this is classical electricity, and nothing in the rest of the deck depends on it beyond the word "oscillator".

Why a junction. A Josephson junction carries a current $I_c\sin\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is a phase difference between the two superconductors (Josephson, 1962). Expanded for small $\varphi$, $\sin\varphi \approx \varphi - \varphi^3/6$: a linear coil plus a correction. The correction is what makes the energy levels unevenly spaced. The derivation of the levels from this is circuit quantisation, and it is not done here (next slide).

The numbers. A transition at 5 GHz is microwave radiation, the band domestic Wi-Fi uses; its wavelength is $c/f = 6.0$ cm, much larger than the half-millimetre device, which is why it can be treated as a circuit rather than an antenna. One quantum is $hf = 3.31\times10^{-24}$ J, or $20.7\ \mu$eV; dividing by Boltzmann's constant gives $0.24$ K. At room temperature thermal agitation would excite it constantly; at 10 mK it essentially never does. Unit 26 makes that quantitative.

History. Superconductivity was discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911. Superconducting qubits began with the Cooper-pair box (Nakamura, Pashkin and Tsai, 1999); the transmon, a Cooper-pair box with a large shunting capacitor that makes it insensitive to stray charge, is Koch et al. 2007. It is the qubit in most superconducting machines today.

25 · the model

The transmon's ladder

the three-level model, ħ restored

On $\mathrm{span}\{\ket0, \ket1, \ket2\} \cong \mathbb C^3$, with $n = \mathrm{diag}(0, 1, 2)$,

$$H_0 = \hbar\omega\,n + \frac{\hbar\alpha}{2}\,n(n - 1),$$

levels $0$, $\hbar\omega$, $\hbar(2\omega + \alpha)$. The anharmonicity $\alpha$ is how much the second gap falls short of the first.

the ladder (cited)

A transmon's levels are those of an oscillator corrected to $E_n \approx \hbar\omega n + \tfrac{\hbar\alpha}{2}n(n - 1)$, with $\alpha \lt 0$ and $|\alpha| \ll \omega$. Koch et al., Phys. Rev. A 76, 042319 (2007), §II.

Typical: $\omega/2\pi \approx 5$ GHz, $\alpha/2\pi \approx -250$ MHz. Transitions $\omega_{n,n+1} = \omega + n\alpha$: $5.00$, $4.75$, $4.50$ GHz.

Evenly spaced, one frequency drives every rung. The transmon's gaps shrink by $|\alpha|$ per rung, so a drive at $5.00$ GHz is off-resonant for the next — by only $250$ MHz.

What is cited and why. Getting these levels means writing the circuit's energy as a function of a charge and a phase, promoting them to operators on $L^2$ of a circle, and solving the resulting eigenvalue problem (Mathieu's equation, in the transmon's case). That needs infinite-dimensional quantum mechanics, which this course deliberately excludes (unit 01's notes). So the ladder is taken as given; everything after it is three-dimensional linear algebra. Koch et al. find, in the transmon regime $E_J \gg E_C$, $\hbar\omega \approx \sqrt{8E_JE_C} - E_C$ and $\hbar\alpha \approx -E_C$, where $E_J$ and $E_C$ are the junction and charging energies; the anharmonicity is set by the size of the pads.

Optional cross-reference. Mechanics to Relativity's unit 21b solves the harmonic oscillator by ladder operators: levels $\hbar\omega(n + \tfrac12)$, evenly spaced, with $a\ket n = \sqrt n\ket{n - 1}$. The transmon is that ladder, bent. (The constant $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ is dropped here: a constant added to a Hamiltonian is a global phase.) Note that unit 19 of that course uses the opposite inner-product convention to this one.

Check the model. $H_0$ is diagonal and real, so Hermitian ✓. $E_0 = 0$; $E_1 = \hbar\omega$; $E_2 = 2\hbar\omega + \tfrac{\hbar\alpha}2\cdot2 = \hbar(2\omega + \alpha)$ ✓; $E_3 = \hbar(3\omega + 3\alpha)$, so the gaps are $\omega$, $\omega + \alpha$, $\omega + 2\alpha$ ✓. Dimensions: $\hbar$ times an angular frequency is an energy ✓.

Why stop at three levels. Leakage first reaches $\ket2$; $\ket3$ is reached only through $\ket2$ and is detuned by a further $2\alpha$. The truncation is checked at the end of section 3, and it matters more than one might hope for the 10 ns pulse.

25 · the model

The drive, and the √2

the truncated lowering operator

$b = \left(\begin{smallmatrix}0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & \sqrt2\\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{smallmatrix}\right)$: $\ b\ket1 = \ket0$, $b\ket2 = \sqrt2\ket1$, $b\ket0 = 0$.

Check: $b^\dagger b = \mathrm{diag}(0, 1, 2) = n$ ✓ — the oscillator's $b\ket k = \sqrt k\ket{k - 1}$, cut at $k = 2$.

the drive (cited form)

A microwave voltage on the pads couples through $b + b^\dagger$:

$$H_d = \hbar\,(\Omega_x\cos\omega t + \Omega_y\sin\omega t)(b + b^\dagger),$$

with slowly varying real envelopes $\Omega_x$ (in-phase) and $\Omega_y$ (quadrature). Koch et al. (2007), §III.

The matrix elements of $b + b^\dagger$. The leakage transition is driven $\sqrt2$ times harder than the qubit transition.

Where $b + b^\dagger$ comes from. The microwave line is coupled to the transmon through a small capacitor, so the drive adds a term proportional to the applied voltage times the transmon's charge operator. In the transmon regime the charge operator's matrix elements are, to leading order, those of an oscillator's $b + b^\dagger$: $\sqrt{k+1}$ between $\ket k$ and $\ket{k + 1}$, zero between levels two apart (Koch et al., §III, cited). Corrections of relative order $|\alpha|/\omega$ are neglected. The overall constant is absorbed into $\Omega_x$ and $\Omega_y$, which is why they are called Rabi frequencies: the next section shows $\Omega_x$ is unit 02's $\Omega$ on the bottom two levels.

Check. $b^\dagger$ is the transpose of $b$ (real entries), with $b^\dagger\ket0 = \ket1$ and $b^\dagger\ket1 = \sqrt2\ket2$. Then $b^\dagger b\ket1 = b^\dagger\ket0 = \ket1$ and $b^\dagger b\ket2 = b^\dagger\sqrt2\ket1 = 2\ket2$ ✓. $b + b^\dagger$ is real symmetric, hence Hermitian, as a term in a Hamiltonian must be ✓. Its eigenvalues are $0, \pm\sqrt3$ (characteristic polynomial $-\lambda^3 + 3\lambda$), so $\lVert b + b^\dagger\rVert = \sqrt3$, a number section 2 uses.

A trap. For the untruncated oscillator, $[b, b^\dagger] = I$. For the truncated $b$ it fails: $[b, b^\dagger] = \mathrm{diag}(1, 1, -2)$. Nothing below uses the commutator, but formulas from oscillator texts that do should not be copied over.

"In-phase" and "quadrature". Two signals at the same frequency a quarter-period apart, $\cos\omega t$ and $\sin\omega t$, are the two quadratures; any signal near $\omega$ is a combination $\Omega_x\cos\omega t + \Omega_y\sin\omega t = \Omega\cos(\omega t - \varphi)$ with $\Omega_x = \Omega\cos\varphi$, $\Omega_y = \Omega\sin\varphi$. Microwave electronics generates the two envelopes separately and mixes them up to 5 GHz. Section 4 uses $\Omega_y$.

25 · time dependence

A Hamiltonian that changes in time

Unit 01's Stone theorem gives $e^{-iHt}$ for a fixed $H$. A pulse makes $H$ change. Over a short step in which $H$ barely moves, the evolution should be $e^{-iH(t)\Delta t}$; in the limit, a differential equation.

evolution under a time-dependent Hamiltonian

For continuous Hermitian $H(t)$, the propagator $U(t, s)$ is the solution of

$$i\,\partial_tU(t, s) = H(t)\,U(t, s),\qquad U(s, s) = I,$$

and the state evolves as $\ket{\psi(t)} = U(t, s)\ket{\psi(s)}$. With $\hbar = 1$ from here on: $H$ in rad/ns, $t$ in ns.

Unitary: $\partial_t(U^\dagger U) = (-iHU)^\dagger U + U^\dagger(-iHU) = iU^\dagger HU - iU^\dagger HU = 0$ ✓. Composes: $U(t, r)U(r, s) = U(t, s)$, by uniqueness.

The running pulse's envelope, and a ten-step staircase. Each step is a fixed Hamiltonian, evolved exactly by unit 01; the next slide bounds the error.

Existence and uniqueness (cited). $i\partial_tU = H(t)U$ is a linear ODE on the finite-dimensional space of matrices with continuous coefficients, so it has exactly one solution on all of $[s, T]$ (the Picard–Lindelöf theorem for linear systems; e.g. Hirsch, Smale and Devaney, Differential Equations, Dynamical Systems, ch. 17). The columns of $U(t, s)$ are the solutions started from the basis vectors.

Composition. Fix $r$. Both $t \mapsto U(t, r)U(r, s)$ and $t \mapsto U(t, s)$ solve the equation and agree at $t = r$, so they agree everywhere. This is what lets the next slide split the evolution into steps.

Is this a new postulate? Unit 01's evolution postulate concerned a closed system whose surroundings do not change, and Stone's theorem turned it into $e^{-iHt}$. A driven system is not closed — the microwave source is outside it — and the time-dependent equation is the natural extension: for a fixed $H$ it reduces to unit 01's, and over a short step it is unit 01's to first order. The next slide makes "to first order" precise. Unit 02's notes promised exactly this justification.

The figure. The envelope is the running example's, $\Omega_x(t) = 0.679\big[e^{-(t-5)^2/12.5} - e^{-2}\big]$ rad/ns; the staircase holds it at $t = 0.5, 1.5, \dots, 9.5$ ns. The deck's numbers use 500 to 8000 steps, not 10.

25 · time dependence

Short constant steps converge

lemma: two evolutions

If $iU' = HU$, $iW' = KW$, $U(0) = W(0) = I$:

$$\lVert U(t) - W(t)\rVert \le \int_0^t \lVert H(s) - K(s)\rVert\,ds .$$
proof

$(W^\dagger U)' = iW^\dagger KU - iW^\dagger HU = -iW^\dagger(H - K)U$. Integrate; $W$, $U$ unitary have norm 1; and $\lVert U - W\rVert = \lVert W^\dagger U - I\rVert$. ∎

theorem: piecewise-constant evolution

Let $M = \max\lVert H'(t)\rVert$, $\Delta t = T/N$, $t_k = (k - \tfrac12)\Delta t$. Then

$$\Big\lVert U(T, 0) - \prod_{k = N}^{1} e^{-iH(t_k)\Delta t}\Big\rVert \le \frac{MT\,\Delta t}{4}.$$
proof

Split $U(T, 0) = U_N\cdots U_1$ into steps. On step $k$, the lemma with $K = H(t_k)$ gives $\lVert U_k - e^{-iH(t_k)\Delta t}\rVert \le \int M|t - t_k|\,dt = M\Delta t^2/4$. For unitaries, $\lVert A_N\cdots A_1 - B_N\cdots B_1\rVert \le \sum\lVert A_k - B_k\rVert$ (telescope). $N$ steps: $NM\Delta t^2/4$. ∎

The pulse: $M = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\max|\Omega_x'| = 0.143$ rad/ns²; at $N = 4000$ the bound is $8.9\times10^{-4}$.

honest, not sharp

Midpoints do far better: 500 and 8000 steps agree on $P_2$ to $5\times10^{-8}$. The deck's numbers rest on that check, not on the bound.

Full proof of the lemma. $(W^\dagger)' = (W')^\dagger = (-iKW)^\dagger = iW^\dagger K$, using $K^\dagger = K$. So $(W^\dagger U)' = iW^\dagger KU + W^\dagger(-iHU) = -iW^\dagger(H - K)U$. Integrating from $0$, where $W^\dagger U = I$: $W^\dagger(t)U(t) - I = -i\int_0^tW^\dagger(H - K)U\,ds$. The operator norm satisfies $\lVert AB\rVert \le \lVert A\rVert\lVert B\rVert$ (unit 00) and $\lVert\int F\rVert \le \int\lVert F\rVert$, and a unitary has norm 1. Finally $U - W = W(W^\dagger U - I)$, and multiplying by a unitary does not change the norm.

The step estimate. On step $k$, $t \in [t_k - \Delta t/2, t_k + \Delta t/2]$, and by the mean value inequality $\lVert H(t) - H(t_k)\rVert \le M|t - t_k|$. Then $\int|t - t_k|\,dt = 2\int_0^{\Delta t/2}u\,du = \Delta t^2/4$. The lemma applies to a step starting at time $t_k - \Delta t/2$ by shifting the clock.

The telescope. $A_N\cdots A_1 - B_N\cdots B_1 = \sum_k A_N\cdots A_{k+1}(A_k - B_k)B_{k-1}\cdots B_1$ — expand and watch adjacent terms cancel — and each product of unitaries has norm 1.

Consequences. The error falls like $1/N$, so the product converges to $U(T, 0)$: this is the time-ordered product, often written $\mathcal T\exp\big(-i\int H\big)$, defined as the limit. The order matters — later times stand to the left — because $H(t)$ at different times need not commute. When they do commute (all $H(t)$ multiples of one matrix), the product is $e^{-i\int H}$ exactly; the slide On two levels: unit 02's drive uses that.

Why midpoints beat the bound. The midpoint rule's first-order errors cancel between the two halves of each step, so its true error is $O(\Delta t^2)$ — the same reason the midpoint rule for integrals is second order. That improvement is not proved here; the deck relies on the convergence check instead. Unit 19's Trotter bounds are the same kind of estimate for a sum of non-commuting terms (unit 19 is not needed here).

Numbers. $\max|\Omega_x'|$ for a Gaussian of width $\sigma$ is $A/(\sigma\sqrt e)$, at $t = T/2 \pm \sigma$: $0.6788/(2.5\times1.6487) = 0.1647$ rad/ns². $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\times0.1647 = 0.1426$. With $T = 10$, $\Delta t = 0.0025$: $0.1426\times10\times0.0025/4 = 8.9\times10^{-4}$. Problem P3 asks for the $N$ that guarantees $10^{-6}$.

25 · the rotating frame

Turning with the drive

the rotating frame

For Hermitian $G$, put $\ket{\tilde\psi} = e^{iGt}\ket\psi$. If $i\partial_t\ket\psi = H(t)\ket\psi$, then

$$i\,\partial_t\ket{\tilde\psi} = \tilde H(t)\ket{\tilde\psi},$$ $$\tilde H = e^{iGt}He^{-iGt} - G .$$
proof

$i\partial_t\big(e^{iGt}\psi\big) = i(iG)e^{iGt}\psi + e^{iGt}H\psi = \big(-G + e^{iGt}He^{-iGt}\big)e^{iGt}\psi$. ∎

The $-G$ is the price of differentiating a changing basis. Here $G = \omega n$, so $e^{i\omega nt} = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\omega t}, e^{2i\omega t})$: diagonal, so populations are the same in both frames. On two levels it is unit 02's $R_z(-\omega t)^\dagger$, up to global phase.

Subtracting $\omega n$ puts $\ket0$ and $\ket1$ level with each other: the drive is on resonance. $\ket2$ is left at $\alpha$, the leakage transition's detuning.

Every step. The product rule gives $\partial_t(e^{iGt}\ket\psi) = iGe^{iGt}\ket\psi + e^{iGt}\partial_t\ket\psi$, and $\partial_t\ket\psi = -iH\ket\psi$. Multiply by $i$: $-Ge^{iGt}\ket\psi + e^{iGt}H\ket\psi$. Insert $I = e^{-iGt}e^{iGt}$ after $H$ to get the slide. $G$ commutes with $e^{iGt}$, which was used in writing $Ge^{iGt}$. The new Hamiltonian is Hermitian: conjugating by a unitary preserves Hermiticity, and $G$ is Hermitian.

Populations. $\braket{k|\tilde\psi} = e^{i\omega kt}\braket{k|\psi}$, so $|\braket{k|\tilde\psi}|^2 = |\braket{k|\psi}|^2$: the probability of finding level $k$ is the same in both frames at every instant. So the rotating frame may be used to compute every population in this deck. Phases between levels are not the same: the lab state's relative phases turn at $\omega$.

Comparison with unit 02. There, $\tilde\psi = V^\dagger\psi$ with $V = R_z(-\omega t) = \mathrm{diag}(e^{i\omega t/2}, e^{-i\omega t/2})$. Then $V^\dagger = e^{-i\omega t/2}\mathrm{diag}(1, e^{i\omega t})$, which is this slide's $e^{i\omega nt}$ restricted to two levels, times a global phase. The theorem here is unit 02's derivation done once in general; unit 02's $-i\hbar V^\dagger\dot V$ is this $-G$.

Worked: the transmon in the rotating frame

the three-level transmon under the drive, every term; $G = \omega n$

1. The bare part. $n$ commutes with $e^{i\omega nt}$, so $H_0$ is unchanged, and

$$H_0 - \omega n = \tfrac\alpha2 n(n - 1) = \alpha\ket2\!\bra2 .$$

2. The ladder. On $\ket k$: $e^{i\omega nt}be^{-i\omega nt}\ket k = e^{-i\omega kt}\sqrt k\,e^{i\omega(k-1)t}\ket{k-1}$, so

$$e^{i\omega nt}\,b\,e^{-i\omega nt} = e^{-i\omega t}b,$$

and its adjoint: $b^\dagger \mapsto e^{i\omega t}b^\dagger$.

3. The products. $\cos\omega t\,e^{\mp i\omega t} = \tfrac12(1 + e^{\mp2i\omega t})$; $\ \sin\omega t\,e^{-i\omega t} = \tfrac1{2i}(1 - e^{-2i\omega t})$; $\ \sin\omega t\,e^{i\omega t} = \tfrac1{2i}(e^{2i\omega t} - 1)$.

4. Collect. Exactly,

$$\begin{aligned}\tilde H &= \alpha\ket2\!\bra2 + \tfrac{\Omega_x}{2}(b + b^\dagger) + \tfrac{\Omega_y}{2}(ib^\dagger - ib)\\ &\quad + \tfrac{\Omega_x}{2}\big(e^{-2i\omega t}b + e^{2i\omega t}b^\dagger\big)\\ &\quad + \tfrac{\Omega_y}{2}\big(ie^{-2i\omega t}b - ie^{2i\omega t}b^\dagger\big).\end{aligned}$$

The $\Omega_y$ term: $\tfrac{1}{2i}(b - b^\dagger) = \tfrac12(ib^\dagger - ib)$.

5. Check. Hermitian: $(ib^\dagger - ib)^\dagger = -ib + ib^\dagger$ ✓, and $(ie^{-2i\omega t}b)^\dagger = -ie^{2i\omega t}b^\dagger$ ✓. With $\Omega_x = \Omega_y = 0$: $\alpha\ket2\!\bra2$, the bare levels in the turning frame ✓.

Step 2 in full. $e^{-i\omega nt}\ket k = e^{-i\omega kt}\ket k$; then $b$ gives $\sqrt k\,e^{-i\omega kt}\ket{k - 1}$; then $e^{i\omega nt}$ multiplies by $e^{i\omega(k-1)t}$. Net factor $e^{-i\omega t}$ for every $k$, including $k = 0$ where both sides vanish. Taking adjoints gives the $b^\dagger$ line: $(e^{-i\omega t}b)^\dagger = e^{i\omega t}b^\dagger$.

Step 4 in full. The drive in the rotating frame is $(\Omega_x\cos\omega t + \Omega_y\sin\omega t)(e^{-i\omega t}b + e^{i\omega t}b^\dagger)$. The $\Omega_x$ part: $\tfrac{\Omega_x}2\big[(1 + e^{-2i\omega t})b + (1 + e^{2i\omega t})b^\dagger\big]$. The $\Omega_y$ part: $\tfrac{\Omega_y}{2i}\big[(1 - e^{-2i\omega t})b + (e^{2i\omega t} - 1)b^\dagger\big] = \tfrac{\Omega_y}{2i}(b - b^\dagger) + \tfrac{\Omega_y}{2i}(-e^{-2i\omega t}b + e^{2i\omega t}b^\dagger)$, and $\tfrac1{2i} = -\tfrac i2$ turns these into $\tfrac{\Omega_y}2(ib^\dagger - ib)$ and $\tfrac{\Omega_y}2(ie^{-2i\omega t}b - ie^{2i\omega t}b^\dagger)$.

What has been assumed. Nothing yet: this $\tilde H$ is exact for the three-level model with the drive as written. The next slide makes the one approximation.

A sign convention. Writing the drive as $\Omega\cos(\omega t - \varphi)$ makes $\Omega_y = \Omega\sin\varphi$ and gives the $+\Omega_y Y/2$ on the qubit that the next slides show. Writing $\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$ instead flips the sign of $\Omega_y$ throughout. DRAG's sign in section 4 is stated for this deck's convention.

The rotating-wave approximation, with three levels

the rotating-wave approximation

Drop the terms in $e^{\pm2i\omega t}$. Justified when

$$|\Omega_x|,\ |\Omega_y|,\ |\alpha| \ll \omega ,$$

with envelopes varying slowly on the scale $1/\omega$: unit 02's condition, now with $\alpha$ in it.

the model from here on $$\begin{aligned}\tilde H(t) = \alpha\ket2\!\bra2 &+ \tfrac{\Omega_x(t)}{2}(b + b^\dagger)\\ &+ \tfrac{\Omega_y(t)}{2}(ib^\dagger - ib).\end{aligned}$$

Its price is unit 02's Bloch–Siegert shift, $\approx\Omega^2/4\omega$ (cited there). At the pulse's peak: $2\pi\times0.44$ MHz, forty times smaller than the shift section 4 finds.

Kept terms change on the scale $1/\Omega$ and $1/|\alpha|$; dropped ones turn forty to a hundred times faster and average away.

Why it works, and what is not proved. Over one period $\pi/\omega = 0.1$ ns of the dropped terms, the state barely moves (it changes on the scale $1/\Omega \approx 1.7$ ns), so each dropped term's push averages to nearly zero. Making that quantitative is averaging theory; the first correction is the Bloch–Siegert shift, cited in unit 02 (Bloch and Siegert 1940; Shirley 1965) and checked numerically there. It is not proved here either.

Leakage is a Fourier coefficient

Write $\ket{\tilde\psi} = c_0\ket0 + c_1\ket1 + c_2\ket2$. The third row of $\tilde H$ is exactly

$$i\dot c_2 = \alpha\,c_2 + g(t)\,c_1,\qquad g = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(\Omega_x + i\Omega_y),$$

since $\ket2$ couples only to $\ket1$.

theorem: the leakage amplitude

If $c_2(0) = 0$, then exactly

$$c_2(T) = -i\,e^{-i\alpha T}\int_0^T e^{i\alpha t}\,g(t)\,c_1(t)\,dt .$$
proof

$\tfrac{d}{dt}\big(e^{i\alpha t}c_2\big) = e^{i\alpha t}(\dot c_2 + i\alpha c_2) = -ie^{i\alpha t}gc_1$. Integrate from $0$. ∎

Read it. The leakage amplitude is $\int e^{i\alpha t}f\,dt$, the Fourier transform of $f = gc_1$ evaluated at the frequency $\alpha$. A function that varies slowly compared with $1/|\alpha|$ has a small Fourier transform at $\alpha$: the oscillating factor cancels it against itself.

So leakage is about smoothness. Where $f$ is smooth, its contributions cancel. Where it is not — and the pulse's envelope has a kink at each end — they do not.

exact, but not closed

$c_1(t)$ is not known in closed form; the formula is exact given it. The next slide estimates it by integrating by parts, which needs only $c_1$'s size at the ends.

The third row. $\tilde H$'s third row is $\big(0,\ \tfrac{\Omega_x + i\Omega_y}{\sqrt2},\ \alpha\big)$ (Worked: the √2, and the detuning α), and $i\dot{\mathbf c} = \tilde H\mathbf c$ gives $i\dot c_2 = \tfrac{\Omega_x + i\Omega_y}{\sqrt2}c_1 + \alpha c_2$. No approximation beyond the rotating-wave approximation and the three-level truncation, both already made.

The proof in full. $\dot c_2 = -i\alpha c_2 - igc_1$, so $\dot c_2 + i\alpha c_2 = -igc_1$. Multiply by $e^{i\alpha t}$, which turns the left side into $\tfrac{d}{dt}(e^{i\alpha t}c_2)$. Integrate over $[0, T]$ with $c_2(0) = 0$ and multiply by $e^{-i\alpha T}$. This is the variation-of-constants formula for a first-order linear ODE.

Why it matters. It turns "how much leaks" into a question about one function's frequency content. Every rule of thumb about leakage — smooth pulses, long pulses, spectral width against anharmonicity — is a statement about Fourier transforms, and this formula is where they come from. It is also the starting point of DRAG in section 4: choose $\Omega_y$ so that the leading term of the integral vanishes.

The rule of thumb, from the Fourier formula: a pulse of width $\sigma$ has spectral width about $1/\sigma$. It leaks badly when $1/\sigma$ reaches $|\alpha|$ — at 4 ns, $1/\sigma = 1$ rad/ns against $|\alpha| = 1.57$ — and gently otherwise, through its tails.

is three levels enough?

Add $\ket3$ (coupling $\sqrt3$, at $3\alpha$). At 20 ns the leakage barely moves: $2.70 \to 2.71\times10^{-5}$. At 10 ns it moves a lot: $3.0\times10^{-4} \to 8.4\times10^{-5}$, while $1 - P_1$ goes from $2.56\%$ to $2.69\%$.

So the running example's $3.0\times10^{-4}$ is the three-level model's number. The trends and the miss survive; at 10 ns the leakage itself is uncertain by a factor of about $3.5$.

What the figure is, and is not. It is the squared Fourier transform of the envelope alone, normalised to $1$ at $\Delta = 0$ (where it is the area, $\pi$). The exact leakage amplitude is the transform of $gc_1$, not of $\Omega_x$, so the figure is a picture of the mechanism, not the computation. At $|\Delta|/2\pi = 250$ MHz it gives $0.194$ (4 ns) and $4.97\times10^{-5}$ (10 ns). The deep notches are zeros of the transform; the side lobes beyond them, falling only as a power of $\Delta$, are the kinks' doing — a Gaussian without kinks would fall as $e^{-\Delta^2\sigma^2/2}$.

The truncation check in full. The four-level model has $H_0/\hbar - \omega n = \mathrm{diag}(0, 0, \alpha, 3\alpha)$ and $b\ket3 = \sqrt3\ket2$. At 10 ns: $P_2 + P_3 = 8.41\times10^{-5}$ ($P_3 = 1.9\times10^{-9}$), and $1 - P_1 = 0.02692$. Five levels change these by less than 1%. At 20 ns: $2.709\times10^{-5}$; at 40 ns: $1.7221\times10^{-6}$ against $1.7219\times10^{-6}$. So the fourth level matters only where $\Omega/|\alpha|$ is not small: it shifts $\ket2$ by an amount of order $\Omega^2/\alpha$ (the next section's mechanism, one rung up), changing the interference that sets the leakage.

What else the model leaves out, named not developed: the transmon's real matrix elements differ from $\sqrt k$ by corrections of order $|\alpha|/\omega$; the higher levels' anharmonicity is not exactly constant; and the pulse a device emits is distorted by the wiring on its way to the chip. Calibration on the device, not this model, fixes the final numbers. What the model is for is the mechanism and the order of magnitude.

DRAG, and the Stark shift

Leakage is a Fourier coefficient; a second quadrature can cancel its leading term. It does — and the $2.6\%$ miss barely moves, because the miss was never leakage. It is the drive shifting the qubit's own frequency.

25 · DRAG

DRAG, derived

Add a quadrature $\Omega_y$, small (of order $1/\alpha$). It enters $g = (\Omega_x + i\Omega_y)/\sqrt2$, so the leakage integral gains $\int e^{i\alpha t}\,i\Omega_yc_1/\sqrt2$. One integration by parts:

$$\int_0^Te^{i\alpha t}\frac{i\Omega_yc_1}{\sqrt2}dt = \frac1\alpha\Big[e^{i\alpha t}\frac{\Omega_yc_1}{\sqrt2}\Big]_0^T + O(\alpha^{-3}).$$

With the $\Omega_x$ term of the last section, the leading boundary term at $T$ is

$$\frac{e^{i\alpha T}c_1(T)}{\sqrt2\,\alpha^2}\big(\Omega_x'(T) + \alpha\,\Omega_y(T)\big).$$
DRAG (Motzoi, Gambetta, Rebentrost and Wilhelm, 2009) $$\Omega_y(t) = -\lambda\,\frac{\Omega_x'(t)}{\alpha},\qquad \lambda = 1$$

cancels the leading leakage for every envelope vanishing at its ends: the term becomes $(1 - \lambda)\,\Omega_x'(T)$.

The running pulse with DRAG: the quadrature is the envelope's slope over $|\alpha|$, peaking at $2\pi\times16.7$ MHz.

the sign, for α < 0

$\Omega_y = +\Omega_x'/|\alpha|$: positive while the pulse rises. Papers differ by signs and factors of 2 in how they write the drive and $\alpha$; this is the sign for this deck's $\tilde H$.

Full derivation. From Leakage is a Fourier coefficient, $c_2(T) = -ie^{-i\alpha T}\int_0^Te^{i\alpha t}gc_1\,dt$ with $g = (\Omega_x + i\Omega_y)/\sqrt2$. Split $gc_1 = f_x + f_y$, $f_x = \Omega_xc_1/\sqrt2$ and $f_y = i\Omega_yc_1/\sqrt2$. The previous slide gave $\int e^{i\alpha t}f_x = \tfrac1{\alpha^2}[e^{i\alpha t}f_x']_0^T + O(\alpha^{-3})$. For $f_y$, one integration by parts: $\int e^{i\alpha t}f_y = [\tfrac{e^{i\alpha t}}{i\alpha}f_y]_0^T - \tfrac1{i\alpha}\int e^{i\alpha t}f_y'$, and since $f_y = O(1/\alpha)$ the last term is $O(\alpha^{-3})$. The bracket is $\tfrac1\alpha[e^{i\alpha t}\Omega_yc_1/\sqrt2]_0^T$. At $t = 0$ everything vanishes because $c_1(0) = 0$. At $t = T$, $f_x'(T) = \Omega_x'(T)c_1(T)/\sqrt2$. Adding: $\tfrac{e^{i\alpha T}c_1(T)}{\sqrt2\alpha^2}\big(\Omega_x'(T) + \alpha\Omega_y(T)\big)$, and with $\Omega_y = -\lambda\Omega_x'/\alpha$ the bracket is $(1 - \lambda)\Omega_x'(T)$. So at $\lambda = 1$ the $O(\alpha^{-2})$ amplitude is gone, and leakage starts at a higher order.

The frame-change view (Motzoi et al.'s own). Change basis by $e^{S(t)}$ with $S = \tfrac{\Omega_x}{\sqrt2\alpha}(\ket2\!\bra1 - \ket1\!\bra2)$, anti-Hermitian, small, and zero at both ends of the pulse (so populations at the end are unchanged). To first order the new Hamiltonian is $\tilde H + [S, \tilde H] + i\dot S$. The commutator with $\alpha\ket2\!\bra2$ cancels the $\Omega_x$ part of the $\ket1$–$\ket2$ coupling; but differentiating the moving frame adds $i\dot S = \tfrac{\Omega_x'}{\sqrt2\alpha}(i\ket2\!\bra1 - i\ket1\!\bra2)$, a new coupling of exactly the form of the $\Omega_y$ term's $\ket1$–$\ket2$ part, $\tfrac{\Omega_y}{\sqrt2}(i\ket2\!\bra1 - i\ket1\!\bra2)$. The two cancel iff $\Omega_y = -\Omega_x'/\alpha$: in that frame $\ket2$ is decoupled to first order, and since the frame is the identity at the ends, nothing leaks at that order. The integration-by-parts argument above is the same expansion in $1/\alpha$, done on the amplitude.

Numbers. $\max|\Omega_x'| = 0.1647$ rad/ns², so $\max|\Omega_y| = 0.1647/1.5708 = 0.1048$ rad/ns $= 2\pi\times16.7$ MHz. At the ends, $|\Omega_y| = 0.0735/1.5708 = 0.0468$ rad/ns: the quadrature switches on and off with a jump, which is harmless at this order.

25 · the Stark shift

The drive moves the qubit

Freeze the drive at $\Omega$ and look at $\ket1$, $\ket2$: the block $\begin{pmatrix}0 & \Omega/\sqrt2\\ \Omega/\sqrt2 & \alpha\end{pmatrix}$, eigenvalues $\tfrac12\big(\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 + 2\Omega^2}\big)$. The one that starts at $0$ (for $\alpha \lt 0$, the $+$ root) is

$$\begin{aligned}E_1(\Omega) &= \tfrac{|\alpha|}{2}\Big(\sqrt{1 + 2\Omega^2/\alpha^2} - 1\Big)\\ &= \frac{\Omega^2}{2|\alpha|} + O\big(\Omega^4/|\alpha|^3\big).\end{aligned}$$
the AC Stark shift

$\ket0$ is not coupled to $\ket2$, so it does not move. The qubit's frequency rises by

$$\delta_S(t) = \frac{\Omega_x(t)^2}{2|\alpha|} = -\frac{\Omega_x(t)^2}{2\alpha}.$$

Levels repel: $\ket2$, below $\ket1$ in this frame, pushes it up. At the pulse's peak: $2\pi\times17.5$ MHz, $19\%$ of $\Omega$.

Eigenvalues of the $\ket1$–$\ket2$ block as the drive grows. The qubit level climbs by $\Omega^2/2|\alpha|$; the drive, fixed at $\omega$, is left below resonance.

The eigenvalues. The block's trace is $\alpha$ and determinant $-\Omega^2/2$, so its eigenvalues solve $\lambda^2 - \alpha\lambda - \Omega^2/2 = 0$: $\lambda = \tfrac12(\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 + 2\Omega^2})$. For $\alpha \lt 0$, $\sqrt{\alpha^2 + 2\Omega^2} = |\alpha|\sqrt{1 + 2\Omega^2/\alpha^2}$, and the $+$ root is $\tfrac12(\alpha + |\alpha|\sqrt{\cdots}) = \tfrac{|\alpha|}2(\sqrt{\cdots} - 1)$, using $\alpha = -|\alpha|$. Then $\sqrt{1 + u} = 1 + u/2 - u^2/8 + \dots$ with $u = 2\Omega^2/\alpha^2$ gives $\tfrac{|\alpha|}2\big(\tfrac{\Omega^2}{\alpha^2} - \tfrac{\Omega^4}{2\alpha^4}\big) + \dots = \Omega^2/2|\alpha| - \Omega^4/4|\alpha|^3 + \dots$. At $\Omega = 0.5869$: exact $0.1029$ rad/ns, second order $0.1097$, fourth order $0.1020$. Check $\Omega = 0$: $0$ ✓.

Why freezing the drive is fair, and what it hides. The drive changes on the scale of nanoseconds; the $\ket1$–$\ket2$ mixing responds on the scale $1/|\alpha| = 0.64$ ns. That separation is what makes the frozen-drive picture the leading order. The honest statement is an effective Hamiltonian on the qubit: change basis by the $e^{S}$ of the last slide's notes, and to order $1/\alpha$ the qubit block of the new Hamiltonian is

$$\tfrac12\Omega_xX + \tfrac12\Omega_yY - \tfrac{\Omega_x^2}{2\alpha}\ket1\!\bra1 .$$

Full proof of that block. With $V_{\rm od} = \tfrac{\Omega_x}{\sqrt2}(\ket2\!\bra1 + \ket1\!\bra2)$ and $S = \tfrac{\Omega_x}{\sqrt2\alpha}(\ket2\!\bra1 - \ket1\!\bra2)$, the second-order terms of $e^S\tilde He^{-S}$ that survive are $\tfrac12[S, V_{\rm od}] + [S, V_{\rm q}]$, where $V_{\rm q}$ is the qubit coupling. Using $[\ket2\!\bra1 - \ket1\!\bra2,\ \ket2\!\bra1 + \ket1\!\bra2] = 2(\ket2\!\bra2 - \ket1\!\bra1)$: $\tfrac12[S, V_{\rm od}] = \tfrac{\Omega_x^2}{2\alpha}(\ket2\!\bra2 - \ket1\!\bra1)$ — the shift of $\ket1$ by $-\Omega_x^2/2\alpha$ and of $\ket2$ by the opposite. $[S, V_{\rm q}]$ couples $\ket0$ to $\ket2$ only (a two-photon term, detuned by $\alpha$), which touches the qubit block at the next order. The $i\dot S$ term couples $\ket1$ to $\ket2$ only. So the qubit block is as written.

Name and history. "AC Stark shift": Johannes Stark found in 1913 that a static electric field shifts atomic spectral lines; an oscillating field near a transition does too, and the shift's sign depends on which side of the transition the drive sits. In atomic physics it is also called the light shift (Autler and Townes, 1955).

Why the π-pulse misses

The qubit model to order 1/α, with a detuning knob $c$ (next slide) added as $c\,\tfrac{\Omega_x^2}{\alpha}\,n$:

$$\tilde H_{\rm q} = \tfrac12\Omega_xX + \underbrace{\tfrac12\Omega_yY + \big(c - \tfrac12\big)\tfrac{\Omega_x^2}{\alpha}\ket1\!\bra1}_{H_{\rm err}}$$

with $\Omega_y = -\lambda\Omega_x'/\alpha$.

theorem: the first-order miss

For a symmetric envelope of area $\pi$, with $\Theta(t) = \int_0^t\Omega_x$ and $I_s = \int_0^T\Omega_x^2\sin\Theta\,dt$,

$$1 - P_1 \approx \kappa^2,\qquad \kappa = \big(\lambda + c - \tfrac12\big)\frac{I_s}{2\alpha}.$$

Proof idea: in the frame turning with $R_x(\Theta)$, the error generator is $K = \int R_x(\Theta)^\dagger H_{\rm err}R_x(\Theta)\,dt$; both parts integrate (by parts) to multiples of $I_sY$, with total coefficient $-\kappa$. Full proof in the notes.

The Stark shift tilts the rotation axis while the pulse is on: the state ends $2|\kappa|$ from the pole, and $\sin^2\kappa$ stays behind.

Check, 10 ns: $I_s = 1.013$ rad/ns, so at $\lambda = c = 0$, $|\kappa| = 1.013/(4\times1.571) = 0.161$: $\sin^2\kappa = 2.58\%$, exact $2.56\%$ ✓. At $\lambda = 1$, $|\lambda - \tfrac12|$ is the same: $2.58\%$ again, exact $2.81\%$ — DRAG cannot help the miss.

Full proof. (i) The frame. Write $U(t) = R_x(\Theta(t))V(t)$. Then $iV' = H_I(t)V$ with $H_I = R_x(\Theta)^\dagger H_{\rm err}R_x(\Theta)$ (the rotating-frame theorem, with the time-dependent change of basis $R_x(\Theta)^\dagger$ in place of $e^{iGt}$; the frame term cancels $\tfrac12\Omega_xX$). (ii) First order. $V(T) - I = -i\int H_IV$, and by the lemma two evolutions, $\lVert V - I\rVert \le \int\lVert H_I\rVert$; so $\lVert V(T) - (I - iK)\rVert \le \int\lVert H_I(t)\rVert\int_0^t\lVert H_I\rVert \le \tfrac12\big(\int\lVert H_{\rm err}\rVert\big)^2$, with $K = \int H_I$. (iii) Conjugation. $R_x(\Theta)^\dagger YR_x(\Theta) = \cos\Theta\,Y - \sin\Theta\,Z$ and $R_x(\Theta)^\dagger ZR_x(\Theta) = \cos\Theta\,Z + \sin\Theta\,Y$ (from $ZX = iY$, $YX = -iZ$). Write $\ket1\!\bra1 = \tfrac12(I - Z)$ and drop $I$ (global phase); put $\delta = (c - \tfrac12)\Omega_x^2/\alpha$. Then $K = \int\big[\tfrac{\Omega_y}2(\cos\Theta\,Y - \sin\Theta\,Z) - \tfrac\delta2(\cos\Theta\,Z + \sin\Theta\,Y)\big]dt$. (iv) By parts. With $\Theta' = \Omega_x$ and $\Omega_x(0) = \Omega_x(T) = 0$: $\int\Omega_x'\cos\Theta = \int\Omega_x^2\sin\Theta = I_s$ and $\int\Omega_x'\sin\Theta = -\int\Omega_x^2\cos\Theta = -I_c$. So $K = -\tfrac{\lambda + c - 1/2}{2\alpha}(I_sY + I_cZ)$. (v) Symmetry. For an envelope symmetric about $T/2$ with area $\pi$, $\Theta(T - t) = \pi - \Theta(t)$, so $\cos\Theta$ is odd about the midpoint and $I_c = 0$. (vi) The miss. $K = -\kappa Y$, and $R_x(\pi)e^{i\kappa Y}\ket0 = R_x(\pi)(\cos\kappa\ket0 - \sin\kappa\ket1) = -i(\cos\kappa\ket1 - \sin\kappa\ket0)$, so $P_0 = \sin^2\kappa$. (Using $e^{-iK}$ in place of $I - iK$ changes nothing at this order.) ∎

What the theorem says. The Stark shift and the DRAG quadrature both produce the same first-order error, a turn about $Y$ of size proportional to $I_s/\alpha$, with coefficients $-\tfrac12$ (the shift) and $\lambda$ (DRAG), plus $c$ from the detuning. So the miss vanishes on the line $\lambda + c = \tfrac12$, and DRAG at $\lambda = 1$ overshoots by exactly as much as no DRAG undershoots.

Numbers. $I_s = 1.01297$ rad/ns for the 10 ns pulse (computed by quadrature). $I_s \propto 1/T$ for a fixed shape, so $\kappa = 1.612/T$ at $\lambda = c = 0$ ($T$ in ns): the miss falls as $2.6/T^2$. At 20 ns, $\sin^2(0.0806) = 0.648\%$ against the exact $0.649\%$; at 40 ns, $0.1624\%$ against $0.1624\%$. The theorem gets better as the pulse gets longer, as a first-order statement in $\Omega/\alpha$ should.

How the search was done. Why first order's $(1, -\tfrac12)$ loses. The detuning term $c\,\Omega_x^2n/\alpha$ shifts $\ket2$ by $2c\,\Omega_x^2/\alpha$ as well as $\ket1$, changing the leakage transition's detuning by a few per cent of $\alpha$ during the pulse — a second-order effect the derivation of DRAG did not include. At $c = -0.47$ it raises the leakage from $4\times10^{-6}$ to $1.4\times10^{-4}$, and the residual miss is $1.1\times10^{-3}$. At $\Omega/|\alpha| = 0.37$ second order is not negligible, and the honest procedure is the one used here and on real devices: take the first-order form, then tune its coefficients.

Why count leakage twice. $1 - P_1$ already includes $P_2$; adding $P_2$ again weights leaked population double. That is a choice, made because leakage is the worse error: a leaked qubit is invisible to unit 22's codes and can disturb its neighbours. The curriculum's "about $4\times10^{-4}$ together" is the $(0.5, -0.03)$ row. With the score replaced by $1 - P_1$ alone, the optimum moves only slightly.

How a detuning is applied. Driving at $\omega + \Delta_d(t)$ and computing in the frame of the actual drive adds $-\Delta_d n$ to $\tilde H$; so the term $c\,\Omega_x^2n/\alpha$ is $\Delta_d = -c\,\Omega_x^2/\alpha$. Electronically it is a phase ramp on the envelopes. At $c = \tfrac12$, $\Delta_d = \Omega_x^2/2|\alpha| = \delta_S$: following the shifted qubit.

T₁ as a gate error

A gate takes time, and while it runs the qubit relaxes. To compare that with the control errors, both need one yardstick: the fidelity averaged over every input.

25 · T₁

One number for a gate

the average gate fidelity

Of a channel $\Phi$ meant to implement $U$, over the uniform (rotation-invariant) measure on the Bloch sphere:

$$F_{\rm avg} = \int\braket{\psi|U^\dagger\,\Phi\big(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi\big)\,U|\psi}\,d\psi .$$

$1 - F_{\rm avg}$ is the average infidelity. Checks: $\Phi = U\cdot U^\dagger$ gives $1$; $\Phi(\rho) = I/2$ gives $\tfrac12$.

a convention

$\braket{\psi|\rho|\psi}$ is unit 21's $F(\psi, \rho)^2$, not $F$. Gate fidelities are always quoted this way.

lemma: the six-state average

A polynomial of degree $\le 2$ in the Bloch vector $\mathbf r$ has the same average over the sphere as over $\pm\hat x, \pm\hat y, \pm\hat z$. The integrand above is one.

Six inputs suffice: the axis states, corners of an octahedron.

proof

Check monomials. $1$: both $1$. $r_i$ and $r_ir_j$ ($i \ne j$): both $0$, by $r_i \mapsto -r_i$. $r_i^2$: both $\tfrac13$ — six points by counting, sphere since the three averages are equal and sum to $\langle|\mathbf r|^2\rangle = 1$. The integrand is linear in $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi = \tfrac12(I + \mathbf r\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$ twice. ∎

The measure. "Uniform" means normalised surface area on the unit sphere of Bloch vectors. It is invariant under rotations, and unit 02 showed every qubit unitary acts on Bloch vectors as a rotation; so averaging over $\psi$ or over $V\psi$ gives the same answer for any unitary $V$. That invariance is used on the slide Damping during the gate, not after it.

The lemma in full. On the sphere, the map $r_i \mapsto -r_i$ (a reflection) preserves the measure, so $\langle r_i\rangle = -\langle r_i\rangle = 0$ and, for $i \ne j$, $\langle r_ir_j\rangle = 0$. Permuting coordinates preserves it too, so $\langle r_1^2\rangle = \langle r_2^2\rangle = \langle r_3^2\rangle$, and their sum is $1$. On the six points: $r_1^2 = 1$ at $\pm\hat x$ and $0$ at the other four, average $\tfrac26 = \tfrac13$; each $r_i$ and $r_ir_j$ averages to $0$ by the $\pm$ pairing. A polynomial of degree $\le 2$ is a combination of these monomials, and both averages are linear. (The six points in fact match the sphere up to degree 3 — they form a spherical 3-design — but degree 2 is all that is needed.)

Why the integrand has degree 2. $\braket{\psi|U^\dagger\Phi(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi)U|\psi} = \operatorname{tr}\big(U\ket\psi\!\bra\psi U^\dagger\,\Phi(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi)\big)$, linear in $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi$ in each slot, and $\ket\psi\!\bra\psi$ is affine in $\mathbf r$ (unit 04). For a leaking three-level evolution $W$, the same holds with $\Phi(\ket\psi\!\bra\psi) = W\ket\psi\!\bra\psi W^\dagger$ on $\mathbb C^3$ and $U\ket\psi$ embedded in $\mathbb C^3$: the close of the deck uses this for the pulses.

Another route (cited). For a qubit, $F_{\rm avg} = (2F_e + 1)/3$, where $F_e$ is the entanglement fidelity: the overlap of the maximally entangled state with itself after $\Phi$ acts on one half (M. Horodecki, P. Horodecki and R. Horodecki, Phys. Rev. A 60, 1888, 1999; M. A. Nielsen, Phys. Lett. A 303, 249, 2002). The six-state lemma makes it unnecessary here; problem P11 compares the two.

The checks. For $\Phi = U\cdot U^\dagger$ the integrand is $|\braket{\psi|\psi}|^2 = 1$. For $\Phi(\rho) = I/2$ it is $\braket{\psi'|\tfrac I2|\psi'} = \tfrac12$ for every $\psi$.

Worked: t/3T₁ for amplitude damping

the gate's decay: unit 21's amplitude damping for time $t$, $\gamma = 1 - e^{-t/T_1}$, $s = \sqrt{1 - \gamma}$, against $U = I$

1. The channel on Bloch vectors (unit 21): $(x, y, z) \mapsto (sx,\ sy,\ \gamma + (1 - \gamma)z)$.

2. The fidelity of a pure input $\mathbf r$ with its output $\mathbf r'$ is $\operatorname{tr}\rho\rho' = \tfrac12(1 + \mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r')$ (unit 04).

3. Six inputs. $\pm\hat x$, $\pm\hat y$: $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r' = s$, fidelity $\tfrac12(1 + s)$, four times. $+\hat z$: fixed, fidelity $1$. $-\hat z \mapsto (0, 0, 2\gamma - 1)$: $\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf r' = 1 - 2\gamma$, fidelity $1 - \gamma$.

4. Average: $\tfrac16\big[2(1 + s) + 1 + 1 - \gamma\big] = \tfrac{4 + 2s - \gamma}{6}$, so exactly

$$1 - F_{\rm avg} = \frac{1 - s}{3} + \frac\gamma6 .$$

5. Short times. $s = 1 - \tfrac\gamma2 + O(\gamma^2)$ and $\gamma = \tfrac{t}{T_1} + O(t^2)$:

$$1 - F_{\rm avg} = \frac\gamma6 + \frac\gamma6 + O(\gamma^2) = \frac{t}{3T_1} + O(t^2).$$

6. Numbers. $t = 20$ ns, $T_1 = 100\ \mu$s: $\gamma = 1.9998\times10^{-4}$, exact $6.6662\times10^{-5}$; $\ t/3T_1 = 6.6667\times10^{-5}$ ✓. For 10 ns: $3.3\times10^{-5}$.

7. Checks. $t = 0$: $\gamma = 0$, $s = 1$, infidelity $0$ ✓. $t \to \infty$: $\gamma = 1$, $s = 0$: $\tfrac13 + \tfrac16 = \tfrac12$ — every input ends in $\ket0$, and $\braket{\psi|0}\!\braket{0|\psi}$ averages to $\tfrac12$ ✓. Dimensions: $t/T_1$ is a pure number ✓.

Step 3 in full. $+\hat x = (1, 0, 0) \mapsto (s, 0, \gamma)$, dot product $s$; likewise $-\hat x \mapsto (-s, 0, \gamma)$, dot product $s$; $\pm\hat y$ the same. $+\hat z \mapsto (0, 0, \gamma + 1 - \gamma) = (0, 0, 1)$. $-\hat z \mapsto (0, 0, \gamma - (1 - \gamma)) = (0, 0, 2\gamma - 1)$, dot product with $(0, 0, -1)$: $1 - 2\gamma$, so fidelity $\tfrac12(2 - 2\gamma) = 1 - \gamma$ — the chance that $\ket1$ has not decayed, unit 21's $e^{-t/T_1}$, as it must be.

Step 5 in full. $\sqrt{1 - \gamma} = 1 - \tfrac\gamma2 - \tfrac{\gamma^2}8 - \dots$, so $\tfrac{1 - s}3 = \tfrac\gamma6 + \tfrac{\gamma^2}{24} + \dots$, and the infidelity is $\tfrac\gamma3 + \tfrac{\gamma^2}{24} + \dots$. With $\gamma = \tfrac t{T_1} - \tfrac{t^2}{2T_1^2} + \dots$: $\tfrac{t}{3T_1} - \tfrac{t^2}{8T_1^2} + \dots$. At $t/T_1 = 2\times10^{-4}$ the correction is $5\times10^{-9}$, which is the difference between the two numbers in step 6.

Where the third comes from. Two thirds of the inputs (the equator, $\pm\hat x, \pm\hat y$) lose coherence at rate $\tfrac{1}{2T_1}$ in amplitude, costing $\tfrac{\gamma}{4}$ each in fidelity; one sixth ($-\hat z$) loses its excitation at rate $\tfrac1{T_1}$, costing $\gamma$; one sixth ($+\hat z$) loses nothing. $\tfrac46\cdot\tfrac\gamma4 + \tfrac16\gamma = \tfrac\gamma3$.

Pure dephasing ($T_\varphi$, unit 21) would add its own contribution, of the same form with $T_\varphi$ in place of $T_1$; this deck counts $T_1$ alone, as the curriculum does.

Damping during the gate, not after it

The qubit decays while the pulse turns it, not after. Does the answer change? Not to first order.

theorem

A gate of duration $t$, any unitary, with amplitude damping at rate $1/T_1$ acting throughout, has average infidelity $\dfrac{t}{3T_1} + O\big(t^2/T_1^2\big)$.

proof, in three facts

(a) $F_{\rm avg}$ is linear in $\Phi$. (b) $F_{\rm avg}(V^\dagger\Phi V) = F_{\rm avg}(\Phi)$ for unitary $V$: substitute $\varphi = V\psi$, the measure being rotation-invariant. (c) Slice the gate; move each slice's damping to the start by conjugating with the unitary before it. Each conjugated slice costs $\gamma_k/3$ by (b) and the worked example; to first order the costs add, by (a). $\sum\gamma_k = t/T_1 + O(t^2)$. ∎

Amplitude damping on the $xz$ cross-section, $\gamma = 0.3$: the ball shrinks toward $\ket0$. Four of the six inputs are drawn; $\pm\hat y$ move like $\pm\hat x$.

Full proof. Divide $[0, t]$ into $N$ slices. On slice $k$ the gate applies a unitary $U_k$ and damping applies $D_k$ with $\gamma_k = 1 - e^{-\Delta t/T_1}$; in the limit the order within a slice does not matter at first order. The whole process is $D_NU_N\cdots D_1U_1$. With $W_k = U_k\cdots U_1$, rewrite it as $W_N\circ(W_N^\dagger D_NW_N)\circ\cdots\circ(W_1^\dagger D_1W_1)$, where $W^\dagger DW$ means the channel $\rho \mapsto W^\dagger D(W\rho W^\dagger)W$. The ideal gate is $W_N$, so the infidelity against it is that of the composition $\Psi$ of the conjugated slices against the identity. Each slice is $\mathrm{id} + E_k$ with $\lVert E_k(\rho)\rVert_1 \le 2\gamma_k$ for states, so $\Psi = \mathrm{id} + \sum_kE_k + R$ with $\lVert R(\rho)\rVert_1 \le (1 + 2\gamma)^N - 1 - 2N\gamma = O\big((t/T_1)^2\big)$ (uniform $\gamma_k = \gamma$, $N\gamma \approx t/T_1$). By linearity (a), $1 - F_{\rm avg}(\Psi) = \sum_k\big(1 - F_{\rm avg}(\mathrm{id} + E_k)\big) + O\big((t/T_1)^2\big)$, and by (b) each term is the worked example's $\gamma_k/3 + O(\gamma_k^2)$. Summing, $\tfrac13\sum\gamma_k + O(N\gamma^2) + O((t/T_1)^2) = \tfrac{t}{3T_1} + O(t^2/T_1^2)$.

Fact (b) in full. $F_{\rm avg}(V^\dagger\Phi V) = \int\braket{\psi|V^\dagger\Phi(V\ket\psi\!\bra\psi V^\dagger)V|\psi}d\psi = \int\braket{\varphi|\Phi(\ket\varphi\!\bra\varphi)|\varphi}d\varphi$ with $\varphi = V\psi$, because $\psi \mapsto V\psi$ is a rotation of the Bloch sphere and preserves the uniform measure.

What this licenses. The control errors (sections 3–4) and the decay can be computed separately and added, at first order in each: the closing comparison does exactly that. When both are small — $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-2}$ here — the cross terms are smaller still.
