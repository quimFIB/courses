The terms this unit introduces, in the order the deck meets them. The slides
stay the definition of record; each entry names the slide it comes from, so a
term can be looked up here and read properly there.

Running example: Max-Cut on the cube graph — vertices the eight bit strings
\(000, \dots, 111\), joined when they differ in one bit, so 12 edges,
3-regular, triangle-free and bipartite. On a \(2\times4\) square grid at most
10 of its 12 interactions are direct; on trapped ions all 12 are. A 5 GHz
qubit has \(hf/k_B = 0.240\) K, so its excited population is
\(6.2\times10^{-6}\) at 20 mK and \(0.485\) at 4 K; all eight qubits start
in \(\ket0\) with probability \(0.99995\) at 20 mK and \(0.0049\) at 4 K.
Depth-one QAOA at \(\beta = \pi/8\), \(\tan\gamma = 1/\sqrt2\) cuts
\(12\big(\tfrac12 + \tfrac1{3\sqrt3}\big) = 8.31\) edges in expectation and
samples a maximum cut with probability 0.186. Classically: a random cut gives
6, Goemans–Williamson guarantees at least \(10.54\), degree-3 algorithms at
least \(11.19\), and colouring by parity finds all 12 in linear time.

* What a quantum computer must have, and four ways to build one

- DiVincenzo criteria :: Five requirements for a quantum computer
  (DiVincenzo 2000): a scalable system of well-characterised qubits;
  initialisation to a simple state such as \(\ket{0\cdots0}\); coherence
  times long compared with the gate time; a universal gate set; qubit-specific
  measurement. Two more for networking: converting between stationary and
  flying qubits, and transmitting flying qubits faithfully. /Slide:
  DiVincenzo's checklist./
- gate time :: How long one gate takes. The ratio of coherence time to gate
  time bounds how many gates fit before decoherence; by amplitude damping an
  idle qubit decays during a gate of length \(t\) with probability
  \(1 - e^{-t/T_1} \le t/T_1\). /Slide: DiVincenzo's checklist./
- superconducting qubit :: The two lowest energy levels of a small electrical
  circuit made of superconductor (resistance-free when cold), a few GHz
  apart; the transmon is the common design. Gates of about 10–100 ns, \(T_1\)
  of tens to hundreds of µs, fixed nearest-neighbour coupling on a flat chip.
  /Slide: Superconducting circuits./
- connectivity :: Which pairs of qubits a device can act on directly with a
  two-qubit gate: a graph, fixed by wiring on a superconducting chip,
  all-to-all within an ion row, reconfigurable for neutral atoms.
- trapped ion :: An atom missing an electron, held in vacuum by electric
  fields; a row of them shares a vibration that couples any pair. Qubit in two
  internal levels; coherence of seconds or more, gates of µs to ms,
  all-to-all connectivity within a row. /Slide: Trapped ions./
- optical tweezer :: A tightly focused laser beam that holds a single neutral
  atom at its brightest point; arrays of hundreds to thousands, and movable.
  /Slide: Neutral atoms./
- neutral atom :: An uncharged atom held in an optical tweezer, storing a
  qubit in two internal levels; entangled by the Rydberg blockade, with
  connectivity reconfigured by moving the tweezers.
- Rydberg blockade :: Two atoms within a few micrometres cannot both be
  excited to a Rydberg state (an orbit of very high principal quantum number),
  because the doubly excited energy is shifted out of resonance; this makes a
  conditional, controlled-\(Z\)-type gate.
- photonic qubit :: One photon in one of two modes — two polarisations or two
  paths. Barely decoheres in flight, but photons do not interact, so two-qubit
  gates are probabilistic. /Slide: Photons./
- heralded :: Of a probabilistic operation: a detector signal announces
  whether it succeeded, so a failure can be discarded and retried. Attempts
  until success are geometric, mean \(1/q\) for success probability \(q\).
- photon loss :: The dominant photonic error: a photon absorbed or missed
  takes its qubit with it. An erasure — an error whose location is known.
- measurement-based computation :: Computation by single-qubit measurements
  on a pre-made entangled resource state. One step: \(\ket\psi\) and \(\ket+\),
  a CZ, and an \(X\)-basis measurement of the first qubit with outcome \(m\)
  leave \(X^mH\ket\psi\) on the second. /Slide: Entangle first, compute by
  measuring./
- resource state :: The entangled state prepared before a measurement-based
  computation (for instance a cluster state, a grid of qubits joined by CZs);
  for photons it is built from heralded probabilistic steps, retried
  offline.
- SWAP routing :: Bringing two qubits that are not coupled next to each other
  by SWAP gates (three CNOTs each) along the coupling graph. The cube on a
  \(2\times4\) grid has 10 of 12 edges direct at best, and no square grid
  holds it with all 12. /Slide: Eight qubits, four platforms./

* The fridge

- thermal equilibrium :: The state a system reaches after long contact with
  large surroundings at temperature \(T\), when nothing changes on average.
  /Slide: Temperature, and Boltzmann's law./
- Boltzmann's law :: In thermal equilibrium at temperature \(T\) a state of
  energy \(E\) is occupied with probability proportional to
  \(e^{-E/k_BT}\) (cited: the physical input). For a qubit with gap \(hf\),
  \(p_1/p_0 = e^{-x}\) with \(x = hf/k_BT\).
- Boltzmann's constant :: \(k_B = 1.380649\times10^{-23}\) J/K, exact in the
  SI; \(k_BT\) is the energy scale of thermal kicks.
- thermal state :: \(\rho_T = p_0\ket0\!\bra0 + p_1\ket1\!\bra1\) with
  \(p_1 = e^{-x}/(1 + e^{-x})\), \(x = hf/k_BT\); Bloch vector
  \((0, 0, \tanh(x/2))\): the pole \(\ket0\) when cold, the centre \(I/2\) when
  warm. For 5 GHz: \(p_1 = 6.2\times10^{-6}\) at 20 mK, \(0.485\) at 4 K.
  /Slide: A qubit in equilibrium is a point on an axis of the ball./
- dilution refrigerator :: A refrigerator that reaches about 10 mK by mixing
  two isotopes of helium; needed because a 5 GHz qubit's gap is the thermal
  energy at 0.24 K, so liquid helium at 4 K leaves it nearly maximally mixed.
  /Slide: Why a dilution refrigerator./

* The algorithm: QAOA at depth one

- Max-Cut :: Split a graph's vertices into two sides to maximise the number
  of edges between them. NP-hard (Karp 1972, cited). /Slide: Max-Cut, and an
  operator that counts cut edges./
- cut fraction :: The number of cut edges divided by the number of edges
  \(m\). Not the same as the approximation ratio, which divides by the maximum
  cut.
- cost operator :: \(C = \sum_{uv\in E}\tfrac12(1 - Z_uZ_v)\), diagonal with
  \(C\ket z = \mathrm{cut}(z)\ket z\).
- QAOA :: The quantum approximate optimisation algorithm (Farhi, Goldstone
  and Gutmann 2014). At depth one, \(\ket{\gamma, \beta} =
  e^{-i\beta B}e^{-i\gamma C}\ket+^{\otimes n}\), measured in the computational
  basis to give a cut; depth \(p\) alternates \(p\) times. /Slide: The
  depth-one circuit./
- mixer :: \(B = \sum_vX_v\), so that \(e^{-i\beta B} = \bigotimes_vR_x(2\beta)\)
  rotates every qubit by \(2\beta\) about the \(x\)-axis.
- variational algorithm :: A short parametrised circuit run many times
  inside a classical optimisation loop, which estimates the objective from
  samples and adjusts the parameters. /Slide: A variational algorithm: the
  classical loop around it./
- light cone :: The qubits an observable can depend on after conjugation by
  a circuit. At depth one, \(\langle C_{uv}\rangle\) depends only on \(u\),
  \(v\) and their neighbours. /Slide: Only a neighbourhood matters./
- triangle-free :: Having no three mutually adjacent vertices. In a
  3-regular triangle-free graph each edge's neighbourhood is a six-vertex
  tree, and depth-one QAOA gives \(\langle C_{uv}\rangle = \tfrac12 +
  \tfrac12\sin4\beta\sin\gamma\cos^2\gamma\), maximal at \(\beta = \pi/8\),
  \(\tan\gamma = 1/\sqrt2\): \(\tfrac12 + \tfrac1{3\sqrt3} = 0.6925\).
  /Slide: Worked: the edge expectation./

* The baselines

- local search :: Move any vertex with more uncut than cut incident edges to
  the other side until none remains; stops within \(m\) moves at a cut of at
  least \(m/2\). /Slide: The floor: a coin per vertex, and local search./
- Goemans–Williamson algorithm :: Relax each label to a unit vector, solve
  the semidefinite program maximising \(\sum\tfrac12(1 - \mathbf v_u\cdot\mathbf
  v_v)\), and round by a random hyperplane. An edge at angle \(\theta\) is cut
  with probability \(\theta/\pi\), giving expected cut at least \(0.8786\)
  times the optimum (Goemans and Williamson 1995). /Slide: Goemans–Williamson:
  vectors, then a random hyperplane./
- approximation ratio :: An algorithm's cut divided by the maximum cut; at
  least the cut fraction. Goemans–Williamson guarantees \(0.8786\) on every
  graph; \(0.9326\) is achievable for maximum degree 3 (Halperin, Livnat and
  Zwick 2004). /Slide: Ratio against fraction, and degree three./
- bipartite :: Having a 2-colouring with every edge joining two colours —
  equivalently a cut of all edges, or no odd cycle. Found by breadth-first
  search in \(O(n + m)\) time; the cube is bipartite by parity. /Slide: The
  running example on one line./

* Advantage claims, read as theorems

- quantum advantage :: A claim that a quantum device performs a precisely
  specified task in time \(t_Q\) while the best known classical algorithm on
  the best available hardware needs \(t_C \gg t_Q\). /Slide: What an advantage
  claim claims./
- classical baseline :: The classical algorithm and hardware an advantage
  claim is measured against. It is estimated, not proved optimal, so a better
  classical algorithm falsifies the claim: the baseline is part of the claim.
- random-circuit sampling :: Sampling from the output distribution
  \(p(x) = |\braket{x|U|0\cdots0}|^2\) of a randomly chosen circuit of native
  gates; hard for all known classical methods at large size and depth, and of
  no known use. /Slide: Random-circuit sampling./
- linear cross-entropy benchmark :: \(F_{\rm XEB} = 2^n\langle
  p(x_i)\rangle - 1\) over observed samples \(x_i\): exactly 0 for a uniform
  sampler, about 1 for an ideal one under Porter–Thomas, about \(f\) for a
  device sampling the ideal distribution with probability \(f\). /Slide:
  Scoring samples you cannot check./
- Porter–Thomas distribution :: The exponential law \(e^{-t}\) for the scaled
  output probabilities \(t = 2^np(x)\) of a deep random circuit (cited); its
  second moment is 2.
- tensor-network contraction :: A classical simulation method that writes a
  circuit as a network of small tensors and sums it out in a chosen order;
  ran the 2019 random-circuit task in about 15 hours on 512 GPUs (Pan, Chen and
  Zhang 2022). /Slide: 2019: the claim, and the baseline that moved./
- sample-and-query access :: Classical access to \(x\in\mathbb R^n\) that
  can query entries, knows \(\|x\|\), and draws \(j\) with probability
  \(x_j^2/\|x\|^2\) — what a tree of partial sums of squares provides, and the
  classical twin of measuring \(\sum_jx_j\ket j/\|x\|\). /Slide:
  Dequantisation: the same input, sampled classically./
- dequantisation :: Finding a classical algorithm, given the same input model,
  that matches a claimed quantum speedup up to a polynomial. Tang (2018) did
  this for the Kerenidis–Prakash recommendation algorithm.
- quantum-inspired algorithm :: A classical algorithm built from
  length-squared sampling to rival a quantum one under the same input
  model; part of the baseline for any claim that assumes such input.
- crossover size :: The problem size at which a quantum algorithm's running
  time drops below a classical one's. For Grover against classical search,
  \(\sqrt{N^*} = \tfrac\pi2rt\): \(2.5\times10^{12}\) items for one core
  against 1 ms iterations, \(2.5\times10^{24}\) and 39 years for a million
  cores. /Slide: Quadratic speedups against a slow clock./
