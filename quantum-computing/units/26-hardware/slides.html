Hardware and the near term: what exists, what it has shown, and what it has not

The course has proved what a fault-tolerant machine could do and what it would cost. This unit asks what has been built, what it has demonstrated, and holds each claim of advantage to the standard of a theorem: against which classical algorithm?

needs 24, 17, 21~3 h slides + 4½ h problemsproblems: ./qc problems 26

Units 22–24 turned noise into arithmetic: below a threshold near 1 per cent, a surface code of distance $d$ makes logical errors as rare as you like, at about $2d^2$ physical qubits each. The capstone asks what such a machine would cost for factoring. Nobody has yet built one. This last unit before the capstone looks at the machines that do exist, and at the three most-cited claims that they — or the algorithms meant for them — do something a classical computer cannot.

The tone is deliberate. A hardware number is an order of magnitude, or a figure cited with its reference and date ("as reported in…"); nothing here is called current, because it will not be by the time you read it. Platforms are named, companies are not, except where a paper's authors are a company's laboratory. And a claim of advantage is read like a theorem: what exactly was compared with what, and what happened when someone looked for a better classical algorithm.

Eight vertices, twelve edges, and a real machine

the running example: Max-Cut on the cube

Vertices: the eight bit strings $000, \dots, 111$. Edges: pairs differing in one bit — 12 of them. Every vertex has three neighbours (3-regular), no three are mutually adjacent (triangle-free), and colouring by the parity of the bits makes every edge join two colours (bipartite).

The question. Run depth-one QAOA (section 3) on it, and ask: on which hardware, at what temperature, with what result — and against what classical competition?

Why an easy instance. The cube's maximum cut is obvious — the parity colouring cuts every edge, and a cut can do no better than all of them. That is the point. A hardware run of a variational algorithm is scored against the instance's known optimum, so the instances run so far have been ones whose answer a classical computer could find; the interesting question is not whether the quantum computer finds a good cut but what its result would have to look like to mean anything. The close returns to this.

The three properties, checked. 3-regular: flipping each of three bits gives three neighbours, so $8\cdot3/2 = 12$ edges. Bipartite: an edge flips one bit, so it changes the parity. Triangle-free: follows from bipartite — a triangle is an odd cycle, and a bipartite graph has none (each step changes colour, so a closed walk has even length).

section 1

What a quantum computer must have, and four ways to build one

A checklist written in 2000, and four kinds of physical system measured against it — each good at something different.

26 · criteria

DiVincenzo's checklist

the DiVincenzo criteria (2000)
  1. a scalable physical system of well-characterised qubits;
  2. the ability to initialise them to a simple state such as $\ket{0\cdots0}$;
  3. coherence times long compared with the gate time;
  4. a universal set of gates (unit 11);
  5. a qubit-specific measurement.

For networking, two more: convert between stationary and flying qubits, and send flying qubits faithfully between places.

Each is a theorem's hypothesis. Unit 24's threshold needs 3 at a rate below $10^{-2}$; it needs 2 for fresh ancillas, 5 for syndromes, and 1 for the $2d^2$ qubits of each logical qubit.

The five criteria in the order a computation uses them; 6 and 7 connect modules with flying qubits — in practice, photons.

The source. D. P. DiVincenzo, "The physical implementation of quantum computation", Fortschritte der Physik 48, 771 (2000). The list was written when the largest devices held a handful of qubits, as a way of comparing proposals that looked nothing alike — nuclear spins in a liquid, ions, superconducting circuits, quantum dots. It has outlived most of them because each criterion is a hypothesis of something the course has proved.

Reading each criterion through the course. Well-characterised: the qubit is a known two-dimensional subspace, and you know its Hamiltonian; a third level just above the qubit's two, which a drive can leak into, is the standard way this fails (unit 25 works it out, for readers who took it). Initialise: unit 24's fault-tolerant syndrome extraction needs a fresh ancilla in $\ket0$ every cycle. Coherence: the ratio of coherence time to gate time bounds how many gates fit before unit 21's channels have acted; Gates per coherence time makes this a number. Universal: unit 11's Clifford + $T$. Qubit-specific measurement: reading one qubit without disturbing its neighbours, which a syndrome measurement must do every cycle.

The networking pair. Stationary qubits (in a chip, an ion, an atom) are good at storing and computing; photons are good at travelling. Linking modules by photons is how a machine too large for one chip or one trap might be built, and it is the only way to do quantum key distribution over distance. Named here, not developed.

26 · platforms

Superconducting circuits

The physics, from nothing. Some metals, aluminium among them, lose all electrical resistance when cold enough — they superconduct. A small circuit made of them has discrete energy levels, like an atom; its two lowest are the qubit, a few GHz apart. It is a superconducting qubit; the common design is the transmon (unit 25).

  • Gates: microwave pulses, about 10–100 ns.
  • $T_1$: tens to hundreds of µs, limited by defects in the materials that absorb the qubit's energy.
  • Connectivity: fixed by wiring — each qubit coupled to its neighbours on a flat chip, at most four.
  • Made by lithography, like classical chips: criterion 1 is the strength.

A square grid: four neighbours at most, and which pairs can interact is decided when the chip is made.

The physics a little further. In a superconductor the electrons pair up and move without resistance, so an electrical circuit of a capacitor and an inductor rings forever at its resonant frequency — a harmonic oscillator whose levels are equally spaced, and so useless as a qubit, since a pulse at the $0\to1$ frequency would also drive $1\to2$. A Josephson junction, a nanometre-thin insulating gap between two superconductors, acts as a nonlinear inductor and makes the spacing unequal; the lowest two levels can then be addressed alone, as unit 25 shows at length. Aluminium superconducts below about 1.2 K, which is one reason for the cold; section 2 gives the other.

What limits each figure. $T_1$: the circuit's energy leaks into microscopic defects — "two-level systems" in the oxide surfaces and interfaces — and into stray radiation; better materials have pushed the best transmons above 0.3 ms (A. P. M. Place et al., Nature Communications 12, 1779, 2021, as reported). Gate fidelity: $T_1$ during the gate, leakage to the third level, and control errors; two-qubit gate errors of a few parts in a thousand are reported on the best large chips. Connectivity: every coupler is a physical structure on the chip, so the coupling graph is planar and fixed. Unit 24's below-threshold experiment ran on a chip of this kind, 105 qubits in a grid.

Measurement uses a second resonator coupled to the qubit, whose frequency shifts with the qubit's state; reading it takes of order a microsecond.

Trapped ions

The physics, from nothing. An atom with one electron removed is an ion: it carries charge, so electric fields can hold it in vacuum. A row of ions so held repel one another and sit in a line, a few micrometres apart. Each trapped ion stores a qubit in two of its internal energy levels.

  • Gates: laser or microwave pulses. A two-qubit gate couples two ions through the vibration of the whole row, which every ion shares: µs to ms.
  • Coherence: seconds or more — the levels are chosen to be insensitive to stray fields.
  • Connectivity: all-to-all within a row, since the shared vibration reaches every ion.
  • Limits: slow gates, and rows of a few tens before the vibrations crowd; larger machines shuttle ions between zones.

Any two ions in the row can be entangled through the vibration they share: connectivity is all-to-all.

The trap. A static electric field cannot hold a charge stably in all three directions (Earnshaw's theorem, cited); a field oscillating at radio frequency can, on average — the Paul trap, after Wolfgang Paul (Nobel Prize 1989). The ions sit in ultra-high vacuum and are cooled by lasers until their motion is nearly at rest.

Qubits and gates. Common choices are two hyperfine levels of the ion's ground state (for $^{171}$Yb$^+$, about 12.6 GHz apart), whose energy difference barely depends on magnetic fields, or a ground and a long-lived excited level. A two-qubit gate pushes two ions with a state-dependent force so that the row's vibration picks up a phase that depends on both qubits, and then returns the vibration to where it started; the net effect is a two-qubit phase gate. The gate is slow because the vibration is slow: its frequency is of order MHz.

Figures, as reported. Two-qubit gate fidelities of 99.9% were reported in 2016 (C. J. Ballance et al., PRL 117, 060504; J. P. Gaebler et al., PRL 117, 060505). A single ion's qubit has kept its coherence for over ten minutes (Y. Wang et al., Nature Photonics 11, 646, 2017). Measurement is by state-dependent fluorescence: a laser makes the ion glow in one state and not the other, read in of order 100 µs.

Why a row has a size limit. A row of $N$ ions has $N$ vibration modes along its axis, at frequencies that crowd together as $N$ grows, so a gate addressing one mode disturbs others and must slow down. Architectures that move ions between separate trapping zones — "shuttling" — or link traps with photons (criteria 6 and 7) are the routes to more.

26 · platforms

Neutral atoms

The physics, from nothing. A tightly focused laser beam attracts a neutral atom to its brightest point: an optical tweezer. Hundreds of tweezers make an array of single neutral atoms, each holding a qubit in two internal levels.

  • Entangling gates: excite atoms to a Rydberg state, an enormous orbit; within a few µm, one excited atom blocks its neighbour's excitation — the Rydberg blockade, a conditional gate in under a µs.
  • Connectivity: reconfigurable — move the tweezers, and any pair can be brought together.
  • Limits: atoms are lost from traps; Rydberg states decay; reading out takes ms.

Two atoms within the blockade radius can be entangled; an atom carried by its tweezer can meet any other.

Tweezers. Light of a frequency below an atom's transition pulls the atom towards high intensity (the same effect that lets a focused beam hold a microscopic bead, Ashkin's optical tweezers, Nobel Prize 2018). Atoms such as rubidium, caesium or strontium are laser-cooled to microkelvin temperatures and loaded at random, about half the tweezers filled; a camera finds the filled ones and moving tweezers rearrange them into a defect-free array. Arrays of thousands have been reported (H. J. Manetsch et al., arXiv:2403.12021, 2024; Nature 647, 60, 2025: about 6100 atoms).

The blockade gate. A Rydberg state has the outer electron in an orbit of very high principal quantum number, hundreds of nanometres across, so two Rydberg atoms interact strongly at micrometres. A laser that would excite either atom alone cannot excite both, because the pair's doubly excited energy is shifted out of resonance. The pulse sequence therefore acts differently on $\ket{11}$ from the other three basis states, and a controlled-$Z$ follows. Two-qubit fidelities of 99.5% have been reported (S. J. Evered et al., Nature 622, 268, 2023).

Moving qubits. Moving tweezers carry atoms, qubit state intact, across the array between gate layers. With this, a 2024 experiment encoded up to 48 logical qubits in 280 atoms and ran small error-detected circuits (D. Bluvstein et al., Nature 626, 58, 2024). Moving takes hundreds of µs, so connectivity is bought with time.

26 · platforms

Photons

The physics, from nothing. Light comes in indivisible quanta, photons. A photonic qubit is one photon in one of two modes — two polarisations, or two paths. Beam splitters and phase shifters apply single-qubit gates; single-photon detectors measure.

  • Coherence: photons barely interact with anything, so a qubit in flight does not decohere — criteria 6 and 7 come free.
  • The catch: photons do not interact with each other either. Two-qubit gates built from beam splitters and detectors succeed only with some probability, and a detector click heralds success.
  • Dominant error: photon loss — a photon absorbed or missed by a detector is gone, along with its qubit.

A path qubit: one photon, two paths. Loss removes the qubit outright; entangling steps are probabilistic but heralded.

Why photons do not interact. In vacuum, light passes through light; in ordinary materials the interaction between two single photons is far too weak to make a gate. Knill, Laflamme and Milburn showed in 2001 that beam splitters, phase shifters, extra photons and detectors with feed-forward suffice for universal computation, with gates that succeed with some probability and announce whether they did (E. Knill, R. Laflamme and G. J. Milburn, Nature 409, 46, 2001, cited). The overhead of that scheme was enormous; the next slide gives the idea that tamed it.

Loss. Optical fibre loses about 0.2 dB per kilometre at telecom wavelengths — 4.5% of photons per km — and every component, coupler and detector adds more. Unlike the Pauli errors of unit 22 a lost photon leaves no qubit to correct, so photonic error-correction schemes are designed around erasures: errors whose location is known, which codes tolerate at higher rates than unlocated ones. The reason is unit 22's proof run again: if the possible errors $E_i$ all act inside a known set $S$ of at most $d - 1$ qubits, every $E_i^\dagger E_j$ acts inside $S$ too, has weight $\le d - 1$, and satisfies Knill–Laflamme. So a distance-$d$ code corrects $d - 1$ erasures, against $\lfloor(d - 1)/2\rfloor$ errors at unknown places, whose products can have twice the weight.

Temperature. Photons themselves are happy at room temperature; the best single-photon detectors are superconducting and need a few kelvin.

A photonic advantage claim. Boson sampling — sampling the output of many photons through a large network of beam splitters — is a photonic cousin of section 5's random-circuit sampling; a 2020 experiment detected up to 76 photons (H.-S. Zhong et al., Science 370, 1460, 2020, cited). Its classical baselines have moved too.

Entangle first, compute by measuring

one step of measurement-based computation

Input $\ket\psi = a\ket0 + b\ket1$ on qubit 0, $\ket+$ on qubit 1; apply CZ; measure qubit 0 in the $X$ basis, outcome $m\in\{0, 1\}$. Qubit 1 is left in $X^mH\ket\psi$.

proof

CZ gives $a\ket0\ket+ + b\ket1\ket-$. Write qubit 0 as $\ket0 = (\ket+ + \ket-)/\sqrt2$, $\ket1 = (\ket+ - \ket-)/\sqrt2$: $$\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ket+\!(a\ket+ + b\ket-) + \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\ket-\!(a\ket+ - b\ket-).$$ Each outcome has probability $\tfrac12$, and $a\ket+ + b\ket- = H\ket\psi$, $a\ket+ - b\ket- = XH\ket\psi$. ∎

For photons: all the entangling is in preparing a resource state — retried offline until heralded — and the computation is single-qubit measurements, the $X^m$ handled by classical bookkeeping (notes).

The input's state moves to the second qubit, with an $H$ applied and a known Pauli correction: one-bit teleportation.

Normalisation, checked. $\ket\psi$ is a unit vector, so $|a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1$; each branch has squared norm $\tfrac12(|a|^2 + |b|^2) = \tfrac12$, and the two probabilities sum to 1 ✓. Measuring in the $X$ basis is $H$ followed by a $Z$-basis meter, since $H\ket\pm = \ket{0}, \ket{1}$. The CZ step: $\mathrm{CZ}\ket{a}\ket{+} = \ket{a}Z^a\ket{+}$, and $Z\ket+ = \ket-$.

From one step to a computation. Measuring instead in the basis $\{R_z(\theta)^\dagger\ket\pm\}$ leaves $X^mHR_z(\theta)\ket\psi$ (the same calculation with $\ket\psi$ replaced by $R_z(\theta)\ket\psi$, since $R_z(\theta)$ commutes with CZ on qubit 0). The byproduct $X^m$ costs nothing quantum. Since $XZX = -Z$, $R_z(\theta)X = XR_z(-\theta)$, so a step meant to apply $R_z(\theta)$ after an earlier outcome $m$ measures at angle $(-1)^m\theta$ instead; and $HX = ZH$, so the Paulis that remain are pushed to the end and undone in software — unit 24's Pauli frame, with measurement angles that adapt to earlier outcomes. Since $HR_z(\alpha)H = R_x(\alpha)$, a chain of such steps applies $R_z$ and $R_x$ rotations alternately, and the Euler argument of unit 02 (with $x$ in place of $y$) makes any single-qubit gate from them; a two-dimensional grid of qubits joined by CZs — a cluster state — supports two-qubit gates too, so measuring single qubits of a fixed, pre-made state is universal (R. Raussendorf and H. J. Briegel, PRL 86, 5188, 2001, cited). Unit 24's gate teleportation of $T$ is the same idea: a resource state prepared in advance, consumed by measurement, with a correction that depends on the outcome.

Why this rescues photons. If an entangling attempt succeeds with probability $q$ and announces success, the number of attempts until success is geometric with mean $1/q$: $\sum_{k\ge1}k(1 - q)^{k-1}q = 1/q$. A failure in the middle of a computation would ruin it; a failure while building the resource state just means trying again before the computation starts. Fusion-based architectures build large resource states from small ones this way (S. Bartolucci et al., Nature Communications 14, 912, 2023, cited).

Gates per coherence time

Orders of magnitude, not any one device. Photons are absent: their error is loss, not decoherence.

Criterion 3, as a number. By unit 21's amplitude damping, an idle qubit decays during a gate of length $t$ with probability $$\gamma = 1 - e^{-t/T_1}\le\frac{t}{T_1},$$ since $1 - e^{-u}\le u$. That is a floor on the error per gate.

Superconducting: $t = 50$ ns, $T_1 = 100$ µs gives $5\times10^{-4}$. Ions: $t = 100$ µs, coherence 1 s gives $10^{-4}$.

orders of magnitude

Fast gates and short coherence, or slow gates and long coherence: the ratios are closer than either number. And the floor is rarely the limit — control errors, leakage and crosstalk usually cost more.

The inequality. $f(u) = u - (1 - e^{-u})$ has $f(0) = 0$ and $f'(u) = 1 - e^{-u}\ge0$ for $u\ge0$, so $f\ge0$. The exact figures: $1 - e^{-5\times10^{-4}} = 4.999\times10^{-4}$ and $1 - e^{-10^{-4}} = 1.000\times10^{-4}$.

The boxes in the figure are orders of magnitude, drawn from the sources cited on the platform slides: two-qubit gates of 10–100 ns and coherence of 10 µs to 1 ms for superconducting circuits; 0.1–1 µs and 1 ms to 1 s for neutral atoms; 1 µs to 1 ms and 1 s to 1000 s for trapped ions. Photons are not drawn: they have no coherence time in this sense, and their error is loss per component and per metre. A device's actual numbers depend on its design and year.

Why the ratio is not the whole story. What a computation feels is the error per step, and in a step many qubits act at once: a superconducting surface-code cycle is about a microsecond for all qubits in parallel (1.1 µs in unit 24's 2024 below-threshold experiment, as reported in that paper), while a trapped-ion machine that runs gates one or a few at a time takes much longer per cycle. Wall-clock time per logical operation — what the capstone multiplies by billions — is set by the cycle, not by the ratio.

Against unit 24. The surface code's threshold is near $10^{-2}$ and the running example of unit 24 assumed $p = 10^{-3}$. The idle floors above sit below both, which is necessary but far from sufficient: every other error source adds to it.

Three layers. The edges split by which bit they flip into three perfect matchings of four, so three rounds of parallel gates do the 12.

Why at most 10. A $2\times4$ grid has $2\cdot3 + 4 = 10$ links, so no placement makes more than 10 of the 12 cube edges direct. The figure's placement reaches 10: along each row the labels run $000, 001, 011, 010$ — each differs from the next in one bit (a Gray code) — and each column pairs $0xy$ with $1xy$. The two missing edges, $000$–$010$ and $100$–$110$, close each row's 4-cycle and sit three positions apart. How few SWAPs suffice (each SWAP is three CNOTs, unit 10) is problem P3.

No square grid of any size holds the cube. Suppose the cube's vertices sat on grid points with every cube edge a grid link. The cube's 4-cycles would be 4-cycles of the grid, which are exactly unit squares. Each cube edge lies in exactly two of the cube's six 4-cycles, while each grid link borders exactly two unit squares — so both squares beside every used link would belong to the image, and stepping from square to neighbouring square would never leave it. The image would be every unit square of the plane, not six. So routing is unavoidable on a fixed square grid; the chip would have to be designed for the graph.

The three matchings. For $k = 0, 1, 2$, the edges flipping bit $k$ pair every vertex with exactly one other: a perfect matching of four edges, disjoint from the other two. Gates on disjoint pairs can run at the same time, so on ions (if the machine can address several pairs at once) or on moving atoms, the 12 two-qubit gates take three layers.

Photons. If each of the 12 entangling operations succeeds independently with probability $\tfrac12$, all succeed in one attempt with probability $2^{-12} = 1/4096 = 2.4\times10^{-4}$. Hence the previous slide's strategy: never run a probabilistic gate inside the computation; build the resource state first, retrying each piece until it is heralded.

Temperature, and Boltzmann's law

The physics, from nothing. A system left long enough in contact with large surroundings stops changing on average: it reaches thermal equilibrium, and the surroundings have a temperature $T$, in kelvin from absolute zero (room: about 300 K; liquid helium: about 4 K).

Boltzmann's law (cited: the physical input)

In equilibrium at temperature $T$, a system is found in a state of energy $E$ with probability proportional to $e^{-E/k_BT}$, where

$$k_B = 1.380649\times10^{-23}\ \mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}$$

is Boltzmann's constant: $k_BT$ is an energy, the scale of the surroundings' random kicks.

A qubit has two energies 0 and $hf$ (unit 02, ħ restored: a gap at frequency $f$ is an energy $hf$). So $p_1/p_0 = e^{-x}$ with $x = hf/k_BT$.

What matters is the ratio $x = hf/k_BT$ of the gap to the thermal energy.

Where Boltzmann's law comes from, briefly. It is derived in statistical mechanics by counting: if the surroundings are large, the number of their states compatible with total energy $E_{\rm tot} - E$ grows with the energy they hold, and its logarithm changes linearly in $E$ over the small range a system can take; the slope defines $1/k_BT$. This course does not derive it; it is the physical input, as the Born rule was in unit 01. Ludwig Boltzmann stated it in the 1870s; $k_B$ has been an exact defined constant of the SI since 2019, which is how the kelvin is now defined.

Dimensions. $hf$: $\mathrm{J\,s}\times\mathrm{s^{-1}} = \mathrm J$. $k_BT$: $\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}}\times\mathrm K = \mathrm J$. So $x$ is a pure number, as an exponent must be ✓. For $f = 5$ GHz, $hf = 6.62607015\times10^{-34}\times5\times10^9 = 3.313\times10^{-24}$ J, and $hf/k_B = 0.240$ K: the qubit's gap is the thermal energy at a quarter of a kelvin.

Two levels, normalised. $p_0\propto e^0 = 1$ and $p_1\propto e^{-x}$; dividing by the sum, $p_0 = 1/(1 + e^{-x})$ and $p_1 = e^{-x}/(1 + e^{-x})$, which sum to 1 ✓. Only energy differences matter: adding a constant to both energies multiplies both weights by the same factor, which the normalisation removes. That is why the ground energy can be set to 0.

Unit 02's frequency. Unit 02 found that a qubit whose Hamiltonian has energy gap $\hbar\omega_0$ precesses at angular frequency $\omega_0$; with $f = \omega_0/2\pi$, the gap is $\hbar\omega_0 = hf$. "A 5 GHz qubit" means this $f$.

26 · the fridge

A qubit in equilibrium is a point on an axis of the ball

the thermal state $$\rho_T = p_0\ket0\!\bra0 + p_1\ket1\!\bra1,$$ $$p_1 = \frac{e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}},\qquad x = \frac{hf}{k_BT}.$$

A density operator (unit 04): positive, trace $p_0 + p_1 = 1$ ✓. Its Bloch vector is $r = (0, 0, p_0 - p_1)$ with

$$p_0 - p_1 = \frac{1 - e^{-x}}{1 + e^{-x}} = \tanh\frac x2 .$$
  • Cold, $x\to\infty$: $r\to(0, 0, 1)$, the pure state $\ket0$ — initialisable.
  • Warm, $x\to0$: $p_1\approx\tfrac12 - \tfrac x4$, so $r\to0$: unit 04's centre, $I/2$, the maximally mixed state.

Thermal states of a 5 GHz qubit lie on the $z$-axis at height $\tanh(x/2)$: the pole when cold, the centre when warm.

The operator form. Boltzmann's law for a quantum system with Hamiltonian $H$ is the density operator $\rho_T = e^{-H/k_BT}/\operatorname{tr}e^{-H/k_BT}$ (cited, with the law). For a qubit with $H = \mathrm{diag}(0, hf)$ in the basis $\ket0, \ket1$, unit 00's functional calculus gives $e^{-H/k_BT} = \mathrm{diag}(1, e^{-x})$, and dividing by the trace $1 + e^{-x}$ gives the slide's $\rho_T$. It is diagonal in the energy basis: equilibrium carries no coherence between energy levels.

The two limits, proved. $\tanh(x/2) = (1 - e^{-x})/(1 + e^{-x})$ by multiplying numerator and denominator of $(e^{x/2} - e^{-x/2})/(e^{x/2} + e^{-x/2})$ by $e^{-x/2}$. For small $x$, $p_1 = 1/(1 + e^x) = 1/(2 + x + O(x^2)) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac x4 + O(x^2)$. For large $x$, $p_1 = e^{-x}(1 - e^{-x} + \cdots)\approx e^{-x}$.

The figure's heights, computed: $\tanh(12.00/2) = 0.999988$ at 20 mK; $\tanh(2.400/2) = 0.834$ at 100 mK; $\tanh(0.0600/2) = 0.0300$ at 4 K. The 50 mK point, $0.984$, would sit just under the 20 mK one and is omitted for legibility.

Relaxation goes to $\rho_T$, not to $\ket0$. Unit 21's amplitude damping sends an excited qubit to $\ket0$ because the environment there was cold — it had no energy to give back. At temperature $T$ the environment also excites the qubit, at a rate smaller by $e^{-x}$, and the fixed point of the resulting channel (generalised amplitude damping, named here) is $\rho_T$. So "wait and it relaxes to $\ket0$" — the simplest initialisation, criterion 2 — works only as well as $p_1$ is small.

Max-Cut, and an operator that counts cut edges

Max-Cut

Given a graph $G = (V, E)$, $|V| = n$, $|E| = m$, split $V$ into two sides to maximise the number of edges between them. Label a split by $z\in\{0, 1\}^n$; $\mathrm{cut}(z)$ counts edges with $z_u\ne z_v$. NP-hard (Karp 1972, cited); the cut fraction is $\mathrm{cut}(z)/m$.

the cost operator $$C = \sum_{uv\in E}\tfrac12\big(1 - Z_uZ_v\big),$$ $$C\ket z = \mathrm{cut}(z)\ket z.$$

Proof. $Z_uZ_v\ket z = (-1)^{z_u\oplus z_v}\ket z$, so $\tfrac12(1 - Z_uZ_v)$ is 1 on cut edges, 0 on the rest. ∎ Diagonal and real, so Hermitian ✓. Check, one edge: $C = \mathrm{diag}(0, 1, 1, 0)$ on $\ket{00}, \ket{01}, \ket{10}, \ket{11}$ ✓.

On the cube, splitting by the first bit cuts 4 edges: this $\ket z$ is an eigenvector of $C$ with eigenvalue 4.

Why a diagonal operator. Every classical optimisation problem over bit strings becomes, in this way, a diagonal Hermitian operator whose eigenvalues are the objective values and whose eigenvectors are the basis states. The largest eigenvalue is the maximum cut. Nothing quantum has happened yet: $C$ is a table of numbers written as a matrix. What is quantum is the state the algorithm prepares and measures.

NP-hardness, cited. The decision version of Max-Cut is one of Karp's 21 NP-complete problems (R. M. Karp, "Reducibility among combinatorial problems", 1972). So no polynomial-time algorithm, classical or quantum, is expected to find the maximum cut of every graph; the question becomes how close one can get, which section 4 makes precise.

The figure's count. Splitting by the first bit puts $0xy$ on one side and $1xy$ on the other; an edge crosses iff it flips the first bit, and those are the four edges $0xy$–$1xy$. In the course's qubit order (qubit 0 the leftmost bit), this is $z = 00001111$ read as vertices $000, \dots, 111$ in order; $C\ket z = 4\ket z$.

The depth-one circuit

The cube's circuit, qubit $000$ on top. Each linked pair of dots is $e^{-i\gamma(1 - Z_uZ_v)/2}$; they commute, so their order is free.

QAOA, depth one $$\ket{\gamma, \beta} = e^{-i\beta B}e^{-i\gamma C}\ket+^{\otimes n},$$ $$B = \textstyle\sum_vX_v .$$

$B$ is the mixer. Measure in the computational basis: the outcome is a cut.

Unitary: $C$, $B$ Hermitian, so the exponentials are unitary (unit 00). The $Z_uZ_v$ commute, so $e^{-i\gamma C}$ is a product over edges; the $X_v$ commute, so $e^{-i\beta B} = \bigotimes_vR_x(2\beta)$.

Check, $\gamma = 0$: $\ket+^{\otimes n}$ up to global phase, a uniform random cut, $\langle C\rangle = m/2$.

Each piece, checked. $\ket+^{\otimes n} = H^{\otimes n}\ket{0\cdots0}$ (unit 13) is the uniform superposition of all $2^n$ cuts. $e^{-i\gamma(1 - Z_uZ_v)/2}$ acts on $\ket{z_uz_v}$ as the phase $1$ if $z_u = z_v$ and $e^{-i\gamma}$ if not: the matrix $\mathrm{diag}(1, e^{-i\gamma}, e^{-i\gamma}, 1)$, unitary ✓. It costs two CNOTs and an $R_z$: $\mathrm{CNOT}_{uv}$ sends $\ket{ab}$ to $\ket{a, a\oplus b}$, $R_z(-\gamma)$ on $v$ multiplies by $e^{i\gamma/2}$ if $a\oplus b = 0$ and $e^{-i\gamma/2}$ if not, and a second $\mathrm{CNOT}_{uv}$ restores $\ket{ab}$ — so the product is $e^{i\gamma/2}\,\mathrm{diag}(1, e^{-i\gamma}, e^{-i\gamma}, 1)$, the gate up to global phase. $e^{-i\beta X} = \cos\beta\,I - i\sin\beta\,X$, which is unit 02's $R_x(2\beta)$ — a rotation by $2\beta$ about the $x$-axis — so the mixer rotates every qubit by the same angle.

The $\gamma = 0$ check. $e^{-i\beta B}$ fixes $\ket+^{\otimes n}$ up to the global phase $e^{-i\beta n}$, since $X\ket+ = \ket+$. Measuring $\ket+^{\otimes n}$ gives every $z$ with probability $2^{-n}$; by section 4's first theorem the expected cut is $m/2$. So the circuit starts at the random cut and the two angles are what improve on it.

The name and the depth. Quantum Approximate Optimisation Algorithm: E. Farhi, J. Goldstone and S. Gutmann, arXiv:1411.4028 (2014). Depth $p$ alternates $p$ times, $\prod_{k=1}^{p}e^{-i\beta_kB}e^{-i\gamma_kC}$, with $2p$ angles. It was motivated as a discretised adiabatic algorithm: for large $p$ and slowly varying angles it approximates a slow interpolation from $B$ to $C$, which finds the maximum cut in the limit (cited). Depth one is the case that can be analysed exactly.

A variational algorithm: the classical loop around it

a variational algorithm

A short parametrised circuit run many times; from the samples a classical computer estimates the objective, $\langle C\rangle = \braket{\gamma, \beta|C|\gamma, \beta}$, and adjusts the parameters. The output is the best cut sampled.

Why it was proposed for near-term hardware: depth one is one layer of two-qubit gates per matching and one of single-qubit rotations — short enough to finish before decoherence, with no error correction.

what is not guaranteed

Nothing promises that the outer optimiser finds the best angles, that the best angles beat a classical algorithm, or that noise leaves $\langle C\rangle$ intact. This section computes what the best angles give at depth one; section 4 asks whether that beats the classical algorithms.

The quantum computer is a sampler inside a classical optimisation loop.

How many samples. $\langle C\rangle$ is estimated by averaging the cut values of samples, so its error falls like $\sigma/\sqrt{s}$ for $s$ samples, with $\sigma$ the spread of the cut under $\ket{\gamma, \beta}$. On the cube at the best angles $\sigma = 2.02$ edges (computed from the distribution on Worked: 8.31 of 12 on the cube), so one standard error of 0.1 edges takes $(2.02/0.1)^2\approx400$ samples per setting of the angles — and an optimiser tries many settings.

The family. The variational quantum eigensolver (Peruzzo et al., Nature Communications 5, 4213, 2014) does the same with a chemistry Hamiltonian in place of $C$, aiming at its lowest eigenvalue. Both share the open problems in the box, and a further one for deep or wide circuits: for many parametrised families the gradient of the objective is exponentially small in $n$ at a random starting point ("barren plateaus", McClean et al., Nature Communications 9, 4812, 2018, cited), so the classical loop cannot tell which way to go.

Noise. Under unit 21's depolarising noise every qubit drifts towards $I/2$, whose measurement is a uniform random cut. So noise pulls $\langle C\rangle$ towards $m/2$, the random cut's value — exactly the baseline the algorithm is meant to beat.

Only a neighbourhood matters

Split $\langle C\rangle$ by edges. By linearity, $\langle C\rangle = \sum_{uv}\langle C_{uv}\rangle$ with $C_{uv} = \tfrac12(1 - Z_uZ_v)$, and

$$\langle Z_uZ_v\rangle = \bra{+^n}U^\dagger Z_uZ_v\,U\ket{+^n},$$

with $U = e^{-i\beta B}e^{-i\gamma C}$.

the light cone of an edge

$U^\dagger Z_uZ_vU$ acts only on $u$, $v$ and their neighbours. So $\langle C_{uv}\rangle$ depends only on the subgraph within distance one of the edge.

Why. Conjugating by $e^{-i\beta B}$ touches only $X_u$, $X_v$: the others commute with $Z_uZ_v$. Conjugating by $e^{-i\gamma C}$ then touches only edges at $u$ or $v$. The next slide does both.

For a 3-regular graph with no triangles, every edge sees the same six-vertex tree — so every edge has the same $\langle C_{uv}\rangle$.

The Heisenberg picture. Unit 01 wrote an expectation as $\braket{\psi|A|\psi}$. With $\ket\psi = U\ket{\psi_0}$ it is $\braket{\psi_0|U^\dagger AU|\psi_0}$: instead of evolving the state forwards, conjugate the observable backwards. Here $U^\dagger = e^{i\gamma C}e^{i\beta B}$, so $U^\dagger AU = e^{i\gamma C}\big(e^{i\beta B}Ae^{-i\beta B}\big)e^{-i\gamma C}$: the mixer acts on $A$ first, then the cost. This is unit 24's propagation rule $UEU^\dagger$ run in the other direction.

Full proof of the light cone

$e^{i\beta B} = \prod_we^{i\beta X_w}$, and $e^{i\beta X_w}$ commutes with $Z_uZ_v$ for $w\ne u, v$; so $e^{i\beta B}Z_uZ_ve^{-i\beta B} = e^{i\beta(X_u + X_v)}Z_uZ_ve^{-i\beta(X_u + X_v)}$, an operator on qubits $u, v$ alone — a combination of products $P_uQ_v$ of Paulis. Next, $e^{i\gamma C} = \prod_{ab\in E}e^{i\gamma(1 - Z_aZ_b)/2}$, and every factor with $\{a, b\}\cap\{u, v\} = \emptyset$ commutes with any operator on $u, v$ and cancels against its inverse. The surviving factors are the edges at $u$ or $v$, which involve only $u$, $v$ and their neighbours. Finally, $\ket{+^n}$ is a product state, so the expectation of an operator on a set $S$ of qubits depends only on the qubits in $S$. ∎

Why "the same tree". In a 3-regular graph each of $u$, $v$ has two neighbours besides the other. With no triangles, $u$'s and $v$'s other neighbours are four distinct vertices — a shared one would close a triangle. The edges among those four, if any, never appear in the surviving factors, so the neighbourhood is always this tree. At depth $p$ the light cone has radius $p$, and the analysis needs no short cycles up to length about $2p + 1$.

lemma

(c) $e^{i\gamma C}Y_ue^{-i\gamma C} = Y_u\prod_{w\in N(u)}\big(\cos\gamma + i\sin\gamma\,Z_uZ_w\big)$.

proof

(b): $e^{i\theta A}P = Pe^{-i\theta A}$ term by term in the series, since $A^kP = (-1)^kPA^k$. (a): (b) with $A = X$, $P = Z$, then $e^{-2i\beta X} = \cos2\beta - i\sin2\beta\,X$ and $-iZX = -i(iY) = Y$. Where triangles enter, and what depth buys

triangle-freeness was used once

In step 5. If $u$ and $v$ share a neighbour $w$, the factors $Z_uZ_w$ and $Z_vZ_w$ together leave $Z_uZ_v$, the $w$'s cancel, and $\langle Y_uY_v\rangle\ne0$. The formula changes.

It must. $K_4$ is 3-regular, 6 edges, maximum cut 4. The triangle-free formula would promise $6\times0.6925 = 4.15\gt4$: impossible.

Deeper circuits. At depth $p$ the light cone has radius $p$; on graphs with no short cycles the edge expectation is again one number per $p$, computed by heavier versions of the same method. It rises with $p$ — and as $p\to\infty$ QAOA finds the maximum cut (cited).

the honest summary

No depth of QAOA has been shown to beat the best known classical algorithm on any family of Max-Cut instances; and for depth one the classical algorithms of the next section provably guarantee more.

What changes on $K_4$. In step 5, each common neighbour $w$ contributes the term $(i\sin\gamma)^2\cos^2\gamma\,Y_uY_vZ_uZ_v$ from choosing $w$ on both sides, and $Y_uZ_u\,Y_vZ_v = (iX_u)(iX_v) = -X_uX_v$, so a single common neighbour contributes $+\sin^2\gamma\cos^2\gamma$ to $\langle Y_uY_v\rangle$. $K_4$ has two common neighbours per edge; problem P6 completes the calculation and finds, at the triangle-free optimum, $0.6925 - \tfrac19 = 0.5813$ per edge. The extra term is negative there: triangles make Max-Cut frustrated (three vertices cannot be pairwise separated), and the formula must respect that.

Depth $p$, cited. For large-girth $d$-regular graphs the optimal values have been computed numerically to $p$ of about 11 or more (for instance Basso, Farhi, Marwaha, Villalonga and Zhou, "The quantum approximate optimization algorithm at high depth for MaxCut on large-girth regular graphs and the Sherrington–Kirkpatrick model", TQC 2022). For 3-regular graphs depth two gives about 0.7559 on graphs of girth above 5 (J. Wurtz and P. Love, PRA 103, 042612, 2021, cited). The large-$p$ limit is the adiabatic algorithm, whose running time depends on a spectral gap that can close exponentially fast on hard instances.

Locality as a limit. At fixed depth QAOA is a local algorithm: each edge's statistics depend on a bounded neighbourhood. Results in the notes of the next section show that local algorithms of any kind, classical or quantum, are limited on some families of graphs, which caps what fixed-depth QAOA can achieve there regardless of hardware.

A quantum algorithm's number means nothing alone. Here are the classical numbers it competes with — from the trivial to the best known.

26 · baselines

The floor: a coin per vertex, and local search

two classical half-guarantees

(i) A uniformly random cut has expected size $m/2$.

(ii) Local search — move any vertex with more uncut than cut edges to the other side, until none is left — stops within $m$ moves at a cut of at least $m/2$.

proof

(i) Each edge's two independent fair bits differ with probability $\tfrac12$; add over edges. (ii) A move raises the cut by at least 1, and the cut is at most $m$. At the end every vertex has at least half its edges cut; summing over vertices counts each cut edge twice: $2\,\mathrm{cut}\ge\tfrac12\sum_v\deg v = m$. ∎

So depth one's 0.6925 is measured against $\tfrac12$ — and local search guarantees $\tfrac12$ only as a floor.

A vertex with two of three edges uncut moves; the cut gains one. When no vertex can move, each has at least half its edges cut.

The proofs in one more line each. (i) For edge $uv$, $\Pr[z_u\ne z_v] = \Pr[z_u = 0, z_v = 1] + \Pr[z_u = 1, z_v = 0] = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14$; linearity of expectation needs no independence between edges. (ii) Moving $v$ swaps its cut and uncut incident edges and changes no other edge, so the cut changes by $(\#\text{uncut at }v) - (\#\text{cut at }v)\ge1$. The handshake identity $\sum_v\deg v = 2m$ finishes it.

Derandomised. (i) is also achieved deterministically by the method of conditional expectations: decide vertices one at a time, each time choosing the side that does not lower the conditional expectation; the final cut is at least $m/2$. Problem P4 asks for a local optimum of the cube, which shows that the floor in (ii) can bind well below the maximum.

The comparison, on the cube. A random cut: 6 edges. Local search: at least 6, and it can stop at a cut worse than the maximum (P4). Depth-one QAOA: 8.31 in expectation.

26 · baselines

Goemans–Williamson: vectors, then a random hyperplane

the Goemans–Williamson algorithm

Relax. Replace each $\pm1$ label by a unit vector $\mathbf v_u\in\mathbb R^n$ and maximise $\sum_{uv}\tfrac12(1 - \mathbf v_u\cdot\mathbf v_v)$ — a semidefinite program, solvable to any accuracy in polynomial time (cited). Its value is $\ge$ the maximum cut: $\mathbf v_u = \pm\mathbf e$ is allowed.

Round. Pick a uniformly random direction $\mathbf r$; put $u$ on the side of $\mathrm{sign}(\mathbf r\cdot\mathbf v_u)$.

the guarantee

An edge at angle $\theta$ is cut with probability $\theta/\pi$, against its relaxed value $\tfrac12(1 - \cos\theta)$. So

$$\mathbb E[\mathrm{cut}]\ge\alpha\cdot\mathrm{OPT},$$ $$\alpha = \min_{0\lt\theta\le\pi}\frac{2\theta}{\pi(1 - \cos\theta)} = 0.8786 .$$

In the plane of the two vectors, the line perpendicular to $\mathbf r$ points in a uniform direction; it separates them iff it falls in one of the shaded wedges, angle $2\theta$ of $2\pi$.

Full proof of the guarantee

The rounding lemma. Let $\mathbf r$ be uniform on the unit sphere (or Gaussian — only its direction matters). Its projection onto the plane of $\mathbf v_u, \mathbf v_v$ points in a uniformly random direction, by rotational symmetry. $\mathbf v_u$ and $\mathbf v_v$ land on opposite sides iff the line through the origin perpendicular to that projection passes between them. That line's direction is uniform on a half-turn of length $\pi$, and it passes between them for an arc of directions of length $\theta$. So $\Pr[\text{cut}] = \theta/\pi$.

The ratio. $\mathbb E[\mathrm{cut}] = \sum_{uv}\theta_{uv}/\pi\ge\alpha\sum_{uv}\tfrac12(1 - \cos\theta_{uv}) = \alpha\cdot\mathrm{SDP}\ge\alpha\cdot\mathrm{OPT}$, the middle step because $\theta/\pi\ge\alpha\cdot\tfrac12(1 - \cos\theta)$ for every $\theta$ by the definition of $\alpha$. Minimising $2\theta/(\pi(1 - \cos\theta))$ numerically: the minimum is $0.878567$ at $\theta = 2.3311$ rad $= 133.56^\circ$. (M. X. Goemans and D. P. Williamson, J. ACM 42, 1115, 1995.)

Where the SDP is solved. The relaxation is a semidefinite program in the Gram matrix $X_{uv} = \mathbf v_u\cdot\mathbf v_v$, which must be positive semidefinite with unit diagonal; interior-point methods solve it to accuracy $\varepsilon$ in time polynomial in $n$ and $\log(1/\varepsilon)$. The sister course Combinatorial Optimization, unit 25, builds semidefinite programming and this algorithm from the beginning (cited; nothing here depends on it).

Is 0.878 the end? Assuming Khot's Unique Games Conjecture, no polynomial-time algorithm achieves a better ratio on all graphs unless P = NP (Khot, Kindler, Mossel and O'Donnell, 2007, cited); unconditionally, beating $16/17 = 0.941$ is NP-hard (Håstad 2001, cited). These are statements about classical algorithms; a quantum algorithm beating them on all graphs would put an NP-hard problem in BQP, which nobody expects (unit 12, for readers who took it).

Ratio against fraction, and degree three

two different numbers

The approximation ratio is $\mathrm{cut}/\mathrm{OPT}$; the cut fraction is $\mathrm{cut}/m$. Since $\mathrm{OPT}\le m$, ratio $\ge$ fraction. QAOA's $0.6925$ is a fraction; Goemans–Williamson's $0.878$ is a ratio. On a bipartite graph $\mathrm{OPT} = m$ and the two agree.

  • simple local classical algorithms — each vertex decides from its own neighbourhood, as depth-one QAOA does — match or beat depth one on triangle-free graphs (Hastings 2019; Barak and Marwaha 2022).

A family of instances, a quantum algorithm and a measured or proved cut ratio beyond what any known classical algorithm achieves on the same instances in comparable time. The running example on one line

Guarantees as cut fractions, with the cube's edge counts beneath; the last two are worst-case ratios that the cube beats.

the cube needs no algorithm

A graph has a cut of all $m$ edges iff it is bipartite, and a breadth-first search 2-colours a bipartite graph in time $O(n + m)$. For the cube, colour by parity: 12 of 12.

And Goemans–Williamson finds 12 too. An SDP value of 12 forces $\mathbf v_u = -\mathbf v_v$ on every edge, so all vectors are $\pm\mathbf v_{000}$, and every hyperplane separates the two classes.

The baselines: guarantees on the line, and on this instance the classical methods reach the maximum.

Bipartite, proved. A cut of all edges is exactly a 2-colouring with no edge inside a colour class: the definition of bipartite. Breadth-first search from any vertex colours by parity of distance; an edge joining two vertices of the same parity would close an odd cycle, and a graph is bipartite iff it has no odd cycle (standard). Each vertex and edge is handled once: $O(n + m)$.

Goemans–Williamson on the cube, in full. The SDP value is at most $m = 12$, and the parity vectors $\pm\mathbf e$ achieve 12, so the optimum is 12. Value 12 needs $\tfrac12(1 - \mathbf v_u\cdot\mathbf v_v) = 1$ on every edge, so $\mathbf v_v = -\mathbf v_u$; the cube is connected, so every $\mathbf v_u = \pm\mathbf v_{000}$ according to parity. A random hyperplane separates $\mathbf v_{000}$ from $-\mathbf v_{000}$ with probability 1. So the algorithm's output is the maximum cut, not merely 0.878 of it.

The numbers on the line. $0.5\times12 = 6$; $0.6925\times12 = 8.31$; $0.8786\times12 = 10.54$; $0.9326\times12 = 11.19$. The last two are worst-case guarantees over all graphs; on the cube, as the previous paragraph shows, Goemans–Williamson does better and finds 12.

section 5

Advantage claims, read as theorems

Two cautionary tales — a real separation whose baseline moved, and an exponential speedup that turned out to be classical — and the arithmetic of a slow clock.

26 · claims

What an advantage claim claims

a quantum advantage claim

A task, precisely specified; a quantum device that performs it in time $t_Q$; and a classical baseline: the best known classical algorithm, on the best available hardware, taking time $t_C\gg t_Q$.

what kind of statement it is

Unlike $t_Q$, $t_C$ is not measured but estimated, and only for the algorithms known that year. A better classical algorithm falsifies the claim without touching the experiment.

Why only "known". Proving that no classical algorithm can do the task is far beyond what anyone can prove (notes). So every claim rests on the baseline, and the baseline is part of the claim.

Three questions to ask of any claim:

  1. Exactly which classical algorithm was $t_C$ computed for?
  2. Is the classical algorithm given the same input — the same access to the data?
  3. Does the task have any use, or is it chosen to be hard?

Why the baseline cannot be proved. For a sampling task, "no efficient classical algorithm" is a statement of the kind unit 12 discusses (for readers who took it): it would imply that some quantum class is strictly larger than its classical counterpart, which in turn would separate P from PSPACE. The strongest available evidence is conditional: if certain sampling tasks were easy classically, the polynomial hierarchy would collapse (Aaronson and Arkhipov 2011; Bremner, Jozsa and Shepherd 2011, cited). Those results concern exact or very accurate sampling; experiments sample with fidelity well below 1, which is where classical algorithms have found room.

The names. "Quantum supremacy" (Preskill, 2012) is the original term for a task beyond any classical computer, useful or not; "advantage" is now more common. Neither has a formal definition; both mean the triple on the slide.

The three questions organise the rest of the section: question 1 is the random-circuit-sampling episode, question 2 is dequantisation, and question 3 is the gap between both and factoring (unit 16, for readers who took it; the capstone uses it).

26 · claims

Random-circuit sampling

random-circuit sampling

Choose a circuit at random: layers of random single-qubit gates and fixed two-qubit gates on neighbouring qubits. Run it on $\ket{0\cdots0}$ and measure, many times. The task: output samples from its distribution $p(x) = |\braket{x|U|0\cdots0}|^2$.

Why it is hard. No structure: each amplitude is a sum over exponentially many paths through the circuit, and a deep enough random circuit leaves no shortcut known.

Why it is chosen. It is the most natural thing a device can do — run its native gates — and it needs no error correction. It has no known use.

A brickwork random circuit. After enough layers every output bit depends on every input, and no structure is left to exploit.

The proposal. S. Boixo et al., "Characterizing quantum supremacy in near-term devices", Nature Physics 14, 595 (2018). The 2019 experiment used a two-dimensional grid of 53 superconducting qubits, single-qubit gates chosen at random from a small set, and a fixed two-qubit gate native to the chip, in 20 cycles.

Why "no shortcut known". The obvious classical method stores the $2^n$ amplitudes: at $n = 53$, $2^{53} = 9.0\times10^{15}$ complex numbers, 72 petabytes in single precision. Other methods trade memory for time: summing over paths (Feynman) needs little memory and time exponential in the number of gates; tensor-network contraction, which treats the circuit as a network of small tensors and contracts it in a clever order, has cost exponential in a "width" of the network that depends on its depth and geometry. Each is exponential in something; the experiment was sized so that all known methods were expensive.

Why "no use". The samples are, by design, featureless: they are interesting only because producing them is hard. Proposals to use certified randomness from such experiments exist and are named here, not developed.

Scoring samples you cannot check

the linear cross-entropy benchmark $$F_{\rm XEB} = 2^n\,\big\langle p(x_i)\big\rangle_i - 1,$$

the average over the observed samples $x_i$ of their ideal probabilities $p(x_i)$, computed classically. A device that favours the likely outcomes scores high.

the Porter–Thomas distribution (cited)

For a random circuit deep enough, the scaled probabilities $t = 2^np(x)$ over outcomes $x$ are distributed approximately as $e^{-t}$, $t\ge0$.

The catch: computing $p(x_i)$ is the hard classical task. At 53 qubits it was possible only for simplified circuits, and for the full ones it was extrapolated.

Computed: a 40-layer random circuit on 12 qubits. Its 4096 values $2^np(x)$, binned, follow $e^{-t}$; their mean is 1, their mean square 2.05.

Porter–Thomas, cited. C. E. Porter and R. G. Thomas (1956) found this distribution for the strengths of nuclear transitions. For random states the statement is exact in a limit: if $\ket\psi$ is uniformly random on the unit sphere of $\mathbb C^N$, the scaled probabilities $Np(x)$ converge in distribution to $e^{-t}$ as $N\to\infty$, and $\mathbb E\sum_xp(x)^2 = 2/(N + 1)$ exactly (cited). That random circuits of sufficient depth approach this is supported by theory and numerics (Boixo et al. 2018, cited) — and by the figure.

The figure's numbers. Bin by bin (width $\tfrac12$), the histogram's densities are 0.792, 0.492, 0.278, 0.168, 0.098, 0.070 against the exponential's bin averages 0.787, 0.477, 0.289, 0.176, 0.107, 0.065. The mean of $t$ is exactly 1 (the probabilities sum to 1); the mean of $t^2$ is $2.05$ against the exponential's $2$.

Why "cross-entropy". The original proposal scored samples by the average of $-\log p(x_i)$, a cross-entropy; the linear version replaces the logarithm by $p$ itself, which is simpler to analyse and estimate. The worked example on the next slide shows what it measures.

26 · claims

Worked: XEB for three samplers

worked example: the expected $F_{\rm XEB}$, with $N = 2^n$

Uniform noise. $x$ uniform: $\mathbb E\,p(x) = \sum_x\tfrac1Np(x) = \tfrac1N$. So $F = N\cdot\tfrac1N - 1 = 0$, exactly, whatever the circuit.

Ideal sampler. $x\sim p$: $\mathbb E\,p(x) = \sum_xp(x)^2$. Under Porter–Thomas each $p(x)\approx t/N$ with $\mathbb E\,t^2 = \int_0^\infty t^2e^{-t}\,dt = 2$: $$\textstyle\sum_xp(x)^2\approx N\cdot\frac2{N^2} = \frac2N,\qquad F\approx N\cdot\frac2N - 1 = 1 .$$

A noisy device, modelled as sampling $q = f\,p + (1 - f)\tfrac1N$ — ideal with probability $f$, uniform otherwise. By linearity, $$F\approx f\cdot1 + (1 - f)\cdot0 = f .$$

Check. $q$ is a distribution: $\sum_xq(x) = f + (1 - f) = 1$ ✓. On the 12-qubit circuit of the figure: ideal $1.05$, uniform $0$, $f = 0.002$ gives $0.0021$.

So $F_{\rm XEB}$ estimates the fidelity $f$: the 2019 experiment reported $F_{\rm XEB}$ of about 0.2% — a signal of 1 part in 500 on top of noise, from a million samples per circuit.

The second moment. $\int_0^\infty t^2e^{-t}\,dt = 2! = 2$ (integrate by parts twice). The approximation in "Ideal sampler" is a law of large numbers over the $N$ outcomes: $\sum_xp(x)^2 = \frac1{N^2}\sum_xt_x^2$, and the average of $t_x^2$ over $N$ outcomes is close to 2 when $N$ is large. For an exactly uniformly random state $\mathbb E\sum p^2 = 2/(N + 1)$, so $\mathbb E F = (N - 1)/(N + 1)$: $0.998$ at $N = 1024$ and $1 - 2.2\times10^{-16}$ at $N = 2^{53}$.

How many samples. For a device near uniform, each $2^np(x_i)$ has mean about 1 and variance about 1 (the exponential's), so the average of $s$ samples has standard error about $1/\sqrt s$. To see $F = 0.002$ at five standard errors needs $1/\sqrt s = 0.0004$: $s = 6.25\times10^6$ samples. Problem P8 does this.

As reported. F. Arute et al., "Quantum supremacy using a programmable superconducting processor", Nature 574, 505 (2019): 53 qubits, 20 cycles, $F_{\rm XEB}\approx0.2\%$, a million samples in about 200 seconds. The fidelity of the full circuits could not be computed directly; it was inferred from simplified versions whose $p(x)$ could be computed, and from a model multiplying the measured error rates of individual gates.

What XEB cannot do. It scores a sampler without checking that the samples come from the circuit; a cleverly designed classical algorithm that outputs strings with somewhat-high $p(x)$ can score above zero without simulating the circuit faithfully. Later classical attacks did exactly that (for instance X. Gao et al., "Limitations of linear cross-entropy as a measure for quantum advantage", arXiv:2112.01657, 2021; PRX Quantum 5, 010334, 2024, cited).

Recommendation systems: an exponential speedup, claimed

The problem. A matrix $A$ of $m$ users by $n$ products, close to rank $k\ll m, n$. To recommend to user $i$: sample a product $j$ with probability proportional to $|(A_k)_{ij}|^2$, $A_k$ the best rank-$k$ approximation.

Kerenidis and Prakash (2017): a quantum algorithm in time polynomial in $k$ and polylogarithmic in $mn$ — while known classical algorithms read the whole matrix, polynomial in $mn$. Exponentially faster.

the input model

The quantum algorithm assumes $A$ is stored in a tree of partial sums of squares (figure), from which it loads a row as a quantum state in polylog time.

The data structure both algorithms are given. Its root-to-leaf walk is the classical twin of measuring the state $\sum_jx_j\ket j/\|x\|$.

The citation. I. Kerenidis and A. Prakash, "Quantum recommendation systems", ITCS 2017 (arXiv:1603.08675, 2016). The algorithm uses phase estimation (unit 15, for readers who took it) on a matrix built from $A$ to project a user's row onto the top singular subspace, then measures. Its running time is polynomial in $k$, in the precision, and in a condition-number-like parameter, and polylogarithmic in $m$ and $n$.

The data structure. A binary tree over each row, whose leaves are $|A_{ij}|^2$ (and signs) and whose internal nodes hold the sum of their leaves; one more tree over the rows' norms. It is built once in time linear in the number of nonzero entries, and updated in $O(\log n)$ per new entry. With it, a quantum computer prepares $\ket{A_i} = \sum_jA_{ij}\ket j/\|A_i\|$ by rotating one qubit per level, each rotation's angle read from the tree (L. Grover and T. Rudolph, arXiv:quant-ph/0208112, 2002, cited). This is the "quantum RAM" assumption.

Why the comparison looked exponential. Classical algorithms for low-rank approximation, such as computing a truncated SVD, read every entry: time at least linear in $mn$ (or in the number of nonzeros). The quantum algorithm read polylogarithmically many entries — but only because the tree had been built in advance.

Dequantisation: the same input, sampled classically

sample-and-query access to $x\in\mathbb R^n$

Query any $x_j$; know $\|x\|$; and draw $j$ with probability $x_j^2/\|x\|^2$. The tree provides each in $O(\log n)$ time.

inner products, independent of $n$

Draw $j$ as above and set $Z = \|x\|^2y_j/x_j$. Then $\mathbb EZ = \braket{x|y}$ and $\mathbb EZ^2\le\|x\|^2\|y\|^2$. Averaging $\lceil1/(\delta\varepsilon^2)\rceil$ copies estimates $\braket{x|y}$ to within $\varepsilon\|x\|\|y\|$ with probability $\ge1 - \delta$.

proof

$\mathbb EZ = \sum_j\frac{x_j^2}{\|x\|^2}\cdot\frac{\|x\|^2y_j}{x_j} = \sum_jx_jy_j$; $\ \mathbb EZ^2 = \sum_j\frac{x_j^2}{\|x\|^2}\cdot\frac{\|x\|^4y_j^2}{x_j^2}\le\|x\|^2\|y\|^2$, sums over $x_j\ne0$. The mean of $s$ copies has variance $\le\|x\|^2\|y\|^2/s$; apply Chebyshev. ∎

Dequantisation (Tang 2018): with sample-and-query access to $A$ — exactly what the tree gives — a classical algorithm samples recommendations in time polynomial in $k$ and the precision and polylogarithmic in $mn$. The exponential speedup is gone; a polynomial one, with large exponents, remains.

the lesson

An exponential speedup claim must state its input model, and the classical rival gets the same model. Such quantum-inspired algorithms are part of the baseline.

The citation. E. Tang, "A quantum-inspired classical algorithm for recommendation systems", STOC 2019 (arXiv:1807.04271, 2018), written as an undergraduate thesis; the original aim was to prove a classical lower bound, and the algorithm appeared instead. The method rests on length-squared sampling of rows and columns to build a small sketch of $A$ whose top singular vectors approximate $A$'s (A. Frieze, R. Kannan and S. Vempala, J. ACM 51, 1025, 2004, cited), with the inner-product estimator above used throughout. Similar dequantisations followed for quantum algorithms for low-rank linear systems, principal component analysis and supervised clustering (Chia, Gilyén, Li, Lin, Tang and Wang, STOC 2020, cited).

The proof's details. Entries with $x_j = 0$ are never sampled, so $Z$ is well defined; the expectation sums over $x_j\ne0$, and the terms with $x_j = 0$ contribute 0 to $\braket{x|y}$ anyway. Chebyshev: for the mean $\bar Z$ of $s$ independent copies, $\Pr[|\bar Z - \braket{x|y}|\ge\varepsilon\|x\|\|y\|]\le\mathrm{Var}\,\bar Z/(\varepsilon\|x\|\|y\|)^2\le1/(s\varepsilon^2)$, and $s\ge1/(\delta\varepsilon^2)$ makes this at most $\delta$. The median of several such means improves the $\delta$ dependence to $\log(1/\delta)$ (Chernoff–Hoeffding, background). For complex vectors draw $j$ with probability $|x_j|^2/\|x\|^2$ and keep $Z = \|x\|^2y_j/x_j$: since $|x_j|^2/x_j = \bar x_j$, $\mathbb EZ = \sum_j\bar x_jy_j = \braket{x|y}$ in the course's convention, and $\mathbb E|Z|^2\le\|x\|^2\|y\|^2$ as before.

Why it is fair. Measuring the state $\ket{x} = \sum_jx_j\ket j/\|x\|$ in the computational basis gives $j$ with probability $x_j^2/\|x\|^2$ — the same distribution the tree samples. A quantum algorithm whose input is "states prepared from this tree" has, implicitly, the classical sampler too. The quantum algorithm can do more with the state than sample it, and that "more" is what a surviving speedup must use. What remains is polynomial: Tang's first bound had very high degree in $k$ and $1/\varepsilon$; later work lowered it, but quantum and classical are within a polynomial of each other.

26 · claims

Quadratic speedups against a slow clock

Unit 17: search over $N$ items takes $\tfrac N2$ classical checks on average, $\frac\pi4\sqrt N$ Grover iterations. Classical checks run at $r$ per second; an error-corrected iteration takes $t$ seconds. Equal times when

$$\frac{N}{2r} = \frac\pi4\sqrt N\,t\iff\sqrt{N^*} = \frac\pi2\,r\,t .$$

Assume $t = 1$ ms — a thousand surface-code cycles of about a microsecond: generous (notes).

  • One core, $r = 10^9$/s: $N^* = 2.5\times10^{12}$, reached after 20 minutes.
  • A million cores: $N^* = 2.5\times10^{24}$, after 39 years.

Classical search parallelises perfectly; quantum search gains only $\sqrt P$ from $P$ machines (Zalka, unit 17).

The crossover size moves with the classical hardware; a quadratic speedup pays only on searches that take decades.

The arithmetic. $\sqrt{N^*} = \frac\pi2\times10^9\times10^{-3} = 1.571\times10^6$, $N^* = 2.467\times10^{12}$, and the common time is $N^*/2r = 1234$ s $= 20.6$ minutes. With $r = 10^{15}$: $\sqrt{N^*} = 1.571\times10^{12}$, $N^* = 2.467\times10^{24}$, time $1.234\times10^9$ s $= 39.1$ years. Each factor $P$ in classical speed moves the crossover by $P^2$ in $N$ and by $P$ in time.

Why a millisecond is generous. A Grover iteration evaluates the oracle reversibly and uncomputes it; for a cipher that is thousands of logical gates in sequence (unit 17's AES notes), each logical operation costs about $d$ code cycles (unit 24) of about a microsecond each (1.1 µs in the 2024 below-threshold experiment, as reported), and every $T$ gate waits for a distilled magic state. A thousand cycles is a millisecond, enough for only a few dozen logical steps at $d = 21$. So $t$ of a millisecond or longer is the right order, which is the point made carefully by R. Babbush et al., "Focus beyond quadratic speedups for error-corrected quantum advantage", PRX Quantum 2, 010103 (2021, cited): with error-correction overheads, quadratic speedups need runs of days to years to break even, while cubic and quartic ones fare better.

Exponential speedups escape this. For factoring, the classical side's cost grows faster than any polynomial, and no classical parallelism closes an exponential gap; this is why Shor's algorithm, not Grover's, sets the capstone's budget.
